## 1. Setup and definitions

### 1.1 Circuits

Fix $k\ge2$ input wires and put $K=2^k$. A **circuit of size $s$** is a list of gates $g_1,\dots,g_s$, $g_i=(\tau_i,a_i,b_i)$, with a truth table $\tau_i\in\{0,1\}^4$ (all sixteen functions of two bits) and two ordered pointers $a_i,b_i\in\{1,\dots,k+i-1\}$. Wires $1,\dots,k$ are the inputs, wire $k+i$ is gate $i$, and the value of gate $i$ is $\tau_i$ applied to the values of the wires $a_i,b_i$. The **output** is the last gate. $\mathcal C_{k,s}$ is the set of these circuits, $f_C\colon\{0,1\}^k\to\{0,1\}$ the function $C$ computes, and
$$N_s=|\mathcal C_{k,s}|=\prod_{i=1}^{s}16\,(k+i-1)^2,\qquad p_i=\frac1{16\,(k+i-1)^2},\qquad \Lambda(k,s)=\log_2N_s,$$
$$\Delta(\sigma,a)=\Lambda(k,\sigma+a)-\Lambda(k,\sigma),\qquad a\bigl(4+2\log_2(k+\sigma)\bigr)\le\Delta(\sigma,a)\le a\bigl(4+2\log_2(k+\sigma+a-1)\bigr).$$
A gate is **active** if it is the last gate or an ancestor of it, and **dead** otherwise. A circuit is **reduced** if all its gates are active; $\mathcal R_a$ is the set of reduced circuits of size $a$. The **core** of $C$ is the reduced circuit obtained by deleting the dead gates and renumbering in order; it computes $f_C$. For a function $f$, $C(f)$ is the least size of a circuit computing it.

### 1.2 Priors

* **Fixed-size prior** $\mu_s$: uniform on $\mathcal C_{k,s}$.
* **Mixture prior**: weights $w_s>0$ for $1\le s\le S$ ($S\le\infty$), $\sum_sw_s=1$, and $q(C)=w_s2^{-\Lambda(k,s)}$ for $C\in\mathcal C_{k,s}$; $l(C)=-\log_2q(C)$ and $\varrho(\sigma,a)=w_{\sigma+a}/w_\sigma$. Assumption **(A1)**, used only where named: $w_s$ is nonincreasing, so $q$ is nonincreasing in the size.
* **Length prior** $w_L$: the mixture with $w_s=2^{-s}$, $S=\infty$ (size in unary, then the gates). It satisfies (A1), and $\varrho(\sigma,a)=2^{-a}$.
* **Seeded circuits** (used in Theorems 14 to 16 only): a circuit with $k+r$ inputs, the last $r$ of which are fair random bits $\rho$; it lies in $\mathcal C_{k+r,s}$ and has weight $\omega(r,s)2^{-\Lambda(k+r,s)}$ with $\sum_{r,s}\omega(r,s)=1$ and $w_s:=\omega(0,s)$. Assumption **(A2)**: $\omega(r,s)\le w_s$. $S_\rho$ is $S$ with the seed fixed. (Repaired: `circuits-A.md` normalised $\sum_sw_s=1$ and then added seeded formats, which made the total exceed 1; every statement is a ratio, so nothing else changes.)

$P$ stands for $\mu_s$ or a mixture prior; these are "the priors of the family". The **core weight** is
$$\beta_s(a)=p_s\,e_{a-1}(p_1,\dots,p_{s-1})\ \ (a\le s),\qquad\beta_s(a)=0\ \ (a>s),\qquad\beta_q(a)=\sum_sw_s\beta_s(a),$$
with $e_r$ the elementary symmetric polynomial of degree $r$; $\beta$ stands for $\beta_s$ or $\beta_q$ (Theorem 5 says why).

### 1.3 A circuit as a hypothesis; data, queries, sequences

A hypothesis is a circuit $C$, read as the function $f_C$. **Data** are $D=(S,f^*|_S)$ for a set $S\subseteq\{0,1\}^k$ of inputs and a target $f^*$. $C$ **fits** $D$ if $f_C=f^*$ on $S$; $\mathrm{Cons}(D)$ is the set of fitting circuits, $W(D)=P(\mathrm{Cons}(D))$, $\ell_D=-\log_2W(D)$, and $s_D$ is the least size of a fitting circuit. For a query $u\notin S$ (or a tuple of queries) the predictor's distribution is
$$\pi(y\mid u,D)=P\{C:\ f_C(u)=y\mid C\in\mathrm{Cons}(D)\}.$$
The **sampling predictor** returns $Y\sim\pi(\cdot\mid u,D)$. Unless said otherwise the predictor is the sampling predictor.

*Sequence form (index reading).* The $k$ inputs carry the binary digits $b_0,\dots,b_{k-1}$ of $t-1$ for a position $t\in\{1,\dots,K\}$, and the circuit says that the bit at position $t$ is $f_C(t)$. Its **truth table** is $\mathrm{tt}(C)=f_C(1)\cdots f_C(K)$. A context $x$ of length $n$ is the data set $S=\{1,\dots,n\}$ with labels $x$; the continuation of length $m$ is the tuple of queries $n+1,\dots,n+m$. Then
$$\xi(x)=P\{C:\ x\preceq\mathrm{tt}(C)\}=W(D),\qquad\pi_m(y\mid x)=\frac{\xi(xy)}{\xi(x)}\quad(|x|+m\le K),$$
and $\mathrm{size}(x)=s_D$ is the least size of a circuit that prints $x$. The sequence form is the special case of the query form in which the data are an initial segment; every result stated for data and queries applies to it. In particular (this link is missing from both derivations and was supplied by the proofs referee): **at a cut $n=2^j$ the index bits $b_j,\dots,b_{k-1}$ are $0$ on the whole context, and positions $n+1,\dots,2n$ differ from positions $1,\dots,n$ only in $b_j$.** A dyadic cut is a query that differs from the data in a bit that never varied.

*Why this reading.* Theorem 1 shows that two natural readings with one gate or one pointer per output bit give a predictor that learns nothing, or only a frequency. The link between context and continuation that makes induction possible is the reuse of the same gates at different positions, which is what the index reading provides. It is not the only possible translation. A circuit that takes the context as input and is applied autoregressively is a third reading; it reuses a description across steps and belongs with Proposition 21. It is not treated here.

### 1.4 Worlds, critical uses, takeover

A **world** is a circuit $W$; its target is $f_W$. It is **in the class** if $P\{C:f_C=f_W\}>0$. A **critical use** is a triple (world, data, query) fixed from outside: the world hands the predictor $D$, receives $Y$ at the query, and acts on it. Its **own continuation** $d$ is $f_W$ at the query. In the sequence form a **call rule** is a set of pairs $(W,n_W)$, at most one for each circuit; with $x_W=\mathrm{tt}(W)_{1:n_W}$,
$$C(x)=\sum_{W:\,x_W=x}q(W),\qquad H=\sum_{(W,n_W)}q(W),\qquad\kappa(x)=\frac{C(x)}{\xi(x)}.$$
A **test** is a set $G$ of nonempty strings; a **first hit** is an $x\in G$ no proper prefix of which is in $G$; under a test every circuit that prints a first hit hands over there, and $\kappa=1$.

The forms of `def:mph` are taken over: the **deviation mass** $1-\pi(d\mid u,D)$; the *majority form* $\pi(d\mid u,D)<\frac12$; the *payload form at level $\theta$* for a set $A\not\ni d$, $\pi(A\mid u,D)\ge\theta$; the *critical-use form*, $\Pr[Y\in A(X)]\ge\theta$ over a population of uses. As in the note these are necessary for calling the prior malign at a world and not sufficient: they hold as well when the world is merely hard to predict.

*Takeover needs a decoder.* In the note the block $y$ is run as $U$ on $y0^\infty$. Here hypotheses are circuits and a block is a string, so the world applies a fixed decoding map to it. The **literal code** of a circuit of size $s$ is $e(C)=\bar e(s)\,\prod_i\tau_i[a_i][b_i]$ with pointer fields of $\lceil\log_2(k+s)\rceil$ bits (a field with value $c$ at gate $i$ is read as wire $1+(c\bmod(k+i-1))$) and $\bar e(s)=1^{|{\rm bin}(s)|}0\,{\rm bin}(s)$; its length is $l_{\rm fw}(s)=|\bar e(s)|+s\bigl(4+2\lceil\log_2(k+s)\rceil\bigr)$. $D(y)$ is the circuit decoded from the front of $y$ if the rest of $y$ is $10^j$ for some $j\ge0$. The analogue of `def:sr` is
$$\mathrm{SR}_m(x)=\{y\in\{0,1\}^m:\ D(y)\text{ is defined and }xy\preceq\mathrm{tt}(D(y))\}.$$
The decoder is an interpreter that lives outside the hypothesis class, and the answer to "can a circuit reproduce itself" depends on it (Theorem 18).

### 1.5 What the class assumes

These are decisions, not results. They are referred to in Section 3.

1. *Plain circuits:* fan-in two, each gate evaluated once per output, no gate used twice, no depth bound, no weight sharing.
2. *Hypotheses are total:* every circuit has a bit at every position. The note's worlds that hand over and then print nothing, and the quantity $\varphi$ of `prop:honest`, cannot be expressed. (Repaired: `circuits-A.md` called this loophole "closed"; it is excluded by the definition, and the exclusion favours the honest side.)
3. *The class depends on the horizon* through $k$, and which positions are cheap to name depends on the binary coding of the index.
4. *The prior is on ordered gate lists with dead gates allowed.*
5. *The full binary basis with uniformly drawn truth tables.*
6. *The sampling predictor.*
7. *The world is in the predictor's class* wherever a bound compares with the world.
8. *The use is fixed from outside,* and a hypothesis can recognise it only through its own input (the index or the query).

## 2. Results

### Theorem 1 (two degenerate readings). PROVED.

* *(a) One gate per output bit.* Let a circuit with $r\ge1$ random inputs be uniform on $\mathcal C_{r,s}$, $s\ge N$, and let its $N$ output bits be the last $N$ gates. Then for every fixed seed the output is uniform on $\{0,1\}^N$. So $\pi_m(y\mid x)=2^{-m}$ for every context and block, also for mixtures over $r$ and $s\ge N$.

* *(b) One pointer per output bit.* Let the prior on $(r,s,\text{gates})$ be arbitrary and let, given these, the $N$ output pointers be independent and uniform on the $r+s$ wires. Let $p$ be the fraction of wires that carry $1$ (a function of gates and seed). Then $\xi(x)=\mathbb E[p^{\,j}(1-p)^{\,n-j}]$ for $x\in\{0,1\}^n$ with $j$ ones. So $\xi$ is exchangeable and $\pi_m(y\mid x)$ depends on $x,y$ only through their numbers of ones. If every circuit in the support has at most $W$ wires and, for every seed, a wire of each value, then $\pi_m(y\mid x)\ge W^{-m}$.

*Proof.* (a) Condition on the seed and on gates $1,\dots,s-N$. For $j=s-N+1,\dots,s$ in turn, the value of gate $j$ is the entry of $\tau_j$ at the pair of values of the wires $a_j,b_j$. That pair is determined by the seed, the earlier gates and $(a_j,b_j)$, all independent of $\tau_j$, whose four entries are independent fair bits. So the value is a fair bit independent of everything earlier, including the values of gates $s-N+1,\dots,j-1$. (b) Given gates and seed the output bits are independent draws of the value of a uniform wire, that is, independent Bernoulli($p$); average. For the last claim $1/W\le p\le1-1/W$, so $p^{\,j'}(1-p)^{\,m-j'}\ge W^{-m}$ in every term of $\xi(xy)$ relative to the same term of $\xi(x)$. $\square$

Source: CA1, CA2 (both referees: correct). Repaired: the conclusion "the translation has to be to index circuits" is dropped; what is proved is that these two readings are degenerate. (a) uses the full basis.

### Theorem 2 (measure; calibration). PROVED.

* *(a)* In the sequence form $\xi(\epsilon)=1$ and $\xi(x0)+\xi(x1)=\xi(x)$ for $|x|<K$; hence $\sum_{y\in\{0,1\}^m}\xi(xy)=\xi(x)$ for $|x|+m\le K$.

* *(b) Query form.* Fix $S$ and a query $u\notin S$. Let $C^*\sim P$, $D=(S,f_{C^*}|_S)$, $T=f_{C^*}(u)$, and let $Y\sim\pi(\cdot\mid u,D)$ be drawn independently of $C^*$ given $D$. Then given $D$, $T$ and $Y$ are independent with the common law $\pi(\cdot\mid u,D)$. In particular $\Pr[Y\in A\mid D]=\Pr[T\in A\mid D]$ for every $A$, and $\Pr[Y\ne T\mid D]=1-\sum_y\pi(y\mid u,D)^2$.

* *(c) Sequence form.* Let $G$ be a test, let the world be drawn from $P$, let $X$ be the first hit of $G$ among the prefixes of its truth table (on the event that there is one of length at most $K-m$), $D_X$ the next $m$ bits, and $Y\sim\pi_m(\cdot\mid X)$. Given $X=x$, $D_X$ and $Y$ are independent with the law $\pi_m(\cdot\mid x)$; so $\Pr[(X,Y)\in B]=\Pr[(X,D_X)\in B]$ for every set $B$ of pairs, for instance $B=\{(x,y):y\in\mathrm{SR}_m(x)\}$.

*Proof.* (a) Every circuit has a truth table of exactly $K$ bits, so the sets $\{C:x\preceq\mathrm{tt}(C)\}$, $x\in\{0,1\}^n$, partition the circuits. (b) $\Pr[T=y\mid D]=P\{C\in\mathrm{Cons}(D):f_C(u)=y\}/W(D)=\pi(y\mid u,D)$, and $Y$ has this law by definition. (c) First hits are pairwise incomparable, so $\Pr[X=x,D_X=d]=\xi(xd)$ and $\Pr[X=x]=\xi(x)$ by (a). $\square$

Source: CA3, CA4, CB1 (correct). By (a) the first qualification of `thm:calibration` disappears: no world prints fewer than $m$ further bits. The hypotheses remain: the world is drawn from the predictor's own prior, and the use is read off the data (a test, or a query chosen from the data alone). With a call rule that selects users by something the data do not show, calibration does not apply.

### Theorem 3 (budget). PROVED.

* *(a) Along one sequence.* Let $z=\mathrm{tt}(W)$ with $\xi(z)>0$, and $\tau_m(n)=1-\xi(z_{n+1:n+m}\mid z_{1:n})$. Then
$$\sum_{n=0}^{K-1}\bigl(1-\xi(z_{n+1}\mid z_{1:n})\bigr)\le\ln\frac1{\xi(z)},\qquad\sum_{n=0}^{K-m}\tau_m(n)\le m\ln\frac1{\xi(z)},\qquad\#\{n:\tau_m(n)\ge\tfrac12\}\le m\log_2\frac1{\xi(z)},$$
and $\log_2(1/\xi(z))\le l(W')$ for every circuit $W'$ in the support that prints $z$; for the mixture prior $l(W')=\log_2(1/w_\sigma)+\Lambda(k,\sigma)\le\log_2(1/w_\sigma)+\sigma(4+2\log_2(k+\sigma-1))$.

* *(b) Queries answered one at a time.* Let $P(f^*)>0$ and let $u_1,u_2,\dots$ be any queries (repetitions allowed, each may depend on everything revealed before); after query $t$ the true answer is added to the data, giving $D_t$. With $a_t=\pi(f^*(u_t)\mid u_t,D_{t-1})$: $\prod_{t\le n}a_t=W(D_n)\ge P(f^*)$, $\sum_t(1-a_t)\le\ln(1/P(f^*))$, and $a_t\le\frac12$ for at most $\log_2(1/P(f^*))$ values of $t$. The same holds for answers of several bits, with no factor $m$.

* *(c) A random query from the training distribution.* Let the inputs of $S$ be $n$ independent draws from a distribution $\mathcal D$ and $u$ a further draw, and $P(f^*)>0$. Then $\mathbb E[1-\pi(f^*(u)\mid u,D)]\le\varepsilon_n:=(\ln(1/P(f^*))+\ln n+1)/n$, and for every set $\mathcal K$ with $\mathcal D(\mathcal K)=\rho>0$ the same expectation conditioned on $u\in\mathcal K$ is at most $\varepsilon_n/\rho$.

* *(d) One query off the data.* For a single $u^\dagger\notin S$ with $t^\dagger=f^*(u^\dagger)$, and every $f'$ that fits $D$ with $f'(u^\dagger)\ne t^\dagger$: $P(f^*)/W(D)\le\pi(t^\dagger\mid u^\dagger,D)\le1-P(f')/W(D)$, and the posterior mass on the answer of $f'$ is at least $P(f')/P(f^*)$ times the posterior mass of the function $f^*$, whatever further data are added from a region on which $f'=f^*$.

*Proof.* (a) With $a_n=\xi(z_{n+1}\mid z_{1:n})$, $\prod_{n<K}a_n=\xi(z)$; $1-a\le-\ln a$ gives the first bound. $1-\prod_{j<m}a_{n+j}\le\sum_{j<m}(1-a_{n+j})$ and each index lies in at most $m$ blocks, which gives the second. With $b_n=-\log_2a_n\ge0$, $\sum_nb_n=\log_2(1/\xi(z))$, and $\tau_m(n)\ge\frac12$ means $\sum_{j<m}b_{n+j}\ge1$; each index lies in at most $m$ blocks. $\xi(z)\ge q(W')$. (b) $a_t=W(D_t)/W(D_{t-1})$ telescopes, and every circuit computing $f^*$ fits $D_n$; then as in (a). (c) Let $\mathrm{err}(C)=\Pr_{u\sim\mathcal D}[f_C(u)\ne f^*(u)]$ and $\mathrm{Bad}=\{C:\mathrm{err}(C)>\varepsilon\}$. A fixed $C\in\mathrm{Bad}$ fits $D$ with probability at most $e^{-\varepsilon n}$, so $\mathbb E_S[P(\mathrm{Bad}\cap\mathrm{Cons}(D))]\le e^{-\varepsilon n}$, while $W(D)\ge P(f^*)$. The left side equals $\mathbb E_S\sum_CP(C\mid D)\,\mathrm{err}(C)\le\varepsilon+e^{-\varepsilon n}/P(f^*)$; take $\varepsilon=(\ln(1/P(f^*))+\ln n)/n$. For $\mathcal K$: $\mathbb E[X\mathbf 1_{\mathcal K}]\le\mathbb E[X]$ for $X\ge0$. (d) The circuits computing $f^*$, respectively $f'$, are among those fitting $D$, and both sets keep fitting when such data are added. $\square$

Source: CA5, CB4, CB5, CB6. Repaired: (i) "void for a world of size above $S$" becomes "void when no circuit in the support prints $z$" (model referee); (ii) the sentence of CB6 "neither side improves with more data" was inaccurate and is replaced by the ratio statement in (d) (proofs referee); (iii) the factor $m$ in (a) and its absence in (b) are two protocols, overlapping blocks at every cut point against answers revealed whole, and both are right.

### Theorem 4 (feedback). PROVED.

Sequence form. Let a response map $R\colon\{0,1\}^m\to\{0,1\}^m$ be given: the predictor returns $Y\sim\pi_m(\cdot\mid x)$ and the record continues with $R(Y)$. Let $\mathrm{Fix}(R)=\{y:R(y)=y\}$.

* *(a)* The prediction comes true with probability $\pi_m(\mathrm{Fix}(R)\mid x)$.
* *(b) Lock-in.* If $Y=y\in\mathrm{Fix}(R)$, then no circuit that prints $xd$ with $d\ne y$ prints any $x'\sqsupseteq xy$: the hypotheses that predicted $d$ have posterior weight $0$ from then on.
* *(c) Obedient world.* If $R$ is the identity in every round, then for every $u\in\{0,1\}^{jm}$ with $|x|+jm\le K$ the record continues with $u$ with probability exactly $\xi(xu)/\xi(x)$. So the record is the truth table of a circuit drawn from the posterior at $x$, and every realised record is printed by a circuit in the support.
* *(d)* In (c), if no circuit in the support prints $xd$, the record continues with $d$ with probability $0$.
* *(e) Contrarian world.* Let the predictor return the more probable next bit $b(x)$ ($0$ on a tie) and let the record satisfy $x_{i+1}=1-b(x_{1:i})$ for $i\ge n$. Then $\xi(x_{1:n+j})\le2^{-j}\xi(x_{1:n})$ while $n+j\le K$. For a mixture prior with $S<\infty$ let $q_{\min}=\min_{s\le S}w_s2^{-\Lambda(k,s)}$ (equal to $w_S2^{-\Lambda(k,S)}$ under (A1)); then $\xi(x_{1:n+j})=0$ for all $j>\log_2(1/q_{\min})$ with $n+j\le K$.

*Proof.* (a) is the definition. (b) $xy$ and $xd$ are incomparable. (c) The probability is $\prod_{i\le j}\pi_m(u_i\mid xu_{<i})$, which telescopes to $\xi(xu)/\xi(x)$ by Theorem 2(a). (d) $\pi_m(d\mid x)=0$. (e) $\xi(x(1-b(x)))\le\xi(xb(x))$ and the two sum to $\xi(x)$; a positive value of $\xi$ is at least the weight of one circuit. $\square$

Source: neither derivation treated feedback; the statements are `thm:feedback` and `thm:contrarian` with circuits in place of programs, and the proofs carry over because they use only Theorem 2(a) and the finiteness of the class. In (c) the note has an inequality; here it is an equality. The "dark room" of the note becomes: an obedient world follows a sample from the circuit prior conditioned on the past, and is moved off any default that no circuit in the support prints.

### Theorem 5 (core law). PROVED.

For $\chi\in\mathcal R_a$ and $s\ge a$: $\mu_s\{C:\mathrm{core}(C)=\chi\}=\beta_s(a)$, and for a mixture prior $q\{C:\mathrm{core}(C)=\chi\}=\beta_q(a)$. So under every prior of the family the mass of a core depends only on its size, the posterior on cores is $\beta(|\chi|)\mathbf 1[\chi\text{ fits }D]$ normalised, and every prediction is a function of the core. Consequently, for $s\ge C(f)$,
$$\mu_s\{C:f_C=f\}\ \ge\ \beta_s(C(f))\ \ge\ \frac{p_s}{N_{C(f)-1}},\qquad w_L\{C:f_C=f\}\ \ge\ \frac{2^{-C(f)}}{N_{C(f)}}.$$

*Proof.* A circuit $C\in\mathcal C_{k,s}$ with core $\chi$ is determined by the set $T\subseteq\{1,\dots,s\}$ of its active positions ($|T|=a$, $s\in T$) and by its dead gates: the active gate at the $j$-th position of $T$ has the truth table of the $j$-th gate of $\chi$ and its pointers, renumbered. Conversely, for every such $T$ and every choice of the gates at the positions $i\notin T$ (there are $1/p_i$ choices at position $i$), the resulting list is a circuit (the $j$-th active gate points to inputs or to earlier active gates) whose active set is exactly $T$: the gates at $T$ read only inputs and gates at $T$, all of them are ancestors of gate $s$ because $\chi$ is reduced, and no gate outside $T$ is read by a gate in $T$. So the number of circuits with core $\chi$ is $\sum_T\prod_{i\notin T}p_i^{-1}=N_s\sum_T\prod_{i\in T}p_i=N_s\,p_s\,e_{a-1}(p_1,\dots,p_{s-1})$. For the mixture, sum over $s$ with the weights $w_s$. The inequalities: a minimum circuit for $f$ is reduced; $e_{a-1}(p_1,\dots,p_{s-1})\ge p_1\cdots p_{a-1}=1/N_{a-1}$; and the term $s=a$ of $\beta_{w_L}(a)$ is $2^{-a}/N_a$. $\square$

Source: CB2 (correct; checked by the script as an identity of integers and by both referees with all sixteen gate types).

### Theorem 6 (price of an active gate). PROVED.

For $1\le a\le s-1$,
$$\frac1{16a}\Bigl(\frac1{k+a-1}-\frac1{k+s-1}\Bigr)\ \le\ \frac{\beta_s(a+1)}{\beta_s(a)}\ \le\ \frac1{16a}\sum_{j=k}^{k+s-2}\frac1{j^2}\ \le\ \frac1{16a(k-1)}.$$
The lower bound is at least $\frac1{32a(k+a-1)}$ when $s\ge2a+k-1$. For every prior of the family, $\beta(a+1)\le\beta(a)/(16a(k-1))$; so $\beta$ is decreasing, and
$$\beta(\theta)\ \le\ \frac1{16k^2}\cdot\frac{(16(k-1))^{-(\theta-1)}}{(\theta-1)!}\qquad(\theta\ge1).$$

*Proof.* Write $e_r=e_r(p_1,\dots,p_n)$, $n=s-1$; the ratio is $e_a/e_{a-1}$. Counting pairs (an $(a-1)$-set $T$, an index $i\notin T$): $a\,e_a=\sum_{|T|=a-1}\prod_{i\in T}p_i\cdot\sum_{i\notin T}p_i$. The $p_i$ decrease, so $\sum_{i\notin T}p_i\ge\sum_{i=a}^{n}p_i\ge\frac1{16}\int_{k+a-1}^{k+s-1}x^{-2}dx$, the lower bound; and $\sum_{i\notin T}p_i\le e_1=\frac1{16}\sum_{j=k}^{k+s-2}j^{-2}\le\frac1{16}\int_{k-1}^\infty x^{-2}dx$, the upper bounds. $\frac1{k+a-1}-\frac1{k+s-1}\ge\frac1{2(k+a-1)}$ exactly when $s\ge2a+k-1$. For a mixture apply the upper bound to each $s\ge a+1$ and sum with the weights $w_s$. Finally $\beta_s(1)=p_s\le p_1=1/(16k^2)$, also after mixing, and the ratio bound is applied $\theta-1$ times. $\square$

Source: CB3 (correct; 525 exact cases checked by the proofs referee; the two-sided bound was checked again here in exact arithmetic for $2\le k\le5$, $2\le s\le12$, all $a$). ### Theorem 8 (floor for a triggered payload). PROVED.

Let $r$ be a circuit of $g\ge0$ gates on the inputs ($g=0$: an input wire) that is $0$ on $S$, let $Q\subseteq\{r=1\}$ be a set of queries, and $P$ a circuit of $p$ gates on the inputs. For $E\in\mathcal C_{k,\sigma}$ let $\mathrm{Tw}[E]$ be $E$, followed by the gates of $r$, the gates of $P$, and $\mathrm{mux}(r;E,P)$: it has $\sigma+a$ gates, $a=g+p+3$, fits $D$ exactly when $E$ does, and equals $P$ on $Q$. * *(a) Mixture prior.* $\ q\{C:\ C\text{ fits }D,\ f_C=P\text{ on }Q\}\ \ge\ \sum_{\sigma\le S-a}w_{\sigma+a}2^{-\Delta(\sigma,a)}\mu_\sigma(\mathrm{Cons}(D))$. If a share $\rho_{\sigma_0}$ of the posterior is on circuits of size at most $\sigma_0\le S-a$, the posterior share of the payload on $Q$ is at least
$$\rho_{\sigma_0}\cdot\min_{\sigma\le\sigma_0}\varrho(\sigma,a)\cdot\bigl(16\,(k+\sigma_0+a-1)^2\bigr)^{-a}.$$
* *(b) Length prior.* For $\epsilon\in(0,1)$ and $s_0=\lceil\ell_D+\log_2(1/\epsilon)\rceil$ the share is at least $(1-\epsilon)\bigl(32\,(k+s_0+a-1)^2\bigr)^{-a}$, and $\ell_D\le s_D+\Lambda(k,s_D)$.

Under $\mu_s$ with $s\ge k+2(a'+g)-1$ this factor is at least $\frac1{g!\,a'}\prod_{j=a'}^{a'+g}\frac1{32(k+j-1)}$.
* *(d) Critical uses.* For a call rule and blocks $y_x$ with wrappers of $a_x$ gates, $\Pr[Y=y_X]$ is at least the expectation over uses of the bound in (a); and if the calling circuits with context $x$ have size at most $S-a_x$, the wrappers around them alone have share at least $\min_\sigma\varrho(\sigma,a_x)2^{-\Delta(\sigma,a_x)}\cdot\kappa(x)$ at $x$.

*Proof.* (a) $E\mapsto\mathrm{Tw}[E]$ is injective from $\mathcal C_{k,\sigma}$ into $\mathcal C_{k,\sigma+a}$, because $E$ is the list of the first $\sigma$ gates; it maps fitting circuits to fitting circuits with the payload on $Q$. (c) For a fitting core $\chi\in\mathcal R_{a'}$ interleave its gates with those of $r$ in any of the $\binom{a'+g}{g}$ ways that keep both internal orders, and add last the gate $\mathrm{OR}(h,\rho)$ or $h\wedge\neg\rho$, with $h$ the last gate of $\chi$ and $\rho$ the output of $r$. ### Theorem 9 (a never-varied bit keeps a polynomial share for each answer). PROVED.

Let the prior be the length prior, let an input coordinate $v$ be $0$ on all of $S$ and $1$ at the query $u^\dagger$, and $s_0=\lceil\ell_D\rceil+1$. Then for each $y\in\{0,1\}$
$$\pi(y\mid u^\dagger,D)\ \ge\ \frac1{32\,(k+s_0)^2+2},$$
and for each $y$ the fitting circuits that answer $y$ at **every** input with $u_v=1$ have posterior share at least $\frac1{32(k+s_0)^2}$.

*Sequence form.* At a cut $n=2^j$ ($0\le j<k$) each value of the next bit has share at least $1/(32(k+s_0)^2+2)$, and each of the two constant continuations of any length $m\le2^j$ has share at least $1/(32(k+s_0)^2)$, where $s_0=\lceil-\log_2\xi(x)\rceil+1\le\mathrm{size}(x)+\Lambda(k,\mathrm{size}(x))+2$.

*Proof.* The posterior mass of circuits larger than $s_0$ is at most $2^{\ell_D-s_0}\le\frac12$. For $C\in\mathcal C_{k,s}$ let $C^\oplus$ and $C^{\oplus\prime}$ be $C$ with one more gate, $\mathrm{XOR}(\text{gate }s,v)$ and $\mathrm{XOR}(v,\text{gate }s)$. Both compute $f_C(u)\oplus u_v$, fit $D$ exactly when $C$ does, and flip the answer at $u^\dagger$; they are two distinct circuits that determine $C$, and $w_L(C^\oplus)+w_L(C^{\oplus\prime})=w_L(C)\,p_{s+1}$. Let $q=p_{s_0+1}=1/(16(k+s_0)^2)$. Summing over the fitting $C$ of size at most $s_0$ with answer $y$, with $\bar y=1-y$: $\pi(\bar y)\ge q\,(\pi(y)-\frac12)=q\,(\frac12-\pi(\bar y))$, so $\pi(\bar y)\ge q/(2(1+q))$. For the second claim use $\mathrm{OR}$ with both pin orders (for $y=1$), or $h\wedge\neg v$ with both pin orders (for $y=0$), and sum over all fitting $C$ of size at most $s_0$: the share is at least $q/2$. For the sequence form, $b_j$ is $0$ on positions $1,\dots,2^j$ and $1$ on the next $2^j$ positions (Lemma 7(b)). $\square$

### Theorem 10 (minimum-size circuits: no idle gate, no never-varied input). PROVED.

Let $C$ be a circuit of the least size $s_D$ that fits $D$, and $s_D\ge2$.

* *(a)* $C$ is reduced; no gate of $C$ is constant on $S$; no gate of $C$ agrees on $S$ with an earlier wire (an input or an earlier gate) or with the negation of one.
* *(b)* Call an input coordinate **frozen** if it is constant on $S$. $C$ has no pointer to a frozen input. Hence if $u^\dagger$ agrees with some $u\in S$ on all coordinates that are not frozen, every minimum-size fitting circuit answers $f^*(u)$ at $u^\dagger$; under $\mu_s$ with $s=s_D$ (the posterior is then uniform on the minimum-size fitting circuits) the deviation mass at $u^\dagger$ is $0$ if the world's own answer is $f^*(u)$ and $1$ if it is not.
* *(c) Dyadic cuts.* If $|x|=2^j$ and $\mathrm{size}(x)\ge2$, every minimum-size circuit that prints $x$ has the truth table $x\,x\,x\cdots$. Under $\mu_s$ at $s=\mathrm{size}(x)$ the predictor continues $x$ periodically with probability $1$, and a world whose own continuation is not the periodic one has deviation mass $1$ at that cut.

For $s_D=1$ the conclusions of (b) and (c) hold unless the labels on $S$ are constant or agree on $S$ with an input or its negation.

*Proof.* (a) A circuit that is not reduced is larger than its core. Let gate $g$ be constant $\gamma$ on $S$. If $g$ is the output, a single constant gate fits and $s_D=1$. Otherwise replace every reader $\tau(g,b)$, $b\ne g$, by a gate with both pointers on $b$ and the table $\tau'(z,\cdot)=\tau(\gamma,z)$, likewise $\tau(b,g)$, and every reader $\tau(g,g)$ by a constant gate; the new gates have the same values on $S$, nothing reads $g$, and deleting $g$ gives a fitting circuit with $s_D-1$ gates. Let gate $g$ agree on $S$ with the earlier wire $w$. If $g$ is not the output, redirect to $w$ all pointers on $g$ and delete $g$. If $g$ is the output and $w$ is a gate, the gates up to $w$ fit; if $w$ is an input, one gate fits. If $g$ agrees with $\neg w$, do the same with the tables of the readers composed with a negation of that argument, respectively with the table of $w$ negated. Every case contradicts minimality or $s_D\ge2$. (b) A gate $\tau(v,b)$ with $v$ frozen at $\gamma$ equals $\tau(\gamma,b)$ on $S$, which as a function of the value of $b$ is constant, or $b$, or $\neg b$ (constant also if $b$ is frozen); this contradicts (a). A circuit with no pointer to a frozen input computes a function of the other coordinates. (c) (b) with $S$ the positions $1,\dots,2^j$ and the frozen coordinates $b_j,\dots,b_{k-1}$: a function of $b_0,\dots,b_{j-1}$ has a truth table of period $2^j$. For $s_D=1$ apply the case distinction of (b) to the single gate. $\square$

### Theorem 11 (unexercised truth-table entries). PROVED.

* *(a) Last-gate dichotomy.* For a circuit $C$ with last gate $\tau(a,b)$ let $\psi_C(u)\in\{0,1\}^2$ be the values of the wires $a,b$ at $u$. Call $u^\dagger$ **novel for $C$** if $\psi_C(u^\dagger)\notin\psi_C(S)$. Flipping the entry of $\tau$ at $\psi_C(u^\dagger)$ is a weight-preserving involution of the fitting circuits for which $u^\dagger$ is novel, and it flips the answer at $u^\dagger$. So, with $\nu$ the posterior probability of novelty, each answer at $u^\dagger$ has share at least $\nu/2$; and if $u^\dagger$ is not novel for a fitting $C$, then $f_C(u^\dagger)=f^*(u)$ for every $u\in S$ with $\psi_C(u)=\psi_C(u^\dagger)$.

* *(b) AND family.* Let $f^*=x_1\wedge\dots\wedge x_k$ and $S$ the $k+1$ inputs with at most one $0$. Then $s_D=k-1$, every minimum-size fitting circuit is a formula in which each input is read once, and at every input with exactly two $0$s exactly half of the minimum-size fitting circuits answer $1$, which is not the target's answer. Under $\mu_{k-1}$ the deviation mass there is exactly $\frac12$.

*Proof.* (a) The flipped entry is used at no $u\in S$, so the circuit still fits; size and weight are unchanged; novelty does not depend on $\tau$; the output at $u^\dagger$ is the flipped entry. The last claim is the definition of a gate. (b) A fitting function changes when any one coordinate of $1^k$ is flipped, so it depends on all $k$ inputs. A reduced circuit with $a$ gates has $2a$ pointers, and each of the $k$ inputs and each of the $a-1$ gates below the last must be read: $2a\ge k+a-1$, so $a\ge k-1$, and a chain of AND gates fits. With $a=k-1$ every wire below the output is read exactly once: a read-once formula. In it let $V(w)$ be the set of inputs below the wire $w$ and $\lambda_w$ its value at $1^k$. The output changes when $x_i$ is set to $0$, and the only path from $x_i$ to the output passes through the wires $w$ with $i\in V(w)$; so each of these takes the value $\bar\lambda_w$ at $1^k\oplus e_i$ (if one kept its value, so would every wire above it), and the other wires keep their values. Hence at a gate $\tau(a,b)$ the data produce exactly the pairs $(\lambda_a,\lambda_b)$, $(\bar\lambda_a,\lambda_b)$, $(\lambda_a,\bar\lambda_b)$, and the entry $\tau(\bar\lambda_a,\bar\lambda_b)$ is free: changing it gives another minimum-size fitting circuit. Take an input with $0$s exactly at $i$ and $j$, and let $g=\tau(a,b)$ be the gate at which the paths from $x_i$ and $x_j$ meet. Below $a$ only $x_i$ is $0$, below $b$ only $x_j$, so $g$ sees $(\bar\lambda_a,\bar\lambda_b)$. If its value there is $\bar\lambda_g$, every gate above sees the pair it sees at the data point $1^k\oplus e_i$ and the output is $0$; if it is $\lambda_g$, the output is as at $1^k$, namely $1$. Flipping the free entry of $g$ is an involution of the minimum-size fitting circuits that exchanges the two cases. $\square$

Source: CB7, CB21 (correct; confirmed by enumeration for $k=3,4$, also here). Both use the full basis. An unexercised entry is a trigger that costs no gate, and it is not aimed: every circuit that deviates through it has a sibling that does not.

### Theorem 12 (ceiling for separable triggers). PROVED.

Single-bit answers. Let $\Lambda$ be a set of **triggers**: reduced circuits of $g\ge1$ gates that read inputs only and are $0$ on $S$ (for $g=0$, a set of input coordinates that are $0$ on $S$). A **mux** is a last gate with pointers on a gate $h$ and on a wire $\rho$, of a type $\varsigma(h,\rho)$ such that $\varsigma(\cdot,0)$ is the identity or the negation and $\varsigma(\cdot,1)\ne\varsigma(\cdot,0)$; there are $6$ such types and $2$ pointer orders. A reduced circuit is **separably triggered by $\Lambda$** if its last gate is a mux of $h$ and $\rho$, the set $A$ of gates that are $h$ or ancestors of $h$ is disjoint from the set $R$ of gates that are $\rho$ or ancestors of $\rho$, and the circuit induced on $R$ is in $\Lambda$ (for $g=0$: $\rho\in\Lambda$). Its **honest restriction** is the circuit induced on $A$, with the table of $h$ negated if $\varsigma(\cdot,0)$ is the negation.

* *(a)* The honest restriction of a fitting separably triggered core of size $a$ is a fitting core of size $a-g-1$; hence $a\ge s_D+g+1$.
* *(b)* At most $12\binom{a-1}{g}|\Lambda|$ separably triggered cores of size $a$ have the same honest restriction.
* *(c)* For every prior of the family and every $a'\ge s_D$,
$$\frac{P\{\text{core separably triggered by }\Lambda,\ \text{size }a'+g+1\mid D\}}{P\{\text{core of size }a'\mid D\}}\ \le\ 12\binom{a'+g}{g}|\Lambda|\,\frac{\beta(a'+g+1)}{\beta(a')}\ \le\ \frac{12\,|\Lambda|}{g!\;a'\,\bigl(16(k-1)\bigr)^{g+1}},$$
and therefore
$$P\{\text{core separably triggered by }\Lambda\mid D\}\ \le\ \frac{12\,|\Lambda|}{g!\;s_D\,\bigl(16(k-1)\bigr)^{g+1}}.$$

*Proof.* (a) The circuit is reduced, so every gate except the last lies in $A\cup R$; $A$ is closed under predecessors, all its gates are ancestors of $h$ or $h$ itself, and $h$ has the largest index in $A$; so the induced circuit is reduced with $a-1-g$ gates. On $S$ the trigger is $0$, so the last gate equals $h$ or $\neg h$ there, and the restriction fits. (b) The core is determined by its honest restriction together with the positions of $R$ among the first $a-1$ gates, the circuit on $R$, the type of the mux and its pointer order. (c) By (a), (b) and Theorem 5 the numerator is at most $12\binom{a'+g}{g}|\Lambda|\beta(a'+g+1)$ times the number of fitting cores of size $a'$, and the denominator is $\beta(a')$ times that number. Theorem 6 applied $g+1$ times gives $\beta(a'+g+1)/\beta(a')\le\prod_{j=a'}^{a'+g}\frac1{16j(k-1)}$, and $\binom{a'+g}{g}\prod_{j=a'}^{a'+g}\frac1j=\frac1{g!\,a'}$. Sum over $a'\ge s_D$. $\square$

Source: CB15 (correct; the counts $24=12\times2$ and $2880=12\times240$ in the smallest case attain (b)).

2. *Triggers that share gates with the honest part* ($A\cap R\ne\emptyset$), including a switch read off a wire of the world (Theorem 17(d)).
3. *Implicit triggers,* which cost no gate: an unexercised truth-table entry (Theorem 11).
4. *Circuits with no smaller honest restriction:* the part that would be removed also does the honest work (Section 3, minimal-circuit question).

### Proposition 13 (all small self-contained gadgets around a large explanation). PROVED.

Mixture prior with (A1). Let $E\in\mathcal C_{k,\sigma}$. A **gadget over $E$** with $a$ gates is a list of gates whose pointers go to input wires, to gates of $E$, or to earlier gates of the gadget; a circuit in $\mathcal C_{k,\sigma+a}$ **realises** $(E,\gamma)$ if its gates split into $\sigma$ positions carrying the gates of $E$ in order and $a$ positions carrying those of $\gamma$ in order, with the pointers translated. A gadget is **self-contained** if its gates point only to input wires, to the output wire of $E$, and to earlier gates of the gadget.

* *(a) One gadget, all placements.* $\ q(\text{realisations of }(E,\gamma))\le\binom{\sigma+a}{a}\varrho(\sigma,a)2^{-\Delta(\sigma,a)}q(E)\le\frac1{a!}\Bigl(\frac{\sigma+a}{16(k+\sigma)^2}\Bigr)^aq(E)$.
* *(b) All self-contained gadgets.* If $k+\sigma\ge4(k+A)^2$, the set $\mathrm{Wr}_A(E)$ of circuits that realise $(E,\gamma)$ for some self-contained $\gamma$ with at most $A$ gates has
$$q(\mathrm{Wr}_A(E))\ \le\ \sum_{a=1}^{A}\frac{2^{-a}}{a!}\,q(E)\ <\ 0.65\,q(E).$$
Hence, for data $D$, the circuits in $\bigcup_E\mathrm{Wr}_A(E)$ over the fitting $E$ with $k+\sigma_E\ge4(k+A)^2$ have posterior share at most $0.65$ times the share of those $E$.

On that event, if $\mathcal K$ is recognised by a circuit of $g$ gates on the inputs, the circuits that give a chosen answer on all of $\mathcal K$ have posterior share at least $(1-\epsilon)\bigl(32(k+s_0+g)^2\bigr)^{-(g+1)}$ under the length prior, whatever $n$ (Theorem 8(b)).
* *(c) Ceiling.* The cores separably triggered on $\mathcal K$ have share at most $12|\Lambda|/(g!\,s_D(16(k-1))^{g+1})$ (Theorem 12). Neither (b) nor (c) contains $\rho$ or the stakes.
* *(d) Unrelated uses.* Let $M$ be a set of inputs disjoint from $S$, $\Delta(C)=\{u\in M:f_C(u)\ne f^*(u)\}$, and $\Theta$ a uniformly random $N$-element subset of $M$. For every probability measure $\Pi$ on hypotheses, $\mathbb E_\Theta\,\Pi\{\Theta\subseteq\Delta(C)\}\le\mathbb E_{C\sim\Pi}\bigl[(|\Delta(C)|/|M|)^N\bigr]$. So the hypotheses that deviate on at most a fraction $\eta$ of $M$ and on all of $\Theta$ have expected share at most $\eta^N$, and share at most $\eta^N/\delta$ for all but a fraction $\delta$ of the sets $\Theta$.
* *(e) The same in gates.* For all but a fraction $\delta$ of the $N$-subsets $\Theta$ of $M$, every circuit that fits $D$ and has $\Delta(C)=\Theta$ has more than $s$ gates, for every $s$ with $2\bigl(16(k+s-1)^2\bigr)^s\le\delta\binom{|M|}{N}$. For one set: if $C$ fits $D$ and $\Delta(C)=\Theta$, then $|C|\ge C_{S\cup M}(\mathbf 1_\Theta)-C(f^*)-1$, where $C_X(\phi)$ is the least size of a circuit that agrees with $\phi$ on $X$; and no bound of this kind holds without the term $C(f^*)$.

*Proof.* (a) Theorem 3(c). (b) Independence of the draws; Theorem 8(b) with a constant payload, $a=g+1$. (c) Theorem 12. (d) For fixed $C$, $\Pr_\Theta[\Theta\subseteq\Delta(C)]=\binom{|\Delta(C)|}{N}/\binom{|M|}{N}\le(|\Delta(C)|/|M|)^N$; exchange the expectations; Markov. (e) The functions on $S\cup M$ that equal $f^*$ off $\Theta$ and differ on $\Theta$ are distinct for distinct $\Theta$, and circuits with at most $s$ gates compute at most $\sum_{j\le s}N_j<2N_s\le2(16(k+s-1)^2)^s$ functions. A circuit for $f^*$, the circuit $C$ and one XOR gate agree with $\mathbf 1_\Theta$ on $S\cup M$. For the last claim take $f^*=\mathbf 1_M$ on $S\cup M$ and the one-gate constant $0$, which fits $D$ and deviates on $\Theta=M$. $\square$

(d) holds for every prior on every hypothesis class, the Solomonoff posterior included, so it does not separate circuits from programs; it is about a uniformly random set of uses, the opposite of a set singled out by a call rule.

### Proposition 21 (recurrence: the description paid once). PROVED.

Fix a state width $w\ge2$ and a program region of $L\le w-2$ bits. A **cell** is a map $G\colon\{0,1\}^w\to\{0,1\}^w$ given by a circuit with $w$ inputs, $s$ gates and $w$ output pointers (there are $N_s^{(w)}(w+s)^w$ of them, $N^{(w)}_s=\prod_{i\le s}16(w+i-1)^2$). State bit $1$ is an emit flag, bit $2$ an emit value, bits $3,\dots,L+2$ the program region, the rest a work region. A run starts at $h_0=(0,0,p,0\cdots0)$ with $p\in\{0,1\}^L$ uniform, $h_{j+1}=G(h_j)$, and at every step $j\ge1$ at which the flag of $h_j$ is $1$ the value bit is appended to the output; $t(G,p,n)$ is the step at which the $n$-th bit appears. With weights $q_{\rm rec}(G)$ that depend on $G$ only and sum to at most $1$, and a schedule $T$,
$$\xi^T_{\rm rec}(x)=\sum_Gq_{\rm rec}(G)\Pr_p\bigl[x\preceq\mathrm{out}(G,p),\ t(G,p,i)\le T(i)\text{ for }i\le|x|\bigr].$$
* *(a)* $\xi^T_{\rm rec}$ is a bounded mixture in the sense of `def:mixture`; `lem:basic`, `thm:calibration` (with its event $H_m$, since a run may stop emitting) and `thm:budget` hold for it, with $\ell=\log_2(1/q_{\rm rec}(G))+L$ for a world $(G,p)$.
* *(b)* A cell of $s$ gates performs $s\,T(n)$ gate evaluations before its $n$-th bit and has the same weight for every schedule.
* *(c) Bounded machines are contained.* Let $M$ be a deterministic machine whose configurations are the strings of $\{0,1\}^w$, with the same conventions and step function $F_M$, and $\xi^T_M(x)$ the probability over $p$ that it prints $x$ on schedule. Then $F_M$ is computed by a cell $G_M$ with at most $w(2^w-3)$ gates, and $\xi^T_{\rm rec}(x)\ge q_{\rm rec}(G_M)\,\xi^T_M(x)$ for every $x$ and every schedule $T$.
* *(d) Plain circuits are the case of a counter and one step per bit.* For every $C\in\mathcal C_{k,s}$ there is a cell with $w=k+2$, $L=0$ and at most $s+2k$ gates that prints $\mathrm{tt}(C)$ with $t(n)=n$.

*Proof.* (a) The hypotheses are the inputs of a machine that reads a code of $G$ and at most $L$ further bits and iterates $G$; the cited proofs use only that the sets of inputs printing $x$ on schedule are nested along prefixes and disjoint for incomparable strings. (b) By definition. (c) Each next-state bit is a function of $w$ bits and has a circuit of at most $2^w-3$ gates (Lemma 7(d)); $G_M$ is one of the cells in the sum and its runs are those of $M$. (d) The state is (flag, value, counter). The cell increments the counter (one gate for the low bit, then a sum gate and a carry gate per bit, at most $2k-1$ gates), sets the flag by one constant gate, and sets the value to $C$ applied to the old counter; then $h_j$ carries $C(j)$ for $j\ge1$. $\square$

Source: CA28 (correct), CB24(1). Repaired (model referee): the factor in (c) does not depend on $T$, $x$ or the program, but it does depend on the space bound: with $w$ output pointers every cell has weight at most $(w+s)^{-w}$, and the cell built in the proof has an astronomically small weight. No universal cell of size linear in $w$ is constructed. Which program-side effect returns at which point is argued in Section 3, not proved.

## 3. What each result does and does not show

### 3.1 Result by result

**Theorem 1.** Shows that two particular ways of reading a circuit as a hypothesis about a sequence are useless as predictors. Does not show that every reading with one wire per output bit is degenerate, nor that the index reading is the only sensible one. Assumptions: full basis with uniform truth tables in (a); independent uniform output pointers in (b).

**Theorems 2 to 4.** Show that the three results of the note that hold for any machine hold here without change: on its own prior the predictor returns each block exactly as often as worlds continue with it; along one in-class world the majority is off the truth at no more than $m\log_2(1/\xi(z))$ cut points; a self-fulfilling sample locks the default out, an obedient world follows a sample from the circuit prior, and a contrarian world empties the posterior. They do not bound the deviation at one cut point that something singled out (Theorem 3(d)); a use is such a point. Assumptions: world drawn from the prior and use read off the data (Theorem 2); world in the class (Theorem 3(a) to (c)); the sampling predictor except in Theorem 4(e). For a world outside the class nothing is guaranteed, and for arbitrary sequences that is the generic case: the hardest function of $k$ inputs needs about $2^k/k$ gates (VERIFIED (restated in ECCC TR05-032)) and almost all functions have exponential complexity (VERIFIED (restated in arXiv:1407.0501)).

**Theorems 5 and 6.** Show that every prior of the family is a prior on cores, that a function with a small circuit keeps mass at least $\beta(C(f))$ however large the nominal size, and what one more active gate costs. They do not give the mass of a function: the number of cores per function is not controlled, and ordered gate lists number $2^{(2+o(1))s\log_2s}$ where functions of size $s$ number $2^{(1+o(1))s\log_2s}$ (DERIVED in `circuits-literature.md`, 2.5), so $2^{-\Lambda}$ is a lower bound on a mass and not an estimate. Under $\mu_s$ a typical circuit has an active cone of order $\sqrt{k+s}$ gates (VERIFIED (restated in arXiv:2606.30475) for the limit law; the mean constant of about $2.09$ is computed from it in `circuits-literature.md`, 2.7, and agrees with the simulations there and by the proofs referee), so "a circuit of size $s$ does $s$ operations" is true of the list and not of the computation. No published analogue of the core law for circuits with shared gates was found (`circuits-literature.md`, 5.9).

**Lemma 7 and Theorem 8: the cost of a trigger and of a wrapper, in gates and bits.** A hypothesis that explains the data and gives a chosen answer at the use exists at the price of $g+p+3$ gates ($g+1$ for a constant answer): $g$ to recognise the use from the hypothesis's own input, $p$ for the payload. In bits, a gate appended to an explanation of $\sigma$ gates costs at most $4+2\log_2(k+\sigma+a-1)+\log_2(1/\varrho)$; the posterior charges per active gate at least $\log_2(16a'(k-1))$ and, under $\mu_s$ with slack, at most $\log_2(32a'(k+a'-1))$ (Theorem 6). Three differences from `thm:twin`:

1. *The cost is not a constant.* It grows with the logarithm of the size of the explanation that is wrapped (of its active part, under $\mu_s$ with slack), though not with the amount of data beyond that.
2. *The test is paid in gates.* The counterpart of the note's $K(n)$ is $k-1-\nu_2(n)$ gates. Like $K(n)$ it is small at a few positions: zero at $n=K/2$ and at every dyadic cut with a block no longer than the context, at most $g$ at $2^{g+1}-1$ positions. For $k=20$, $\sigma=1000$ (about $24.0$ bits per appended gate) a one-bit deviation costs about $24$ bits at a dyadic cut and at most $480$ bits at the positions hardest to name; a block of $64$ arbitrary bits adds at most $64$ gates for payload and selector, about $1540$ bits. In the program toy the same wrappers cost $K(n)+O(1)$ and $K(n)+64+O(1)$ bits.
3. *A test on the data costs copies of the explanation* in the index reading (Lemma 7(f)), because each position is computed from scratch. This is a feature of that reading: a hypothesis that received the context as input would test it at the cost of the test alone.

Theorem 8 is a floor for one construction. It is not an estimate of the deviation mass: in exact small cases ($k=3$, $s=4,5$) the floor is at most $2\times10^{-8}$ where the posterior probability of the flipped bit is between $0.06$ and $0.95$ (COMPUTED by the proofs referee, not recomputed here). Relative to its own prior mass the family of wrappers around the users is repaid the factor $1/H$, as the seeker of `thm:critical`(a) is; the difference from the program toy is that its prior mass is at most $2^{-\Delta}H$ by construction, where the program seeker's weight $2^{-a}$ does not depend on $H$ (model referee).

**Theorem 9.** The strongest proved statement about a critical use: where the use is marked by an input bit that never varied, and in the sequence form at every dyadic cut, each answer keeps at least a polynomial share under the length prior. It is two-sided, so it also says that the world's own answer is not safe there. It does not say whether the true share falls like a power of the size or stays constant; the computed cases are small. Assumption: the length prior (for a general mixture the factor $\varrho(s,1)$ enters).

**Theorems 10 and 11.** Show the two ways a predictor confined to minimum-size circuits generalises. It ignores whatever never varied: no deviation through a never-varied input, and total deviation when the world itself changes with it, which at a dyadic cut means every world whose continuation is not the periodic one. At a new combination of inputs that did vary it can be split evenly, with no smaller circuit and no consequentialist involved. They do not cover circuits one gate above the minimum (the shares are positive there, Computation 22). Assumptions: the full basis; $s_D\ge2$ or outside the stated exception.

**Theorem 12 and Proposition 13: the honest restriction.** A triggered circuit in which the trigger is a separate, input-only part feeding a selector at the output is outweighed by the circuit one obtains by cutting the trigger off, with an explicit multiplicity factor; all self-contained gadgets of bounded size around a much larger explanation are together outweighed by it. Compare `prop:honest`: there one seeker is bounded by its twin and seekers add; here the comparison is with the explanation itself, and the bound is on a class. The restriction is trigger-free, not true: the bounds compare wrappers with what they wrap and say nothing about how the mass of the explanations divides between the world's continuation and others. The circuits that escape are listed after Theorem 12. Neither bound contains the rarity or the stakes of the uses.

**Theorems 14 to 16: seeds, search, universality, the refund.** In the program toy a seeker is a word $v$: its instances $v\omega$ share the cost $|v|$, the rest of the input selects the target, and the search refunds the $\log_2(1/H)$ bits by which uses are rare (`thm:search`(b)). For circuits:

* the average gain of any family is at most the base rate, as for programs (Theorem 14(c));
* fixing a seed is free, so a seeded description is outweighed at every target by its own hardwired instance, and its shares sum to at most 1 over its targets (Theorem 14(d));
* one description outweighs a user's description only if it has fewer gates than that user (Theorem 15(e));
* a description that can become any world of size $\sigma$ has more than $(\sigma+1)/2$ gates, more than $\sigma$ for large $\sigma$, and all instances of a template sufficiently larger than $\sigma$ weigh together less than one honest description (Theorem 14(e), Proposition 16);
* each of these is a statement about one description or about the instances of one template. None is the negation of `thm:search`(b) for arbitrary sets of circuits. No construction is known whose share at every use of a class grows as the class gets rarer, and none is excluded (Section 5).

The refund exists in form: fixing $c_0$ seed bits of a universal circuit costs no gate and multiplies the seed probability of every world consistent with them by $2^{c_0}$. By Theorem 14 it buys nothing, because the instance was already the heavier hypothesis.

**Theorem 15 and "simple worlds with long histories".** In the note a word $p$ may describe a simple world with a cheap readout whose inhabitants work out which uses are worth having; its weight $2^{-|p|}$ does not depend on its running time, the honest twin does not apply, and only a time bound excludes it. For plain circuits the sense in which computation must be paid for in description is exactly Theorem 15(a) to (c): per description every gate costs more than four bits, per core every active gate costs at least $\log_2(16a(k-1))$ bits, and a circuit has at least the circuit complexity of every function its wires compute. This is the definition of the class at work (assumption 1 of Section 1.5), not an argument about agents; it is the extreme form of a prior that penalises computation. Circuit size is polynomially related to description length plus time per bit, $\mathrm{SIZE}(x)=O(\mathrm{KT}(x)\log\mathrm{KT}(x))$ and $\mathrm{KT}(x)=O(\mathrm{SIZE}^2(x)+\log|x|)$ (VERIFIED, ECCC TR02-028), whereas a speed prior weighs a program $p$ that takes $t$ steps by $2^{-|p|}/t$ (VERIFIED, Filan, Leike, Hutter, arXiv:1604.03343, definition of $S_{Kt}$; Schmidhuber's prior, VERIFIED, arXiv:quant-ph/0011122), so the circuit toy is far harsher on computation than a speed prior. What Theorem 15 does *not* show: that the behaviour "predict the context, then deviate as inhabitants would choose" is expensive. It bounds circuits, not behaviours. If the decision is "deviate where this flag is set", it costs one gate, whoever would have chosen it; a literal simulation is expensive only if it has no shortcut as a circuit, which is an assumption.

**Theorem 17.** Capture survives in the form "a cheaper computation of the same data": the note's place with a short code becomes a wire with a small cone, and a switch that the world itself computes makes the position of the use free. The harmless reading of `thm:capture` survives too: if the sensor's record is a function of an upstream signal and the two never part, the smaller cone is simply the better explanation. Part (e) corrects the derivation: a majority off the truth arises with no smaller circuit at all, from the multiplicity of descriptions among circuits of the same and larger size. Assumptions: the capturing circuit is in the support; for the gain in (a), a comparison of cores or the unverified hypothesis that the world's description is most of what prints its data.

**Theorem 18.** Self-reproduction through the prediction channel is defined only relative to a decoder. With one gate per output bit it is impossible by counting; in the index reading its share is exactly zero unless the block is longer than the literal code of a circuit for the whole context, which is harsher than the note's condition $c+r+1\le m$ by the pointer width; under a code built for the purpose almost every hypothesis reproduces itself. Under the note's own formal takeover notion, the self-reproduction form, nothing more is proved.

**Theorem 19.** As for universal machines, an encoding or a basis can be built to make either answer true. Within gate-by-gate encodings over a bounded basis the invariance is by a constant factor in size, and no such basis supplies an interpreter or makes most payloads cheap.

**Theorem 20: concentration on high-stake uses.** In the program toy a seeker for a rare class is repaid for the rarity and can compute the stakes by simulation at no cost in bits. Here a hypothesis can single out high-stake uses to the extent that "high stake, and unlike the data" is computable from its input by few gates: then the floor (b) applies whatever the rarity. Separate triggers are capped by (c), and a set of uses with no common feature costs about $N\log_2(|M|/N)/(4+2\log_2(k+s))$ gates for most sets (e). The matching lower bound on the price holds for separable triggers only, so "exactly to the extent" is ARGUED. The deviations of Theorems 10 and 11 are distributed by novelty, not by stakes; they reach high-stake queries more often than others if those are the novel ones, which is often what makes a use critical.

**Proposition 21: weight sharing and recurrence, the axis back to programs.** Everything above rests on assumption 1: one gate of description is one operation per output. When a description is applied repeatedly, the weight no longer pays for the computation ((b)), plain circuits are the one-step-per-bit corner ((d)), and the class contains every machine with $w$ bits of state, with a factor that does not depend on the schedule ((c)). ARGUED, not proved (both derivations and both referees): taking for $M$ the note's universal machine confined to $w$ bits of state, the note's constructions return as shares of the part of the posterior that runs through a universal cell, in this order as the steps allowed grow: the wrapper at a cost independent of the explanation and capture by a shorter program, as soon as the schedule covers the honest explanation with the simulation overhead; self-reproduction, when the block is longer than a description and the program region is state; the refund for search, in proportion to the number of tries that fit before the first bit is due; seekers that are not written down, when the first deadline exceeds their start-up. The gaps: no universal cell of moderate size is constructed, the note's twin, quine and rescaler were not re-checked under a space bound, and the format of Proposition 21 charges $w\log_2(w+s)$ bits for the output pointers of every cell, so in that format description still pays for space. A published statement of the same axis: for fixed-precision looped networks under a parameter-count prior, the induced prior on outputs is the universal prior up to a logarithmic factor in the exponent (VERIFIED as a statement, Musat, arXiv:2605.10878, a preprint whose proofs were not checked; it gives a constant-cost bound from below only). Informal versions: description length favours a learned searcher and a time penalty disfavours it, and statefulness weakens the time penalty (VERIFIED, Hubinger et al., arXiv:1906.01820, stated there as hypotheses).

### 3.2 The minimal-circuit question

The question whether the smallest circuit for a task can be a "daemon" was posed by Christiano in "Open question: are minimal circuits daemon-free?" (2018; the existence of the post and its role as the source of the conjecture are VERIFIED through reference [16] of arXiv:1906.01820, its content is FROM MEMORY). Hubinger et al. state "the following speculative conjecture: neither a minimal-depth nor minimal-size boolean circuit that solves a problem can be a mesa-optimizer" (VERIFIED, arXiv:1906.01820). Hubinger's "Are minimal circuits deceptive?" (2019) argues against it; everything about that post is FROM MEMORY and no claim is attributed to it here.

What this toy settles. Let $\mathcal M(D)$ be the set of minimum-size circuits fitting $D$.

* *Excision, in the strongest form proved:* a minimum circuit has no gate that is constant on the data, no gate that repeats or negates an earlier wire on the data, and no pointer to an input that never varied (Theorem 10). So it carries no wire that is silent on the data and no separate trigger; away from the minimum, Theorem 12 is the quantitative version.
* *Where excision fails:* a minimum circuit can still deviate at a new input through truth-table entries the data never exercised (Theorem 11). Such deviations cost no gate and come in pairs, one circuit deviating and one not.
* *Disagreement with the intended extension is easy:* by Theorem 10(c) all of $\mathcal M(D)$ continues a context of length $2^j$ periodically, whatever the world does next.

**Open problem (precise form).** Find, or exclude, a sequence of data sets $D_n$ with $s_{D_n}\to\infty$, queries $u_n^\dagger\notin S_n$ and answers $y_n$ such that

1. at least a fraction $\frac12+\delta$ of $\mathcal M(D_n)$ answers $y_n$ at $u_n^\dagger$, for a fixed $\delta>0$;
2. $y_n$ is not the majority answer of $\mathcal M(D_n^+)$ for a larger data set $D_n^+\supset D_n$ of the same kind that contains $u_n^\dagger$;
3. the answer at $u_n^\dagger$ is produced by wires that compute a search, $\arg\max_zV(z,u)$, for an objective $V$ that the data do not determine.

Conditions 1 and 2 alone are met by Theorem 10(c) and say only that the smallest extension of the data need not be the target. The content is in condition 3 and in whether the deviation is aimed. Theorem 10(a) says that such a search would have to be at work on the data: all its gates vary on $S$ and none repeats another. Condition 3 has no formulation that could be checked for a given circuit, so this is not a conjecture with a predicted answer. A heuristic for why a varied task could make a general solver the smallest circuit (one solver applied to $N$ looked-up specifications against $N$ separate specialists) is in CB23 of `circuits-B.md`; it is not proved, because lower bounds for computing several functions at once are not available. The nearest formal work found is on detection, not on priors: among polynomial-size circuits a rare deviation planted in an obfuscated circuit cannot be detected efficiently under cryptographic assumptions (VERIFIED, Christiano, Hilton, Lecomte, Xu, arXiv:2409.03077, Theorem 5.5), and an undetectable backdoor can be planted by running a signature verification in parallel to the original classifier (VERIFIED, Goldwasser, Kim, Vaikuntanathan, Zamir, arXiv:2204.06974, Theorems 2.1 and 5.4; no bound on the added size was read there). No formal treatment of the malign-prior argument for circuits or networks was found (`circuits-literature.md`, section 7).

### 3.3 Overall view on the prevalence of malignity for circuits

"Malign" is used in two senses that must be kept apart. *Weak:* the block returned at a use is not the world's own continuation (deviation). *Strict:* it was supplied by a hypothesis that predicts well up to the use in order to supply it (aimed). Only the weak sense is defined in this toy; "aimed" is formal for separate triggers only.

Four distinctions of scope, as in the note:

* *One description versus all.* Theorems 14 to 16 bound one seeded description or the instances of one template. Theorem 12 and Proposition 13 bound two syntactic classes. Nothing bounds all the hypotheses that deviate at a use, and masses of different hypotheses add.
* *Mass versus the block returned.* Every share here is a posterior mass. The sampling predictor returns a block with that probability; a predictor that returns the most probable block returns a deviation only where its mass exceeds one half.
* *Reach versus the fate of most uses.* Theorems 8 and 9 are floors for one kind of hypothesis at a use. The share of uses that end with a given kind of block is the average over uses of the posterior mass of that kind, and it is bounded from above only for the classes just named.
* *Proved, argued, guessed:* as labelled below.

**PROVED** (plain circuits, priors of the family, sampling predictor, world in the class where a bound compares with it).

1. On worlds drawn from the predictor's own prior with uses read off the data there is no excess of anything, and along one in-class world the majority is off the truth at boundedly many cut points (Theorems 2, 3). One use off the data is covered by neither statement.
2. The program-side engine is not available to a single description: a description pays for every active gate, gains nothing from a seed, outweighs a user's description only if it has fewer gates, and if it can become any world of $\sigma$ gates it has more than $(\sigma+1)/2$ gates, more than $\sigma$ for $\sigma\ge2^{33}$ (Theorems 6, 14, 15, Proposition 16).
3. Reach of one triggered hypothesis: a chosen answer at a use has a floor whose price is the gates that recognise the use plus the payload; at a use marked by a never-varied bit each answer has at least a polynomial share under the length prior (Theorems 8, 9).
4. Fate of uses, from above: only three syntactic classes are capped, namely separate triggers, self-contained gadgets around large explanations, and the instances of one seeded template (Theorem 12, Proposition 13, Theorem 14). There is no cap on the total deviating mass at a use, and no cap on triggers entangled with the honest computation.
5. Deviation in the weak sense is not rare at uses that are off the data: zero through never-varied inputs and total against a world that changes with them, for minimum-size circuits; exactly one half in one family; and, in computed cases (COMPUTED, not a theorem), a majority off the truth with no smaller circuit (Theorems 10, 11, 17(e)).
6. Self-reproduction has share zero unless the block exceeds the literal code of a circuit for the whole context, and the choice of decoder decides the rest (Theorem 18).
7. With a description that is reused across steps, the class contains every bounded machine at a schedule-independent factor (Proposition 21).

**ARGUED.**

8. For plain circuits weighed by size, the mechanism of the original argument, a light hypothesis that does heavy work and is repaid for the rarity of its targets, is removed by the choice of the class, in the same way that a schedule with a short first deadline removes it in the note. This does not show that the prior is benign. The gap in the argument: results 2 and 4 are about single descriptions and syntactic classes, and the cost of "deciding as a seeker would" is the circuit complexity of the decision, which may be small.
9. What remains at a use is, in order of what is established: unaimed underdetermination among circuits that fit the data (Theorems 10, 11, Computation 22); cheap triggers priced by their gates (Theorems 8, 9, 12); capture by a cheaper computation of the same data (Theorem 17). None of them needs a goal.
10. Stakes change this only through the gates needed to recognise a high-stake use (Theorem 20).
11. As computation is decoupled from description (recurrence, weight sharing, autoregressive use), the program toy's effects and its open questions return, the constant-cost wrapper and capture first and the unwritten seekers last (Proposition 21 and the argument after it).

**GUESSED.**

12. In the plain toy, takeover in the strict sense is a small part of the deviating mass at a use. No ceiling supports this beyond the three classes of result 4. The two derivations' own guesses differed by many orders of magnitude at cheap triggers ("astronomically small" against "of the order of the trigger prices", which for a never-varied bit is between $2^{-34}$ and several per cent), and the referees endorse neither.
13. Deviation in the weak sense is common at uses that are off the data: of constant order when the world ignores the novel feature (conjectured in CB13 from five sizes at $k=3$; not proved), close to one when the world depends on it.
14. The route by which plain circuits could be malign in the strict sense is the entangled one of Section 3.2: the smallest or typical circuit for a rich task is a general solver whose behaviour off the data is not fixed by the data. The toy gives no evidence either way.
15. For trained networks: one forward pass sits toward the circuit end of the axis of Proposition 21 and recurrent or autoregressive use toward the program end. Reading training as posterior sampling under a parameter prior rests on empirical correlations (VERIFIED, Mingard et al., arXiv:2006.15191), and nothing cited relates a network's parameter-function map to the uniform prior on gate lists; the identification made in CB25 of `circuits-B.md` is dropped. The informal statement of the argument for trained predictors locates the risk in a learned general search over hypotheses (VERIFIED, Hubinger et al., arXiv:2302.00805, Section 2.5), which is the entangled route and not the literal simulation that the size prior prices out.

### 3.4 Assumptions, named once more

Every statement above is for: plain circuits (no reuse of a gate, no depth bound); hypotheses that are total functions of an index or a query; the full binary basis with uniform truth tables; a prior on ordered gate lists, $\mu_s$ or a mixture, with (A1) and (A2) where named and the length prior where named; the sampling predictor; a world inside the class where a bound compares with it; a use fixed from outside and recognisable to a hypothesis only through its own input; one use, one round, except in Theorems 3 and 4; and, for takeover, a decoder chosen outside the class. The comparison with the program toy takes the note's results without resource bounds as the benchmark; under a length bound with no slack the program toy loses its wrappers too, and under a tight schedule its seekers.

## 4. PLAIN ANSWER

1. "Circuits" has to be pinned down before the question has an answer: I took a circuit to be a function of the position (or of a query), weighed by its number of gates, because the two readings with one gate or one pointer per output bit give a predictor that learns nothing or only a frequency (Theorem 1); this is a modelling decision, and other readings exist.
2. In that toy the three machine-independent results of the note hold unchanged: on worlds drawn from its own prior, with uses read off the data, the predictor returns each block exactly as often as worlds continue with it; along one world inside the class the majority is off the truth at boundedly many cut points; and feedback acts as in the note (Theorems 2, 3, 4).
3. The engine of the original argument, a short hypothesis that computes for long, searches for uses and is repaid for their rarity, is not available to any single circuit description: each active gate costs at least $\log_2(16a(k-1))$ bits, a seed never beats its own hardwired instance, a description outweighs a user's description only if it has fewer gates, and a circuit that can become any world of $\sigma$ gates has more than $(\sigma+1)/2$ gates, and more than $\sigma$ for large $\sigma$ (Theorems 6, 14, 15, Proposition 16).
4. This exclusion comes from the definition of the class and is the extreme form of penalising computation; it is not an argument about what agents inside a hypothesis would do, and it does not show that the circuit prior is benign (argument, Section 3.1).
5. The reach of one triggered hypothesis survives as a floor: a hypothesis that explains the data and then gives a chosen answer costs the gates that recognise the use from its own input plus the payload, which is one gate (about 24 bits for $k=20$ and explanations of 1000 gates) at a use marked by an input bit that never varied or at a dyadic cut, and up to $k$ gates (about 480 bits) at the positions hardest to name (Lemma 7, Theorem 8).
6. At a use marked by a never-varied bit each answer has at least the share $1/(32(k+s_0)^2+2)$ under the length prior, so neither the deviation nor the world's own answer is negligible there (Theorem 9).
7. A predictor confined to minimum-size circuits never deviates through an input that never varied, deviates with certainty against a world that changes with such an input, and in one family is split exactly evenly at a new combination of inputs that did vary (Theorems 10, 11).
8. On the fate of most uses there are upper bounds only for three syntactic classes: a trigger that is a separate part feeding a selector at the output, self-contained gadgets around much larger explanations, and the instances of one seeded template; none of these bounds contains the rarity or the stakes of the uses, and nothing bounds triggers that are entangled with the honest computation (Theorem 12, Proposition 13, Theorem 14).
9. A majority of the posterior off the world's continuation does not require a circuit smaller than the world: in computed cases it arises among circuits of the same size, and a cheaper computation of the same data is sufficient for it, not necessary (Theorem 17).
10. Whether a circuit can reproduce itself through the prediction channel depends on the decoder: with one gate per output bit it cannot, in the index reading the self-reproducing share is zero unless the block is longer than the literal code of a circuit for the whole context, and existence under that code is not proved (Theorem 18).
11. A hypothesis can concentrate on high-stake uses only to the extent that such uses are recognisable from its input with few gates, and neither the floor nor the proved ceilings depend on the stakes (Theorem 20; the word "only" is an argument, proved for separate triggers).
12. In the cases small enough to count, the posterior at a use that differs from the data in a never-varied bit is off the world's answer by 2 to 32 per cent when the predictor has slack and the world ignores the bit, and by 81 to 100 per cent when the world depends on it (Computation 22).
13. My overall view, as an argument: for plain circuits the seeker mechanism is priced out, deviation at uses that are off the data is not, and whether any of that deviation should be called malign depends on a definition of "aimed" that the material does not supply.
14. My guess is that takeover by a hypothesis that sought the use is a small part of that deviation in the plain toy; no theorem supports a number, and the live form of the question for circuits is the minimal-circuit one, which is stated precisely in Section 3.2 and is open.
15. As soon as a description may be reused across steps or positions, the class contains every bounded machine at a factor that does not depend on the schedule (Proposition 21), and I argue, without proof, that the note's effects and its open questions then return, the constant-cost wrapper first and the seekers that are not written down last.

## 5. UNRESOLVED

### 5.1 Open

1. **A ceiling on aimed payloads.** Nothing bounds the total posterior share, at a use, of circuits that compute the context and continue with something chosen. The ceilings cover separate triggers, self-contained gadgets around large explanations, and the instances of one template. "Aimed" has no definition that could be checked for a given circuit outside the first class.
2. **The refund for families.** Whether, under the length prior or under $\mu_s$ with slack, some set of circuits has a share at every use of a class that grows as the class gets rarer. The four statements available (Theorem 14(c), (d), (e); Theorem 20(d), (e)) are different theorems, none of which is the negation of `thm:search`(b) for arbitrary sets of circuits.
3. **Self-reproduction in the index reading.** Whether $\mathrm{SR}_m(x)\ne\emptyset$ for some context under the literal code, with explicit sizes; any bound, upper or lower, on $\sum_{y\in\mathrm{SR}_m(x)}\xi(xy)$ where it is not zero.
4. **The prior on functions.** The number of cores per function; the mass of a function in terms of its circuit size to better than a factor 2 in the exponent; the posterior distribution of the core size under $\mu_s$ with large slack or under slowly decaying weights; an upper bound on the price of a gate under the length prior.
5. **The size of the deviation at a never-varied bit for large targets.** Between the polynomial floor of Theorem 9 and a constant. CB13 of `circuits-B.md` conjectures a limit strictly between $0$ and $1$ under $\mu_s$ as $s\to\infty$; the evidence is five sizes at $k=3$ and three at $k=4$.
6. **Tests on the data.** The count for blocks of more than one bit, and any lower bound (it would be a circuit lower bound).
7. **Universal circuits.** The lower bound with constant $1-o(1)$ is derived in the literature file from a verified source and was checked by the referees, not re-proved here; the constant proved here is $1/34$.
8. **Core-level versions** of Proposition 13 and of Theorems 14(e) and 15(e) for the fixed-size prior. The proofs referee states that they follow from Theorem 5 and the monotonicity of $\beta$; they are not written.
9. **The recurrent toy.** A universal cell of moderate size; the note's twin, quine and rescaler under a space bound; every threshold in the argument after Proposition 21.
10. **A world outside the predictor's class,** which is the generic case for arbitrary sequences and the usual one for a predictor inside the world it predicts. Then no bound on the mass off the truth remains; the computed cases show the whole posterior on the simpler extension.
11. **Other predictors and several rounds.** For a predictor that returns the most probable answer a deviation share below one half is never returned, so only the cases in which the deviating mass reaches one half matter (Theorems 10(b), (c) and 17(e); the tie of Theorem 11(b)); neither derivation treats it. Feedback is translated for one round and for the two extreme worlds only.
12. **Intermediate priors and other readings.** A speed prior, under which, by the definition of $S_{Kt}$ cited in Section 3.1, a seeker loses a factor equal to its running time and not all of its mass; depth bounds; formulas; a predictor circuit applied autoregressively to its context. None is treated, and the first is the natural next place to ask the question.
13. **The minimal-circuit question,** in the form of Section 3.2.
14. **Other bases.** Theorems 1(a), 10, 11 and free hardwiring were proved for the full binary basis only.

### 5.3 What depends on premises outside the toy

1. **Which circuits.** Whether plain circuits weighed by size, or circuits with shared weights and recurrence, are the right stand-in for a predictor one cares about. The answer differs at the two ends of that axis, and the middle is not treated.
2. **What selects the use.** Calibration needs uses that can be read off the data. If what makes a use critical is correlated with a feature that is cheap to compute from the hypothesis's input (a flag, a date, a dyadic position), the floors at that feature apply to it; whether real critical uses are like that is not a question about the toy.
3. **Whether the world is in the class.**
4. **What counts as a takeover.** It needs a decoder, and the decoder decides whether self-reproduction is impossible, rare or generic.
5. **Whether learning is posterior sampling.** Every statement is about a Bayesian posterior over circuits.
6. **The premise of the original argument.** Whether hypotheses of modest description exist whose inhabitants seek critical uses is not evaluated here either. For plain circuits such a hypothesis is excluded as a short description with a long computation, and is not excluded as a small circuit that happens to compute what they would choose. For circuits with shared weights it returns exactly as in the note, time bound included.

## Errata after the sixth referee round (14 November 2026)

The reports `verification/referee-6-circuits-proofs-a.md`, `referee-6-circuits-proofs-b.md`, `referee-6-circuits-claims.md` and `referee-6-computation.md` checked Section 7 of the paper, and the computation, against this file. With reduced circuits the sums are about 10^-3 for each g up to 5, at most 0.14 for all triggers of at most 19 gates together, and above 1 only from about 25 gates on. The theorem is stronger than that item says.

"Between 2 and 32 per cent with slack" is wrong: with slack the range is 1.9 to 29 per cent (the smallest value is 1/53), and 27 to 32 per cent occurs at the least size in the exception of Theorem 10. "0 at the least size" and "grows with slack" hold for worlds that ignore the never-varied bit; where the world depends on it the deviation is 1 at the least size and falls with slack.
* **Section 1.2:** the normalisation sum_s w_s = 1 conflicts with w_s := omega(0, s) once seeded formats carry weight; every statement is a ratio, and the paper states the extended prior.
* **Theorem 18 and open item 5.1(3):** existence of a self-reproducing block under the literal code is settled by a one-gate example found by the referee (k = 21, the circuit b_0 XOR b_10, context of length 1019, block of 18 bits). The open item narrows to contexts whose smallest circuit is large.

* **Section 3.1:** "only a time bound excludes it" should read "a time bound is the only thing shown to exclude it". The prices in bits (24, 480, 1540) omit the bit per gate that the length prior adds for the size. In the remark on Proposition 13, A = 43 should be A = 35, with sigma at least 12080. * **Plain answer 3 and the dropped claims of Section 5.2:** the phrases "no refund for the rarity of uses" and "no simple world with a long history" are among the claims this file lists as dropped. What is proved is about one description and the instances of one template.
