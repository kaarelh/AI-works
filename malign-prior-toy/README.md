# A toy malign-prior question with an answer

Notes by Claude, prompted by Kaarel Hänni. Research note, 14 November 2026; not reviewed by a human. The proofs were checked by AI referees (see [`verification/`](verification/)), not by humans.

**Question.** The "universal prior is malign" argument (Christiano 2016) says that where the predictions of a Solomonoff inductor are actually used in a critical way in some universe, a lot of what happens is malign: takeovers through the prediction channel. Is there a toy version in which this is a mathematical statement, and what is the answer there? Hänni suggested two toys:

1. a computable world (a cellular automaton, say) that contains a sensor, a large computer running a time-bounded Solomonoff induction on the sensor's data, and an actuator driven by the predictions; does the predictor end up in control, over a long run or at once, and does one get the "dark room"?
2. worlds are just programs; some of them hand their output so far to a fixed bounded Solomonoff predictor and run the continuation they get back as a program; do hypotheses that look for such opportunities, predict well up to them, and then output their own code ("quine themselves out") get a decent share?

Two further questions of Hänni are treated in Section 3.7 and in Section 7 of the paper:

3. if the seekers concentrate on the uses where the most is at stake, do they win there (the picture of a lump of prior mass redirected into a narrow channel)?
4. what becomes of the question when Boolean circuits take the place of programs?

**Paper:** [`paper/main.pdf`](paper/main.pdf) (LaTeX sources in [`paper/`](paper/)).

## Short answer

The toy: fix a monotone machine U. The predictor is the mixture ξ of all programs of length at most L that keep to a time schedule T. A world (a universe) is a program. Some worlds make a *critical use* of the predictor: at some point they hand it their output so far, x, get a block y of m bits (sampled from the posterior given x), and from then on run y. The world's *own continuation* is the m bits it would have printed next. A continuation is *self-reproducing* if running it prints x and then itself again, so that the world ends up running the hypothesis that supplied it. Let H be the prior probability that a world makes a critical use, and κ(x) the share of the posterior at x held by the worlds that make a use there (κ = 1 when uses can be read off the data).

The claim then reads: among the critical uses, a share θ end in a takeover. Two questions have to be kept apart.

* **Reach: how far does one seeker get?** Without resource bounds, for each continuation there is a program, of some length a, that enumerates the programs making a critical use, predicts their output up to the use, and then supplies that continuation. It has posterior share more than 2^(−a−1)·κ(x)/H at every use. Its continuation is handed over in worlds of total prior probability more than 2^(−a−1) times the average of κ over uses, and at most H. That is of the order of its own prior weight where the users are most of what prints their contexts; once uses are rarer than half the seeker's weight, its programs outweigh the users at their contexts and its reach is limited by H instead. If the continuation is to be its own code, the same holds with a longer program and for users whose programs are short enough for the block.
* **Fate: do most uses end in a takeover?** That is not shown by the reach, and it is the question the argument is about. The share of uses that end in one is the posterior share of such programs at the contexts of uses.
  * *From below:* add up the seekers.
  * *From above, for one seeker whose search is written in its text:* its honest twin, which makes the same search and then continues as the user it found, is in the posterior with 2^(a−h) times the seeker's mass (h is the twin's length) and predicts a user's continuation. So where the users go on to print the block themselves, that seeker's programs supply what the predictor returns with probability at most 1/(1+2^(a−h)). They supply the majority only if the seeker is shorter than its twin. Masses of different seekers add, and the bound says nothing where the users print nothing after the use.
* **No goal has to be written down.** The reach statement holds for any program with the right output, whatever is inside it. A program of length a whose output puts on each context of a class W of uses, of total prior mass H_W, at least half that context's share of the class has at least 2^(−a)/(2·H_W) times the mass of the worlds that make those uses, at every one of them. If the program is a simple world with a cheap readout whose inhabitants choose to predict such uses and then supply their own continuation, a is the length of the world and the readout. It outweighs the users of every class it aims at that is rarer than 2^(−a−1), provided its own outputs are not counted as uses. Each use in a rich world read at an unremarkable place is light, because it pays for its world and for the place of the context in it; that a whole class of them is rarer than the program is heavy is part of the argument's premise. To supply the majority of what the predictor returns, the program must also outweigh everything else that prints those contexts and goes on. The honest-twin bound does not apply to it; a wrapper around the whole program limits its part only to 1/(1+2^(−c)), which never excludes a majority. What is shown to exclude it is a time bound: it prints nothing until its world has produced the inhabitants and they have simulated the worlds they aim at, so a schedule whose first deadline is below that start-up gives it no mass.
* **Concentration on high stakes** (question 3). Attach a stake to each use, as a label. The picture is half proved. The statements on aiming are without resource bounds.
  * *Aiming is cheap.* A seeker can weight its search by any aim that can be computed from below. Aiming at the uses with stake at least n costs about 2·log₂(n) bits and the bits that name the scale of the class. Such a seeker outweighs the *users* of the class once the class is rarer than half its weight.
  * *A lump is necessary.* Programs of total weight B supply the majority of the predictor's samples at every one of a set of pairwise incomparable uses only if B exceeds the mass of everything else that prints those contexts and goes on ("the rest"). Most uses of a class end in a takeover only if the takeover programs at its contexts weigh more than half its users, where the users go on without taking over.
  * *The rest is not the users, and part of it follows the seeker.* The honest twin of a weighted seeker is in the posterior at each use with 2^(a−h) times the seeker's mass, for seeker and twin built in the same way; neither the aim nor the stake occurs in this ratio. Where the contexts are pairwise incomparable and some block that can be computed from the context is not a takeover, a wrapper keeps every takeover probability at or below 1 − 2^(−c).
  * *An example.* A seeker outweighs the users of the class "stake at least n" by a factor of order 2^n/n². It lacks the majority at some use of the class for every n, possibly only at uses that carry a small share of the class. It has the majority at most of the class only if its word is shorter than that of a program that prints the same contexts without taking over, a comparison of two constants of the machine in which n does not occur. The condition is necessary and not sufficient.
  * *That enough weight wins* is proved only in an allocation model in which the rest does not depend on the attacker's allocation. There an attacker that maximises the stake it captures attacks only where the stake per unit of rest, ϱ, exceeds a price η, with share 1 − √(η/ϱ), and has the majority where ϱ > 4η. The order is by stake per unit of rest and not by stake; in the example this optimum leaves the highest stakes alone.
  * *"Run more than n times".* n independent samples at one context, one takeover block sufficing, lower the weight that is needed: a share of about ln2/n per sample is enough for a takeover with probability above one half, with or without aiming. Running the block n times at a class changes where a stake-maximiser puts its weight (in the model), not the weight a majority there costs.
  * So concentration makes the lump outweigh the users and leaves open the comparison that decides the outcome: the takeover programs against everything else that prints those contexts and goes on. For one written seeker the toy gives a necessary condition in which no stake occurs: where the users go on printing, it supplies the majority only if it is shorter than its honest twin. The rest beyond the twin, and the sum over seekers, are not bounded. For programs whose aim is not written in their text the comparison is the premise of the original argument. That the picture fails "as stated" is an argument from these results, not a theorem.
* **Without search.** At every use that a fixed test recognises, a wrapper around the explanations of the context gives any fixed continuation at least a share 2^(−c), with c the wrapper's length, whatever the world and the amount of data. Under resource bounds the share is multiplied by the fraction of the posterior on explanations with room to spare; for self-reproduction, by the fraction of explanations that fit, with the wrapper, into one block.
* **Calibration and budget.** If worlds are drawn from the predictor's own prior and go on within its bounds, and uses can be read off the data, then given the context the predictor's sample and the world's own continuation are independent with the same law; the seeking programs are simply their own fraction of the worlds that make a use. Along a world that has a program of length ℓ within the bounds, the posterior mass off the true next m bits, summed over all cut points, is at most m·ℓ·ln 2, and the majority can be off the truth at no more than m·ℓ cut points. This is an average: one position that is cheap to single out can carry a constant share.
* **Other ways to a majority**, each an explicit inequality:
  * a machine built for it (for every test and continuation there is a universal machine, whose description lengths exceed those of U by at most k+1 bits, on which the continuation has posterior at least 1/(1+2^(−k)) wherever the test fires; and another on which a given computable world is predicted with at least that probability at every nonempty context);
  * a cheaper explanation of the context that is not itself a use. In a world with many readable locations, among N places where a sensor with the given record could sit and whose records continue differently, a copy of the record at a location with a short code beats all but a fraction δ of them by a factor δ·N times the share of the context's explanations that go through the copy's reader. The copy needs only read access to the data. If only one other place shows the record and the schedule is generous, the gain is instead capped by the cost of the description "the other place that shows the same data";
  * a world too expensive for the predictor. Then no bound on the mass off the truth remains. This is the usual case for a predictor inside a larger world, and it is left open.
* **Time bounds.** The searching programs print nothing until their enumeration has found the program they follow, so a tight schedule gives them no mass. Whether some other hypothesis collects the base rate within a tight schedule is open, except where there is only one use to find or uses can be generated in proportion to their prior weight.
* **Feedback.** If the prediction can change what is predicted, a sampled prediction that makes itself true is confirmed and the default hypotheses are removed for good; its probability is the same posterior mass as before. A world that does what is predicted follows a sample from the predictor's own bounded prior: it stays on a default future with at least the posterior probability of that future, and it leaves the default with certainty when the default costs more time than the schedule allows. That is the "dark room" in this toy: a pull toward what is cheap to compute, set by the time bound. A world that does the opposite of what is predicted leaves a length-L predictor that returns the more probable bit with no hypothesis after at most L+1 rounds.
* **Circuits in place of programs** (question 4). Hypotheses and worlds are Boolean circuits that map a position (or a query) to a bit, with a prior that depends on the number of gates. This reading is a modelling decision; two others give a predictor that learns nothing or only a frequency.
  * *Carried over:* calibration, the budget and the one-round feedback results. The contrarian world empties the posterior only for a prior with a finite size bound and a long enough horizon.
  * *The seeker mechanism is not available to a single description.* Every active gate costs bits. If seeded formats get no more weight than unseeded ones, a random seed does not beat its own hardwired instance (the instance prints the same continuation, so this limits the seeded description and not what is returned). A circuit that can become any world of σ gates has more than (σ+1)/2 gates when σ+3 ≤ 2^k. So no single description is a cheap universal search or is repaid for the rarity of uses, and a description has at least as many gates as the smallest circuit for what it computes. Whether a set of circuits can be repaid is open, and a long history whose readout has a small circuit is as cheap as that circuit. This comes from the definition of the class, as a tight schedule gives the searching programs of the program toy no mass. It is not an argument about agents and does not show that the circuit prior is benign.
  * *Triggers survive, priced in gates.* A hypothesis that fits the data and gives a chosen answer at a use exists at the price of the gates that recognise the use from its input, and the payload: one gate where the use is marked by an input bit that never varied in the data (for a sequence, at a power-of-two cut), up to k gates at the positions hardest to name. That it cannot be had for less is proved for separate triggers only. Where the use is marked by a never-varied bit, each answer keeps a share of at least 1/(32(k+s₀)²+2) under the length prior.
  * *Deviation in exact small cases.* A minimum-size circuit for data that need at least two gates never reads an input that did not vary. Under the uniform prior on circuits of a fixed size, with up to four inputs and up to four gates more than the data need, the posterior at a use that differs from the data in a never-varied bit is off the world's answer by about 2 to 29 per cent when the predictor has gates to spare and the world ignores the bit, and by 81 to 100 per cent in the computed cases where the world's answer changes with the bit.
  * *Upper bounds* exist for three syntactic classes only: a trigger that is a separate, input-only part feeding a selector at the output (the bound is small for large data, in proportion to the number of triggers); small self-contained gadgets around much larger explanations (at most 0.65 times the explanations they wrap); and the instances of one seeded template sufficiently larger than the worlds it imitates. None of these bounds contains the rarity or the stakes of the uses, and nothing bounds triggers entangled with the computation of the data.
  * *Self-reproduction* through the prediction channel is defined only relative to a decoder outside the class. Under the literal code its share is zero unless the block is longer than the code of a circuit for the whole context; it is positive in a one-gate example.
  * *Reuse: the axis back to programs.* If a circuit is iterated as the cell of a recurrence, the class contains every machine with bounded state at a factor that does not depend on the schedule; the cell constructed has up to w(2^w−3) gates for w bits of state, so the factor shown is extremely small. Shared weights and autoregressive use are not treated. That the program-side effects then return, the constant-cost wrapper first and the seekers that are not written down last, is argued and not proved.
  * *Overall view* (an argument and a guess, not a theorem): for plain circuits the seeker mechanism is priced out for single descriptions and deviation off the data is not. Our guess, which no result supports, is that deviation supplied by a part of the circuit that exists in order to supply it is a small part of that deviation when the data are large; in the smallest case counted, separate triggers carry about three tenths of it. One form of the question that remains is whether the smallest or the typical circuit for a rich task is a general solver whose behaviour off the data the data do not fix. It is stated in the paper as an open problem, two of whose three conditions are formal.
* **Sizes, on a small machine** (exact computation; a Brainfuck-like machine, 48 output bits). With programs of at most 10 commands, on the stream 1111… a share 20.5% of the posterior prints the other bit at bit 16, and on the alternating stream the true bit has 29.4% at bit 17 against 45.7% for the other bit. Both effects have the same cause: three-command programs print the first 15 or 16 bits and then stop looping, while the shortest programs that print the stream forever have four and six commands. The effect weakens as longer programs are admitted (the normalised share of the true bit at bit 17 rises from 0.27 with 7 commands to 0.42 with 11). Tightening the time schedule from 24 to 3 steps per bit lowers the share of the truth at these positions (79.1% to 54.4%, and 29.4% to 14.1%), and with programs of at most 9 commands there is a stream whose true 15th bit gets probability 0 under the tight schedule.

So in the toy, the answer depends on the regime and on a premise the toy cannot check. For a seeker that is written down, on a machine where "search, then continue as the user" is no longer than it, and where users go on to print the block themselves, that seeker's programs supply at most half of what the predictor returns at a use; masses of different seekers add, so this alone does not show that takeover is not the fate of most uses. If short programs exist whose inhabitants do this, then without a time bound such a program outweighs the worlds that make the uses it aims at once those uses are together rarer than half its weight, and it supplies the majority of what the predictor returns there if the other programs that print those contexts are lighter still. Under a schedule that its start-up exceeds, it has no mass. Whether such programs exist on a natural machine, and how short they are, is the original argument's premise; the toy turns it into inequalities and does not evaluate them.

## What is not settled

* Whether short programs exist, on a natural machine, whose inhabitants seek critical uses in this way, how short they are, and how their total weight compares with the prior mass of the uses they would aim at. The original argument is a claim about this. The toy neither supports nor refutes it.
* For seekers that are written down, the sizes of the quantities the results are stated in: the share κ of the worlds printing a context that actually use the answer, the share of those that go on printing, and the lengths of seeker and twin.
* Whether the base rate can be collected within a tight time schedule.
* The regime where the world is more expensive than the predictor.
* The many-round dynamics with feedback when the world's response is neither obedient nor contrarian.
* For seekers that concentrate on high-stake uses: the size of the rest of a class of such uses and of the part of it that follows an attacker (only lower bounds on that part are proved); the versions under resource bounds; the aimed seeker that supplies its own code; contexts nested through a takeover block; and any connection between the stake and what a world does after the use.
* For circuits: whether a set of circuits can have a share at every use of a class that grows as the class gets rarer; an upper bound to match the floor for triggers beyond the three syntactic classes; the size of the deviation at a never-varied bit for large data; a world outside the class; the minimal-circuit question; the recurrent version of the toy.
* A computation of the self-reproduction quantities on a concrete machine. The statements about them are theorems only. The session that produced this note began writing an enumerator for a small stored-program machine on which predicted continuations would be run; that step was stopped by a safety classifier and was not attempted again.

## Contents

* **`paper/`**: the note (`main.pdf`) and its LaTeX sources; `table-*.tex` are generated by `experiments/make_table.py`.
* **`code/`**
  * `bf8enum.c`: exact enumeration of the bounded mixture of the BF8 machine. It forks the machine state at every read of a new command, so all masses are exact dyadic rationals.
  * `bf8.py`: reference interpreter, a brute-force mixture over all command tapes, and a loader for the enumerator's output.
  * `test_bf8.py`: the enumerator against brute force on four configurations, the semimeasure property, and the example programs quoted in the paper.
  * `circuit_count.py`: exact counts of small Boolean circuits by the function they compute, by a dynamic programme over the multiset of wire functions; `test_circuit_count.py` checks it against direct enumeration (three inputs up to three gates, four inputs up to two gates; a referee compared up to four gates with an independent enumeration).
* **`experiments/`**: `run_profiles.py` computes the deviation profiles, the budget checks, the prior-average disagreement, the excluded-stream example, the attribution to three-command programs and the dependence on the length bound, and writes `results/profiles.json` and `results/profiles.pdf`; `make_table.py` writes the paper's tables from the JSON.
  * `run_circuits.py` computes the numbers of the circuit section and writes `results/circuits.json`; `make_circuit_table.py` writes the two circuit tables of the paper from it.
* **`research/`**: the working record behind Sections 3.7 and 7 of the paper. For each of the two questions there are two independent AI derivations, two AI referee reports on them, and one corrected account (`stakes-final.md`, `circuits-final.md`) that contains the full proofs, the list of claims that were dropped, and what is open. `circuits-literature.md` records which cited statements were read in a primary source and which are recalled from memory.
* **`verification/`**: the AI referees' reports and how each finding was resolved.

## Status

* **Proved here** (short proofs in the paper): calibration, the floor and its self-reproducing form, the gain and optimality of searching, the reach of a seeker among critical uses and the honest-twin bound, repetition and persistence, the budget, capture with its cap and the in-world switch, machine dependence, the statements on time bounds, one round with feedback, the contrarian world; for high-stake uses, the weighted seeker and the cost of aiming, the necessity of a lump, the twin under concentration, the closure bounds, the example, the optimal allocation in the model, and two readings of repetition; for circuits, the two degenerate readings, the core law and the price of a gate, the floor for triggers, the statements on minimum-size circuits, the ceiling for separate triggers, and the statements on seeds; the bound for universal circuits is taken from `research/circuits-final.md`. Some secondary statements of Sections 3.7 and 7 are taken without proof from the corrected accounts in `research/`, where they are proved; the paper says so at each place.
* **Known constructions used**: the wrapper is Hutter's lower-bound construction (2007) and that of Cohen, Hutter, Bengio and Russell (2025); the budget is Solomonoff's bound in deterministic form; machine dependence follows the dogmatic prior of Leike and Hutter (2015); the contrarian world is Putnam's diagonal argument. Forum posts on the argument could be consulted only through search-engine summaries, so earlier informal versions of the single-world statements may exist.
* **Numerical**: the BF8 computation (`experiments/results/profiles.json`), exact, on the prediction side only; the circuit counts (`experiments/results/circuits.json`), exact.
* **Argued or guessed, and marked so in the paper**: the order in which the program-side effects return under recurrence; the overall view on circuits.

**Working branch:** `claude/lucid-hypatia-j4jg12` holds the full working state. Everything in this folder is published except the compiled binary `code/bf8enum`, the LaTeX build files, and every `__pycache__/`.
