\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Solomonoff's \emph{operator induction} problem asks for the probability of each answer to a new question, given an unordered set of question--answer pairs \citep{solomonoff1999two,solomonoff2008three}: supervised learning. Solomonoff gave Bayesian solutions with a universal prior, and a convergence theorem \citep{solomonoff2008three}; as in the companion paper \citep{claudeSFI2026fundamentals}, we call the family of such solutions \emph{Solomonoff operator induction} (SOI). It is the supervised-learning counterpart of Solomonoff induction (SI), and like SI it learns every computable regularity of the labels. We study the following proposal for Solomonoff's per-query solution \citep[eq.~(10)]{solomonoff2008three}, a stronger simplicity prior: penalise the length of a hypothesis more than its likelihood term, so that what is selected is a simple model with noise.\footnote{The proposal first appeared in H\"anni's note \citep{hanni2026grokking} and in an online discussion (on LessWrong), where the absorption objection described below was also raised. So did the suggestion that private randomness at each input blocks this objection, the three-hypothesis calculation below (in both versions of \cref{rem:teacher:hanni}), the question whether its good model is a vertex of the convex hull, and an absolute complexity bound as an alternative. The one-tape version of SOI first appeared in H\"anni's working notes \citep{hanni2026notes}.} In the form studied here, a hypothesis is a program $\pi$ of length $\ell(\pi)$ with likelihood $P_\pi$, and one minimises $J_\lambda(\pi;D)=\lambda\,\ell(\pi)-\log P_\pi(D)$ with $\lambda>1$, or uses the power prior $2^{-\lambda\ell(\pi)}$ (\cref{def:setup:objectives}), over probabilistic programs that use fresh coins at every input: private randomness, as in the per-query solution, which is the mixture with the prior $2^{-\ell(\pi)}$, the case $\lambda=1$ (\cref{sec:setup}).

We give two reasons for such a penalty. The first, and the main one, is to split data into a model and noise: labels drawn by a simple sampler are explained by the sampler and by \emph{fill-ins}, deterministic programs that store its coin flips, and at $\lambda=1$ the two get similar posterior weight on a data set, in the per-query solution and in SI (\cref{thm:split:lambda1,prop:split:si}). Does a steeper prior select the sampler? One motivation is imitation: a learner trained on a person's answers should return a copy of the person, a sampler with its own randomness, rather than the sampler with some of its sampled bits stored, or a different system that predicts the person's answers better than the person does. If the person's coins are private this is the first reason; if the environment drives them and the input partly reveals them, it needs the schedule of the second reason below (\cref{sec:imit}). The second reason is not to learn a teacher's systematic mistakes, as a human learner grasps what a teacher means by a word without learning to predict what the teacher would say. With a $100$-bit simple model and systematic errors on a $2^{-10}$ fraction of the inputs, whose set takes $10{,}000$ bits to specify, the simple model with noise beats a model that learns the errors and a fair coin for a range of $\lambda/n$ (\cref{rem:teacher:hanni}); but three hypotheses are not all hypotheses. Both reasons rest on one geometry, a kink of the hull of the points (length, fit) with the discarded part to its right; noise is used at one input and an error rule at many, so noise needs only a fixed $\lambda>1$ and errors need $\lambda$ proportional to the number $n$ of samples (\cref{tab:discussion:reasons}).

A standard objection, the \emph{absorption objection}, is that under a steeper penalty a hypothesis that is itself a universal mixture is short, penalises complexity inside itself only in the usual way, and so absorbs all structure, noise and errors included. It holds in SI and in the one-tape version of SOI, whose random bits are shared by all inputs (\cref{thm:absorb:absorption,thm:split:shared}); in the class of the per-query solution, where each input samples its own random bits, no hypothesis is universal (\cref{cor:absorb:nouniversal}).

\paragraph{Conventions.} Logarithms are to base $2$; $N=2^d$, $c=\lambda/n$, and $\kappa$ is a constant depending only on the reference machine and fixed programs; numbers computed with $\kappa=0$ are illustrations. A shared hypothesis $P$ (\cref{def:setup:shared}) predicts $P(D+(x,y))/P(D)$ (the \emph{semimeasure convention}, whose log loss telescopes in every order to at most $-\log P(D)$), or normalises by $\sum_{y'}P(D+(x,y'))\le P(D)$, which never increases losses. The data objects DS (one data set), FR (frozen labels, one per distinct input) and PQ (fresh labels at every query) are those of \cref{def:setup:data}.

\paragraph{Main results.} \textbf{Theorem A} (informal; exact recovery). \emph{Let the inputs be i.i.d.\ from any law $\mu$ on $\{0,1\}^*$ and each label drawn by a sampler $\nu$ with fresh coins at every query, so that a repeated input gets a fresh label. Let the hypotheses be the programs of a countable class with Kraft weights that contains a program for $\nu$, each a kernel with private randomness (all lower-semicomputable ones, or a time-bounded, circuit or finite subclass). For every schedule $1\le\lambda_n=o(n)$, $\lambda=1$ included, almost surely every $\lambda_n$-MAP is eventually a shortest program whose kernel equals $\nu$ $\mu$-almost everywhere. For fixed $\lambda>1$, the $\lambda$-MAPs are exactly these programs with probability at least $1-\varepsilon$ at every $n\ge\lambda(\ell_0+\log(2/\varepsilon))\ln2/(2\min(1,\lambda-1)\gamma)$, where $\ell_0$ is their length and $\gamma$ a margin, a lower bound on the average squared Hellinger distance to $\nu$ (the Hellinger gap of \cref{def:split:setting}) of the inequivalent programs up to a length fixed by $\lambda$, $\ell_0$ and $\varepsilon$; and at every $n$ the fill-ins have posterior mass exponentially small in the sampled randomness. At $\lambda=1$ no recovery time depends only on $(\ell_0,\gamma,\varepsilon)$; in a class of parities with a uniform margin it is $\Theta(2^{\ell_0/2})$, against $O(\lambda\ell_0/(\lambda-1))$ for fixed $\lambda>1$. With frozen labels no procedure recovers every computable sampler, and every $\lambda\ge1$ eventually selects programs equivalent to $\nu$ on the run, with summable Hellinger gaps to $\nu$ along the queried inputs.} (\Cref{thm:recovery:main,prop:recovery:lambda1,prop:recovery:frozen,thm:split:private}.)

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Theorem B} (informal; a kink implies the split). \emph{On one data set, plot each program at its length and its fit, minus its log-likelihood; the $\lambda$-MAPs lie on the lower convex hull where its slope crosses $-\lambda$. Let a program be $b$-sufficient (its two-part code length is within $b$ bits of $K(y_D\mid\dom D)$, the least possible up to a constant) and sit at a kink: every program more than $w$ bits shorter fits worse by at least $\sigma>1$ bits per bit saved. Then for every $\lambda\in(1,\sigma)$ every $\lambda$-MAP is between $w$ bits shorter and $(b+\kappa)/(\lambda-1)$ bits longer, fits at most $\lambda w$ bits worse and $\lambda(b+\kappa)/(\lambda-1)$ bits better, and is $(b+(\lambda-1)w)$-sufficient. Conversely, MAP lengths that stay in an interval for all $\lambda$ in an interval occur only at kinks.} (\Cref{lem:setup:hull,cor:kink:split,thm:kink:converse}.) With high probability a per-query sample has a kink of the same kind at the shortest equivalent program, with steepness linear in $n$ and the Ville deficiency in place of the sufficiency slack (\cref{thm:recovery:kink}). The full table of a noisy random parity has one at the parity, and for large $d$ and typical error sets ($\delta_E=O(d)$) every fixed $\lambda>1$ selects ``parity plus noise'' at each dyadic flip rate below $1/4$; set models have no steep kink there (\cref{prop:parity:kink,prop:parity:range,prop:parity:sets}). A universal member flattens every kink far beyond its own length (\cref{prop:kink:universal}).

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Theorem C} (informal; the teacher's mistakes). \emph{Let the inputs lie in $\{0,1\}^d$, let the simple model be a random parity $f_a$, and let a teacher flip its labels on the inputs whose first $m$ bits lie in an error rule $S\subseteq\{0,1\}^m$ of density $p<0.05596$, so that the error set $S\times\{0,1\}^{d-m}$ has product form. Score each hypothesis $\pi$ with private randomness by the population objective $c\,\ell(\pi)+R(\pi)$, with $R(\pi)$ its expected log loss on the teacher's labels. If $S$ is incompressible relative to the parity, the inputs are uniform and $\lambda=cn$ with $c$ in an explicit window $(2^{-m},c_{\rm hi})$, every near-optimal product hypothesis of the population objective knows the parity, is short, holds little information about $S$ and gives the erroneous labels average probability at most $0.200$ $(\kappa=0)$ or $0.274$ $(\kappa=30)$ at $c=10^{-4}$ and the sizes given below, against $1$ for a memoriser. Below about $2^{-m}$ the memoriser beats every hypothesis that knows the parity and fits no better than noise.} (\Cref{thm:teacher:main,prop:teacher:kinks}.)

\smallskip\noindent The example's sizes are $d=100$, $m=20$, $p=2^{-10}$ and $11{,}710$ bits for $S$, with deficiency $\delta_S=0$; there the window is $(9.54\cdot10^{-7},8.06\cdot10^{-3}]$ at $\kappa=0$ and $(9.54\cdot10^{-7},3.65\cdot10^{-3}]$ at $\kappa=30$ (\cref{rem:teacher:numbers}). The lower end $2^{-m}$ is the \emph{reuse factor}: each bit of the error rule serves about $n2^{-m}$ of the $n$ samples, a bit of noise one, which is why errors need $\lambda\propto n$ and noise only a fixed $\lambda>1$ (\cref{lem:teacher:reuse,prop:teacher:schedules}). Asymptotically in $m$, windows exist at every flip rate $p\le0.1233$ (\cref{prop:teacher:rates}).

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=0pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \emph{Absorption} (\cref{sec:absorb}). With a universal member every $\lambda$-MAP is shorter than a data-independent bound, a computable teacher's errors are eventually predicted for fixed $\lambda>1$, and no sampler longer than that bound is selected (\cref{thm:absorb:absorption,thm:split:shared}); no private-randomness hypothesis is universal, at a price on shared latent coins and up to labelled history (\cref{cor:absorb:nouniversal,prop:absorb:price,prop:absorb:history}).
\item \emph{Kinks} (\cref{sec:kinks}). One kink lemma for both reasons (\cref{lem:kink:main}) and Theorem B; a universal member flattens every kink far beyond its own length (\cref{prop:kink:universal}).
\item \emph{Exact recovery, the first reason} (\cref{sec:recovery}). Theorem A, with each ingredient of its rate necessary separately, up to logarithms (\cref{thm:recovery:main,prop:recovery:lambda1,prop:recovery:necessity}).
\item \emph{The noisy random parity} (\cref{sec:parity}). The full-table kink, with certified $\lambda$-ranges (\cref{prop:parity:kink,prop:parity:range}); no steep set kink, and product sophistication above set sophistication by $d-O(\log d)$ bits (\cref{prop:parity:sets,prop:algstat:parity}).
\item \emph{The teacher's mistakes, the second reason} (\cref{sec:teacher}). Theorem C as the kink lemma with right slope $2^{-m}$ (\cref{prop:teacher:kinks,thm:teacher:main}); the reuse factor, the schedules that keep the identification, and plain Bayes, MDL and SI, which learn the errors (\cref{lem:teacher:reuse,prop:teacher:schedules,prop:limits:plain}); full tables for every $m$ (\cref{cor:teacher:fulltable}); flip rates below $0.1233$ (\cref{prop:teacher:sharp,prop:teacher:rates}).
\item \emph{Limits and computable classes} (\cref{sec:limits,sec:computable}). Structured error rules add a second kink, and cheap frequent rules defeat every $c$ (\cref{prop:limits:envelope,prop:limits:rateblind,lem:limits:robust}); selection sorts components by rate, not validity (\cref{prop:limits:separable}); both reasons survive resource bounds (\cref{prop:related:computable}).
\item \emph{Imitation} (\cref{sec:imit}). If a sampler's coins are an incompressible function of an environment variable on many more cells than the sampler has bits, other hypotheses can predict better, yet for $\lambda/n$ in a window every near-optimal private-randomness hypothesis, whatever its structure, is close to the sampler in behaviour (\cref{thm:imit:main}); imitation data identify only the data kernel, not the mechanism or the split into person and environment, cheap couplings defeat every $\lambda/n$, and no procedure reliably copies a sampler entangled with its coupling (\cref{prop:imit:limits,prop:imit:cheap}).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Scope.} We do not claim to solve the problems that motivate the two reasons, imitating a person and learning what a teacher means rather than what the teacher says. We offer tools for them: the private-randomness class, the kink geometry, the reuse factor and a per-sample threshold on the rate at which a component buys fit. With them we solve toy cases, for idealised learners that minimise the objective exactly over a hypothesis class, in the main results all programs of a fixed reference machine: exact recovery of a computable sampler from per-query samples with i.i.d.\ inputs (\cref{thm:recovery:main}); the full table of a noisy random parity with a dyadic flip rate below $\frac14$, for large $d$ and typical error sets (\cref{prop:parity:range}); a teacher who flips a random parity on an incompressible error rule of product form, with uniform inputs and flip rates below $0.05596$, or $0.1233$ asymptotically (\cref{thm:teacher:main,rem:teacher:hypotheses,prop:teacher:rates}); and, up to near-copies and for $\lambda/n$ in a window, a sampler whose coins are an incompressible function of the environment, with far more entropy summed over the cells than the sampler has bits (\cref{thm:imit:main}); the last two for the population objective, and from samples only asymptotically or with large constants. For realistic error patterns we have counterexamples but no positive result, and conceptual problems remain open (\cref{sec:discussion}), among them that imitation data identify behaviour, not mechanism, and settle neither which structure belongs to the person nor the complexity weight. Exact recovery holds per query; from labels of a computable function flipped at a computable rate below $\frac12$ it recovers the function ($\mu$-a.e.) and the rate (\cref{cor:recovery:noisy}). With $\lambda=cn$ the learner is a family of batch objectives (\cref{rem:setup:persample}).
