\begin{abstract}
Parity is easy to compute: the XOR of $d$ input bits has a Boolean circuit
with $O(d)$ gates and a polynomial-size, polynomial-weight $\tanh$ network.
We show that a family of Bayesian neural-network learners nevertheless
requires exponentially many examples to learn it. The networks have fixed
depth, independent Gaussian weights, and a width-dependent prior specified
by an exponent $\gamma$. For every fixed $\gamma<1$, there is $c>0$
such that each sample size $n\le e^{cd}$ leaves at least $1/16$ of Boolean inputs without
correct-label posterior probability greater than $2/3$, with probability
at least $3/4$ over uniform independent training inputs. This conclusion
takes width to infinity before dimension or sample size grows. The prior
is conditioned on a fixed absolute output margin at every input, and
training conditions on the observed labels. We prove the bound for that
posterior, including mixtures of tied minimum-energy sign functions.
The proof combines the network's Gaussian width limits with kernel
spectra, uniform sampling bounds, and estimates of parity correlation.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Introduction}
A target function may have a small neural-network implementation without
being easy to learn under a particular neural-network prior. The
statistical question is whether the prior gives enough preference to
simple implementations that their labels can be inferred from few
examples. Parity provides a useful test. On $d$ Boolean inputs, it is
computed by a circuit with $O(d)$ gates; it also has a polynomial-size,
polynomial-weight $\tanh$ implementation even at fixed depth. A learning
guarantee polynomial in these complexity measures would therefore learn
parity from polynomially many examples.

We prove that such a guarantee fails for the family of priors studied
here. The learner is a fully connected $\tanh$ network with Gaussian
weights and a linear readout. Its prior variances depend on width $N$
through an exponent $\gamma$, with the exact scaling given in
\eqref{eq:weights}. We call the range $\gamma<1$ \emph{subcritical}.
For every exponent in this range and every fixed depth, full parity
requires $\exp(\Omega(d))$ examples under the width-first limiting
learner. More precisely, below that sample scale, a constant fraction
of test inputs fails the requirement that the correct label have
posterior probability strictly greater than $2/3$.

The conditioning and prediction conventions matter. Before training,
we restrict the prior to networks whose real output has absolute value
greater than a fixed margin at every Boolean input. We then condition
on agreement with the observed training labels. Prediction uses the
posterior probability of a label. These conventions give a different
learning problem from Gaussian regression on exact real-valued
observations or prediction by a posterior mean. Our lower bound applies
to the resulting label probabilities themselves.

The order of limits also matters. We first send width to infinity at a
fixed input dimension and a fixed training sample; only afterward do we
study increasing dimension and sample size. The theorem covers every
specified sample size below the exponential threshold, without assuming
that error is monotone in the number of examples. It concerns full parity
on all $d$ coordinates. It does not establish the conjectured
$\exp(\Omega(k\log(ed/k)))$ scale for parity on $k$ coordinates inside a
larger ambient dimension $d$.

The proof identifies three limiting posteriors within the subcritical
range. Negative exponents give a Gaussian process conditioned on the
training signs; exponent zero retains the global margin condition;
positive exponents concentrate on sign functions of minimum Gaussian
energy. We treat these cases separately after developing their common
spectral and sampling estimates. In the last case, the argument allows
arbitrary posterior weights among tied minimizers, which is necessary
for a conclusion about posterior confidence.

\section{Model and main theorem}
Let $\mathcal X=\{-1,1\}^d$, with uniform probability measure, and let
$f(x)=\prod_{j=1}^d x_j$. We study the following precisely specified prior.
All hidden layers have width $N$, the depth $L\ge2$ is fixed, and
\[
 h^0(x)=(1,x),\qquad h^s(x)=\tanh(W^sh^{s-1}(x))\quad(1\le s<L),
 \qquad z_N(x)=W^Lh^{L-1}(x).
\]
There are no further biases. All weights are independent centered
Gaussians, with variances
\begin{equation}\label{eq:weights}
 \Var W^1_{ij}=N^{1-\gamma}/d,\qquad
 \Var W^s_{ij}=N^{-\gamma}\quad(1<s<L),\qquad
 \Var W^L_i=N^{-\gamma-1}.
\end{equation}
Fix a margin $a>0$; the convention $a=1/2$ is included. Before training,
condition on $|z_N(x)|>a$ for every $x\in\mathcal X$. Given independent
uniform inputs $S=(X_1,\ldots,X_n)$, subsequently condition on
$\sign z_N(X_i)=f(X_i)$ for all $i$. These two conditioning operations
are equivalent to conditioning on their intersection.

Define the correct-label posterior probability and its confidence-failure
fraction by
\begin{align*}
 q_{N,S}(x)&=\mathbb P\{f(x)z_N(x)>0\mid
       |z_N(y)|>a\ \forall y;\ f(X_i)z_N(X_i)>0\ \forall i\},\\
 F_N(S)&=\mathbb P_x\{q_{N,S}(x)\le2/3\}.
\end{align*}
Thus a prediction succeeds only when its actual posterior probability of
being correct is strictly greater than $2/3$. This includes equality among
failures. Expectations over a fresh input always use the uniform cube
distribution independently of the training inputs.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
For every fixed $L\ge2$ and $a>0$, there are constants $c>0$ and $d_0$
such that, for every $d\ge d_0$, every integer $0\le n\le e^{cd}$, and
every fixed $\gamma<1$,
\[
 \mathbb P_S\!\left\{\liminf_{N\to\infty}F_N(S)\ge\frac1{16}\right\}
 \ge\frac34.
\]
The conditioning probabilities are positive for all sufficiently large
$N$, at each fixed $d,L,a,\gamma,S$.
\end{theorem}

The width limit is taken at fixed dimension and fixed training sample.
The exponential statement then concerns $d=k\to\infty$. No joint limit
or quantitative minimum width is asserted here. The probability guarantee
holds for each specified sample size with uniform constants; it does not
assert a single event for every prefix of an infinite sample sequence.
In particular, the proof does not assume monotonicity of this learner's
fixed-target risk in sample size.

Appendix~\ref{sec:paritycircuit} supplies the small bounded-weight
$\tanh$ realization of parity used to compare this lower bound with
implementation complexity. The obstruction is a property of the specified
Bayesian learner: identifying parity from examples is not intrinsically
exponentially difficult.

\subsection{Proof structure and the three limiting posteriors}
Write $\Psi(t)=2\arcsin(t)/\pi$ and $\beta=2^{-(L-1)}$. The relevant
covariance is
\[
 K(x,y)=\Psi^{\circ(L-1)}\!\left(\frac{1+x\cdot y}{d+1}\right).
\]
All population function norms use the uniform probability measure on the
cube. Write $\Pi_{\le r}g=g_{\le r}$ for the Walsh projection to degrees
at most $r$, and $g_{>r}=g-g_{\le r}$. For the positive-definite covariance
$K$, the Gaussian energy norm is $\|g\|_K^2=g^{\mathsf T}K^{-1}g$;
this uses the covariance matrix itself, without an extra factor $2^{-d}$.
At $\gamma<0$ the limit is a Gaussian process conditioned on the training
signs. At $\gamma=0$ it retains the fixed absolute margin at every input.
For $0<\gamma<1$ the actual network posterior concentrates on compatible
globally separated functions of minimum Gaussian energy; the proof needs
no formula for the weights of ties.

The proof has three tasks. First, we derive these width limits from
the network prior and estimate the Fourier spectrum of $K$. Second, we
control low-degree functions chosen after seeing the training sample.
For this we prove a uniform squared negative-part estimate, using the
stronger metric bound inside Lee's chaining argument \cite{Lee2023}.
A discrete Laplacian identity converts the resulting $L^2$ control of a
degree-$r$ polynomial into a clipped parity-correlation bound with factor
$r/d$. A short additional band of Fourier levels covers the gaps between
adjacent sampling thresholds. Third, we turn these estimates into
confidence lower bounds for each of the three posteriors. The case
$\gamma=0$ requires separate Gaussian entropy bounds and an auxiliary
independent sample to retain the exact global margin conditioning.

\section{Proof of the main theorem}
The positive-exponent, zero-exponent, and negative-exponent conclusions
proved above each hold with probability at least $3/4$ for every specified
$n\le e^{cd}$. Decrease $c$ and increase $d_0$ to satisfy their finitely
many requirements. The width-limit propositions transfer these conclusions
to the stated neural-network posterior.

For clarity, when the posterior's mean classification error is $R_N$,
the confidence fraction always satisfies
\[
 R_N=\mathbb E_x(1-q_{N,S}(x))
 \le\frac{1-F_N}{3}+F_N,
 \qquad F_N\ge\frac{3R_N-1}{2}.
\]
The nonnegative-exponent arguments give $\liminf R_N\ge3/8$, hence the
required $1/16$. The negative-exponent argument instead directly bounds
the fraction with limiting confidence at most $13/20<2/3$, so convergence
of each of the finitely many posterior label probabilities gives the same
conclusion. This proves Theorem~\ref{thm:main}.
