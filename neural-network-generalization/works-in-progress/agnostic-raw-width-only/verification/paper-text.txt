                                                        Abstract
              Can Bayesian neural-network regression compete with the best approximation of width
          polynomial in the sample size, when the comparison network may have arbitrarily large coef-
          ficients? We give a positive result for one continuous full-support prior on a prescribed dense
          tanh architecture. Hidden outgoing weights have continuous gamma multipliers and Student
          factors; the Gaussian readout has a Dirichlet allocation of its variance. The likelihood uses raw
          squared errors and prediction is the ordinary raw posterior mean after all observations. For
          marginally
          √            sub-Gaussian labels, a deterministic Gaussian observation variance proportional to
             n gives a coefficient-one expected-risk oracle. At fixed input dimension and comparison depth
          h, it competes with arbitrary-coefficient ReLU networks of width nu for some u > 0, with a
          vanishing polynomial remainder, at every prescribed learner width N ≥ n. With only a finite
          second label moment, a data-calibrated observation variance gives a slower vanishing oracle for a
          growing width class. The input distribution may be arbitrary on the Boolean cube, or satisfy slab
          anticoncentration on a continuous domain, including uniform spheres. A binary-input compiler
          avoids a power-of-accuracy width cost; it uses logarithmic extra depth for continuous inputs.
          Margin-restoring tanh gates make the comparison cost additive in extra learner depth. For noisy
          realizable conditional means, a separate theorem keeps one fixed likelihood variance and gives
          an almost 1/n rate at fixed teacher resources. For exactly Gaussian noise, a further theorem
          uses its true variance without inflation, with a larger complexity polynomial. For marginally
          sub-Gaussian labels with bounded conditional mean, a separate full-support readout with faster-
          decreasing tails gives a nearly inverse-sample agnostic complexity penalty at observation variance
          proportional to log2 n. Removing the conditional-mean bound adds an explicit n−1+o(1) term.
          The learners use no output clipping, architecture mixture, or holdout selection.


1        Approximation quality rather than exact realizability
A complicated regression function can still admit a useful narrow-network approximation. A learning
guarantee should then compare test risk with that approximation’s risk, rather than require the
target itself to have small complexity. Width alone is a demanding measure: a narrow network may
use arbitrarily large real coefficients, encode steep transitions, or have large raw outputs.
    This paper proves such a comparison for one fixed family of Bayesian tanh networks. The prior
is specified in Section 2. It is hierarchical in continuous weights and has full support on a prescribed
dense architecture. It is not the independent Gaussian weight prior. The observation model is
ordinary Gaussian regression with a specified sample-size-dependent variance. Both its likelihood
and final posterior mean use unrestricted real outputs.

efficient posterior computation, a pointwise risk domination with no estimation term, or a guarantee
at all widths as small as the uncompiled teacher. The explicit threshold is the compiled width, and
N ≥ n is a simple sufficient choice in the growing-class corollary.

Relation to earlier results. Dense Bayesian neural networks with heavy-tailed weights and
deterministic architectures already have posterior-contraction guarantees for structured smoothness
classes[8]. The present comparison target is different: arbitrary-coefficient finite-depth ReLU
approximations, a coefficient-one agnostic risk inequality, every sufficiently large prescribed width,
and an ordinary raw posterior mean. The prior and sample-size-dependent observation variance
are part of the theorem; the result is not asserted for every reasonable prior. The underlying
coefficient-reduction idea also has earlier precedents in neural complexity theory[6, 7]. We use a
precise finite-input compiler[4] and standard threshold arithmetic [5], and prove the continuous
quantization and Bayesian comparison consequences needed here.
