# Finite-sample Bayesian learning beyond parameter counting

Dmitry Vaintrob, Kaarel Hänni, and OpenAI Codex · 19 October 2026

**Work in progress; not Lean verified.** This note gives an explicit finite-sample calculation for a standard Gaussian neural-network prior. It separates two questions: whether useful Bayesian bounds exist in an overparameterized regime, and whether local asymptotic learning coefficients alone provide such bounds. The first has a positive answer in the model below; the second does not.

A depth at least three tanh network with hidden width $N$ has order $N^2$ parameters. When the target has a fixed smaller implementation, our certificate requires only order $N$ samples, at fixed accuracy and confidence. The guarantee concerns the final posterior with a fixed Gaussian likelihood evaluated on clean teacher outputs. It follows from an explicit variational calculation, not an asymptotic formula from singular learning theory (SLT). The bound still grows with width and makes no claim of practical numerical sharpness.

The [mathematical review](verification/README.md) records the independent checks. See the [FAQ comparison](../../TECHNICAL-FAQ.md#does-slt-already-give-comparable-generalization-bounds) and the separate [note on likelihood choices](../slt-likelihood/README.md).

The companion [analysis of raw-prior costs](PRIOR-MASS-OBSTRUCTIONS.md) proves an obstruction to removing width dependence from direct prior-mass certificates: across all power scalings of a one-hidden-layer Gaussian tanh network, these costs either grow with width or are exponential for some small teacher. This is a limitation of that proof route, not a sample-complexity lower bound.

## 1. Network, prior, and data

Let $d\ge1$, $D=d+1$, $L\ge3$, let $C,N$ be positive integers with $C\le N$, and let inputs have any probability distribution $\mu$ on $[-1,1]^d$. Augment each input by a constant coordinate: $\bar x=(1,x)\in\mathbb R^D$. A depth $L$ learner has $L-1$ hidden layers of width $N$, coordinatewise tanh activations, and a scalar linear readout:

$$
h^1(x)=\tanh(W^1\bar x),\qquad
h^\ell(x)=\tanh(W^\ell h^{\ell-1}(x)),\qquad
z_w(x)=v^{\mathsf T}h^{L-1}(x).
$$

Here $W^1\in\mathbb R^{N\times D}$, each subsequent hidden matrix is $N\times N$, and $v\in\mathbb R^N$. There are no separate internal biases. The parameter count is

$$
P=ND+(L-2)N^2+N.
$$

Use independent centered Gaussian coordinates, with variance $1/D$ in $W^1$ and variance $1/N$ in every subsequent matrix and in $v$. Write this standard fan-in prior as $\pi_N$.

A teacher has the same depth, hidden width $C\le N$, matrices $T^1\in\mathbb R^{C\times D}$ and $T^\ell\in\mathbb R^{C\times C}$, and readout $u\in\mathbb R^C$. Every teacher weight has magnitude at most $B\ge1$. Its exact real output is $f(x)$.

Draw independent training inputs $X_1,\ldots,X_n\sim\mu$ and observe the **clean labels** $f(X_i)$. Use the ordinary Gaussian statistical likelihood with fixed variance $s^2>0$. With $\beta=1/(2s^2)$, its posterior is

$$
Q_S(dw)=\frac{1}{Z_S}
\exp\left[-\beta\sum_{i=1}^n(z_w(X_i)-f(X_i))^2\right]\pi_N(dw).
$$

There is no global-margin conditioning and no exact-interpolation requirement. The Gaussian likelihood defines the learner, although the clean data are not generated by a Gaussian observation-noise model.

## 2. A finite-sample posterior-confidence theorem

Define

$$
\begin{aligned}
E_N&=\frac12\left[D\lVert T^1\rVert_F^2
+N\sum_{\ell=2}^{L-1}\lVert T^\ell\rVert_F^2
+N\lVert u\rVert_2^2\right],\\
Q&=CD+(L-2)CN+N,\\
a&=C^2B^2,\qquad A_L=\sum_{j=0}^{L-1}a^j,\\
K_t&=E_N+\frac Q2(t-1-\log t)\qquad(0<t\le1).
\end{aligned}
$$

In particular,

$$
E_N\le\frac{B^2}{2}\left[CD^2+N\big((L-2)C^2+C\big)\right].
$$

**Theorem.** Fix output tolerance $\tau>0$, test-error target $0<\varepsilon<1$, and training failure probability $0<\delta<1$. Set

$$
c=1-e^{-\beta\tau^2},\qquad r=b=\frac{\varepsilon}{6},\qquad
 t=\min\left(1,\frac{cr}{2\beta A_L}\right).
$$

If

$$
n\ge\frac{2}{cr}\left[K_t+\log\frac{1}{\delta b}\right],
$$

then, with probability at least $1-\delta$ over the training inputs,

$$
\mu\left(\left\lbrace x:
Q_S\big(\lvert z_w(x)-f(x)\rvert\le\tau\big)\le\frac23
\right\rbrace\right)\le\varepsilon.
$$

Thus the posterior gives greater than two-thirds probability to an output within tolerance on all but an $\varepsilon$ fraction of test inputs. If the teacher is Boolean valued on the input distribution's support and $\tau<1$, the same conclusion implies greater than two-thirds confidence in its correct latent sign. This is a statement about the posterior distribution of the function, not the noise in a fresh Gaussian observation.

For fixed teacher resources, depth, likelihood variance, and tolerances, the displayed sample requirement is $O(N)$ while $P=\Theta(N^2)$. The dependence on depth is also polynomial: although $A_L$ can grow exponentially, it enters the sample bound only through the logarithmic cost of shrinking variances. Indeed,

$$
\log A_L\le\log L+2(L-1)\log(CB).
$$

An explicit sufficient sample threshold is

$$
\begin{aligned}
n\ge\frac{12}{c\varepsilon}\bigg[ E_N
+\frac Q2\bigg(\log L+2(L-1)\log(CB)
+\log\max\left(1,\frac{12\beta}{c\varepsilon}\right)\bigg)
+\log\frac{6}{\delta\varepsilon}\bigg].
\end{aligned}
$$

Here $E_N$ and $Q$ are linear in $L$, and $1/c\le1+2s^2/\tau^2$. Thus, for fixed positive $s$, this is polynomial in width, depth, teacher weight bound, input dimension and inverse tolerances, with logarithmic dependence on $1/\delta$. The bound retains a linear learner-width cost; it does **not** settle the question of polynomial depth dependence with a bound independent of learner width. Its constants can be large, and no practical numerical sharpness is claimed.

## 3. Proof

### A reference distribution that leaves most parameters unconstrained

Zero-pad the teacher into width $N$. For $0<t\le1$, define a product Gaussian $q_t$ with this padded teacher as its mean. Multiply the prior variance by $t$ only on:

- the $D$ incoming weights of each of the first $C$ neurons in the first hidden layer;
- the $N$ incoming weights of each of the first $C$ neurons in every subsequent hidden layer;
- all $N$ readout weights.

Every other coordinate retains its original prior distribution. The number of changed variances is exactly $Q$. The product-Gaussian relative-entropy formula gives

$$
\mathrm{KL}(q_t\Vert\pi_N)=E_N+\frac Q2(t-1-\log t)=K_t.
$$

Call the first $C$ hidden neurons in each layer the selected neurons. The other neurons need not approximate any teacher value: tanh keeps their activations bounded by one. All edges entering selected neurons are controlled, including edges from these otherwise unconstrained neurons.

### Parameter-space extent and the role of averaging

The comparison distribution is an auxiliary measure used to lower-bound the posterior normalizer. It is not an approximation asserted for the posterior itself, and it is not supported on a small parameter ball. Every unselected hidden row retains its complete Gaussian prior law. If $w_T$ denotes the zero-padded teacher and $U$ the coordinates of these unselected rows, then

$$
\mathbb E_{q_t}\lVert(w-w_T)_U\rVert_2^2=(L-1)(N-C).
$$

The first layer contributes $(N-C)D$ variances of size $1/D$; each of the $L-2$ subsequent hidden layers contributes $(N-C)N$ variances of size $1/N$. At fixed teacher width and depth, this expected squared displacement grows linearly with learner width. Shrinking $t$ does not narrow the distribution in these directions.

The approximation estimate below concerns the expectation of squared error at each fixed input. Independent centered weight perturbations add in variance within a neuron, and their cross term with the discrepancy propagated from previous layers has conditional expectation zero. There is no cancellation of squared losses across inputs, and no claim that every parameter vector in the support computes an accurate function. The order is a supremum over inputs of an expected squared error, not an expected supremum over inputs.

These moment estimates and the explicit relative-entropy cost supply a finite-sample variational certificate. They do not come from a local Taylor approximation or an SLT learning coefficient. SLT can itself integrate over redundant directions or neighborhoods of a set of equivalent parameters; a small isotropic ball is not a universal description of its proofs. The particular guarantee here relies on the stated finite-scale probabilistic bounds.

### Uniform expected approximation error

For the first hidden layer, each selected preactivation has the teacher value as its mean and Gaussian noise variance at most

$$
\frac tD\lVert\bar x\rVert_2^2\le t.
$$

Since tanh is 1-Lipschitz, each selected activation has expected squared error at most $t$.

Suppose each selected activation in one layer has expected squared error at most $r_0$. In the next selected preactivation, the deterministic mean discrepancy is a sum of $C$ errors with coefficients bounded by $B$. Cauchy–Schwarz bounds its expected square by $C^2B^2r_0=ar_0$. Conditional on all previous layers, its centered Gaussian noise has variance at most

$$
\frac tN\sum_{j=1}^N h_j^2\le t.
$$

The cross term vanishes conditionally. Applying the Lipschitz inequality again gives the recursion $r_{\mathrm{next}}\le ar_0+t$. The same calculation applies to the linear readout. Therefore

$$
\sup_{x\in[-1,1]^d}\mathbb E_{q_t}
\lvert z_w(x)-f(x)\rvert^2\le A_Lt.
$$

### Posterior normalization and exclusion of bad predictors

For any training sample, the variational form of Jensen's inequality gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
Z_S
&\ge\exp\left[-\mathrm{KL}(q_t\Vert\pi_N)
-\beta\sum_{i=1}^n\mathbb E_{q_t}
\lvert z_w(X_i)-f(X_i)\rvert^2\right]\\
&\ge e^{-K_t-\beta n A_Lt}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Let $R_\tau(w)=\mu(\lvert z_w-f\rvert>\tau)$. For each fixed $w$,

$$
\mathbb E_X e^{-\beta(z_w(X)-f(X))^2}
\le1-cR_\tau(w).
$$

Consequently, independence of training inputs and Tonelli's theorem imply

$$
\mathbb E_S\int_{R_\tau(w)>r}
 e^{-\beta\sum_i(z_w(X_i)-f(X_i))^2}\pi_N(dw)
\le e^{-ncr}.
$$

Markov's inequality, followed by the deterministic normalizer bound, shows that with training probability at least $1-\delta$,

$$
Q_S(R_\tau>r)
\le\delta^{-1}e^{K_t+\beta n A_Lt-ncr}
\le b.
$$

The last step uses $\beta A_Lt\le cr/2$ and the stipulated sample size. Hence the posterior average of $R_\tau$ is at most $r+b=\varepsilon/3$. Applying Tonelli and Markov over test inputs gives the theorem. All integrals are finite because hidden activations are bounded and the reference readout is Gaussian.

## 4. A matched noisy-Gaussian variant

There is also a standard predictive-KL certificate when the observations really satisfy

$$
Y\mid X=x\sim\mathcal N(f(x),s^2).
$$

Let $p_i(\cdot\mid x)$ be the Bayesian posterior predictive density after $i$ observations, including $i=0$ for the prior predictive. The relative-entropy chain rule and the same reference distribution give

$$
\frac1n\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}
\mathbb E_{S_i,X}\,
\mathrm{KL}\big(\mathcal N(f(X),s^2)\Vert p_i(\cdot\mid X)\big)
\le\frac{K_t}{n}+\frac{A_Lt}{2s^2}.
$$

Indeed, the sum is the relative entropy between the true joint observation law and the Bayesian marginal law. Applying Jensen to the marginal likelihood bounds it by the reference KL plus the reference average of $n$ one-observation Gaussian KL divergences. Each such divergence is $(z_w(x)-f(x))^2/(2s^2)$.

For desired expected predictive KL $\eta>0$, it suffices to take

$$
t=\min(1,\eta s^2/A_L),\qquad
n\ge\frac{2E_N+Q\log\max(1,A_L/(\eta s^2))}{\eta}.
$$

This controls the average over training stages. Convexity gives the same expected bound for the uniform mixture of those predictive densities. It does not, by itself, give the same bound for the final predictive density. The clean-data theorem above and this noisy-data statement have different guarantees and should not be conflated.

## 5. Identical SLT coefficients can accompany arbitrarily delayed learning

A one-dimensional regular model already shows why leading asymptotic coefficients cannot specify a finite-sample learning threshold. Let

$$
Y_i\sim\mathcal N(1,1),\qquad
p_\theta=\mathcal N(\theta,1),\qquad
\theta\sim\mathcal N(0,M^{-1}),
$$

where the prior precision $M>0$ may be arbitrarily large. Ordinary Bayes gives posterior variance $1/(n+M)$ and posterior mean $n\bar Y/(n+M)$. The expected Gibbs excess KL is exactly

$$
\mathbb E_S\mathbb E_{\theta\mid S}
\mathrm{KL}(\mathcal N(1,1)\Vert\mathcal N(\theta,1))
=\frac{2n+M+M^2}{2(n+M)^2}.
$$

This follows by adding posterior variance, sampling variance of the posterior mean, and its squared bias, then dividing by two. Every fixed $M$ has the same regular-model coefficients $\lambda=\nu=1/2$, and the expression is $1/n+o(1/n)$ as $n\to\infty$. But whenever $n\le M$, it is at least $1/8$. The same asymptotic coefficients therefore permit an arbitrarily long interval before small excess loss appears.

This example concerns Gibbs log loss, matching the corresponding $\lambda+\nu$ formula. It is not a claim about sign of the posterior mean. Nor does it contradict a finite-sample bound that explicitly tracks prior scale: such a bound detects the large value of $M$.

## 6. What the comparisons establish

The standard asymptotic coefficients do not alone reduce finite-sample generalization to local geometry. Prior-dependent constants and the onset of the asymptotic approximation matter, even in a regular Gaussian model. Width-dependent prior choices make uniform control particularly relevant.

Conversely, useful finite-sample bounds need not charge for every parameter. Our witness leaves most unused hidden weights at their prior law and pays only for the weights entering selected neurons and the readout. For depth at least three, this permits sample sizes much smaller than $P$, where a generic capacity bound obtained solely by substituting parameter count can be uninformative. This does not beat every classical argument: norm, sparsity, compression, or distribution-dependent bounds can also exploit structure. On a fixed finite Boolean domain, its cardinality eventually provides a separate simpler bound; the continuous-domain formulation avoids that ceiling.

The certificate retains a linear learner-width cost. It therefore does not reproduce the arbitrary-width, teacher-complexity guarantees in the main papers, and it becomes uninformative at fixed sample size as width grows. Its polynomial depth dependence is compatible with this limitation: width independence is the missing property. The proof requires finite-scale bounds on approximation error and relative entropy with respect to the prior. These quantitative inputs are not supplied by $\lambda$ and $\nu$ alone.
