**Not new, and not attributed** (Finding M1): the core of N2 is the lower bound of active learning for classes with a large star number (Dasgupta; Hanneke and Yang), with the same proof; Protocol AB is the algorithm of Hegedűs and Moshkov for exact learning with membership queries, with the same threshold, and the pair "Theorem 3.1, Theorem 3.3" mirrors the known tight pair for that problem.

**Settled, though listed as open** (Proposition X1): the factor (2m + 1)/(m + 1) between N5 and R3. There is a scaffold, with inputs that depend on the answers, that tolerates m faults and still needs (1 − 4δ)(L − m)/(4(m + 1)δ) questions; and for no family that tolerates m faults can the patch method give more than L/(4(m + 1)δ). The factor m + 1 is the one in R3.

**Additions for the editor** (Section 3, all mine and unrefereed): X1 (N5 for a sequential majority; the packing bound with m + 1); X2 (the correct first half of the sharper form for a stochastic human); X3 (Theorem 3.3 with the smallest set X₀); X4 (S2 without an oracle, with an additive term); X5 (the conclusion in terms of a failure budget: the number of human answers replaced per answer spent is at most 4F/(1 − 4δ), where F is the expected number of failed runs that the guarantee allows and δ the failure rate per run, and it is of order F·log(L/δ)/K for classes of K bits).

### MAJOR

**M1. The core of N2 and Protocol AB are known results; the file gives no source for the first and calls the second new.**

*(a) N2.* Lemma 1.10 for a single call, and with it Theorem 2.1 for L = 1, is the lower bound of active learning in terms of the star number. Hanneke and Yang (2015; exact statement and what I read in Section 6, item 2) prove that the minimax number of label requests is more than t/2 with t = min{s, (1 − ε)/ε} rounded down, where s is the largest number of points x₁, …, x_s for which the class contains a centre h₀ and members h_i that differ from h₀ at x_i and at no other x_j. Their proof takes the uniform law on x₁, …, x_t and the t + 1 targets h₀, …, h_t and uses one step: the learner's output under h_i is its output under h₀ unless x_i was asked. They credit the bound to Dasgupta (2004, 2005). The needle class is a centre with N₀ one-point changes, and the proof of Lemma 1.10 is this step. What the file adds to it: the scaffold (a per-call error 1/N₀ becomes a run failure p = 1 − (1 − 1/N₀)^L, which is where the factor L comes from); deployed systems that see the whole run; a bound on the expected number of questions; the constant 1/4 with a protocol that attains it (Proposition 2.3); and the version with the human available during the run (Proposition 2.5). Section 11 of the file cites nothing for the argument. The digest `understand/active-queries.md`, Section 3.8, already makes the link.

*(b) Protocol AB.* The summary table calls Theorem 3.3 a "new upper bound", and its first remark is headed "What is new compared with Protocol R". The protocol is the algorithm that Bshouty's survey gives as the proof of the bound of Hegedűs and Moshkov (Section 6, item 1): if some input splits the surviving members so that both sides keep at least a fraction ε, ask it; otherwise form the majority function of the survivors and test it, and a refuted majority leaves at most a fraction ε. Steps of type (A) are the first case with γ in the place of ε. Steps of type (B) are the second case, in which the test of the majority function by a specifying set is replaced by audits of random runs, one call per run, as in Protocol R. Weights replace counts. The choice γ = log(L/ε)/(L/ε) of Theorem 3.3(3) is the survey's "ε is roughly log ETD(C)/ETD(C)" with L/ε in the place of the extended teaching dimension ETD. The resulting bound (L/ε)·K/log(L/ε) is the known (ETD/log ETD)·log|C|, and the survey states that a class C_{t,ℓ} of Moshkov attains the latter up to a constant; Theorem 3.1 is the run-level counterpart of that statement (I could not see Moshkov's class and do not claim that it is the block class). The check report says that the mechanism is that of Hegedűs's algorithm; the correspondence of the threshold, of the bound and of its tightness is added here.

*What to change.* In the note: "N2 is the lower bound of active learning for a class with large star number (Dasgupta 2004, 2005; Hanneke and Yang 2015, Theorem 3), carried from one call to a run of L calls." "Protocol AB is the membership-query algorithm of Hegedűs (1995) and Moshkov (1983) with a prior in place of counting and with random audits of runs in place of a specifying set; the order K·(L/ε)/log(L/ε) is the order (ETD/log ETD)·log|C| of exact identification with L/ε in the place of ETD." Call new only: the transfer to runs, the exact constants for N2, Proposition 2.5, and the failure-budget reading.

**M2. "Rehearsal with random questions is not optimal" is asserted and not proved.** (The check report's first finding; I agree and add two facts.)

The file proves that the *guarantee* L·K ln 2/δ of Protocol R exceeds the minimax number of questions by a factor of order log(L/δ) (Theorems 3.1 and 3.3). Corollary 3.4(5) and Section 10, item 4, conclude that Protocol R is not optimal. That needs a lower bound for Protocol R itself.

*First fact: the file's own hard instance does not give one.* On the block class with N = L/(2δ) the question of a round of Protocol R is a uniformly chosen cell among the m·N cells, independent of the past, because the inputs of the block scaffold do not depend on the answers. A needle is therefore still unasked at round t with probability (1 − 1/(mN))^(t−1). A run in a block whose needle is unasked fails with probability about 2p (the mixture misses the needle when it is visited, probability about p, and answers 1 at some other unasked cell with probability about L/N ≈ p). So the failure of M^(T) is about 2p·(mN/n)(1 − e^(−n/(mN))), which equals δ = p/2 (to first order) at n ≈ 3.9·mN. An exact computation of the least number of rounds (`lower-bounds_attack_budget.out`, part D; the state of a block is the number of its asked cells while its needle is unasked) gives 3.59, 3.65, 3.80 and 3.69 times mN in four cases; the check report's simulation gave 3.4 to 3.8. In the units of the file this is about 1.9·m·L/δ: about seventeen times the lower bound of Theorem 3.1 in these cases, and in all four cases *below* the number 1 + 2.7·K·L/(δ·log(L/δ)) that Theorem 3.3 guarantees for Protocol AB on the same class (for example 7 480 rounds against 9 678 for m = 16, N = 128, L = 4). (Rounds whose audited cell was asked before cost no new answer of a deterministic human; counted without them, Protocol R has asked about 0.98·mN distinct cells at that point, against the 0.49·mN that the prover's simulation reports for Protocol AB.) So on the classes that are hardest for every protocol, random questions are within a constant factor of optimal, and the logarithm that Theorem 3.3 removes is an artefact of the bound R1 there.

*Second fact: the check report's Proposition A1 does give one, and it is correct.* I re-derived it. On its rare-points class (one frequent input, d rare inputs of total frequency 4ε/L per call, all 2^d labellings of the rare inputs) the questions of Protocol R are independent draws from the call distribution, each rare input stays unasked through n rounds with probability (1 − 4ε/(Ld))^n, an unasked rare input is a fair bit for every deployed system, and a run meets an unasked rare input with probability at least (4ε|U|/d)(1 − 2ε) when U is the set of unasked ones. Failure at most ε for every member therefore needs n ≥ ln(2(1 − 2ε))·(L·d/(4ε) − 1). This is the passive-learning bound d/ε′ at the per-call error ε′ = 4ε/L. Here d = K chosen questions suffice.

*What to change.* Say: "the minimax number of questions over classes of K bits is of order K·L/(δ·log(L/δ)); the worst case of Protocol R is of order K·L/δ (R1 from above, the rare-points class from below); the two are separated on classes whose informative inputs are rare in runs, not on the block classes."

**M3. The conclusion (Theorem 7.1, the paragraph "In words", and the last paragraph of Section 4.2 of `note.md`) is stated more generally than it is proved, and it does not say where it is empty.**

*(a) "One run of L critical calls is not amplified."* Parts (A) to (D) are statements about the OR scaffold with a needle class whose number of cells is matched to L and δ (N₀ = L/(2δ), or ⌈L/√(2δ)⌉). The summary row, the sentence after Proposition 2.5 ("a run of L critical calls costs about L human answers whatever is done"), the paragraph "In words" and the note ("to obtain one run of L critical calls …") drop the class. Example 5.5 of the file is a run of L calls every one of which is critical, and one question gives failure 0 for every class. The quantity that the theorems bound is the number of *separately patchable places* that a critical call can fall on, at the scale L/δ; it is not the number of critical calls. (The check report makes the same point about the paragraph "In words".)

*(b) "There is no amplification for any r" (part (D)).* The class has rL/(2ε) cells: it grows with r. For a fixed needle class with N₀ cells, N₀ questions identify the human, and all later runs are right; the ratio of answers replaced to answers spent is then at least rL/N₀, which is unbounded in r even under one failure budget. Part (D) is true as "for every r there is a class of about log(rL/ε) bits". (Also in the check report.)

*(c) Part (E) says the ratio is "unbounded in r" and stops.* The number of runs after which the questions spent equal the human answers replaced is r* = n/L. With the per-run failure δ of the guarantee, the expected number of unfaithful runs that the guarantee allows up to that point is δ·r*. From the bounds of the file (`lower-bounds_attack_budget.out`, part A):

| | questions n | δ·r* at break-even |
|---|---|---|
| Protocol R (sufficient) | L·K ln 2/δ | K ln 2 |
| Protocol AB (sufficient) | 1 + 2.7·K·L/(δ·log(L/δ)) | about 2.7·K/log(L/δ) |
| block class (necessary, Theorem 3.1) | (1 − 10δ)·K·L/(8δ·log(L/(2δ))) | (1 − 10δ)·K/(8·log(L/(2δ))) |
| needle class (necessary, Corollary 2.2) | L/(4δ) − L | 1/4 − δ |

For L = 1000, δ = 0.01 and K = 10⁹ the three numbers that depend on K are 6.9·10⁸, 1.6·10⁸ and 7.2·10⁶ allowed failed runs before imitation has replaced as many human answers as it cost. The outline's style rule ("say plainly where a bound is empty, for example where K for a person makes it so") applies to part (E) and to the sentence "what imitation buys is reuse". Part (E) is correct and, for a human, empty unless failed runs by the million are acceptable. Proposition X5 (Section 3) states parts (A), (B), (D) and (E) as one inequality between the amplification and the failure budget, for a class that is matched to the per-run failure rate; this makes the price visible and puts the dependence of the class on the number of runs, point (b), into the statement.

*(d) "If the scaffold survives m faults the price drops by a factor between m + 1 and 2m + 1"; "by no protocol unless 4(2m + 1)δ > 1 − 4δ" (part (F)).* Both are statements about the block-majority scaffold. Neither bound on the factor is proved for scaffolds in general (the check report says this). By Proposition X1, for scaffolds whose inputs depend on the answers the factor is m + 1 both in an example of a necessary number and in the sufficient number of R3.

**M4. The first half of the candidate's "sharper form" of R3 is false for a human who is not deterministic; the file calls it correct.**

The candidate's sentence is stated for samplers: the robustness curve δ_Π(β) is the largest probability of a bad run over samplers C with E Σ_j TV(C, P)(x_j) ≤ β, and the claim is "failure ≤ (concave majorant of δ_Π)(L·K ln 2/n)". Proposition 5.2 proves this for a function, and the table of Section 0 says "for functions". Section 10, item 5, says without qualification: "The upper half is correct (Proposition 5.2)". For a stochastic human it is false. Take one input, answers {0, 1}, the class {P₊, P₋} with P_±(1) = 1/2 ± θ and weights 1/2 (K = 1), the human P₋, the scaffold with one call (L = 1), and call a run good if the answer is 0. Then δ_Π(β) = min{1, 1/2 − θ + β}, which is concave, and the sentence claims that the deployed answer is 1 with probability at most 1/2 − θ + ln 2/n. The mixture of round t answers 1 with probability 1/2 − θ + 2θ·w_t, where w_t is the posterior weight of P₊ after t − 1 answers. The exact excess 2θ·(1/n)Σ_t E[w_t] over the human system is (`lower-bounds_attack_sharper.out`):

| θ | n | excess failure (exact) | ln 2/n (the sentence) | ratio | √(ln 2/(2n)) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1/20 | 20 | 0.0457 | 0.0347 | 1.3 | 0.132 |
| 1/20 | 60 | 0.0389 | 0.0116 | 3.4 | 0.076 |
| 1/10 | 60 | 0.0451 | 0.0116 | 3.9 | 0.076 |
| 0.02 | 5000 | 0.00343 | 0.000139 | 25 | 0.0083 |
| 0.01 | 5000 | 0.00500 | 0.000139 | 36 | 0.0083 |

(The first three rows are exact rational arithmetic.) While n is below about 1/θ² the posterior has hardly moved, the excess is about θ, and the ratio is about θ·n/ln 2; it is unbounded. The correct statement is Proposition X2: the argument of the majorant is L·√(K ln 2/(2n)) for a general human. The second half of the sharper form is false as the file says (Section 2.2). *What to change:* in Section 10, item 5, and wherever the note states the robustness-curve bound: "for a deterministic human"; for a general human use X2.

**m6. Hidden assumptions of Theorem 3.3 that the comparison with Protocol R should carry.** (i) Deterministic human, and a class of total functions: one answer must refute a member. (ii) A2 is used at inputs that the protocol seeks out because the class is split there. With the X₀ of X3 these are inputs of runs of members, in general not inputs of runs of the human system: the human is asked what the answer would be after earlier answers that this human does not give. Protocol R also asks such inputs, but with the probability with which its runs produce them. If the class describes the human correctly only on inputs that follow human-like answers, Protocol AB finds the misdescribed inputs first, and after a refutation of the true member the weight argument is void. (iii) A step of type (A) needs the exact posterior at every input of X₀; with the X₀ of X3 and a scaffold with finitely many seeds this is a finite computation for a finite class.

**m7. N2 is a statement about a guarantee for every member, not about a Bayes risk; the note does not say so.** Remark 2.4(4) of the file says it; Section 4.2 of `note.md` does not. If the prior gives the N₀ needles the total weight η and the zero function the rest, the protocol that asks nothing and outputs 0 has Bayes failure η·p, which is below δ for every η < 1/2 (`lower-bounds_attack_budget.out`, part C). In a universal class the ratio of the total weight of the one-cell patches to the weight of the zero function is a constant of the reference machine (the patches cost log N₀ + 2 log log N₀ + c bits each). So whether N2 bites for a reader who trusts the prior is a fact about the machine; for the worst case over the class it is not.

**m8. Order of events, in the language of closed systems.** Theorem 2.1 needs the questions to be asked before the top-level input of the deployed run is drawn (Remark 2.4(1)). In a closed system we sample the scaffold's randomness ourselves. For one known task with randomness that we may fix in advance, the L calls of that run can be put to the human, the answer to "how many questions" is L, and Proposition 2.5 says that it is not much less. The factor 1/(4δ) of N2 is the price of preparing for a task that is not yet known. The note should say this in one sentence, because its Section 1 presents "a fixed research question worked on by a tree of calls" as the leading example of a closed system.

**m9. Definition 1.1 fixes the number of steps in advance,** and Protocol AB stops at a time that depends on the data (check report). A stopping rule that is finite on every path is what the proofs use.

## 2. The points singled out by the task

### 2.1 Theorem 3.3: is Protocol AB well defined when only single calls on chosen inputs can be asked, and does the count of questions hold?

*Single calls.* Yes. A step of type (A) puts one input to the human. A step of type (B) puts to the human the input of one call of a run that the protocol has computed itself with the vote h_t. The human never carries out a run and is never asked about one.

*Well defined.* Not as written, and yes after a repair. The vote h_t is defined on X₀ only, and its run can leave the set of inputs that runs of members produce (Finding m1). Two repairs work: X₀ contains every input that the scaffold produces under arbitrary answers (check report), or X₀ contains the inputs of runs of members and h_t is an answer of largest weight elsewhere (Proposition X3). Under the second, every question of type (A) is the input of a call in a run answered by some member of the class: a call that would occur if the earlier calls had been answered as that member answers them. Everything else is well defined: the posterior weight of the consistent members is at least w(f) > 0; a largest answer weight exists because the weights at an input sum to one; for γ < 1/2 an answer of weight above 1 − γ is unique; the number of steps of type (A) is at most K ln 2/γ on every path, so the protocol stops.

*The count.* Yes. The number of questions is at most n_B + a ≤ n_B + K ln 2/γ, and with the parameters of (3) at most 1 + (u/(u − log u) + ln 2)·K·L/(ε·u) ≤ 1 + 2.7·K·L/(ε·log(L/ε)), where u = log(L/ε) ≥ 4 (u/(u − log u) is 2 at u = 4 and decreases). Exact evaluation (`lower-bounds_attack_ab.out`): in 2 100 random cases with X₀ = X, and in 4 200 with the two smaller sets, the largest number of questions over all paths is within the bound, the exact failure of the deployed vote is within the bound of claim (2) (largest ratio 0.55 for X₀ = X, 0.80 for the smaller sets), and the recipe (3) stays within ε and within its count in 15 instances.

*What the count does not say.* (i) It is an upper bound for every class; it is not what Protocol AB needs on a given class. On the rare-points class of the check report the steps of type (A) identify the human with K questions, and the protocol then makes its n_B audits although none can fail. (ii) On the block class Protocol R needs fewer rounds than this count (Finding M2). (iii) The saving against R1 is the factor ln 2·log(L/ε)/2.7 = log(L/ε)/3.9 with the constant 2.7: 1.03 at L/ε = 16, 5.1 at L/ε = 10⁶, 7.7 at 10⁹ (7.0 and 11 with the exact constant u/(u − log u) + ln 2 in place of 2.7; log(L/ε)/2.44 in the limit).

### 2.2 The claim that part of the "sharper form" of R3 is false

The candidate's sentence (tagged there as a conjecture) has two halves.

*Second half: "for classes closed under patches about L/β questions are needed to get below δ_Π(β)".* False, as the file says. Example 5.5 is a valid counterexample; recomputed: L calls at one input x₀, outcome the maximum of the answers, f₀ ≡ 0, good runs have outcome 0. The sampler that answers 1 with probability β/L has β faults in expectation and a bad run with probability 1 − (1 − β/L)^L ≥ β − β²/2, so δ_Π(β) ≥ β(1 − β/2) > 0 for 0 < β ≤ 1; one question at x₀ gives failure 0 for every class of functions, the class of all functions included. The replacement, Proposition 5.4(a), is correct (it is Lemma 1.10(1) averaged over the patches). Proposition 5.4(b) is correct for scaffolds whose inputs do not depend on the answers; for the others the constant 2m + 1 becomes m + 1 (Proposition X1), which the file lists as open.

*First half: "failure ≤ (concave majorant of δ_Π)(L·K ln 2/n)".* True for a deterministic human (Proposition 5.2; the proof is right, and, as the check report notes, it needs a deployed imitator that draws afresh at every call). False for a human who is not deterministic (Finding M4, exact counterexample). The file's Section 10, item 5, should say so. The correct general form is Proposition X2.

So of the candidate's sentence only this survives: for a deterministic human the bound through the robustness curve holds; for a general human it holds with L·√(K ln 2/(2n)); the converse needs many disjoint places where a patch that is in the class has influence, and tolerance of m faults limits their number to L/((m + 1)·influence).

### 2.3 The concluding theorem

Parts (A) to (F) follow from the results cited in their proofs; I recomputed the numbers of (A), (C), (D) and (E) by hand. The reduction in (D) is right: if all r outcomes are right, so is their maximum, which is the outcome of one OR run with rL calls, and a deployed system for the long run may see all rL inputs. ### X1. Fault tolerance when the inputs depend on the answers

Notation of Section 5 of the file: a replacement rule sees (z, ρ), the history and the human's answer, may answer any element of Y ∪ {⊥}, and replaces at most m answers in a run; the human system of f *tolerates m faults with failure 0* if under every such rule the outcome is out_f(z, ρ) almost surely.

**Proposition X1.** (a) *Packing.* Let Π be any scaffold of horizon L, f₀ a function, B_1, …, B_N disjoint sets of inputs, and f_i functions with f_i = f₀ outside B_i whose human systems tolerate m faults with failure 0. Then, almost surely, out_{f_i} ≠ out_{f₀} implies that the run of f₀ has at least m + 1 calls with input in B_i. Hence Σ_i Pr[out_{f_i} ≠ out_{f₀}] ≤ L/(m + 1), and no lower bound obtained from Lemma 1.10 for such a family exceeds L/(4(m + 1)δ).

(b) *Attained.* Let m ≥ 0, B ≥ 1, N₀ ≥ 1, X := [N₀] × [2m + 1], Y := {0, 1}, and take the region class of Definition 5.2′ (f₀ ≡ 0, and f_i equal to 1 on region i and 0 elsewhere). The *sequential-majority scaffold* has z = (R_1, …, R_B) independent and uniform on [N₀] and no internal randomness. Block b asks the inputs (R_b, 1), (R_b, 2), … one after another and ends as soon as m + 1 of its answers are equal to the same element of {0, 1}; that element is the value of the block. The outcome is the largest block value. The horizon is L := B(m + 1) + m; a run that would need a further call, or a call beyond (R_b, 2m + 1), stops with the outcome 0. Then: (1) the human system of every member tolerates m faults with failure 0, and it makes exactly m + 1 calls in each block; (2) for every protocol and deployed system some member has failure at least (N₀ − n)·p(B, N₀)/(N₀ + B), with n the expected number of questions when the human is f₀; (3) if N₀ = B/(2δ) is an integer, failure at most δ for every member needs

  n ≥ B/(4δ) − B = (1 − 4δ)·(L − m)/(4·(m + 1)·δ).

*Proof.* (a) Let D_i be the set of inputs of B_i at which f_i and f₀ differ, and fix (z, ρ). Apply to the human system of f_i the rule "if the input of the call lies in D_i and fewer than m answers have been replaced, replace the answer by f₀'s". It replaces at most m answers and sees only the input of the call. If the run of f₀ has at most m calls with input in D_i, the run under this rule is the run of f₀, by induction over the calls: all its answers are those of f₀. Its outcome is then out_{f₀}(z, ρ), and by the tolerance of f_i it is almost surely out_{f_i}(z, ρ). So out_{f_i} ≠ out_{f₀} implies, almost surely, at least m + 1 calls of the run of f₀ in D_i ⊆ B_i. With c_i the number of calls of the run of f₀ with input in B_i, Markov's inequality gives Pr[out_{f_i} ≠ out_{f₀}] ≤ E[c_i]/(m + 1), and Σ_i c_i ≤ L because the sets are disjoint. The last claim is the computation of Proposition 5.4(c) of the file with L/(m + 1) in the place of L/(2m + 1).

(b) (1) Let the human be a member; it is constant on every region, with value v_b on the region of block b. Let a rule replace k_b answers in block b, with Σ_b k_b ≤ m. In block b every answer that is not replaced equals v_b, and at most k_b ≤ m answers differ from v_b; so the block cannot end with another value, and among its first m + 1 + k_b calls at least m + 1 answers equal v_b. The block therefore ends with the value v_b after at most m + 1 + k_b ≤ 2m + 1 calls, the run has at most B(m + 1) + m calls and is not cut off, and the outcome is the largest v_b, which is the outcome of the human system. With no replacement each block has exactly m + 1 calls. (2) Lemma 1.10(2), which holds for every scaffold, with B_i := {i} × [2m + 1], o₀ = 0, o_i = 1[i ∈ {R_1, …, R_B}], Δ_i = p(B, N₀) and M := B, exactly as in Theorem 5.3(2). (3) Corollary 2.2(1) with B in the place of L, and B = (L − m)/(m + 1). ∎

*Check.* Brute force over all replacement rules, with replaced answers in {0, 1, ⊥}, all members and all z, for ten triples (m, B, N₀) with m ≤ 3: tolerant to m faults in every case, never more than B(m + 1) + m calls, and m + 1 faults can change the outcome (`lower-bounds_attack_tolerance.out`).

*What it gives.* R3 says that L·K ln 2/((m + 1)δ) questions suffice for every human system that tolerates m faults, and the check report's Proposition A2 that 1 + 2.7·K·L/((m + 1)δ·log(L/((m + 1)δ))) suffice with chosen questions. For the sequential-majority scaffold and the region class, where K = log(N₀ + 1) is about log(L/((m + 1)δ)), the necessary number is (1 − 4δ)(L − m)/(4(m + 1)δ) and the sufficient one about 2.7·L/((m + 1)δ): the number of questions is known up to a constant factor (about 11·L/((L − m)(1 − 4δ))), and the factor that tolerance of m faults buys is m + 1 on both sides. The block-majority scaffold of Theorem 5.3 pays 2m + 1 calls for each protected decision because it asks all copies before it looks at the answers; at equal horizon the sequential scaffold has about (2m + 1)/(m + 1) times as many protected decisions and needs that many times more questions (table in Section 7). So item (iii) of Section 5.3 of the file is settled from below, and in Theorem 7.1 "a factor between m + 1 and 2m + 1" can be replaced by "the factor m + 1, in an example and as the limit of the method".

### X2. The first half of the sharper form, for a general human

**Proposition X2.** Let P ∈ H be any sampler, G a set of runs, δ_Π(β) the supremum of Q_C(not G) over samplers C with E_{Q_C} Σ_j TV(C(·|x_j), P(·|x_j)) ≤ β, and δ̄_Π its least concave majorant on [0, L]. After Protocol R with n rounds the run deployed with M^(T), T uniform on [n], satisfies

  Pr[deployed run ∉ G] ≤ δ̄_Π(min{L, L·√(K ln 2/(2n))}).

For a deterministic human the argument may be replaced by min{L, L·K ln 2/n} (Proposition 5.2 of the file). For a human who is not deterministic it may not (Finding M4).

*Proof.* Given T and the data, C := M^(T) is a sampler with β_T := E_{Q_C} Σ_j TV(C, P)(x_j) ≤ L, so Q_C(not G) ≤ δ_Π(β_T) ≤ δ̄_Π(β_T). A run has at most L calls, so by Pinsker's inequality and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality Σ_j TV_j ≤ Σ_j √(KL_j/2) ≤ √(L·Σ_j KL_j/2), where KL_j := KL(P(·|x_j) ‖ C(·|x_j)). By Jensen's inequality and Theorem R1 of the note (E E_{Q_C} Σ_j KL_j ≤ L·K ln 2/n), E[β_T] ≤ L·√(K ln 2/(2n)). Since δ̄_Π is concave and nondecreasing, E δ̄_Π(β_T) ≤ δ̄_Π(E β_T). ∎ (Theorem R1 belongs to another part; I read its proof in `note.md` and found nothing wrong, but it is not checked here.) In the counterexample of Finding M4 this bound holds in every row.

### X3. Theorem 3.3 with the smallest set X₀

**Proposition X3.** In the setting of Theorem 3.3 let X₁ be the set of inputs that occur in a run of Π answered by a member of H, for some (z, ρ). Let X₀ ⊇ X₁, and for x ∉ X₀ let h_t(x) be an answer of largest weight M_t(·|x) (the first in a fixed order in case of a tie). Run Protocol AB(γ, n_B) with this X₀. Let a be the number of steps of type (A) and b the number of steps of type (B) at which the audited input lies in X₀ and the human's answer differs from h_t's. Then:

1. on every path a·ln(1/(1 − γ)) + b·ln(1/γ) ≤ K ln 2, so the number of questions is at most n_B + K ln 2/γ;
2. Pr[the deployed run differs from run_f(z, ρ)] ≤ L·K/(n_B·log(1/γ)), and claim (3) of Theorem 3.3 holds unchanged;
3. if L·γ < 1, then at every step of type (B) every run of h_t is the run of a consistent member of H; in particular no audited input lies outside X₁, and b counts all disagreements;
4. if b is taken to count all disagreements, claim 1 can fail when L·γ ≥ 1 (the designed instance of Finding m1).

*Proof.* (1) A question answered f(x) multiplies the weight W of the consistent members by M_t(f(x)|x) ≤ 1. At a step of type (A) the factor is at most 1 − γ. At a step of type (B) with a disagreement at an input x ∈ X₀ it is at most 1 − M_t(h_t(x)|x) < γ, because at such a step every input of X₀ has an answer of weight above 1 − γ. W starts at most 1 and stays at least w(f). (2) If the run of h_t differs from the run of f on (z, ρ), let j* be the first call of the run of h_t at whose input the two functions differ. The calls 1, …, j* have the same inputs in both runs, so the input of call j* occurs in the run of f and lies in X₁ ⊆ X₀. With probability 1/L the audited call is j*, and then the step adds one to b. So the conditional probability that b increases at the step is at least u_t/L, where u_t is the probability that the run of h_t differs from that of f; the rest is the proof of the file. (3) At a step of type (B) the consistent members that agree with h_t at a given input of X₀ have posterior weight above 1 − γ. Suppose the first j ≤ L − 1 calls of a run of h_t have inputs in X₀. The consistent members that agree with h_t at all j inputs have weight above 1 − jγ > 0; let g be one. The run of g has the same first j calls and therefore the same input at call j + 1, which thus lies in X₁. The first input lies in X₁. Induction over j gives the claim; at the last call the same argument with j = L gives a consistent member whose run is the whole run of h_t. ∎

*Check.* `lower-bounds_attack_ab.out`: 2 100 exact cases with X₀ = X₁ and 2 100 with X₀ the inputs of runs of the human system alone (a set the protocol cannot know; it tests the proof of (2)): no violation of (1) or (2). The designed instance: X₁ has nine inputs, the run of the vote reaches a tenth, and with b counting all disagreements the product is 7/64 < 1/9.

### X4. S2 with ordinary compute, at the price of an additive term

**Proposition X4.** Fix a machine with fair coins, let the programs p be the binary strings with weights π_p := 2^(−2|p|−1) (the weights of Proposition 4.4(b); they sum to 1), and let b(1) ≤ b(2) ≤ … be time bounds. Modify Protocol R: in round t draw p with probability π_p and run it for at most b(t) steps; if it has stopped with an output z, carry out the round with top-level input z; otherwise the round asks nothing. Deploy M^(T), T uniform on [n]. Let the human be a function f ∈ H with K = log(1/w(f)). Then for every task law D that is sampled by a program p_D halting with probability one,

  Pr[the deployed run with z ~ D is unfaithful] ≤ 2^(2|p_D|+1)·L·K ln 2/n + (1/n)·Σ_{t ≤ n} τ_D(b(t)),

where τ_D(b) is the probability that p_D runs for more than b steps. If b(t) → ∞ the second term tends to 0 for every such D.

*Proof.* Let D^(t)(z) be the probability that p_D outputs z within b(t) steps; then D^(t) ≤ D and D − D^(t) has total mass τ_D(b(t)). The top-level input of round t has the sub-probability law ξ^(t) ≥ π_{p_D}·D^(t). Given the data before round t let β_t(z) be the expected number of unfaithful calls of the run of M^(t) on z, and u_t(z) ≤ min{1, β_t(z)} the probability that there is one. As in Lemma 5.1′ of the file, the audited call is a uniformly chosen call of the rehearsal run, so Σ_t E Σ_z ξ^(t)(z)·β_t(z) ≤ L·K ln 2. Hence E Σ_z D(z)·u_t(z) ≤ E Σ_z D^(t)(z)·β_t(z) + τ_D(b(t)) ≤ E Σ_z ξ^(t)(z)·β_t(z)/π_{p_D} + τ_D(b(t)); average over t ≤ n. ∎

The rounds in which no program stops cost compute and no question. The second term is not bounded uniformly over the samplable laws; Proposition 4.4(c) shows that it cannot be. So what S2 needs is this: an oracle for the inequality with a finite factor for every samplable law at every n; nothing beyond ordinary compute for the same factor with a term that vanishes at a rate depending on the law's sampler; and a fixed time bound for a factor valid for the laws sampled within that bound.

### X5. The conclusion in terms of a failure budget

Let a protocol ask its questions in advance and then answer r successive runs of a scaffold with fresh, independent top-level inputs; the deployed system may use its earlier runs. Its *failure budget* F is a bound, valid for every member of the class, on the expected number of runs among the r with a wrong or silent outcome. Its *amplification* is A := r·L/n: human answers replaced per answer spent.

**Proposition X5.** Let L, r ≥ 1 and F > 0, and put δ := F/r.

(a) (Simplest classes.) If δ ≤ 1/4 and N₀ := rL/(2F) = L/(2δ) is an integer, every protocol with failure budget F for the needle class with N₀ cells and the OR scaffold has n ≥ L/(4δ) − L in expectation when the human is the zero function. Hence A ≤ 4F/(1 − 4δ).

(b) (Classes of K bits.) If δ ≤ 1/20 and N := L/(2δ) is an integer, every protocol with failure budget F for the block class 𝒩^m(N) and the block scaffold has n ≥ (1 − 10δ)·m·L/(8δ) in expectation over a uniformly chosen member. Hence A ≤ 8F·log(L/(2δ))/((1 − 10δ)·K) with K = m·log(L/(2δ)).

(c) (Sufficiency.) For every class of functions with weights, every scaffold of horizon L and every member of K bits, Protocol R with n = ⌈L·K ln 2/δ⌉ rounds has failure budget F, so A ≥ F/(K ln 2 + δ/L); and, if K is known and L/δ ≥ 16, Protocol AB has failure budget F with n ≤ 1 + 2.7·K·L/(δ·log(L/δ)), so A is at least about F·log(L/δ)/(2.7·K).

(d) (Tolerance.) If the human system tolerates m faults with failure 0, the sufficient numbers of (c) divide by m + 1 (R3; the check report's Proposition A2); for the sequential-majority scaffold of X1, A ≤ 4(m + 1)·F·L/((L − m)(1 − 4δ)).

*Proof.* (a) From the deployed system for r runs build one for a single run with top-level input z: draw k uniformly from [r], draw z_1, …, z_{k−1} independently from the law of the top-level input, let the given system answer the runs z_1, …, z_{k−1}, z in this order, and output its outcome for the last. For every member its failure probability is the average over k of the failure probabilities of the k-th run, which is at most F/r = δ. Corollary 2.2(1) of the file gives n ≥ L/(4δ) − L, and A = rL/n. (b) The same reduction and Theorem 3.1. (c) Lemma 5.1′ and Theorem 3.3(3) with ε = δ; each run is unfaithful with probability at most δ, so the expected number among r is at most rδ = F. (d) Proposition 5.2 with δ₀ = 0, Proposition A2 of the check report, and X1(b)(3). ∎

*Reading.* For a deterministic human and a guarantee that covers every member of the class, the number of human answers that imitation replaces per answer it costs is, in the worst case over classes of K bits, between F·log(L/δ)/(2.7·K) and 8F·log(L/(2δ))/((1 − 10δ)·K): of order (failure budget)·log(L/δ)/K. For the simplest classes it is at most 4F/(1 − 4δ). A budget below 1/4 − δ of a failed run buys no amplification (A < 1), for any number of runs; this is parts (A) and (D) of Theorem 7.1. The class depends on the per-run rate δ = F/r and on nothing else: for a fixed class and δ tending to 0 the number of questions stays bounded (by the number of cells), which is why "no amplification for any r" cannot be said of one class. Amplification by the factor A has to be paid for with a budget of about A·K/(8·log(L/(2δ))) failed runs on the block classes; this is part (E) with its price. Tolerance of m faults multiplies the amplification by m + 1. Part B of `lower-bounds_attack_budget.out` checks (a) exactly against the protocol of Proposition 2.3, whose amplification is 4F.

## 4. The researcher's seven tests, applied to this part

The tests are those of `understand/notes.md`, Section 5. A part about limits passes most of them by construction; the list says what each one still catches.

1. **Guard** (name an input property that no asked input has; what happens there, and with what probability?). N1 is this test turned into a theorem that holds for every protocol, and Corollary 1.7 is its form for rules that may decline. The test catches the hypothesis that N1 and N2 share and the prose forgets: the class must contain the patch. For N2 the guards are the L/(2δ) cells, each of per-call frequency 2δ/L; the theorem is about the *number of kinds* of critical input that the class can patch one by one, not about the number of critical calls (Finding M3(a)). For Protocol AB the test has a second catch. The steps of type (A) remove every guard whose posterior weight at some input of X₀ exceeds γ; the deployed vote is then wrong at an unasked input only for members of weight below γ there. That is a statement about weights. For each such member the vote is wrong at that input with certainty, and Theorem 3.3 bounds only how often a run meets such inputs.
2. **Machine** (is the answer a fact about the scheme or about the reference machine?). The lower bounds for explicit finite classes with equal weights do not involve a machine, and in a universal class the needles cost log N₀ + 2 log log N₀ + c bits on every machine. What depends on the machine is the reading: the total prior weight of the needles relative to the zero function is a machine constant, so the Bayes risk of asking nothing is small on one machine and near p on another (Finding m7). The file states the worst case; the note should not let it be read as a statement about the mixture. The number K in the sufficient numbers is machine-relative; the number of blocks in Theorem 3.1 is not.
3. **Referent** (does a hypothesis that models how the data were made do as well as the intended one?). For the lower bounds this test gives a stronger statement than the file makes. If a protocol chooses its questions without coins, then for every computable f₀ the function "f₀ on the inputs this protocol asks when f₀ answers, and a different answer everywhere else" is one member of the universal class, of complexity at most K(f₀) + K(protocol) + c, with the same transcript as f₀; by Lemma 1.10(1) with this single patch (the complement of the asked set, which the protocol never hits), f₀ or that member fails with probability at least Δ/2, where Δ is the probability that the two human systems have different outcomes; for the OR scaffold Δ is the probability that the run meets an unasked input. No family of needles is needed. This is the lower-bound side of assumption A3. In Protocol AB the questions of type (A) are functions of the data; a member that agrees with the human on them and nowhere else is refuted only by the audits of type (B). The theorem covers this, since its proof is a weight count, but the note should not describe the chosen questions as protection against such hypotheses.
4. **Copy** (does anything distinguish the sampler from a better predictor of it, or from the sampler with coins filled in?). Nothing in Sections 1 to 5 does, and Protocol AB deploys an object that is openly not a copy: the vote of the surviving members, in general not a member of the class. Section 6 is where the test bites, and the file handles it: under the criterion "law of one deployed run, averaged over the training", replaying one recorded answer is perfect (Remark 6.0), which is the sampler with its coins filled in; the bounds of Theorems 6.1 and 6.3 are for the criterion that excludes this, the expected distance of the trained imitator's own run law. The note should say that the stochastic lower bounds are for an imitator that is used more than once.
5. **Budget** (how many human answers, of what length, on which questions?). The numbers of Finding M3(c). For the needle class with L = 1000 and δ = 0.01, at least 24 000 answers are necessary for humans of 16 bits; with K bits the sufficient numbers are K times larger up to a logarithm, and for K = 10⁹ they are between 10¹³ and 10¹⁴; the break-even point lies behind 10⁸ allowed failed runs. For a person the sufficient numbers of this part are empty, and the necessary numbers say that for some classes of that size this cannot be helped. "On which questions": the questions of type (A) of Protocol AB are calls that follow answers which some hypothesis gives and the human may never give. "Of what length": the part counts answers; a call that takes a year counts as one. N3: all shifts of description length k cost 2η·2^k questions up to a factor polynomial in k, about 10⁶·2η at k = 20.
6. **Rock** (does the scheme still act, or does it abstain everywhere new?). Corollary 1.7 and Remark 4.5 are the theorem behind this test: a rule that is never silently wrong for every member of a class with patches is silent with probability one at every unasked input, and under a shift that was not paid for it abstains with probability at least 1 − π_i − 2σ. The lower bounds count silence as failure, which is the right convention for this test. The file does not offer a scheme that abstains, so there is nothing further to catch.
7. **Horizon** (granting perfect imitation of each call, does the scaffold do anything?). The scaffolds of this part are instruments for lower bounds: the OR of L table look-ups, and majorities over repeated questions. Two things follow. The fault tolerance in Theorem 5.3 and in X1 comes from asking one question several times of a human who, being deterministic and stateless, gives the same answer every time; it protects against the imitator's errors only (Finding w6). And the conclusion is about counts of answers: nothing in it says that a human system with L critical calls, or a fault-tolerant one, achieves anything. The file does not claim that it does; the note's sentence "it saves work within a run exactly to the extent that the human system tolerates faults" comes close.

**Read in a source in this session.**

1. *N. H. Bshouty, "Exact Learning from an Honest Teacher That Answers Membership Queries", arXiv:1706.03935 (13 June 2017), Section 2.1, Lemma 3 with its proof (pages 20 to 21 of the PDF).* The lemma, attributed there to Hegedűs (1995) and Moshkov (1983): for every class C of Boolean functions, OPT_AD(C) ≤ (ETD(C)/log ETD(C))·log|C| + ETD(C), and OPT_AD(C) ≥ max(ETD(C), log|C|), where OPT_AD is the least worst-case number of membership queries of an adaptive algorithm that identifies every member and ETD the extended teaching dimension. The proof given there is this algorithm, quoted with the survey's symbol ε for the threshold: with C_i the members consistent with the answers so far, "the algorithm searches for an assignment a ∈ X such that ε|C_i| ≤ |{f ∈ C_i | f(a) = 0}| ≤ (1 − ε)|C_i|. If such a ∈ X exists, then it asks the membership queries MQ(a)"; "If no such a ∈ X exists, then the algorithm finds a specifying set T_h for h := Majority(C_i)" and asks it; "Otherwise, there is a ∈ T_h such that MQ(a) ≠ h(a)", and then |C_{i+1}| ≤ ε|C_i|; "ε is roughly log ETD(C)/ETD(C)". The survey continues: "Moshkov gives, for any two integers t and ℓ, an example of a class C_{t,ℓ} where OPT_AD(C_{t,ℓ}) = Ω((ETD(C_{t,ℓ})/log ETD(C_{t,ℓ}))·log|C_{t,ℓ}|). So the upper bound in the above lemma is the best possible." Its open problem 3.4 adds: "Lemma 3 is just a reduction from the Halving algorithm." References as given there: T. Hegedüs, "Generalized teaching dimensions and the query complexity of learning", Proceedings of the Eighth Annual Conference on Computational Learning Theory (COLT), pp. 108–117, 1995; M. Y. Moshkov, "On conditional tests", Problemy Kibernetiki 40, pp. 131–170, 1983 (also Sov. Phys. Dokl. 27(7), pp. 528–530, 1982). I did not read Hegedűs or Moshkov themselves, and the survey does not describe the class C_{t,ℓ}.
2. *S. Hanneke and L. Yang, "Minimax Analysis of Active Learning", arXiv:1410.0996 (the JMLR article is 16(109):3487–3602, 2015), Theorem 3 and its proof in Appendix B.1.* Theorem 3: for ε ∈ (0, 1/9), δ ∈ (0, 1/3), max{min{s, 1/ε}, d, Log(min{1/ε, |C|})} ≲ Λ_RE(ε, δ) ≲ min{s, d/ε, s·d/Log(s)}·Log(1/ε), with s the star number, d the VC dimension and Λ_RE the minimax number of label requests in the realizable case. The proof of the term min{s, 1/ε} (Appendix B.1): "a lower bound proportional to s ∧ 1/ε may be credited to Dasgupta (2005, 2004). It can be proven as follows." Take a centre h₀ and members h₁, …, h_t that differ from h₀ at one point x_i each, the uniform law on {x₁, …, x_t}, t = s ∧ (1 − ε)/ε (rounded down; the brackets are lost in my text extraction, and the points must have mass above ε); "ĥ_i = ĥ₀ whenever x_i ∉ Q₀, since x_i ∉ Q₀ implies that all of the labels observed by the algorithm are identical to those that would be observed if f* = h₀ instead of f* = h_i"; conclusion Λ_RE(ε, δ) > t/2. The proof of the term s·d/Log(s) uses the algorithm MEMB-HALVING-2, "due to Hegedüs (1995)", and quotes: "Hegedüs (1995) proves that, letting XTD(C[U], U) = max_{h: X → Y} TD(h, C[U], U) …, if n ≥ 2·(XTD(C[U], U)/(1 ∨ log₂(XTD(C[U], U))))·log₂(|C[U]|), then the classifier ĥ_n returned by MEMB-HALVING-2 satisfies ĥ_n = h*".
3. *S. Hanneke, "The Optimal Sample Complexity of PAC Learning", arXiv:1507.00473v4, Section 3.* "A second lower bound of (d − 1)/(32ε) was supplied by Ehrenfeucht, Haussler, Kearns, and Valiant (1989), for 0 < ε ≤ 1/8 and 0 < δ ≤ 1/100." Reference given there: A. Ehrenfeucht, D. Haussler, M. Kearns and L. G. Valiant, "A general lower bound on the number of examples needed for learning", Information and Computation 82:247–261, 1989. This confirms, through a secondary source, the statement that the attacked file quotes from memory (its "d ≥ 2" is not in the restatement). The original could not be opened.
