Only the certificate V1 has a human-sized price (300 answers with a tolerated fraction 0.1), and it certifies an imitator, it does not produce one.
* "The human must not stay in the loop for ever" is met only in a per-run sense. Over S deployed calls the bounds give (number of questions) × (expected number of unfaithful calls) ≤ S·K ln 2, for the frozen imitator and for the online form alike (Proposition X6). ### MAJOR

**M1. What can be certified for a human who is not deterministic: the note's paragraph is wrong in three directions, and the lower bound it needs is missing.**

The note, after Theorem V1: "Part (b) is what can be measured when the human is not deterministic. No number of single answers at inputs that never repeat reveals the distribution P(·|x) at any one input; only frequencies of tests can be estimated." The file, Theorem 13.1(c): "The rate of recorded disagreement says nothing about faithfulness", and "no finite family of tests bounds the number of unfaithful calls without an assumption on P", with an example in which P and A are functions.

*(a) The file's example does not concern a human who is not deterministic.* In it the human is a function, and for a function part (a) of the same theorem certifies β(A) directly. It shows that a finite family of tests fixed without regard to A can miss unfaithful calls; it is no lower bound for a randomised human. (The check report adds that for an imitator that is a function the single test "graph of A" measures β(A)/L for every human.) So the sentence of the note rests on assertion, against the outline (Addendum 2, item 5).

*(b) Certificate (a) is valid for every human.* For every frozen answerer A and every human satisfying H1, the probability of a recorded disagreement in a validation round is at least β(A)/L and at most β(A)/L plus the human's own noise along the runs of A (Proposition X1; 3 000 random instances, `rehearsal-core_attack_v1.py`, part A: smallest value of L·ρ_dis − β(A) is −4·10⁻¹⁶, largest value of L·ρ_dis − β(A) − L·s_P is 4·10⁻¹⁶). So "no disagreement in m rounds" certifies β(A) ≤ L·ln(1/δ)/m with a false certificate of probability below δ whatever the human is. What fails for a human who is not deterministic is that the certificate is hardly ever issued: it is issued with probability at most exp(−m·s_P/2). The first sentence of 13.1(c) is therefore wrong in one direction, and V1(a) in the note should not assume a deterministic human for its validity. (The check report found the same inequality.)

*(c) Under A2 the number of unfaithful calls is certified at the rate √(K/m).* This is the file's own Theorem 13.1(b3), which I checked: for a kernel A and the tests B_ν := {(x, y) : A(y|x) > ν(y|x)}, with probability 1 − δ, β(A)/L ≤ max over ν with w(ν) ≥ 2^(−K₀) of the difference of recorded frequencies, plus √(2(K₀ ln 2 + ln(1/δ))/m). The note does not mention it, and its paragraph reads as if no such certificate existed.

*(d) Under A1 the same question may be put again.* Assumption A1 says that every answer is a fresh draw from P(·|x). A protocol may therefore put one question r times. For an answer set of s elements, m rounds of r repetitions certify β(A)/L up to ½·√((s − 1)/r) + √(ln(1/δ)/(2m)), with no assumption on the human and for a randomised imitator (Proposition X3; simulation in part C of the script: 0 violations in 4 000 trials for each of six settings; an imitator with coins filled in, β/L = 0.40, receives the bound 0.48). The file and the note do not consider repeated questions. They are also the place where A1 is least credible for a real person, and that should be said in the note.

*(e) The lower bounds that do hold* (Proposition X2). (B1) Single answers, no input asked twice: a fair coin at every one of N inputs and a uniformly random function give the same law of answers, the imitator "fair coin" has β = 0 against the first and β = L/2 against every function, and the exact total variation between the two laws of a validation transcript of m rounds is 2.5·10⁻⁴ for N = 10⁹, m = 1000 (part B1 of the script; it is at most m(m − 1)/(2N)). (B2) Any questions, repeated or not: uniform answers on a set Y against uniform answers on a random half of Y, a new half at every input; the total variation between the laws of everything a protocol with m questions sees is at most m(m − 1)/(2|Y|) (exact values in part B2), and the imitator "uniform" has β = 0 against the first human and β = E[len]/2 against the second. So no certificate of the form "β(A) < E[len]/2" that is false with probability at most δ for every human can be issued with probability above δ + m(m − 1)/(2|Y|) when A is perfect. For answers of 100 bits and m = 10⁹ the second term is 4·10⁻¹³. These are birthday bounds of the kind behind the lower bound for uniformity testing (Paninski 2008, cited in `research/sections/related-work.md`; not opened by me).

*What to change.* In the note, replace the two sentences by: "For a human who is not deterministic the picture is this. (i) The rate of recorded disagreement is at least β(A)/L, so certificate (a) stays valid, but it is issued only if the human's own answers rarely differ from one another. (ii) If the imitator is a function, one test (its graph) measures β(A)/L. (iii) Under A2, the tests {A > ν}, ν ∈ H, certify β(A)/L at the rate √(K/m) (Theorem 13.1(b3)). (iv) Without A2 and for a randomised imitator, no procedure with m ≪ √|Y| questions can certify that β(A) is small (Proposition X2), and none with single answers at m ≪ √N distinct inputs; what remains is part (b). (v) If the same question may be put again (A1 allows it; a person remembers), β(A)/L can be estimated for a small answer set (Proposition X3)." In the file, replace 13.1(c) accordingly.

If one round T is drawn once for good, then in addition, with probability δ over T, the frozen imitator is worse than the bound by the factor 1/δ, and this factor is needed (the file's Proposition 5.2(c), (d)). **M3. The protocol, its analysis for a deterministic human and both halves of its low-stakes guarantee are published; the note presents the first as its own observation, and the file tags the second as new.**

`research/sections/related-work.md` and the outline (Addendum 2, item 1) already record the attribution to Li and Zhang. I confirm it in the sources and add three facts that are not there.

*(a) Read in this session* (Section 5 has the exact statements). Li and Zhang (2025), Algorithm 1 ("Stagger"): in each round the online learner supplies a policy, the policy is executed, one state is sampled from its state-visitation distribution, the expert is asked for its action there, the learner is updated with the log loss, and the output is a "first-step uniform mixture" of the iterates, one iterate drawn uniformly and executed for the whole episode; the learner is exponential weights over the each-step mixtures. Their Theorem 3: for a deterministic expert in a finite class of size B that is μ-recoverable, with probability 1 − δ the gap is at most μH·(log B + 2 log(1/δ))/N. This is Protocol R, Theorem 5.1(c), Proposition 5.2(b) and Theorem 8.6 of the file for the uniform prior.

*(b) Not in the related-work text.* Ross, Gordon and Bagnell (2011), footnote 3: the guarantee "holds for the policy which uniformly randomly picks one policy in the sequence π̂_{1:N} and executes that policy for T steps". So the deployment rule "one uniformly chosen round for a whole run", which the file's Proposition 5.2 and Section 15, item 1, develop, is theirs. Their Algorithm 3.1 ends with "Return best π̂_i on validation": the three phases rehearse, validate, deploy are the outline of DAgger. Their Theorem 2.2 is the recoverable form (the file says so).

*(c) Sentences to change.* Note, Section 2, last sentence: say that the protocol in this form (log loss, one uniformly chosen call per run, a uniformly chosen iterate for a whole run) is Stagger, and that the reduction is Theorem 4.1 of Ross, Gordon and Bagnell. File, summary table: the tag NEW is defined there as "in neither the candidate theory nor the note", but a reader of the table will take 4.3, 5.2(b) and 9.2 for new results. 4.3(b) is Lemma A.14 of Foster, Kakade, Qian and Rakhlin (the file says so in the text); 5.2(b) for a function is the first display of Li and Zhang's Theorem 3; 9.2 has a published relative (Section 4). ### MINOR

**m1. Proposition R3a as written in the note fails on a scaffold that may stop early.** Section 1 of the note lets a scaffold stop after any call. R3a asks for a sequential scaffold with a state, a human who raises φ by at least μ in expectation and changes it by at most 1, and no answer that lowers φ by more than D; a run is good if φ rises by (1 − η)·μL. Take the scaffold in which the answer "go" raises φ by 1 and any other answer stops the run, and a human who always answers "go". Then μ = 1, the human's steps have size 1, no answer lowers φ (D = 0), and the condition of R3a, m·(1 + D) + √(8L·ln(1/δ)) ≤ η·μL, admits m = 308 for L = 1000, η = ½, δ = 0.01 (`rehearsal-core_attack_r3.out`, part 3). One replaced answer at call 1 stops the run with φ unchanged: the run is bad with probability 1. Proposition 12.2 of the file adds "all of whose runs have exactly L calls" and is correct. *What to change.* In the note: "every run has exactly L calls". In the file: "In brief" (1) and Section 15, item 8, should say that the note's statement is false without this hypothesis, not that it is correct. Two further conditions deserve a clause in the note. "From every state" includes the states that only a run with replaced answers reaches. And D bounds decreases only: an answer that raises φ without real progress makes the run good by definition, so φ must be a quantity that a wrong answer cannot raise. (The check report also asks for the hypothesis.)

**m2. Theorem 13.1(c), first sentence, is false in one direction; V1(a) holds for every human.** See M1(b) and Proposition X1. (Also in the check report.)

**m3. "Whenever it is issued it is false with probability less than δ" (Corollary 13.2), and "with confidence 1 − δ" in the note.** Theorem 13.1(a1) bounds the probability that a certificate is issued and false, for every frozen imitator. It does not bound the probability that an issued certificate is false. For a fixed imitator with ρ equal to twice the certified value, the certificate is issued with probability 2·10⁻³ (m = 100, δ = 0.05) and is false whenever it is issued; if the frozen imitator is good with probability 0.01 and has twice the certified ρ otherwise, 17 per cent of the issued certificates are false (`rehearsal-core_attack_v1.out`, part D). The second case is the one in which f ∉ H, which is where the corollary says "whether or not f ∈ H". Related: the note does not say what happens when validation fails. Validating the same or another imitator again multiplies the bound δ by the number of attempts (the file's Remark 13.3(v) says so for several candidates). *What to change.* "The probability that a false certificate is issued is less than δ, whatever A is"; and one sentence on repeated attempts in Section 2 of the note. (The first point is also in the check report.)

**m4. The clause "sees the human's answer" in the definition of tolerance is essential, and the file asserts this without an example.** Remark (ii) after Theorem 8.4 says that tolerance against rules that do not see the human's answer "would not suffice". Example (`rehearsal-core_attack_r3.out`, part 2): L calls at one input; the human answers 1 with probability μ and 0 otherwise; a run is good if some answer is 1. Call a rule blind if it decides before the human's answer is drawn whether it answers the call itself, and count every call it answers as a fault. The worst blind rule with k faults answers 0 at k calls, and the run is bad with probability (1 − μ)^(L−k). The imitator that always answers 0 has β = μL and a bad run with probability 1.

| L | μ | k | human system fails | worst blind rule, k faults | worst rule that sees the answer, k replacements | β/(k + 1) | "R3 with blind tolerance" | true failure of the imitator |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 60 | 0.05 | 3 | 0.046 | 0.054 | 0.647 | 0.750 | 0.804 | 1 |
| 400 | 0.01 | 39 | 0.018 | 0.027 | 1.000 | 0.100 | 0.127 | 1 |
| 4000 | 0.002 | 399 | 0.0003 | 0.0007 | 1.000 | 0.020 | 0.021 | 1 |

So fault tolerance in the engineering sense (some components fail, chosen without knowledge of what the good component would have said) does not give Theorem R3 for a human who is not deterministic. The coupling replaces exactly the rare good answers. The check report gives another example under another count (a blind rule's answer that happens to equal the human's is free); there the gap needs an answer worse than the human's worst. Under the count used here two answers suffice. *What to change.* One sentence and this example after the definition of tolerance in Section 3.2 of the note; the outline's gloss of "low stakes" ("survives a number of arbitrarily replaced answers") should read "answers replaced by a rule that sees them".

**m5. The zero-disagreement certificate is issued only to an imitator that is better than what is certified by the factor ln(1/δ)/δ′.** By Corollary 13.2 the certificate E[N] ≤ L·ln(1/δ)/m is issued with probability 1 − δ′ once n ≥ m·K ln 2/δ′. For a certified failure γ of the run this is m = L·ln(1/δ)/γ and n ≥ (ln(1/δ)/δ′)·L·K ln 2/γ: thirty times the number of questions of Theorem R1 for δ = 0.05 and δ′ = 0.1. The reason is in the rule, not in the proof: no disagreement in m rounds is likely only if ρ is below δ′/m. The form (a2) of Theorem 13.1, which allows recorded disagreements, certifies ρ ≤ ρ̂ + √(2ρ̂·ln(1/δ)/m) + 2·ln(1/δ)/m, about 4.4·ln(1/δ)/m when the true ρ is ln(1/δ)/m. *What to change.* State (a2) in the note next to the zero-disagreement rule, and say which of the two the protocol uses.

**m6. The tolerance of V1(b) can be halved.** Δ(B) = a_A(B) − h_A(B), and a_A(B), the frequency of B among the answers of A, is a property of A and of the scaffold alone. With the compute the note allows it is computed, or estimated from as many runs of A as one likes, with no question to the human. Only h_A(B) needs the human, and ĥ(B) is a mean of m independent variables in [0, 1]. Hoeffding's inequality then gives, with probability 1 − δ and simultaneously for all B: |Δ(B) − (a_A(B) − ĥ(B))| ≤ √(ln(2/(π(B)·δ))/(2m)), half the tolerance of V1(b), or the same tolerance with a quarter of the questions. In particular the half of (b2) that concerns the imitator ("A gives a bad kind of answer rarely") needs no human answers at all.

**m7. For a human who is not deterministic the dependence on the excess failure is 1/γ′, not 1/γ′².** Proposition 9.1(b) and 9.3(iii), and the sentence after R3 in the note, give the excess failure √(K ln 2/(2n))/θ, hence n = K ln 2/(2θ²γ′²) questions for an excess γ′. Proposition X4 gives 2e²·K ln 2/(θ²·n) + 2^(−θL), hence n = 4e²·K ln 2/(θ²·γ′) once 2^(−θL) ≤ γ′/2. It is the smaller of the two for γ′ below about 0.02. In the family of Proposition 9.3 with K = 1 the exact probability of more than θL unfaithful calls falls like 1/n (9.1·10⁻⁶ at n = 10⁶ for L = 400, θ = 0.05), the Markov bound of the file like n^(−1/2) (1.2·10⁻²), and X4 gives 4.1·10⁻³ (`rehearsal-core_attack_lowstakes.out`). The file's open question 5 and the sentence "under low stakes this costs of the order K/θ²" ("In brief" (4)) should carry the dependence on γ′; the lower bound 9.3(ii) has none, so a factor 1/γ′ remains open. The sentence after R3a in the note ("the number of questions needed … does not grow with the length of the run") is for a deterministic human and should say so.

**m8. Hidden assumptions of the closed system.** (i) *Runs must be free of effects.* Rehearsal runs the scaffold n times with poor imitators. The note's definition ("we can sample z and ρ ourselves, so that we can run it with any answerer we have, as often as we like") implies it; it should be said that a scaffold that acts on the world between calls is not closed. (ii) *The answerer must not be able to tell a rehearsal or validation run from the run that counts.* For the mixtures this is automatic, because hypotheses are functions of the input. Theorem V1 is stated for "any answerer, however it was obtained". For such an answerer it is an assumption: A is frozen, answers from the history of the run alone, and nothing in the inputs of the deployed run (a date, a counter, a fact about the world) differs in law from the validation runs. The file's Definition 13.0 and Remark 13.3(i) say this; the note does not. (iii) *A fixed top-level input makes the human answer the same question many times.* The note's own example of a closed system is "a fixed research question worked on by a tree of calls". Its first call has the same input in every round and is put to the human in about n/L rounds; A1 is used exactly there. (iv) *The human answers inputs that the human system never produces.* The questions of the early rounds come from runs of the prior mixture. A1 is assumed there, and K counts the human's behaviour there (Proposition X7). The file's Section 11, Remark (ii), says the first half.

**m9. The last round: scope of what is proved.** (i) The class of Proposition 6.3 contains samplers that are not functions (the file says so in a remark and in open question 1; "In brief" (3) and Section 11 do not). (ii) Proposition 6.4 has content only when n exceeds about 2L²·K ln 2·(ln L + 3): for n between L·K and L²·K, where the bound for the random round is already below 1, it says nothing about the last round (the check report's item 8; I agree). (iii) My searches did not move the open question: on 250 random instances and a local search over classes with stochastic members the largest E u(M^(n+1)) was 0.39 times the bound L·ln(W/w(f))/n of the random round, and the largest E β(M^(n+1)) 0.50 times (`rehearsal-core_attack_lastgeneral.out`; n ≤ 12, so this says little). (iv) A heuristic for why the probability of an unfaithful run may not exceed the bound by more than a constant: an input of the human system's run can be hidden from the audits only by rehearsal runs that leave that run before reaching it, and such runs are counted in Σ_t u_t ≤ L·K ln 2. (v) That the last iterate of such a scheme carries no guarantee of its own has published relatives (Cheng and Boots 2018, named in `research/sections/related-work.md`; not opened by me).
