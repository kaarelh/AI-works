## 0. Result in brief

**Broken as worded** (sentences, not theorems; each with an exact counterexample):

1. *"This is also all that can be checked"* (`note.md`, Section 8.2), and in the file the Reading of Theorem 6.2: "no procedure that uses m single answers can certify … a bound on the distance between the imitator's and the human's answer laws". Assumption A1 allows a question to be put twice. Two answers to one input give an unbiased estimate of Σ_y (P(y|x) − A(y|x))². For binary answers, m probe inputs asked twice certify the mean total variation per call on the imitator's own inputs (Proposition X1 below). The coin and every function are told apart by one input asked twice. The sentence is true only for validations that never repeat a question, or for answers from a set so large that two answers never coincide (Finding M1).
2. *Theorem 4.11 as read* ("K cannot be replaced by a function of R"; "a constant-length property"). On the machine U′ of the file the property is not cheap. This is the check report's main finding, not mine; I confirm it and its repair (Finding M3).

4. *The sentence on Bousquet, Kane and Moran in Example 3.11(a).* On the four-point space of that example the member nearest to the empirical measure is the better one with probability 1 − 8·10⁻¹⁸ at N = 101; their lower bound is for sample sizes that do not grow with the domain (Finding m4).

**Claims in words that the theorems do not carry:**

* "The mixture does not give the per-property price" (title of Section 8.5 of the note). What is proved is: *need not*. The level "near 1" of Theorem 4.5 is the prior probability that a memoriser's answer outside its table violates R, which the class sets to 1; with a prior λ on that answer the level is exactly λ(violating) times the value of the theorem. For one fixed machine the phenomenon of Theorem 4.11 lasts for a bounded number of questions. The machine test of the researcher applies in full (Finding M3, Section 2).
* "Robust when the human is not a stateless sampler" (candidate theory, Pr4). A human who merely repeats himself within a run is at total variation √2 − 1 = 0.414 from every stateless sampler; three times that exceeds 1, so the oracle inequality of Theorem 3.6 is empty for every class of samplers (Finding m3).
* "Nothing is assumed about the human." The batch statements assume that the N runs are independent *and identically distributed*, which for one person means no memory and no change from run to run; the certificate assumes A1 for the probes (Finding m2).
* The floor "never-violators keep posterior weight 1/C_R" (Corollary 4.3(c)) can consist of hypotheses that give no answer. In the closed memoriser class with C_R = 5 the mixture at an unseen input violates with probability 0.711, is silent with probability 0.178 and gives an abiding answer with probability 0.111; given that it answers, it violates with probability 0.865 > 1 − 1/C_R. And in the model of the note, where a sampler always answers, the closure assumption (Cl_R) cannot hold as stated (Finding m5; Proposition X3 gives a form that can).

**Not new, and to be attributed** (Finding M2). Theorem 2.3 is the Occam's razor bound. That a constant predictor cannot be told from a deterministic Nature by outcomes, which is the content of Proposition 5.2 and Theorem 6.2, is said in the introduction of Dwork, Kim, Reingold, Rothblum and Yona, "Outcome indistinguishability" (read in this session). Theorem 3.6 is the modified minimum-distance estimate of Mahalanabis and Štefankovič with a weighted union bound (the file says so; all its statements about that paper are exact). Theorem 4.7 is the bound for the Bayes posterior over consistent hypotheses in the form that holds for all n at once.

**What the seven tests catch** (Section 2). Budget: Pr3 and Pr4 in batch form need N independent runs *of the human system*, that is N·L answers given in whole runs, and the estimator of Definition 3.4 compares up to e^(2N) candidates. Machine: Pr2(b) and (c) are statements about the prior. Copy: test matching cannot tell the sampler from the sampler with its coins filled in; that is Theorem 6.2. Rock: silence satisfies the hindsight bound. Guard: a test-matched imitator may do anything on a kind of input whose frequency is below the tolerance.

**Additions for the editor** (Section 5; all mine, unrefereed, each checked numerically): X1, a certificate for a human who is not deterministic, from two answers per probe, when the set of answers is small; X2, a two-sided form of Theorem 6.2(d) with one function for a whole weighted family of decision rules, which is the "two humans" statement that the task asked for; X3, a closure under conditioning that keeps samplers proper, with a floor that is made of answers; X4, the coin is a *member* of the matching set 𝓜_N with probability at least 1 − δ − C(N, 2)·coll(μ).

### MAJOR

**M1. "Frequencies of tests are all that can be checked for a human who is not deterministic" is false as the note and the Reading of Theorem 6.2 put it.**

*Where.* `note.md`, Section 8.2: "For a human who is not deterministic this is also all that can be checked: each input is seen once, so P(·|x) is not observable at any single x, and only frequencies of tests can be estimated." The file, Section 6.2, Reading: "no procedure that uses m single answers can certify, for a human who may be non-deterministic, a bound on the distance between the imitator's and the human's answer laws: not at one input, not on average over inputs, not for runs." (Section 3.3 of the note has the proviso "at inputs that never repeat" and is correct as it stands. So is item (5) of the file's "In brief".)

*What Theorem 6.2 proves.* Statement (c): a procedure distinguishes the random deterministic human P_F from P₀ with advantage at most ξ, the probability that it puts some input to the human twice. Nothing is proved about procedures with ξ near 1, and A1 puts no limit on ξ: by A1 "each time a question x is put to the human, the answer is a fresh draw from P(·|x), independent of everything else given x". "Each input is seen once" describes the validation phase as the note designed it. It is not a fact about the problem. (The check report makes this point in the form "a proviso, not a fact". What follows is the counterexample and what it is worth.)

*Counterexample (exact; `property-batch_attack_sec56.out`, blocks E and E2).*
(i) Imitator: the fair coin. Procedure: put input 1 to the human twice and accept iff the two answers are equal. It accepts with probability 1/2 if the human is the coin and with probability 1 if the human is any function. With 50 inputs asked twice, the coin gives 50 equal pairs with probability 2⁻⁵⁰, every function with probability 1. The statistic is the frequency of "the two answers agree", a test of a run that puts one question twice. It is not a frequency of a test of single pairs, so it is not among the quantities of Theorem V1(b).
(ii) For answers in {0, 1}, with a := A(1|x) and p := P(1|x), two independent answers y₁, y₂ of the human to x satisfy E[(y₁ − a)(y₂ − a)] = (p − a)² (checked for all p, a in {0, 0.1, …, 1}). So m probe inputs, each asked twice, certify the mean of (P(1|x) − A(1|x))² over the calls of the imitator's own runs to within √(2 ln(2/δ)/m), and the mean total variation per call is at most the square root of that mean. This is a bound "on average over inputs" of exactly the kind the Reading says no procedure can certify. For the coin as imitator the statistic equals 1/4 with certainty when the human is a function and has mean 0 when the human is the coin: 100 inputs asked twice leave an error probability below 4·10⁻⁶.
(iii) For any countable set of answers the statistic 1[y₁ = y₂] − A(y₁|x) − A(y₂|x) + Σ_y A(y|x)² has mean Σ_y (P(y|x) − A(y|x))² (checked on 300 random pairs of laws). Proposition X1 (Section 5) states what this certifies.

*Where the sentence is true.* When the answers come from a set so large that two answers to one input almost never coincide, repetition does not help. Let P₀(·|x) be uniform on s answers and let the other human be uniform on a random half of them, so that the two are at total variation 1/2 at every input. The law of r answers of P₀ to one input and the law of r answers of the other human, averaged over the choice of the half, are then at total variation 0.004 for (s, r) = (256, 2), 0.007 for (4096, 8) and (65536, 32), and about 0.1 when r² = s/4 (exact, block F); no procedure that puts r questions to that input has a larger average advantage. This is the birthday bound behind the lower bound for identity testing, of order √s/ε² samples for a law on s points (Paninski 2008, from memory, see Section 4; `research/sections/related-work.md` cites it for the same purpose, and item 6 of the notes in that file raises the same objection to the sentence). For answers that are long strings, s exceeds any budget.

*What to change.* In Section 8.2: "With one answer per input, as in validation, frequencies of tests are all that can be certified: Theorem 6.2. A1 allows an input to be put again. Two answers per probe certify the mean squared distance between the answer laws, which is of use when the set of possible answers is small (Proposition X1) and of none for long answers, where two answers do not coincide." In the file: in the Reading replace "no procedure that uses m single answers" by "no procedure that puts each input to the human at most once", and drop "not on average over inputs" unless that proviso is repeated.

*Two smaller points on the same theorem.* (a) Statement (d) is one-sided, as the check report says. For a fixed f a procedure does tell P_f from P₀: "ask input 1, accept iff the answer is 0" accepts the coin with probability 1/2 and every function with probability 0 or 1. Only the average over F is indistinguishable. Proposition X2 gives a two-sided statement with one function for a whole family of decision rules. (b) Example 6.3 (parity against the coin) is presented correctly as "exactly equal frequencies on a family", but it is not an example of two humans that answers cannot tell apart: the one question "ask any x, accept iff y = parity(x)" accepts the parity human with probability 1 and the coin with probability 1/2 (block G). The last sentence of Section 6.2 says this; the summary row "Theorem 6.2, Example 6.3 … cannot tell" does not.

**M3. What Pr2(b) and Pr2(c) show is that the mixture *need not* pay the per-property price; the note's title says it *does not*, and the reading of Theorem 4.11 was wrong on the machine it constructs.**

*(a) The gap in the reading of Theorem 4.11 (the check report's first finding; confirmed).* On U′ every program that decides R, and every program for a conditioned memoriser, lies behind the prefix 1^c. So on that machine R costs at least c + 1 bits and the constant of (Cl_R) is of order 2^c, while the theorem covers n ≤ (c − 4)/(d + 2) questions. The theorem therefore does not separate K from K(R) on one machine. The credit for this is the check report's: on my own I had only the weaker observation of fact 2 below, that on a fixed machine the construction works for a bounded number of questions. After reading its statement of the gap, and before reading its repair, I worked out a repair myself: give the conditioned memorisers and a decider of R short programs of their own; then K(R) is a constant, the closure constant is max{17, 2^(K_U(R)+c₀)} with c₀ the composition constant of U, neither depends on c, and the lower bound 0.51 holds for about (c − 4)/(d + 2) questions. The check report's machine U″ is the same construction with the lengths worked out, so the repair was found twice. I recomputed its three claims by hand: the code is prefix-free and its weights sum to less than 2^(−c) + 0.24; the closure constant of the memoriser part is (1/8 + 1/128)/(1/128) = 17; and for k·(d + 2) ≤ c − 5 the lower bound is (3/4)·(16/17)·(10/11)·(4/5) = 0.513. I did not enumerate U″ by program.

*(b) Even after the repair, the statement is about the choice of the prior.* Three facts, the first two exact:

1. *The level is a parameter of the class.* In H_mem every memoriser answers b outside its table. Let the answer outside the table be a default d, drawn once with prior λ from {b, g}, where g is an answer that abides by R and that the human never gives. Then at an unseen input the mixture answers b with probability λ(b)·(value of Theorem 4.5(b)) exactly: 0.889, 0.444 and 0.018 for λ(b) = 1, 1/2 and 1/50 at u = 4, K = 30, one pair seen (`property-batch_attack_pr2.out`, block H). So "stays near 1" is "stays near the prior probability that what a memoriser does off its table violates R". Corollary 4.6 is the case where the other default is silence.
2. *The memorisers that Theorem 4.11 adds lose to the coin after a number of questions that is a constant of the machine.* On U′ the fair coin is a program of length c + k_c (k_c its length for U) and has likelihood 2⁻ⁿ on every data set with answers in {0, 1}; the added memorisers that are consistent with n distinct inputs have total weight at most 0.42·(4u)⁻ⁿ. Their share of the posterior is therefore below 2^(c+k_c−n(d+1)), for every human with answers in {0, 1}. What the mixture does after about (c + k_c)/(d + 1) questions is decided by the programs of U, about which neither report proves anything.
3. *For a single fixed machine the question is a mathematical one, and it is open.* Item 4 of 4.13 calls the question for "natural" machines "not a mathematical question as it stands". For each fixed universal machine U the following is a precise statement: there is a constant c_U such that for every decidable R, every human in the class that never violates R, and every law μ, with probability 1 − δ, V(D_n) ≤ (c_U + K_U(R))·ln 2/n + ln(1/δ)/n for all n. Theorem 4.11, repaired, shows that c_U cannot be bounded in terms of the constants that govern hindsight on that machine. It does not refute the statement for any machine. Neither report proves it for any machine.

*(c) What to change.* Title of Section 8.5: "The mixture need not give the per-property price". Body: as in the replacement text of the check report (its Part IV), with one sentence added from fact 1: "the level at which the mixture violates the property in this example is the prior probability that a memorising hypothesis violates it where it has no data; the example sets it to 1". **m3. The oracle inequality is empty for a human with memory inside a run, unless the class contains hypotheses with memory.** The file says, after Theorem 3.6, that for such a human the approximation term is the distance to the best stateless imitation, "which may be large". It is large in the simplest case. Scaffold: the same question twice. Human: answers a fair bit and repeats it. The run law is (0,0) or (1,1) with probability 1/2 each. A stateless sampler that answers 1 with probability p has run law (p², p(1−p), (1−p)p, (1−p)²); its total variation from the human system is minimal at p = 1 − 1/√2 and p = 1/√2, with value √2 − 1 = 0.4142 (exact; `property-batch_attack_pr4.out`, block 7). The right side of (O) is then at least 3·0.4142 = 1.24. With the factor 3, the inequality says something only when some member of the class is within 1/3 of the human system. The sentence of the candidate theory "robust when the human is not a stateless sampler" must not be carried over (the check report says the same for another reason). What is true: Section 3 holds for a countable class of *answerers*, as its first paragraph says; the note should state Pr4 for answerers, or say that statelessness of the human enters through the approximation term.

**m4. Example 3.11(a): the sentence "no estimator which must return a member of the class can have a factor below 3" needs its quantifier.** Read in the source (Section 4): Bousquet, Kane and Moran count a class as α-learnable on a finite domain only if the number of samples grows at most polylogarithmically with the size of the domain; "if X is finite then any class of distribution is α-learnable for α = 1 with sample complexity O(|X|/ε²)" (their Section 1.5). Their Theorem 19 is for an infinite domain; Theorem 21 for a domain of M points and at most √(Mβ) samples. On the four-point space of Example 3.11(a) a rule that returns a member of the class does much better than the factor 3: the member nearest in total variation to the empirical measure is the far one with probability 5.9·10⁻³ at N = 11 and 8.5·10⁻¹⁸ at N = 101 (ε₀ = 0.05), while the estimate of Definition 3.4 returns the far one with probability exactly 0.500000 at N = 101 and N = 1001 (block 4). The file's own claim, "for no c < 3 does *this* estimator …", is correct. Two further facts from the sources that bear on Corollary 3.7(c): the lower bound is for guarantees that hold with probability above 2/3 (their Corollary 20), and for two densities a randomised rule that returns a member has factor 2 in expectation (Mahalanabis and Štefankovič, Theorem 10, read: their Algorithm 5 outputs f₁ or f₂ at random and has E‖f − g‖₁ ≤ 2·d₁(g, F) + Δ; Bousquet, Kane and Moran report it in their Sections 1.6 and 1.8). So the factor 3 in the bound in expectation of Corollary 3.7(c) is not known to be best possible. Their Remark 1 (version 5, December 2025) records that the original proof of the lower bound had a gap, now corrected; cite that version. (The check report has the quantifiers and the remark; the two facts on expectation are added here.)

**m5. The floor of Corollary 4.3(c) may be made of silence, and in the note's model the closure assumption cannot hold as stated.**
*(a) Silence.* Definition 4.1 counts "no answer" as not violating, and Remark 4.4 explains why it must. A consequence that the file does not draw: "the hypotheses that never violate R have posterior weight at least 1/C_R" does not mean that the mixture gives an abiding answer with probability 1/C_R. In the closed class of Corollary 4.6 with u = 4, K = 30, C_R = 5 and one pair seen, at an unseen input the mixture gives a violating answer with probability 0.7111, no answer with probability 0.1778, and an abiding answer with probability 0.1111 (the memorisers whose table happens to contain the input). The never-violators hold 1/C_R = 0.2 of the posterior, and 0.178 of that is silence. If the mixture is normalised over the answers given (Solomonoff's normalisation; or the note's rule "draw a hypothesis from the posterior and let it answer", repeated until an answer comes), the violation probability is 0.865, above 1 − 1/C_R = 0.8; for C_R = 100 it is 0.888 and the silent part is 0.009 (`property-batch_attack_pr2.out`, block B). So the floor is a statement about the semimeasure, and the rock test of the researcher fails it: a hypothesis that falls silent wherever it has no data counts as abiding.
*(b) The model of the note.* `note.md`, Section 1, defines a sampler as a probability distribution on Y for every x, and the class as a set of samplers. For such a ν with ν(R_x|x) = 0 at some x, the conditioned ν^R gives no answer at x and is not a sampler in that sense; a class of samplers that contains one such ν does not satisfy (Cl_R) for any constant. The file works with semimeasures (Section 1, "Semimeasures"), which removes the problem for the class of all programs, at the price of (a). If Section 8.5 of the note states the hindsight bound, it must either admit hypotheses that may give no answer, and say that silence counts as abiding, or use a conditioning that always answers. Proposition X3 (Section 5) is such a form: with a fall-back sampler G that never violates R, the class of all programs is closed with a constant 2^(K(R)+K(G)+c), the product bound holds with 1/(1 − (1−θ)·v) in place of 1/(1 − v), and the never-violators of the floor are samplers that answer.

**m6. The universal family of tests cannot be used as it is defined.** Section 1.3: "When π(R) = 2^(−K(R)) with K(R) the length of a shortest program that decides R, the family consists of all decidable events". As a statement about probabilities Theorem 2.3 holds for this family. To *apply* Corollary 2.6, Proposition 3.8 or 3.9, or to evaluate the certificate for all tests of price below some bound, one must decide for each recorded run whether it lies in R, for every R of that price; that needs a list of the programs that halt on every input, which no program produces, and K(R) itself is not computable. Item (C4) of Section 3.5 asks that "R must be decidable from what is recorded of a run" and does not mention this. Unlimited computation, which the researcher grants, does not include a halting oracle (the digest `research/understand/notes.md`, Section 6.2, Observation B, makes the same remark about sep). **m7. ## 2. The researcher's seven tests, applied to this part

The tests are those of `research/understand/notes.md`, Section 5. For each: what the claims of this part answer, and what the test catches.

**1. Guard** ("name an input property that no training input has; what does the scheme do there, and with what probability?").
*Batch form (Pr3, Pr4).* The recorded runs are runs of the human system at full depth, and the object that is constrained is the law of the imitator's own run. So a guard on the depth of a call, or on any property of inputs that only the imitator's errors create, is covered as far as it shows in a test: if a behaviour B is a test of the family and never occurred in N recorded runs, every imitator of 𝓜_N shows it with probability at most (K(B)·ln 2 + ln(2/δ))/N per run, whatever the complexity of the guard that triggers it (Corollary 2.6(iii)). This is the one place where test matching is stronger than the mixture, whose bound has K in the place of K(B).
*What the test catches.* (i) The bound is a frequency. On a kind of input whose frequency is below the bound the imitator may do anything (w3). (ii) Only behaviours with a simple test are bounded. A behaviour that no simple test detects is not bounded at any N: Proposition 5.2 is the example, with "being wrong" as the behaviour. (iii) With the certificate of Section 6 the frequencies are per call. A bad kind of pair that the human never showed in m probes has frequency at most ln(2/(π(B)δ))/m per call, so up to L times that per run: m must exceed L·(K(B)·ln 2 + ln(2/δ))/ε for a run to be free of it with probability 1 − ε. The factor L of Theorem N2 returns.

**2. Machine** ("is the answer a fact about the scheme, or about the reference machine?").
*Tests.* The machine enters the price of a test additively: K(R) + c in the place of K(R) changes the tolerance from √(2(K(R)·ln 2 + ln(2/δ))/N) to √(2((K(R) + c)·ln 2 + ln(2/δ))/N). This is benign. The machine still decides which properties are cheap, and nothing in the theorems says that the cheap ones are the ones that matter; Finding m6 adds that the family must be computable.
*Hindsight (Pr2(a)).* C_R = 2^(K(R)+c). The count of violations over the inputs seen is ln C_R, linear in the machine constant. The floor 1/C_R is exponential in it and, by m5(a), may be silence.
*Foresight (Pr2(b), (c)).* The whole of Pr2(b) and (c) is a fact about the prior, in the sense of Finding M3: the level is the prior probability that a memoriser violates R off its table; the duration on a fixed machine is a constant of the machine; for a fixed machine the positive statement is open. The file is careful about this in 4.13; the note's Section 8.5 is not.

**3. Referent** ("does a hypothesis that models how the data set was made do as well as the intended one?").
For closeness in total variation the file answers: the hypothesis "replay the recorded runs" wins without a penalty and loses with one (Example 3.11(b)). For test matching alone it does not answer, and the test catches this: let the closed system have a fixed top-level input and no randomness of its own (the note's own example: "a fixed research question worked on by a tree of calls"). The answerer "choose one of the N recorded runs at random and give its answers in order" has run law Q̂_N. It lies in 𝓜_N, it minimises the discrepancy d_N of Proposition 3.9 with value 0, and Corollary 2.6 is true of it. It contains no imitation: N human runs are replayed. So the sentence "any imitator whose run law matches the empirical frequencies …" (Corollary 2.6; the table of Section 5.1) includes the copy of the data. What excludes it is outside the theorem: a top-level input whose law has no atoms together with tests that tie the answers to the input, or a penalty on the description length of the imitator as in Section 3.3.

**4. Copy** ("does anything in the theorem distinguish the sampler from a better predictor of it, or from the sampler with coins filled in?").
For tests: nothing does, and this is what Proposition 5.2 and Theorem 6.2 prove. P_F is P₀ with its coins filled in. Proposition X2 sharpens it: one fill-in f passes, two-sidedly, every decision rule of a weighted family applied to m independent probes. For total variation at the price K (Corollary 3.7(a)): a class that contains both P₀ and f separates them after about K(f) recorded runs, by the Yatracos set "the answer equals f(x)". So of the two prices only the first says anything about being a copy, and it is the one that is empty for a person. The note should say this where it compares the prices; the file's Section 5.3 says "being right" where it could also say "being the sampler and not one of its fill-ins".

**5. Budget** ("how many human answers, of what length, on which questions?").
*What the batch statements cost.* N independent runs of the human system: up to N·L answers, given in whole runs, by a human who is the same in every run (m2). Numbers from the file's own bounds with δ = 0.05: a property of 50 bits that never failed holds for the imitator with frequency at least 0.99 once N ≥ (50·ln 2 + ln 40)/0.01 = 3835 runs; a two-sided tolerance of 0.05 for one such test needs N ≥ 2·38.35/0.05² = 30 677 runs; total variation 0.1 at K = 10⁶ bits needs N ≥ 1.4·10⁸ runs (Corollary 3.7(d)).
*What the test catches.* The digest of the researcher's notes (`notes.md`, Section 6.1, constraint 5) concludes that "any theorem that needs the human system itself to be run many times is restricted to small scaffolds", and that the scaffolds he cares about are trees that no human team could run. Pr3 in batch form and all of Pr4 are of that kind. The file says so in one clause (item (4) after Theorem 3.6) and in no table. The subsection of the note on the batch estimator should open with it: these are statements for scaffolds small enough for a person to run thousands of times, and for larger ones the per-call certificate (m single answers; Theorem 6.1) and the online rule are what is left.

**6. Rock** ("does the scheme still act on the inputs that matter, or does it abstain everywhere new?").
(i) Two-sided test matching passes: if "the answer is a refusal" is a test, the imitator refuses at the human's rate. (ii) The hindsight bound and its floor do not pass: silence counts as abiding (m5(a)). (iii) The test raises a question that the part does not put: for a decidable property that the human never violates, an imitator with unlimited computation can simply be conditioned on the property (the map ν ↦ ν^R of Definition 4.1 applied to the imitator, or a filter that declines). That keeps the property with certainty, at every input, with no data and no theorem; the researcher calls it the "trivial case … mathematically silly" (`notes.md`, Section 2.5). What Pr3 adds to the filter is not enforcement. It is the *list*: all properties of at most k bits that held in N runs hold again with frequency at least 1 − (k·ln 2 + ln(2/δ))/N, without anyone having named them; and the two-sided rates of properties that hold only sometimes. One sentence to this effect belongs in Section 8.2, because "why not filter" is the first objection of a reader who knows the notes.

**7. Horizon** ("granting perfect per-call imitation, does the scaffold do anything?").
Outside this part. Example 5.3 belongs to it from the other side: per-call matching, even perfect, says nothing about how the calls of a run hang together (the probability of "the two answers agree" is 1 against 1/2).

## 5. Additions for the editor

All four statements are mine and unrefereed. Each was checked numerically (Section 6); none was read by a second person.

**Proposition X1 (two answers per probe: a certificate for a human who is not deterministic, when the set of answers is small).** Take the setting of Theorem 6.1 of the file with one change: in every round that is not empty the probe input x* is put to the human twice, and the answers y₁, y₂ are independent draws from P(·|x*) (this is A1). Let A be a sampler whose probabilities A(y|x) can be computed. (For an answerer with memory, read A(·|x*) as the law of its answer at the probe call given its run so far.) Put

 s := 1[y₁ = y₂] − A(y₁|x*) − A(y₂|x*) + Σ_y A(y|x*)² (and s := 0 in an empty round),
 d(x)² := Σ_y (P(y|x) − A(y|x))², D₂ := (1/L)·E_{Q_A}[Σ_{j ≤ len(τ)} d(x_j)²],

and let s̄ be the average of s over m rounds. Then:
(a) E[s] = D₂, and −1/2 ≤ s ≤ 2.
(b) With probability at least 1 − δ: D₂ ≤ s̄ + (5/2)·√(ln(1/δ)/(2m)).
(c) If at every input the two laws P(·|x) and A(·|x) are carried by a common set of at most q answers, the expected number β of unfaithful calls in a run of A satisfies β ≤ (L/2)·√(q·D₂). For answers in {0, 1}: β ≤ L·√(D₂/2).
So with at most 2m answers of the human, Theorem R3 of the note applies with β ≤ (L/2)·√(q·(s̄ + (5/2)·√(ln(1/δ)/(2m)))), whatever the human and however A was made.

*Proof.* (a) Given x*, E 1[y₁ = y₂] = Σ_y P(y|x*)² and E A(y_i|x*) = Σ_y P(y|x*)·A(y|x*), so E[s | x*] = Σ P² − 2 Σ P·A + Σ A² = d(x*)². The probe is the J-th call of a run of A with J uniform on {1, …, L}, which gives E[s] = D₂. If y₁ = y₂ = y then s = 1 − 2A(y) + Σ A² ≥ (1 − A(y))² ≥ 0 and s ≤ 2. If y₁ ≠ y₂ then s ≥ −a − b + a² + b² with a = A(y₁), b = A(y₂), a + b ≤ 1, which is at least −1/2; and s ≤ Σ A² ≤ 1. (b) Hoeffding's inequality, one side, for m independent terms in an interval of length 5/2. (c) TV(P(·|x), A(·|x)) = ½·Σ_y |P − A| ≤ (√q/2)·d(x) by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. With the coupling of Section 1 of the note a call at x is unfaithful with probability TV(P(·|x), A(·|x)). Hence β ≤ (√q/2)·E Σ_j d(x_j) ≤ (√q/2)·√(E[len])·√(E Σ_j d(x_j)²) ≤ (√q/2)·√L·√(L·D₂). For two answers Σ_y (P − A)² = 2·TV², which gives the second form. ∎

*What it does not say.* Nothing for answers that are long strings: q is then beyond any budget, and by the birthday bound of Finding M1 no statistic of repeated answers helps. Nothing about one input: P(·|x) at a given x stays unknown; only the average over the imitator's own calls is certified. And the rate is slow: for a perfect imitator D₂ = 0 is certified only to within about m^(−1/2), so β/L is certified to within about m^(−1/4), against 1/m for a deterministic human (Theorem V1(a)).

**Proposition X2 (Theorem 6.2(d) two-sided, with one function for a family of decision rules).** Let P₀, p_max, F and coll(μ) be as in Theorem 6.2. A *decision rule with m probes* draws x_1, …, x_m independently from μ, receives one answer of the human to each, and accepts with a probability D((x_1, y_1), …, (x_m, y_m)) ∈ [0, 1]. (This is the form of a validation phase: the probes do not depend on the human's answers.) Let 𝒟 be a countable family of decision rules, the rule D using m_D probes, with weights π_D > 0 of total at most 1, and let η ∈ (0, 1). With probability at least 1 − η over F, for every D ∈ 𝒟:

 |Pr_{P_F}[D accepts] − Pr_{P₀}[D accepts]| ≤ C(m_D, 2)·coll(μ) + m_D·√(coll(μ)·ln(2/(π_D·η))/2).

So there is one function f, chosen before any rule of the family is, with TV(P_f(·|x), P₀(·|x)) ≥ 1 − p_max(x) at every x, that every rule of the family accepts with the probability with which it accepts P₀, up to the error shown. For m_D = 1 and D the indicator of a set B of pairs this is Proposition 5.2(a) and Theorem 6.2(b), with the same constant.

*Proof.* Put a_F(D) := Pr_{P_F}[D accepts] and a₀(D) := Pr_{P₀}[D accepts]. By Theorem 6.2(c), |E_F a_F(D) − a₀(D)| ≤ C(m_D, 2)·coll(μ). As a function of the independent values F(x), x ∈ X, the number a_F(D) changes by at most m_D·μ(x₀) when F is changed at the one input x₀, because the transcript changes only if x₀ is among the m_D probes. By the bounded-differences inequality, Pr[|a_F(D) − E_F a_F(D)| ≥ t] ≤ 2·exp(−2t²/(m_D²·coll(μ))). Take t = m_D·√(coll(μ)·ln(2/(π_D·η))/2) and sum over D. ∎ (The bounded-differences inequality is McDiarmid's, 1989; standard, from memory; Section 4.)

*Reading.* For the uniform law on u inputs the error is m²/(2u) + m·√(ln(2/(π_D·η))/(2u)): small as soon as u is large against m²·ln(1/π_D). This is the statement "two humans, far apart at every input, with nearly equal test frequencies, that m such samples cannot tell apart" with the quantifiers in the strong order: one deterministic human for all rules of the family. It is for rules whose probes are independent draws from μ; for adaptive procedures Theorem 6.2(c) and (d) remain what is proved.

**Proposition X3 (a conditioning that always answers).** Let G be a sampler with Σ_y G(y|x) = 1 that never violates R (so R_x is not empty for any x), and θ ∈ (0, 1]. For a sampler ν put

 ν^{R,θ}(y|x) := [ν(y|x)·1[(x, y) ∈ R] + θ·v_ν(x)·G(y|x)] / (1 − (1 − θ)·v_ν(x)).

It is the law of: "draw an answer from ν; if it abides, give it; if it violates, give an answer of G with probability θ and otherwise start again". If Σ_y ν(y|x) = 1 then Σ_y ν^{R,θ}(y|x) = 1; ν^{R,θ} never violates R; and ν^{R,θ}(y|x) ≥ ν(y|x)/(1 − (1 − θ)·v_ν(x)) ≥ ν(y|x) for every abiding pair. Suppose the class is closed under ν ↦ ν^{R,θ} with constant C (as in (Cl_R), with ν^{R,θ} in the place of ν^R). Then for every data set D_n that abides by R and has M(D_n) > 0:
(a) the posterior mean of Π_{i ≤ n} 1/(1 − (1 − θ)·v_ν(x_i)) is at most C; hence, for θ < 1, Σ_{i ≤ n} v_{M_{D_n}}(x_i) ≤ ln C/(1 − θ);
(b) the hypotheses in the image of the map have posterior weight at least 1/C; they never violate R, and those that are images of samplers that always answer always answer. So in a class of samplers that always answer, the mixture gives an answer that abides by R with probability at least 1/C at every input.
For the class of all programs and a decidable R with R_x never empty, the procedure is a program of length ℓ(p) + ℓ(r) + ℓ(g) + c, so C = 2^(K(R)+K(G)+c); G may be "the first abiding answer in a fixed enumeration", which is computed from the decider of R.

*Proof.* As for Theorem 4.2 and Corollary 4.3(c) of the file, with ν^{R,θ}(D_n) ≥ ν(D_n)·Π_i (1 − (1 − θ)·v_ν(x_i))^(−1) ≥ ν(D_n) in the place of the identity for ν^R, and ln(1/(1 − (1 − θ)v)) ≥ (1 − θ)·v. ∎

With θ = 1 the map is the filter "replace a violating answer by an answer of G"; then (a) is empty and (b) remains. With θ = 1/2 both hold, at twice the count of Corollary 4.3(a).

**Proposition X4 (the coin is a member of the matching set).** In the setting of Proposition 5.2 let the human be P_F, with F random as there, and let 𝓜_N be the matching set of Corollary 2.6 for N recorded runs of the system answered by P_F and any test family. Then with probability at least 1 − δ − C(N, 2)·coll(μ) over F and the runs, the run law Q_{P₀} lies in 𝓜_N.

*Proof.* On the event that the N recorded inputs are distinct, the N recorded pairs have the law of N independent runs of the system answered by P₀ (answer the values of F lazily, as in the proof of Theorem 6.2(c)). For N independent runs with law Q_{P₀}, Theorem 2.3 with Q = Q_{P₀} gives Q_{P₀} ∈ 𝓜_N with probability at least 1 − δ. The N inputs are distinct with probability at least 1 − C(N, 2)·coll(μ). ∎

So for N small against √u a rule that returns "any member of 𝓜_N" may return the coin when the human is a function, and Corollary 2.6 holds for it, while the coin differs from the human at every call with probability 1 − p_max(x).
