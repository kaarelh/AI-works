## 1. The model

**The human.** X and Y are countable sets of input and answer strings. A *sampler* S gives every input x a probability distribution S(·|x) on Y. Assumption **A1**: the human is a sampler P, and a question x put to the human is answered by a draw from P(·|x), whatever was realised before. If every P(·|x) is a point mass, the human is *deterministic* and f denotes the function that gives the answer; otherwise the human *varies*.

**The class.** H is a countable set of *hypotheses* ν with weights w(ν) > 0 of total at most 1. (A hypothesis may be a program that sometimes gives no answer; that counts as one more answer.) Assumption **A2**: P is in H. We write K := log₂(1/w(P)). After data D the posterior weight of ν is proportional to w(ν) times the probability that ν gives to the recorded answers, and the *mixture* M_D(y|x) := Σ_ν (posterior weight of ν)·ν(y|x) answers every question with a hypothesis drawn afresh from the posterior. For the class of all programs it is Solomonoff's predictor for question–answer pairs (Solomonoff 2008, equation (10), as Paper 1 restates it; see References).

**The scaffold.** A *scaffold* Π draws a top-level input z from a law D₀ and random bits ρ, then computes each question x_j from z, ρ and the answers so far, or stops, after at most L questions (the *horizon*). One execution is a *run*; its questions are its *calls*, and from the finished run the scaffold computes an *outcome*. An *answerer* A gives the law A_j(·|h_j) of the j-th answer given the history h_j = (z, ρ, y₁, …, y_{j−1}); a sampler looks at x_j only. Q_A is the law of the run answered by A. The scaffold answered by the human is the *human system*, and its run law Q_P is what is to be imitated. An *imitator* is an answerer that takes the human's place.

**Which K.** In the upper bounds of Sections 3.1 to 3.4, K may be read as log₂(1/w([P])), where [P] is the set of hypotheses that answer as the human does at every input that the scaffold can produce, and w([P]) is their total weight. This is the number of bits that the prior needs to single out the human's behaviour on those inputs, and it is at most K. It is smaller than K when hypotheses agree with the human on those inputs and differ elsewhere, which can happen when those inputs are few; when the scaffold can pose every question there are no such hypotheses (`proofs/rehearsal-core.md`, Remark 4.4(d)).

**Unfaithful calls.** Couple a run of an imitator A with the human: at each call, A's answer y_j and a *shadow* answer y′_j from P(·|x_j) are drawn jointly so that they differ with the least possible probability, the total variation distance TV(A_j(·|h_j), P(·|x_j)), and the run continues with y_j. The call is *unfaithful* if y_j ≠ y′_j, and a run is *unfaithful* if at least one of its calls is; β(A) is the expected number of unfaithful calls of a run. The run of A is thus a run of the human system in which a rule that sees the human's answer has replaced the answers at the unfaithful calls (`proofs/rehearsal-core.md`, Lemma 3.2 and Proposition 3.4). For a deterministic human a call is unfaithful exactly if y_j ≠ f(x_j), and a run without such calls is the human system's run on the same z and ρ.

**The protocol's coins.** Assumption **A3** is about the random choices of the protocol of Section 2 and has five parts. (i) The call put to the human is drawn uniformly after the run, independently of it. (ii) The round T whose mixture is deployed is drawn independently of everything else, and the deployed run uses a fresh z and ρ and fresh draws of the imitator. (iii) Class and weights are fixed before any coin is drawn. (iv) The human is shown the string that the imitator was shown, and nothing marks it as a question of the protocol. (v) Every rehearsal and validation run uses fresh coins.

**Closed deployment.** A deployment is *closed* if: (C1) given all that the protocol saw and did, the z and ρ of the deployed run are a fresh draw from the scaffold's laws; (C2) the runs of rehearsal and validation were generated by the protocol on fresh draws from the same laws; (C3) the answers of the deployed run depend on its history and the imitator's coins alone: no clock, no mark that this run is the real one, no state of the world. The scaffold is then a *closed system*. A fixed question worked on by a tree of calls is closed. A service answering questions from a changing world is not: it is an *open system*, whose inputs arrive from outside and cannot be rehearsed (Section 6).

## 2. The protocol: rehearse, validate, deploy

The protocol has three steps, and we call it **Protocol R**. Its *rounds* are the n rounds of the first step. The second step may be left out.

**Rehearse.** For each round t = 1, …, n:

1. Let M^(t) be the mixture given the questions answered so far.
2. Run the scaffold to the end with M^(t) answering every call.
3. Draw J uniformly from {1, …, L}. If the run made at least J calls, put its J-th question to the human and add question and answer to the data.

**Validate.** Take the imitator A that is to be deployed and *freeze* it: fix it, and let it answer every run with fresh coins. After rehearsal, A is the mixture M^(T) of a round T drawn uniformly from {1, …, n}, with T drawn once or afresh for every run; the step works as well for an imitator obtained in any other way. For i = 1, …, m: run the scaffold with A, draw J as before, and if the run made at least J calls put its J-th question to the human; record the human's answer next to the answer that A gave in the run.

**Deploy.** Run the scaffold with the validated imitator. Without validation: for each run draw T uniformly from {1, …, n}, and let M^(T) answer the whole run.

The human answers at most n + m single calls, taken from the imitator's own runs at every depth of a decomposition, and never carries out a run.

**Compute.** If the hypotheses are programs that always halt, for instance programs that carry their own time bound, and the weights are 2^(−length) in a decidable prefix-free code and add up to 1, a posterior draw can be made by rejection: read a program from coin flips, run it with fresh coins on the recorded questions, and keep it if it reproduces the recorded answers. A kept program has exactly the posterior law. The expected number of trials is at most 2^K divided by the probability that the human gives the recorded answers, and one draw is needed at every call of every run; for a human who varies at the questions asked, that probability falls exponentially with the number of recorded answers. This is the "very much compute" of the question. For the class of all programs a trial may not halt; the theorems then concern the mixture as a mathematical object, whose likelihood sums can be approximated from below and whose conditional probabilities can be approximated only in the limit (`proofs/rehearsal-core.md`, Remark 5.9).

None of this is new. Training a learner without regret on the inputs that its own behaviour produces is the reduction of Ross, Gordon and Bagnell (2011, Theorem 4.1); their algorithm returns the best iterate on validation, and they note that the guarantee also holds for one uniformly chosen iterate run for a whole episode. With logarithmic loss, a finite class with equal weights and a deterministic expert, rehearsal followed by the deployment of the mixture of a uniformly chosen round is the algorithm "Stagger" of Li and Zhang (2025). The note adds a prior on a countable class (bounds in K), a human who is not deterministic, and the uses and limits below.

## 3. What rehearsal guarantees

Throughout, A1 to A3 hold and the deployment is closed. Numbers refer to `proofs/rehearsal-core.md`.

### 3.1 Each call

**Theorem R1 (proved here; for a finite class with equal weights and a deterministic human, the bound on unfaithful calls is the estimation step of Li and Zhang 2025, Theorem 3).** Write KL_t(x) for the divergence KL(P(·|x) ‖ M^(t)(·|x)) in nats. Then

  Σ_{t=1}^{n} E[ Σ_{calls x of the run of round t} KL_t(x) ] ≤ L·K ln 2.

So the run of M^(T) has E[Σ_{calls x} KL(P(·|x) ‖ M^(T)(·|x))] ≤ L·K ln 2/n, and for a deterministic human E[β(M^(T))] ≤ L·K ln 2/n.

*Proof.* Let (x_i, y_i) be the pairs put to the human and ξ_i := Σ_ν w(ν)·ν(y₁|x₁)⋯ν(y_i|x_i), so ξ₀ ≤ 1. The mixture gives the answer it is about to see the probability M(y_i|x_i) = ξ_i/ξ_{i−1}, and the product of these over all pairs is at least w(P)·Π_i P(y_i|x_i); so Σ_i ln(P(y_i|x_i)/M(y_i|x_i)) ≤ K ln 2 almost surely. By A1 the answer of round t is a draw from P(·|x_t) given the past, so the summand of round t has conditional mean KL_t(x_t) ≥ 0 (finite and integrable: Lemma 4.1). Hence Σ_t E[KL_t(x_t)·1{round t asks}] ≤ K ln 2. By A3(i) and (v) the run of round t is a run of M^(t), and J is uniform and independent of it; so the term of round t is (1/L)·E[Σ_{calls x of that run} KL_t(x)]. By A3(ii) the second claim is the average over t. For a deterministic human a call at x is unfaithful with probability e := 1 − M^(t)(f(x)|x), and KL_t(x) = −ln(1 − e) ≥ e. ∎

The inequality is the standard regret bound for Bayes mixtures (Hutter 2003, Theorem 1; Solomonoff 2008 for question–answer pairs; for questions chosen from earlier pairs, Proposition 5.7(b) of Paper 1, see References).

*What it does not say.* It averages over runs: an input reached with probability below about L·K ln 2/n may be answered in any way (Theorem N1). For a deterministic human, it bounds the probability of an unfaithful run by less than 1 only for n above L·K ln 2, and the expected number of unfaithful calls by less than the trivial bound L only for n above K ln 2. Where it is empty the imitator need not resemble the human. A *table* of the data is a hypothesis that gives the recorded answers at the asked inputs and answers in some other way elsewhere. If the class contains the tables and the prior charges a table about b bits for each recorded question, the tables that fit outweigh the human until n·b exceeds K, and while they do the imitator answers an input not yet asked more as the tables do than as the human does (`proofs/assumptions.md`, Remark 3.2a).

K for a person under a generic prior is of the order of 10⁹ bits (Section 9.1), and there the theorem is empty. Already for K = 10⁴ bits, L = 10 and 0.1 unfaithful calls per run it asks for 6.9·10⁵ answers, which at one hour per answer are about 350 working years of 2000 hours. And a fixed number of questions buys a constant rate of unfaithful calls for as long as the imitator is used, not a bounded number.

* *Equality* is attained for the divergence and approached for the unfaithful calls (Theorem 5.1(d)).
* *The round.* T is drawn afresh for every run. If one T is drawn once and kept for all runs, then for a deterministic human the imitator's expected number of unfaithful calls exceeds 1/δ times the bound with probability at most δ, and for δ ≤ 0.63 no factor below 0.45/δ will do in general (Proposition 5.2).
* *The last round* cannot replace M^(T) in general: in an example the mixture after all n answers has a summed divergence of at least (L − 1)/8 times L·K ln 2/n (Proposition 6.2). It can for a deterministic human on a scaffold whose questions do not depend on the answers, with at most L·(K ln 2 + 1)/n unfaithful calls in expectation (Proposition 6.1).
* *A new round at every call* is a different imitator and is not covered: for a deterministic human its run is unfaithful at most L times as often, and about L/4 times as often in an example (Proposition 5.5).
* *Asking several calls of each rehearsal run* does not improve the bound: for n rounds it stays L·K ln 2/n, however many calls of a run are put to the human besides the one drawn in step 3, and in an example asking all L calls gives the same mixtures for L times as many answers (Proposition 5.3).

### 3.3 Coverage

**Theorem R7 (coverage; proved here).** Let X₀ be a set of inputs fixed before the protocol starts. The probability that the deployed run of M^(T) makes a call in X₀ although no question of rounds 1, …, T − 1 lay in X₀ is at most L/n; for N such sets, the probability that this happens for at least one of them is at most N·L/n. Nothing is assumed about the human or the class; of A3 only (i), (ii) and (v) are used. (Without A2 it can happen that no hypothesis gives the recorded answers positive probability, and the mixture of Section 1 is then not defined. Let M^(t) then be the mixture under the prior weights, or any other imitator computed from the data of the earlier rounds; the proof uses nothing else.)

*Proof.* If the run of round t has a call in X₀, the question of round t lies in X₀ with probability at least 1/L, because J is uniform given the run. The events "the first question in X₀ is asked in round t" are disjoint for different t. So the probabilities that the run of round t enters X₀ before any question lay in X₀ add up to at most L. Given the data before round t, the deployed run for T = t has the law of the run of round t; average over T. ∎

The argument is the random-stopping argument of the researcher's notes (see References), applied to the round T.

*In bits.* Fix a prefix-free language of programs before the protocol starts. At most 2^b of its programs have at most b bits. So with probability at least 1 − 2^b·L/n, the deployed run enters no set that a program of at most b bits decides unless a question of an earlier round lay in that set (`proofs/assumptions.md`, Corollary 3.2′). For probability 1 − δ, b may be as large as log₂(δ·n/L): programs of at most 9 bits for n = 10⁵, L = 10 and δ = 0.1. No choice of δ reaches beyond log₂(n/L) bits, here 13.

*What it does not say.* It holds for each fixed set, not for all sets at once: when inputs seldom repeat, the deployed run enters the set of inputs not yet asked with probability near 1. And it is about where questions fell, not about answers. That a question lay in X₀ says nothing about the imitator's answers elsewhere in X₀, because the human's answer to it tests each hypothesis at the asked input only. When the deployed run does enter X₀ unasked, the data do not tell apart two hypotheses that differ only inside X₀, and their weights relative to each other are still the prior's (see Theorem N1).

### 3.4 Low stakes

Fix a set G of good runs. The human system *tolerates k faults with failure δ* if, for every rule, randomised or not, that at each call sees the run so far and the human's answer and may replace that answer, and that replaces at most k answers in any run, the run is in G with probability at least 1 − δ. ("Low stakes" is the term of Christiano 2021; the definition by tolerated faults is this note's.)

**Theorem R3 (proved here).** If the human system tolerates k faults with failure δ, the run of an imitator A is in G with probability at least 1 − δ − β(A)/(k + 1). For M^(T), a deterministic human and k + 1 ≥ θL, the failure probability is at most δ + K ln 2/(θ·n), whatever L.

*Proof.* Stop the replacing rule of Section 1 after k replacements. The stopped rule is admissible, so its run is in G with probability at least 1 − δ. The two runs coincide unless the run of A has more than k unfaithful calls, which has probability at most β(A)/(k + 1) by Markov's inequality. ∎

*What it does not say.* It says nothing unless the human system itself tolerates faults, and that it does is a hypothesis which the protocol never tests. And the rule must see the human's answer: a system can survive 399 blindly replaced answers with failure 0.0007 and fail with probability 1 under an imitator for which δ + β/(k + 1) is 0.021 (Example 8.5(d)). "Whatever L" holds for a fixed fraction θ. If the tolerance comes from doing each critical step several times, θ is of the order of one over the number of critical steps, and the price grows with that number (N8, Section 9.1). The bound is in K: for a person under a generic prior, with K of the order of 10⁹ bits, it is empty, and so is the bound below for a human who is not deterministic. At θ = 0.1 an excess failure of 0.1 would take 7·10¹⁰ answers of a deterministic human (Section 9.1).

In the recoverable form (standard: Ross, Gordon and Bagnell 2011, Theorem 2.2) every run has a cost between 0 and 1. If one replaced answer followed by the human costs, in expectation and from every history, at most μ more than any answer the human might have given there, then the run of A costs, in expectation, at most μ·β(A) more than the human system's (Theorem 8.6).

For a human who is not deterministic, E[β(M^(T))] ≤ L·√(K ln 2/(2n)) (Proposition 9.1), so the failure probability is at most δ + √(K ln 2/(2n))/θ. The rate is right: for every n ≥ K/2 there are a scaffold and a class of 2^K members with equal weights for which every protocol that asks at most n questions in expectation leaves, for some member, at least 0.088·L·√(K/n) unfaithful calls in expectation (Theorem 9.2), and in an example failure γ ≤ ½ needs K·(1 − 2γ)²/(32θ²) questions (Proposition 9.3). The order is K/θ², against K/θ for a deterministic human.

Whether a human system tolerates faults can sometimes be read off a measure of progress that the human's answers raise on average and that no single answer can set back by much:

**Proposition R3a (bounded damage; proved here).** Let every run have exactly L calls, and let Φ be a real function of the history. Suppose that from every history, also those reached only after replaced answers, an answer of the human raises Φ by at least a > 0 in expectation and changes it by at most 1, and no answer lowers Φ by more than D. Fix ζ ∈ (0, 1] and call a run good if Φ rises by at least (1 − ζ)·a·L. Then the human system tolerates k faults with failure δ whenever

  k·(1 + D) + √(2L·ln(1/δ)) ≤ ζ·a·L,

and, for a deterministic human, with failure 0 whenever k·(1 + D) ≤ ζ·a·L.

*Idea.* Each replacement costs at most 1 + D, and the Azuma–Hoeffding inequality (Azuma 1967; Hoeffding 1963) controls the sum of the steps that the human's answers would have caused. Proof: Proposition 12.2, where a and ζ are μ and η.

So K ln 2·(1 + D)/(ζ·a·δ′) questions give an excess failure δ′ for a deterministic human, whatever L.

*What it does not say.* Without "exactly L calls" the statement is false: if a wrong answer can stop the run, one fault spoils it. The conditions are required from every history, and a run is good by the value of Φ alone. So Φ must be a quantity that a wrong answer cannot raise, and the proposition does not help to find one.

### 3.5 The certificate

**Theorem V1 (validation; proved here from standard inequalities).** Let A be a frozen answerer, however obtained. Assume A1 for the validation questions; A2 is not assumed. Validation has m rounds. A round whose run has fewer calls than the drawn J asks nothing; it stays in the count of the m rounds and records nothing.

(a) Let r be the probability that a round records a disagreement, and s_P the probability that a round would record one if a second, independent answer of the human stood in the place of A's answer at the asked call: s_P is the expected sum of 1 − Σ_y P(y|x)² over the calls x of a run of A, divided by L. Then β(A)/L ≤ r ≤ β(A)/L + s_P, for every human. So the rule "if no round records a disagreement, certify β(A) ≤ L·ln(1/δ)/m" certifies an imitator for which this is false with probability less than δ. With r̂ the recorded rate of disagreement, that is, the number of rounds that record one divided by m, β(A)/L ≤ r̂ + √(2r̂·ln(1/δ)/m) + 2·ln(1/δ)/m with probability at least 1 − δ. Theorem R3 applies with the certified β.

(b) A *test* is a set B of pairs (question, answer). Fix, before validation, countably many tests with weights π(B) of total at most 1, and put Λ(B) := ln(1/π(B)). Let Δ(B) be the expected number of calls of a run of A whose question and answer form a pair in B, minus the expected number of its calls at which the question and the human's answer would form a pair in B, divided by L. The *recorded difference* of B is the number of rounds in which the recorded answer of A forms a pair in B with the question, minus the number in which the human's answer does, divided by m. With probability at least 1 − δ, for all B at once, Δ(B) is within √(2·(Λ(B) + ln(2/δ))/m) of the recorded difference of B.

(c) Assume A2 and K ≤ K₀ for a known number K₀, and let A be a sampler. For each hypothesis ν with w(ν) ≥ 2^(−K₀) let B_ν := {(x, y) : A(y|x) > ν(y|x)}. With probability at least 1 − δ, β(A)/L is at most √(2·(K₀ ln 2 + ln(1/δ))/m) plus the largest of the recorded differences of these tests.

*Proof.* Theorem 13.1. For (a): given the run up to the asked call, the two recorded answers are independent with the laws a := A_j(·|h_j) and p := P(·|x_j). They differ with probability 1 − Σ_y a(y)p(y), which lies between TV(a, p) and TV(a, p) + 1 − Σ_y p(y)². The asked call is uniform among L, so r is 1/L times the expected sum of these probabilities over the calls of a run. The rounds are independent, so none of them records a disagreement with probability (1 − r)^m ≤ e^(−r·m), which is below δ if r > ln(1/δ)/m; the bound with r̂ is a Chernoff bound for the m rounds. For (b): the differences of the single rounds are independent with values in {−1, 0, 1} and mean Δ(B); apply Hoeffding's inequality (1963) with failure probability π(B)·δ to the test B and add up over the tests. For (c): at every input x the set of answers y with A(y|x) > P(y|x) has probability TV(A(·|x), P(·|x)) more under A than under P, so Δ(B_P) = β(A)/L, because A looks at the input only. By the one-sided form of Hoeffding's inequality, with failure probability w(ν)·δ for the test B_ν, with probability at least 1 − δ every Δ(B_ν) is at most the recorded difference of B_ν plus √(2·(ln(1/w(ν)) + ln(1/δ))/m). Under A2 and K ≤ K₀ the test B_P is one of those of (c), and ln(1/w(P)) = K ln 2 ≤ K₀ ln 2. ∎

*What it does not say.* The certificate of (a) is valid for every human, but it is issued with probability at most exp(−m·s_P/2): only if the human is nearly deterministic on the inputs visited. The exact imitator of a human who varies fails it. For such a human, without A2, one answer per input cannot certify that a randomised imitator has few unfaithful calls (Proposition 13.4); what can be certified is (b) and, with questions put twice, a squared distance of answer laws (Section 8.7). The bound of (c) contains K₀, and for a person it is as empty as R1. A test costs Λ(B), whatever its complexity; this is the point of outcome indistinguishability and multicalibration (Dwork et al. 2021; Hébert-Johnson et al. 2018). Each further attempt at validation adds δ.

For a deterministic human in the class, validation of M^(T) records no disagreement with probability at least 1 − m·K ln 2/n (Corollary 13.2). The certificate does not contain K: 300 rounds without a recorded disagreement certify, at δ = 0.05, an expected number of at most 0.01·L unfaithful calls per run, and so by R3 an excess failure of 0.1 if a tenth of the answers may be replaced (θ = 0.1). It certifies an imitator; it does not produce one.

### 3.6 Asking while the system runs

The idea of asking the human again with some probability can also be used while the system runs, with no rehearsal. If every call, as it arrives, is put to the human with probability q and answered by the current mixture otherwise, the expected divergence between human and current mixture, summed over all calls, is at most K ln 2/q, however the questions arise; for a deterministic human the mixture gives at most (1 − q)·K ln 2/q wrong answers in expectation, however long the system runs (S5, Section 6.2; here Theorem 10.1). This statement needs no closed deployment. With q = 1/L in a closed system it has the price of R1: r runs take at most r questions in expectation and have a summed divergence of at most L·K ln 2 in expectation (Corollary 10.2).

*What it does not say.* It does not say which calls are answered wrongly. And the human stays in the loop for as long as the system runs: the statement is about the system with the human in it, not about an imitator that could then be used alone.
