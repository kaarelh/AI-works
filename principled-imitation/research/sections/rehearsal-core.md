## 1. The model

**The human.** X and Y are the countable sets of input strings and answer strings. A *sampler* S gives every input x a probability distribution S(·|x) on Y. Assumption **A1**: the human is a sampler P, and a question x put to the human is answered by a draw from P(·|x), whatever was realised before. If every P(·|x) is a point mass, the human is *deterministic* and f denotes the function.

**The class.** H is a countable set of *hypotheses* ν with weights w(ν) > 0 of total at most 1. A hypothesis is a sampler, or a program that sometimes gives no answer, which then counts as one more answer. Assumption **A2**: P is in H. K := log₂(1/w(P)) counts the bits that the prior lacks about the human's answers to inputs the scaffold can produce (`proofs/rehearsal-core.md`, Remark 4.4). The *mixture* M_D answers a question x with a hypothesis drawn afresh from the posterior given the data D: M_D(y|x) := Σ_ν (posterior weight of ν)·ν(y|x). For the class of all programs it is Solomonoff's predictor for question–answer pairs (2008, equation (10)).

**The scaffold.** A *scaffold* Π draws a top-level input z from a law D₀ and random bits ρ, then computes each question x_j from z, ρ and the answers so far, or stops, after at most L questions (L is the *horizon*). One execution is a *run*; its questions are its *calls*. An *answerer* A gives the law A_j(·|h_j) of the j-th answer given the history h_j = (z, ρ, y₁, …, y_{j−1}); a sampler looks at x_j only. Q_A is the law of the run answered by A, and Q_P, the *human system*, is what is to be imitated.

**Unfaithful calls.** Couple a run of an imitator A with the human: at each call draw A's answer y_j and a *shadow* answer y′_j from P(·|x_j) jointly, so that they differ with the least possible probability, TV(A_j(·|h_j), P(·|x_j)); the run continues with y_j. The call is *unfaithful* if y_j ≠ y′_j, and β(A) is the expected number of unfaithful calls of a run. The run of A is then a run of the human system in which a rule that sees the human's answer has replaced the answers at the unfaithful calls. For a deterministic human a call is unfaithful exactly if y_j ≠ f(x_j), and a run without such calls is the human system's run on the same z and ρ (Lemma 3.2, Proposition 3.4).

**The protocol's coins.** Assumption **A3** has five parts. (i) The call put to the human is drawn uniformly after the run, independently of it. (ii) The round T is drawn independently of everything else, and the run that counts uses a fresh z and ρ and fresh draws of the imitator. (iii) Class and weights are fixed before any coin of the protocol or of the human is drawn. (iv) The human is shown the string that the imitator was shown, and nothing marks it as a question of the protocol. (v) Every rehearsal and validation run uses fresh coins.

**Closed deployment.** A deployment is *closed* if: (C1) given all that the protocol saw and did, the z and ρ of the run that counts are a fresh draw from the scaffold's laws; (C2) the runs of rehearsal and validation were generated by the protocol on fresh draws from the same laws; (C3) the answers of the run that counts depend on its history and on the imitator's coins alone: no clock, no mark that this run is the real one, no state of the world. The deployment is then a rehearsal run that happens to count. A fixed question worked on by a tree of calls is closed; a service that answers questions from a changing world is not (Section 6).

## 2. The protocol: rehearse, validate, deploy

**Rehearse.** For t = 1, …, n:

1. Let M^(t) be the mixture given the questions answered so far.
2. Run the scaffold to the end with M^(t) answering every call.
3. Draw J uniformly from {1, …, L}. If the run made at least J calls, put its J-th question to the human and add question and answer to the data.

**Validate.** Freeze an imitator A: fix it, and let it answer every run with fresh coins. For i = 1, …, m: run the scaffold with A, draw J as before, and if the run made at least J calls put its J-th question to the human; record the human's answer next to the answer that A gave in the run.

**Deploy.** Run the scaffold with the validated imitator. Without validation: for each run draw T uniformly from {1, …, n}, and let M^(T) answer the whole run.

The human answers at most n + m single calls and never carries out a run. The questions come from the imitator's own runs, at every depth of a decomposition.

None of this is new. Training a learner without regret on the inputs that its own behaviour produces is the reduction of Ross, Gordon and Bagnell (2011, Theorem 4.1), who also deploy one uniformly chosen iterate for a whole episode and return the best iterate on validation. With logarithmic loss, a finite class and a deterministic expert, the protocol is "Stagger" of Li and Zhang (2025). The note adds a prior on a countable class, so that the bounds are in K, a human who is not deterministic, and the uses and limits below.

## 3. What rehearsal guarantees

Throughout, A1 to A3 hold and the deployment is closed. Numbered references are to `proofs/rehearsal-core.md`.

### 3.1 Each call

**Theorem R1 (proved here; for a finite class and a deterministic human it is in Li and Zhang 2025).** Write KL_t(x) for KL(P(·|x) ‖ M^(t)(·|x)) in nats. Then

  Σ_{t=1}^{n} E[ Σ_{calls x of the run of round t} KL_t(x) ] ≤ L·K ln 2.

So the run of M^(T) has E[Σ_{calls x} KL(P(·|x) ‖ M^(T)(·|x))] ≤ L·K ln 2/n, and for a deterministic human E[β(M^(T))] ≤ L·K ln 2/n.

*Proof.* Let (x_i, y_i) be the pairs put to the human, in order, and ξ_i := Σ_ν w(ν)·ν(y₁|x₁)⋯ν(y_i|x_i), so ξ₀ ≤ 1. The mixture gives the answer it is about to see the probability ξ_i/ξ_{i−1}, and the product of these is ξ_n/ξ₀ ≥ w(P)·Π_i P(y_i|x_i); so Σ_i ln(P(y_i|x_i)/M(y_i|x_i)) ≤ K ln 2 almost surely. By A1 the answer of round t is a draw from P(·|x_t) given the past, so the summand of round t has conditional mean KL_t(x_t) ≥ 0 (finite and integrable: Lemma 4.1). Hence Σ_t E[KL_t(x_t)·1{round t asks}] ≤ K ln 2. By A3(i) and (v) the run of round t is a run of M^(t), and J is uniform and independent of it; so the term of round t is (1/L)·E[Σ_{calls x of that run} KL_t(x)]. By A3(ii) the second claim is the average over t. For a deterministic human a call at x is unfaithful with probability e := 1 − M^(t)(f(x)|x), and KL_t(x) = −ln(1 − e) ≥ e. ∎

The inequality used is the standard regret bound for Bayes mixtures (Hutter 2003, Theorem 1; Solomonoff 2008 for question–answer pairs; Paper 1 of this repository, Proposition 5.7(b), for questions chosen from the earlier pairs).

*What it does not say.* It averages over runs: an input reached with probability below about L·K ln 2/n may be answered in any way (Theorem N1). It is empty for n below L·K ln 2, and K for a person under a generic prior is of the order of 10⁹ bits (Section 9): at one hour per answer, L = 10 and 0.1 unfaithful calls per run cost 350 years already for K = 10⁴. And it is per run: a fixed number of questions buys a constant rate of unfaithful calls, not a bounded number.

* *Equality* is attained for the divergence and approached for the unfaithful calls (Theorem 5.1(d)).
* *The round.* A fresh T for every run gives every run the bound in expectation. One T kept for good exceeds it by the factor 1/δ with probability at most δ, and for δ ≤ 0.63 a factor 0.45/δ is needed (Proposition 5.2).
* *The last round* cannot replace M^(T) in general: in an example the mixture after all n answers has a summed divergence of at least (L − 1)/8 times L·K ln 2/n (Proposition 6.2). For a deterministic human on a scaffold whose questions do not depend on the answers it can, with L·(K ln 2 + 1)/n (Proposition 6.1).
* *A new round at every call* is a different imitator and is not covered: its run can be unfaithful up to L times as often, and about L/4 times in an example, "the ladder" (Proposition 5.5).
* *More questions per run* do not improve the bound (Proposition 5.3).

### 3.2 Whole runs

**Theorem R2 for a deterministic human (proved here).** The run of M^(T) is the human system's run on the same z and ρ with probability at least 1 − L·K ln 2/n (Theorem 7.1). Theorem N2 gives a class and a scaffold for which every protocol needs of the order of L/δ questions for failure δ.

### 3.3 Coverage

**Theorem R7 (coverage; proved here).** Let Q be a set of inputs fixed before the protocol starts. The probability that the deployed run of M^(T) makes a call in Q although no question of rounds 1, …, T − 1 lay in Q is at most L/n; for N such sets it is at most N·L/n. Nothing is assumed about the human or the class; only A3(i), (ii) and (v) are used.

*Proof.* If the run of round t has a call in Q, the question of round t lies in Q with probability at least 1/L, because J is uniform given the run. The events "the first question in Q is asked in round t" are disjoint for different t. So the probabilities that the run of round t enters Q before any question lay in Q add up to at most L. Given the data before round t, the deployed run for T = t has the law of the run of round t; average over T. ∎

*What it does not say.* It holds for each fixed set, not for all sets at once: when inputs seldom repeat, the deployed run enters the set of inputs not yet asked with probability near 1. Nor is the imitator right in Q after one question there.

### 3.4 Low stakes

Fix a set G of good runs. The human system *tolerates k faults with failure δ* if, for every rule that at each call sees the run so far and the human's answer and may replace that answer, and that replaces at most k answers in any run, the run is in G with probability at least 1 − δ. ("Low stakes" is Christiano's term.)

**Theorem R3 (proved here).** If the human system tolerates k faults with failure δ, the run of an imitator A is in G with probability at least 1 − δ − β(A)/(k + 1). For M^(T), a deterministic human and k + 1 ≥ θL, the failure probability is at most δ + K ln 2/(θ·n), whatever L.

*Proof.* Stop the replacing rule of Section 1 after k replacements. The stopped rule is admissible, and the two runs coincide unless the run of A has more than k unfaithful calls, which has probability at most β(A)/(k + 1) by Markov's inequality. ∎

*What it does not say.* Nothing, unless the human system itself tolerates faults. And the rule must see the human's answer: a system can survive 399 blindly replaced answers with failure 0.0007 and fail with probability 1 under an imitator for which δ + β/(k + 1) is 0.021 (Example 8.5(d)).

In the recoverable form (standard: Ross, Gordon and Bagnell 2011, Theorem 2.2), one replaced answer followed by the human raises the expected cost by at most μ, and the run of A then costs at most μ·β(A) more than the human system's (Theorem 8.6).

For a human who is not deterministic, E[β(M^(T))] ≤ L·√(K ln 2/(2n)) (Proposition 9.1), so the failure probability is at most δ + √(K ln 2/(2n))/θ. The rate is right: every protocol has at least 0.088·L·√(K/n) unfaithful calls in expectation for some member of a class of K bits (Theorem 9.2), and in an example failure γ ≤ ½ needs K·(1 − 2γ)²/(32θ²) questions (Proposition 9.3). The order is K/θ², against K/θ for a deterministic human.

**Proposition R3a (bounded damage; proved here).** Let every run have exactly L calls, and let Φ be a real function of the history. Suppose that from every history, also those reached only after replaced answers, an answer of the human raises Φ by at least a > 0 in expectation and changes it by at most 1, and no answer lowers Φ by more than D. Call a run good if Φ rises by at least (1 − σ)·a·L. Then the human system tolerates k faults with failure δ whenever

  k·(1 + D) + √(2L·ln(1/δ)) ≤ σ·a·L,

and, for a deterministic human, with failure 0 whenever k·(1 + D) ≤ σ·a·L.

A replacement costs at most 1 + D against the step that the human's answer would have caused, and the Azuma–Hoeffding inequality bounds the sum of those steps from below (Proposition 12.2, where a and σ are μ and η). So K ln 2·(1 + D)/(σ·a·δ′) questions give an excess failure δ′ for a deterministic human, whatever L. Without "exactly L calls" the statement is false: if a wrong answer can stop the run, one fault spoils it.

### 3.5 The certificate

**Theorem V1 (validation; proved here from standard inequalities).** Let A be a frozen answerer, however obtained. Assume A1 for the validation questions; A2 is not assumed. Run validation with m rounds.

(a) Let ρ be the probability that a round records a disagreement, and s the expected sum of 1 − Σ_y P(y|x)² over the calls x of a run of A, divided by L. Then β(A)/L ≤ ρ ≤ β(A)/L + s, for every human. So the rule "if no round records a disagreement, certify β(A) ≤ L·ln(1/δ)/m" certifies an imitator for which this is false with probability less than δ. With ρ̂ the recorded rate of disagreement, β(A)/L ≤ ρ̂ + √(2ρ̂·ln(1/δ)/m) + 2·ln(1/δ)/m with probability at least 1 − δ. Theorem R3 applies with the certified β.

(b) A *test* is a set B of pairs (question, answer). Fix, before validation, countably many tests with weights π(B) of total at most 1, and put Λ(B) := ln(1/π(B)). Let Δ(B) be the expected number of calls of a run of A whose answer lies in B, minus the expected number of its calls at which the human's answer would lie in B, divided by L. With probability at least 1 − δ, for all B at once, Δ(B) is within √(2·(Λ(B) + ln(2/δ))/m) of the difference of the two recorded frequencies.

(c) Under A2, for a sampler A and K ≤ K₀: with probability at least 1 − δ, β(A)/L is at most √(2·(K₀ ln 2 + ln(1/δ))/m) plus the largest recorded difference among the tests {(x, y) : A(y|x) > ν(y|x)} with w(ν) ≥ 2^(−K₀).

*Proof.* Theorem 13.1. For (a): the two recorded answers are independent with laws a and p, and differ with probability 1 − Σ_y a(y)p(y), which lies between TV(a, p) and TV(a, p) + 1 − Σ_y p(y)². For (b): Hoeffding's inequality and a union bound. ∎

*What it does not say.* The certificate of (a) is valid for every human, but it is issued with probability at most exp(−m·s/2): only if the human is nearly deterministic on the inputs visited. The exact imitator of a human who varies fails it. For such a human, without A2, single answers cannot certify that a randomised imitator has few unfaithful calls (Proposition 13.4; not independently checked); what can be certified is (b) and, with questions put twice, a squared distance of answer laws (Pr7, Section 8). A test costs Λ(B), whatever its complexity; this is the point of outcome indistinguishability and multicalibration (Dwork et al. 2021; Hébert-Johnson et al. 2018). Each further attempt at validation adds δ.

For a deterministic human in the class, validation of M^(T) records no disagreement with probability at least 1 − m·K ln 2/n (Corollary 13.2). The certificate does not contain K: 300 rounds certify an excess failure 0.1 under θ = 0.1 at δ = 0.05. It certifies an imitator; it does not produce one.

### 3.6 The online form

If every call, as it arrives, is put to the human with probability q and answered by the current mixture otherwise, the expected divergence between human and current mixture, summed over all calls, is at most K ln 2/q, however the questions arise (Theorem 10.1). With q = 1/L in a closed system this is the price of R1, but the human stays in the loop for as long as the system runs.
