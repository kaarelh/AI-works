## 8. The property idea

The researcher proposed that a good imitator keeps the properties the answers had so far, and objected: at a new input some such property must break. This section says what survives. In short, the objection stands: properties that held so far can contradict one another at a new input, and then no imitator keeps them all. What can be had is that each property of a list fixed in advance is kept about as often as the human keeps it, at the property's own price and not the human's; this is less than imitation. Numbers in Sections 8.1 to 8.3 refer to `proofs/property-batch.md`.

### 8.1 All properties at once

**Pr1** (standard: it is the definition of total variation). TV(Q_A, Q_P) ≤ ε holds exactly when every event of runs has, with A answering, a probability within ε of its probability in the human system. Keeping every property up to a small error is thus closeness in total variation, not a weaker goal. Under A2 its price is K, times the horizon L under rehearsal: for a deterministic human the deployed run is a run of the human system with probability at least 1 − L·K ln 2/n (R1); in general E TV ≤ √(2·L·K ln 2/n) (R2(b)); from N recorded runs of the human system, E TV ≤ √(K ln 2/(2N)) (H1). For a person, with K of the order of 10⁹ bits under a generic prior, these bounds are empty.

*What it does not say.* The error is additive. For an event whose probability in the human system is below ε, such as a rare bad one, the probability with the imitator is bounded only by that probability plus ε; it may be many times as large as with the human.

### 8.2 One property at a time

A *test* is a set B of runs; for a scaffold of one call it is a set of pairs (x, y). Fix in advance countably many tests with weights π(B) > 0 of total at most 1; Λ(B) := ln(1/π(B)) is the *price* of B. With π(B) = 2^(−length of a program for B), in a prefix-free language whose programs all halt, cheap tests are simple properties.

**Theorem Pr3** (standard: the Occam's razor bound; Langford 2005). Let τ₁, …, τ_N be independent runs with one law Q, let Q̂(B) be the fraction of them in B, and a(B) := (Λ(B) + ln(2/δ))/N. With probability at least 1 − δ, for all B at once,

  kl(Q̂(B) ‖ Q(B)) ≤ a(B),

where kl(p ‖ q) is the divergence KL between coins of bias p and q. On this event |Q̂(B) − Q(B)| ≤ √(a(B)/2), and Q(B) ≤ a(B) if B never occurred. Call a law Q′ of runs *matching* if kl(Q̂(B) ‖ Q′(B)) ≤ a(B) for all B, and call an imitator matching if the law of its runs is. On the same event Q is matching, and every matching Q′ has |Q′(B) − Q(B)| ≤ √(2a(B)) for all B, and Q′(B) ≤ a(B) if B never occurred.

*Proof.* N·Q̂(B) is binomial; the Chernoff bound in divergence form gives Pr[kl(Q̂(B) ‖ Q(B)) > a(B)] ≤ 2e^(−N·a(B)) = π(B)·δ; sum over B. Pinsker's inequality and kl(0 ‖ q) ≥ q give the rest (Theorem 2.3, Corollaries 2.4 and 2.6). ∎

That a property costs its own complexity whatever the source, and that a model passing all tests need not be close to the source, are central points of outcome indistinguishability and multicalibration (Dwork et al. 2021; Hébert-Johnson et al. 2018). Nothing is assumed about Q: there is no class, and the human may remember within a run. But the recorded runs must be independent with one law, so the human must not change, learn or remember from one run to the next; and the human must carry out N whole runs.

Theorem V1(b) of Section 3.5 is Pr3 used to *check* a given imitator, for the scaffold of one call that puts a uniformly chosen call of the imitator's run to the human (Theorem 6.1). It needs m single answers and no run of the human system. Its tests are tests of single calls, and it assumes A1 for the questions of validation.

*The researcher's objection, in this form.* Two properties that held in every recorded run may be such that, at an input of some new kind, no answer has both. Let the family contain the tests B₁, "the run breaks the first property", and B₂, "the run breaks the second". Neither occurred in the records, and every run that meets an input of the new kind lies in B₁ or in B₂. Pr3 does not ask an imitator to stay out of both tests. It asks that the imitator enter each about as often as the human system does, and this can be done, since the human system does it. On the event of Pr3 the human system enters B₁ with probability at most a(B₁) and B₂ with probability at most a(B₂), and so does every matching imitator; so a run of either meets an input of the new kind with probability at most a(B₁) + a(B₂) (Remark 5.5(c)). The conflict is thereby confined to rare kinds of input. It is not removed: the records do not show which property the human breaks at such inputs, and the tests leave open what an imitator does there. If a run of the human system meets a kind of input with probability q, an imitator that answers as the human does until the first input of that kind, and in any way from then on, is still matching with probability at least 1 − δ − N·q (Remark 5.5(a)). So on a kind of input much rarer than 1/N a matching imitator may do anything. Section 8.4 takes the objection up again for an imitator built from tests alone, while the human still answers every question: there a property that the human always keeps is broken at most log₂(1/π(B)) + 2 times in all, B being the test "the answer breaks the property" (Pr5(ii)); after the human has left, the conflict is again confined to rare kinds of question and not removed (Section 8.4, after Pr6).

*What it does not say.* It does not say that a matching imitator imitates (proved here: Propositions 5.2 and 5.4, Example 5.3).
* *The coin.* The scaffold makes one call, at an input drawn uniformly from u inputs. The human is a function f: X → {0, 1}, and the imitator is a fair coin. For all but a fraction δ of the functions f, every test B has |Q_coin(B) − Q_f(B)| ≤ √((Λ(B) + ln(2/δ))/(2u)); and if f is drawn uniformly at random, then after N recorded runs the coin is matching with probability at least 1 − δ − N²/(2u). Yet the coin differs from f with probability 1/2 at every call.
* *The same question twice.* The scaffold puts one uniform input to the answerer twice. The human is a fair coin at each call, and the imitator is a function g. For all but a fraction δ of the functions g, every test of single calls has the human's frequency up to the same error, while the test of runs "the two answers agree" has probability 1 against 1/2.

For N recorded runs there are thus two prices. All properties at once cost an error of order √(K/N) (H1; Pr4 below), which is empty for a person. Each test alone costs an error of order √(Λ(B)/N). Neither guarantee contains the other at its own price.

### 8.2a The batch estimator

This subsection belongs to the property idea because its estimate is chosen by the recorded frequencies of tests. The tests are made from the class: for each ordered pair of hypotheses, the set of runs that the first makes more likely than the second. The total variation between two hypotheses is the difference of their probabilities on one of these tests, so hypotheses that keep the recorded frequencies of these tests are close to one another on all events at once. The price of such a test is the weight of a hypothesis, and in this way K returns.

Let K_ν := log₂(1/w(ν)); under A2, K = K_P. The likelihood of a run under ν is the product of ν(y_j | x_j) over its calls. For ν ≠ ν′ let A_νν′ be the set of runs more likely under ν than under ν′, and let r(ν) := √((K_ν ln 2 + ln(2/δ))/(2N)), which is the error √(a(B)/2) of Pr3 for a test of weight w(ν). From N recorded runs, the estimate ν̂ minimises

  r(ν) + sup_{ν′ ≠ ν} max{0, Q_ν(A_νν′) − Q̂(A_νν′) − r(ν′)}.

A hypothesis ν is thus charged for its own description, and for producing the runs of a test A_νν′ more often than the records show them, beyond the error allowed to a test of weight w(ν′).

**Theorem Pr4** (proved here; minimum-distance estimation after Yatracos 1985). Let the N recorded runs be independent with one law Q, whatever Q is. With probability at least 1 − δ, for every ν ∈ H:

  TV(Q_ν̂, Q) ≤ 3·TV(Q_ν, Q) + √((2K_ν ln 2 + 2 ln(2/δ))/N).

*Idea.* TV(Q_ν, Q_ν′) = Q_ν(A_νν′) − Q_ν′(A_νν′), and Q̂ must be close to Q only on the sets that involve the hypothesis ν for which the right side is least. Proof: `proofs/property-batch.md`, Theorem 3.6. The estimate is the modified minimum-distance estimate of Mahalanabis and Štefankovič (2008), one-sided, with weights. For this estimate the factor 3 cannot be lowered, and it is optimal at confidence above 2/3 for all rules that return a member and whose number of runs does not grow with the space of runs (Bousquet, Kane and Moran 2019; Theorem 19 and Corollary 20 of arXiv version 5).

*To compute it* one needs a list of the hypotheses above a weight threshold (up to about e^(2N)); exact comparison of two likelihoods on a recorded run (possible for time-bounded programs, not for lower semicomputable ones); and the numbers Q_ν(A_νν′), hence a closed system or simulated runs (Theorem 3.10).

*What it does not say.* For a human with memory inside a run the bound can be empty: if the scaffold puts one question twice and the human answers with one fair bit both times, the human system is at total variation at least √2 − 1 from the run law of every sampler, and 3·(√2 − 1) > 1. The human must carry out N whole runs. And for a person the bound is empty, as in Section 8.1: its right side is below 1 only for a hypothesis ν whose runs are within total variation 1/3 of the human system's, and then only if N exceeds 2·K_ν ln 2; for the human's own index, if the human is in the class, K_ν = K is of the order of 10⁹ bits under a generic prior.

### 8.5 The mixture need not give the per-property price

Numbers in Sections 8.6 and 8.7 refer to `proofs/property-batch.md`.

This subsection asks whether the Bayes mixture of Section 1 keeps a property that the human always keeps, at the price of the property and not of the human. It does for the inputs already seen, and in general it does not at a *fresh* input, that is, an input drawn independently of the questions asked so far.

Let R be a *property*: a set of pairs (x, y). An answer y to x *violates* R if (x, y) is not in R; giving no answer is not a violation. (A test B of Sections 8.2 to 8.5 is a set that a run or a pair may enter. R is the set that a pair must not leave: "the answer violates R" is the test formed by the pairs outside R.) In Pr2(b), in "What holds" and in Pr2(c) the human never violates R; Pr2(a) asks this of the data only.

**Pr2(a), hindsight** (proved here; Theorem 4.2, Corollary 4.3). *For the inputs already seen, the price of R is enough.* Suppose that the class is *closed under redrawing with the constant C_R*: with each hypothesis ν it contains ν^R, which answers as ν does but redraws until the answer does not violate R (a draw that gives no answer ends the redrawing with no answer), and the hypotheses which this operation sends to one member weigh together at most C_R times that member. The class of all programs of a universal prefix machine that simulates every prefix machine after a fixed prefix is closed in this way for every decidable R, with C_R = 2^(K(R)+c), where K(R) is the length of a shortest program that decides R and c is a constant of the machine (Remark 4.4). Take any data without a violation of R to which the mixture gives positive probability, and for each hypothesis ν let p_ν be the probability that independent answers of ν, one at each input seen, do not violate R. Then the posterior mean of 1/p_ν is at most C_R. There are two consequences. If the inputs seen are asked again, the mixture violates R at most ln C_R times in expectation. And at every input, seen or not, the mixture violates R with probability at most 1 − 1/C_R. For all programs this proves the claim of the researcher's notes that a property is kept with probability at least 2^(−C(R)), C(R) being the complexity of the property, in this reading: for data that keep the property, up to the constant c, and with no answer counted as keeping the property.

*What it does not say.* It does not say that the mixture answers well at a fresh input, and the bound 1 − 1/C_R does not improve as the data grow. The remaining probability 1/C_R may be the probability of no answer: in one example with C_R = 5 the mixture violates R with probability 0.71, gives no answer with probability 0.18 and gives an answer that keeps R with probability 0.11 (Remark 4.14; for samplers that always answer see Proposition 4.15).

**Pr2(b), a fresh input** (proved here; Theorem 4.5). *At a fresh input the price of R is not enough: there is a class in which the mixture gives a forbidden answer with probability above 0.51 for about the first K/log₂(4u + 2) questions, u being the number of inputs.* Let there be u ≥ 5 inputs, let b be an answer that the human never gives, and let R be "the answer is not b". The human is a function f with answers in {0, 1} and weight 2^(−K), K ≥ 2. The other hypotheses are the *memorisers*: a memoriser holds a table of answers in {0, 1} for some of the inputs and gives the answer b at every other input. Its weight is proportional to (4u)^(−size of the table), and together the memorisers have weight 1 − 2^(−K). After n questions at distinct inputs the mixture answers b, at an input drawn uniformly and independently of the questions, with probability

  ((u − n)/u)·(2u/(2u + 1))/(1 + (4u + 2)^n/(2^K − 1)),

and with probability above 0.51 after any n ≤ min{u/4, (K − 2)/log₂(4u + 2)} questions, distinct or not. This class is not closed under redrawing. If every memoriser shares its weight, in the ratio C_R − 1 to 1, with a twin that gives no answer where the memoriser answers b, the class is closed under redrawing with the constant C_R, and the value is multiplied by 1 − 1/C_R (Corollary 4.6). The level of the violation is the prior probability that a memoriser violates R off its table, which this class sets to 1: the statement is a fact about the prior.

**What holds** (proved here; Theorem 4.7, Proposition 4.9). Assume A1 and A2, and let the questions be drawn independently from the law from which the fresh input is drawn. With probability at least 1 − δ, for all n at once, the mixture after n questions violates R at a fresh input with probability at most (K ln 2 + ln(1/δ))/n. Under rehearsal (A1 to A3) a deployed run has at most L·K ln 2/n calls that violate R, in expectation. The price in both bounds is K and not the price of R, and for a person both are empty.

**Pr2(c), the machine** (proved here; Theorem 4.11(B), Remark 4.4). *The class of all programs does not prevent this on every universal machine.* Let U be a universal prefix machine as in Pr2(a), and let ℓ ≥ 5 and d ≥ 3 be integers. There is another machine of the same kind on which every program of U runs behind a prefix of ℓ bits, and on which the property R of Pr2(b) is decided by a program of 6 bits and the class of all programs is closed under redrawing with a constant C_R that depends neither on ℓ nor on d. Yet on this machine, for every human with answers in {0, 1} on u = 2^d inputs, the mixture violates R with probability above 0.51 at an input drawn uniformly and independently of the questions, after any n ≤ min{u/4, (ℓ − 5)/(d + 2)} questions. (In Theorem 4.11 the letter ℓ is c.) So no bound on this probability that is a function of the price of R, of C_R, of δ and of n alone, nondecreasing in the first two and tending to 0 as n grows, holds on all universal machines of this kind. The machine is built for the purpose, and the statement is again a fact about the prior. *Open:* whether, for one fixed universal machine, the first bound of "What holds" is true with c_U + K(R) in the place of K, where c_U is a constant of that machine, for every decidable R that the human never violates.

### 8.6 What the human's answers can certify

A *procedure with m questions* puts m questions to the human, each chosen in view of the earlier answers and of its own coins, and then accepts or rejects. Tests are here sets B of pairs (x, y) with weights π(B) and prices Λ(B) as in Section 8.2. For a law μ on inputs, the *frequency* of B under a sampler S is the probability that (x, y) lies in B when x is drawn from μ and y from S(· | x).

**Theorem Pr7** (proved here; the observation is that of Dwork et al. 2021). Let P₀ be a sampler, and let F be a random function with independent values F(x) ∼ P₀(· | x); the human F answers F(x) at x and is deterministic.

(a) *One answer per input.* At every input x the human F is at total variation 1 − P₀(F(x) | x) from P₀. Every procedure with m questions accepts the human F, on average over F, as often as it accepts the human P₀, up to the probability that it puts some input to P₀ twice. And with probability at least 1 − δ over F, every test B has under F a frequency within √(Σ_x μ(x)²·(Λ(B) + ln(2/δ))/2) of its frequency under P₀.

(b) *Answers from a large set.* Let P₀(· | x) be uniform on a set of s answers, s even. Consider the human who answers at each input uniformly on a random half of that set, the halves at different inputs being drawn independently. This human is at total variation 1/2 from P₀ at every input, and every procedure with m questions, which may repeat inputs at will, accepts this human, on average over the halves, as often as it accepts P₀, up to m²/(2s).

*Idea.* Answer a random function lazily: each first-time question gets a fresh draw from P₀. Proof: `proofs/property-batch.md`, Theorems 6.2 and 6.6.

So no procedure certifies, for every human who may vary, that an imitator's answer distributions are close to the human's: not with one answer per input (a), and not with repeated questions when the human may spread the answers over far more than m² strings (b). What such answers do estimate is frequencies of tests.

*What it does not say.* It does not say the same for repeated questions when few answers are possible; A1 allows a question to be put twice, and the two answers are then independent draws from P(· | x). Let the imitator A be a sampler whose answer distributions A(· | x) can be computed. Two answers y₁, y₂ of the human to one question x give the collision statistic

  1[y₁ = y₂] − A(y₁ | x) − A(y₂ | x) + Σ_y A(y | x)²,

whose mean is Σ_y (P(y | x) − A(y | x))² (after Goldreich and Ron 2000; Batu et al. 2000). Now validate A as in Section 2 with m rounds, but put each question to the human twice, and let ĉ be the average of the statistic over the m rounds, a round that asks nothing counting 0. If at every input the answers of the human and of A lie in a common set of at most q answers, then with probability at least 1 − δ

  β(A) ≤ (L/2)·√(q·(ĉ + (5/2)·√(ln(1/δ)/(2m))))

(Proposition 6.4). This is a certificate for a human who varies, valid without A2, and Theorem R3 applies with it. Its rate is slow: for a perfect imitator it certifies β(A)/L only to about √q·m^(−1/4), against ln(1/δ)/m for a deterministic human in V1(a).
