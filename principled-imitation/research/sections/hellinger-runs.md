### 3.2 Whole runs

For a deterministic human Theorem R1 settles whole runs: the run of M^(T) is the human system's run on the same (z, ρ), except with probability at most L·K ln 2/n (`proofs/rehearsal-core.md`, Theorem 7.1). For a human who varies, the laws of the runs are compared.

**Lemma L4 (from calls to runs; proved here; with 7 in the place of 4, part (a) is Lemma D.2 of Foster, Han, Qian and Rakhlin 2024).** For two distributions p and q on a countable set let H²(p, q) := 1 − Σ_y √(p(y)·q(y)) be their *squared Hellinger distance*; for the laws of runs the sum is over runs. With this normalisation, which the whole note uses, 0 ≤ H² ≤ 1, H²(p, q) ≤ KL(p ‖ q)/2 and TV(p, q) ≤ √(2·H²(p, q)). Let A and B be answerers on a scaffold. Let H²_j := H²(A_j(·|h_j), B_j(·|h_j)) be the squared Hellinger distance between the distributions from which they draw the j-th answer of a run, given its history h_j, and let σ := E_{Q_A}[Σ_j H²_j], the sum being over the calls of a run of A. Then

(a) H²(Q_A, Q_B) ≤ 4σ;
(b) TV(Q_A, Q_B) ≤ min{1, σ + √(σ² + 2σ)} ≤ 2√σ;
(c) Q_A(G) ≤ 2·Q_B(G) + 4σ for every set G of runs, and Q_A(G) ≤ 2σ if Q_B(G) = 0.

*Proof.* Follow a run of A, and let Ψ be the square root of the likelihood ratio of B to A along it: Ψ_0 := 1 and Ψ_j := Ψ_{j−1}·√(B_j(y_j|h_j)/A_j(y_j|h_j)). Write Ψ, without an index, for its value after the last call of the run. The scaffold contributes the same factors to the probability of a run under both answerers, so Ψ² is the probability of the run under Q_B divided by its probability under Q_A. Hence, with all means taken over the run of A,

  E[Ψ] = 1 − H²(Q_A, Q_B),  E[max(1 − Ψ², 0)] = TV(Q_A, Q_B),  E[Ψ²·1_G] ≤ Q_B(G),

where 1_G is 1 on the runs in G and 0 on the others. Given h_j, the factor √(B_j(y_j|h_j)/A_j(y_j|h_j)) has mean Σ_y √(A_j(y|h_j)·B_j(y|h_j)) = 1 − H²_j, and its second moment is at most 1: it is the probability that B_j gives to the answers that A_j can give.

*One step.* Let m > 0 and c ≥ 0 be numbers and v ≥ 0 a random variable with E[v²] ≤ 1, so that E[v] ≤ 1. Then

  E[max(m − c·v, 0)²] ≤ max(m − c, 0)² + 2m²·(1 − E[v]).

If c ≤ m, the left side is at most E[(m − c·v)²] ≤ m² − 2mc·E[v] + c² = (m − c)² + 2mc·(1 − E[v]), and 2mc ≤ 2m². If c > m, then max(m − c·v, 0) ≤ m·max(1 − v, 0) and E[(1 − v)²] ≤ 2·(1 − E[v]), so the left side is at most 2m²·(1 − E[v]).

Apply this at call j, given h_j, with c = Ψ_{j−1} and with v the factor of that call, and add up along the run. For every m ≥ 1 this gives

  E[max(m − Ψ, 0)²] ≤ (m − 1)² + 2m²·σ.

*Part (a).* For every number u ≥ 0, ½·max(2 − u, 0)² ≥ 3/2 − u: for u ≤ 2 the difference is ½·(u − 1)², and for u > 2 the right side is negative. With m = 2, therefore, ½ + H²(Q_A, Q_B) = 3/2 − E[Ψ] ≤ ½·E[max(2 − Ψ, 0)²] ≤ ½ + 4σ.

*Part (b).* For m > 1 and u ≥ 0, max(m − u, 0)² ≥ (m² − 1)·max(1 − u², 0): for u ≤ 1 the difference is (1 − m·u)². So TV(Q_A, Q_B) ≤ ((m − 1)² + 2m²·σ)/(m² − 1). With m := 1 + σ + √(σ² + 2σ) the right side is σ + √(σ² + 2σ) (for σ = 0 let m tend to 1). This is at most 2√σ as long as σ ≤ ¼, and for σ > ¼ the bound 1 is below 2√σ.

*Part (c).* For κ > 1 and u ≥ 0, max(1 − u, 0)² ≥ (1 − 1/κ)·(1 − κ·u²): for u ≤ 1 the difference is (1 − κ·u)²/κ, and for u > 1 the right side is negative. So, by the last display with m = 1, Q_A(G) − κ·Q_B(G) ≤ E[(1 − κ·Ψ²)·1_G] ≤ (κ/(κ − 1))·E[max(1 − Ψ, 0)²] ≤ 2κ·σ/(κ − 1). For κ = 2 this is the first claim. If Q_B(G) = 0 it holds for every κ > 1, and 2κ·σ/(κ − 1) tends to 2σ as κ grows. ∎

The same proof, with every step for runs of unequal length and for laws of z and ρ that are not discrete, is in `proofs/hellinger-runs.md` (Corollaries 2.3 and 2.11, from Theorem 2.1 and Lemma 2.10).

*The constants.* Foster, Kakade, Qian and Rakhlin (2021, Lemma A.13) prove part (a) with 100·log L in the place of the constant, L being the number of steps. The constant 4 can be lowered to 2.9642 by a longer argument of the same kind, and not below 2.9639: for an explicit pair of samplers, evaluated in 30-digit arithmetic, H²(Q_A, Q_B) is 2.96399·σ (Theorem 2.7 and Example 2.8 there). Parts (b) and (c) have the constants that the same bounds have for a single call, (b) to first order in σ (Remark 2.12 there).

*What it does not say.* The lemma goes from calls to runs. In the other direction σ ≤ 2L·H²(Q_A, Q_B), and the factor L cannot be replaced by a constant: in an example σ ≥ (L − 1)·H²(Q_A, Q_B) (Proposition 2.5 there). So run laws that are close do not make the single calls close.

**Theorem R2 (whole runs; proved here).** Assume A1 to A3 and put s := L·K ln 2/(2n). In expectation over the rehearsal and T:

(a) H²(Q_{M^(T)}, Q_P) ≤ 2·L·K ln 2/n;
(b) TV(Q_{M^(T)}, Q_P) ≤ min{1, s + √(s² + 2s)} ≤ √(2·L·K ln 2/n); by the first of the two bounds it is at most ε once n ≥ (1 + ε)·L·K ln 2/ε²;
(c) Q_{M^(T)}(G) ≤ 2·Q_P(G) + 2·L·K ln 2/n for each set G of runs fixed in advance, and Q_{M^(T)}(G) ≤ L·K ln 2/n if the human system never produces a run in G.

If neither the questions of the scaffold nor their number depend on the answers, then, in the same expectation, KL(Q_P ‖ Q_{M^(T)}) ≤ L·K ln 2/n and TV(Q_{M^(T)}, Q_P) ≤ √(L·K ln 2/(2n)).

*Proof.* Fix the data and T, and let σ be the sum of Lemma L4 for A = M^(T) and B = P. At every call H²_j ≤ KL(P(·|x_j) ‖ M^(T)(·|x_j))/2, so Theorem R1 gives E[σ] ≤ s. Parts (a) and (c) of the lemma are linear in σ and pass to the expectation as they stand, G being fixed in advance. For (b), the function u ↦ min{1, u + √(u² + 2u)} is non-decreasing and concave; so by Jensen's inequality the mean of its value at σ is at most its value at E[σ], and that is at most its value at s. And u + √(u² + 2u) = ε exactly at u = ε²/(2(1 + ε)). In the last case the questions of a run have the same law under every answerer; so the sum of divergences of Theorem R1, taken along the run of M^(T), has the same mean along the run of the human system, where it is KL(Q_P ‖ Q_{M^(T)}) by the chain rule. Pinsker's inequality, with Jensen's inequality for the square root, gives the total variation. (In full: `proofs/hellinger-runs.md`, Theorem 3.1.) ∎

*What it does not say.* R2 bounds expectations over the rehearsal and T. For one imitator M^(T) kept for good, Markov's inequality gives (a) to (c) with δ·n in the place of n, with probability at least 1 − δ. R2 concerns one run: r independent runs of one imitator M^(T) are, in expected total variation, within √(2r·L·K ln 2/n) of r runs of the human system. For a person under a generic prior, with K of the order of 10⁹ bits (Section 9.1), R2 is empty.

**The factor L under the root is necessary** (N6, Section 4.5). Here a *protocol* is any way of choosing questions, adaptive and randomised, followed by any imitator built from the answers (Section 4). By N6 there are a class of 2^K samplers with equal weights and a scaffold that puts one question, drawn uniformly from K questions, L times, for which every protocol needs n ≥ L·K/(8192·ε²) questions if the expected total variation between the run law of its imitator and Q_P is to be at most ε for every member (ε ≤ 1/64). Two variants are proved in `proofs/hellinger-runs.md`. A class of two samplers already needs more than L/(740·ε²) answers before that expected total variation is below ε for both (Proposition 3.5 there; L ≥ 3 odd, ε ≤ 1/12): the factor L is needed already for a class of one bit. And Proposition 3.4 there is N6 with other constants on a scaffold that asks each of K questions L/K times. A run of L calls is a sample of size L from the human, and such a sample shows a difference of order 1/√L per call; the factor has nothing to do with errors that add up along a run.

### 5.2 Recorded runs of the human system

If the human can carry out whole runs, N independent runs of the human system can be recorded; the posterior given the runs is the posterior given their question–answer pairs.

**Theorem H1 (a posterior draw for each run; standard).** Let the recorded runs be independent with a common law Q*; under A1, Q* = Q_P. To imitate a run, draw t uniformly from {1, …, N}, draw ν from the posterior given the first t − 1 runs, and let ν answer every call. Let Q̂ be the law of this run given the records. Then, for every ν in H,

  E[KL(Q* ‖ Q̂)] ≤ ln(1/w(ν))/N + KL(Q* ‖ Q_ν).

Under A1 and A2, E[KL(Q_P ‖ Q̂)] ≤ K ln 2/N and E[TV(Q̂, Q_P)] ≤ √(K ln 2/(2N)), and a set of runs that the human system never produces has expected probability at most K ln 2/N under Q̂.

This is the progressive mixture rule (Catoni 1997; Yang 2000) applied to runs. For a finite class of densities with equal weights, the expected divergence of the true density from the average of the Bayes predictive densities after 0, 1, …, N − 1 observations exceeds its divergence from the best member by at most the logarithm of the size of the class, divided by N (Juditsky, Rigollet and Tsybakov 2008, Corollary 5.4).

*Idea.* The regret bound behind Theorem R1, with whole runs in the place of question–answer pairs; then the convexity of the divergence in its second argument, Pinsker's inequality and, for a set G of runs that Q* never produces, Q̂(G) ≤ −ln(1 − Q̂(G)) ≤ KL(Q* ‖ Q̂). Proof: `proofs/hellinger-runs.md`, Theorem 4.2.

*What it does not say.* The imitator is not a sampler: one hypothesis answers every call of a run, so the calls of a run must share a seed. The bound is for the uniformly drawn t, not for the posterior after all N runs: the expected divergence is not monotone in the number of runs (Example 4.4 there). Whether the last posterior alone has such a bound was posed as an open problem by Grünwald and Kotłowski (2011); a brief search found no solution.

**The per-call mixture fails here** (proved here). If every call is answered by a fresh posterior draw, as the committee of Section 5.1 answers, the statement of H1 is false. Let z be uniform on 2N values and let the L calls of a run be (z, 1), …, (z, L). The human is f ≡ 0, with weight ½; for each value of z, the function that answers 1 at its L calls only has weight 1/(4N): K = 1, and whatever the records, the divergence of Q_P from the imitator's run law is at least L/(8N), against ln 2/N (so the bound of H1 fails once L ≥ 6). A hypothesis that is wrong on a whole run spoils few runs when it is drawn once for the run, and up to L times as many when it is drawn afresh at each call. The loss is at most the factor L if the human is deterministic or the questions do not depend on the answers; otherwise it is open (`proofs/hellinger-runs.md`, Section 4.3).

**Theorem H2 (one program for good; proved here).** Assume A1 and A2, let λ > 1, and compute the posterior from the N recorded runs with the weights w(ν)^λ. For 0 < ε ≤ 1 and every s in [1/λ, 1), the expected posterior weight of the hypotheses ν with H²(Q_ν, Q_P) ≥ ε² is at most

  2^(λsK)·(1 − ε²)^(2·min(s, 1−s)·N),

and so is the probability that such a ν has at least the posterior weight of P; H² is the squared Hellinger distance of Lemma L4. With λ = 2 and s = ½: after (K ln 2 + ln(1/δ))/ε² runs, a program drawn once from the posterior, or the one of largest posterior weight, has TV(Q_ν, Q_P) < √2·ε with probability at least 1 − δ.

*Idea.* The posterior weight of ν is at most the s-th power of its posterior odds against P. By Hölder's inequality the s-th power of the likelihood ratio of ν to P has, for one run, a mean of at most (1 − H²(Q_ν, Q_P))^(2·min(s, 1−s)) under Q_P, and the runs are independent. Summed over ν, the s-th powers of the prior odds are at most 2^(λsK), because Σ_ν w(ν)^(λs) ≤ 1 when λs ≥ 1. As s must be below 1, this is where λ > 1 is needed. Proof: `proofs/hellinger-runs.md`, Theorem 5.1.

The weights w^λ with λ > 1 are the steeper prior that the researcher's notes propose, for hypotheses that answer every call with fresh coins, in order to obtain the sampler and not the sampler with its coins filled in; Paper 2 (see References) studies it for induction on question–answer pairs, and Section 5.1 has its use after rehearsal (R6(c)). A rate in probability for λ > 1 is due to Barron and Cover (1991, Theorem 4, as Zhang 2006 reports it); for a finite class with equal weights the proof of H2, at λ = 1, gives Proposition 2.1 of Foster, Block and Misra (2024) for maximum likelihood (`proofs/hellinger-runs.md`, Remark 5.3).

*What it does not say.* H2 makes the law of each single run on this scaffold close, and no more: single calls are close only up to the factor L (Lemma L4), nothing is said about inputs that the human system never visits, and many runs together are not close (for r runs the bound on the total variation is √(2r)·ε, and the laws of r runs separate as r grows unless the two run laws are equal).

**At λ = 1 the statement fails** (proved here; the phenomenon is known). Let the human be a fair coin with weight ½, so that K = 1, let the class contain every table from {0,1}^d to {0,1} with weight 2^(−2^d−1), and let the scaffold ask one uniform input. Until an input repeats, the tables that fit the records have, taken together, the posterior weight of the coin; so a posterior draw is a table, at H² = 0.29 from the coin, with probability at least ¼ for all N ≤ 2^(d/2), however large d is (`proofs/hellinger-runs.md`, Example 5.4). A table is the human with the coins filled in: at each input it gives one answer that the human might give. The phenomenon is known (Zhang 2006, Section 4.4, after Barron, Schervish and Wasserman 1999); H1 is not affected. Two forms hold in expectation, also for a human outside the class. For a draw from the posterior with the weights w and the likelihood raised to the power ½ (Zhang 2006, Theorem 5.1), and for the program of largest posterior weight with the weights w² (his Theorem 4.1), the chosen program ν̂ has E[H²(Q_ν̂, Q*)] ≤ ln(1/w(ν))/N + ½·KL(Q* ‖ Q_ν) for every ν in H (`proofs/hellinger-runs.md`, Theorems 5.5 and 5.7).

**Cost.** This route needs N whole runs carried out by the human: it suits small scaffolds only, and none when one answer is a year of work. It saves no answers beyond a constant factor: rehearsal with n = N·L rounds has the same bound on the total variation when the questions do not depend on the answers (the last case of R2), and twice it otherwise (R2(b)); and the lower bound N6 (Section 4.5) counts the human's answers however they are obtained, recorded runs included. For a person under a generic prior (K of the order of 10⁹ bits, Section 9.1) both theorems are empty.
