## 0. The question, and the answers in brief

**The question.** A human is modelled as a sampler P: given an input string x, the human returns a random string y drawn from P(·|x), afresh each time. We may put questions to the human for a while. Then the human leaves, and a sampler built from the answers takes the human's place inside a larger computation: a *scaffold* that splits a task into pieces and calls the human many times, writing later inputs from earlier answers. Compute is free; answers from the human are not. We want a protocol, and a theorem saying that the computation behaves with the imitator as it would with the human, although the imitator's inputs are then written by the imitator itself; or at least that it fails only in ways that show. Ideally the imitator would be a copy of the human, and not a predictor of the human that also reasons about the world.

**In short.** Asking the human again on the inputs that the imitator's own runs produce works, with a theorem, for a computation that can be run in advance, and all the asking can be done beforehand; in its core this is a known algorithm. The price is of the order of L·K/δ answers, where 2^(−K) is the prior weight of the human, L the number of calls of a run and δ the failure probability, and in the worst case no protocol does much better. For a person, under a prior that does not already know the person, K is of the order of 10⁹ bits, and every bound on imitation in this note is empty. What can be afforded is a guarantee for each simple property that the human keeps; a check of a given imitator, for a human who is nearly deterministic; and imitation under a prior that already holds the human within a few thousand bits. Nothing here says how to obtain such a prior. The imitator is not a copy of the person, and its failures are not marked. A rule that marks its failures can be had on the rehearsed system, and with a small rate of unmarked errors on batches of new inputs, but not for new inputs as they arrive. Most statements are instances or variants of published results; Section 12 says which.

**The answers.**

1. **A protocol with a theorem exists for a computation that can be run in advance** (Sections 2 and 3). Fix a countable class of candidate samplers with prior weights. Their *Bayes mixture* answers a question by the candidates' answer distributions, each weighted by its prior weight and by the probability that it gave to the answers recorded so far; for the class of all programs it is the Solomonoff predictor for question–answer pairs. *Rehearse:* run the scaffold with the current mixture giving every answer; choose one call of that run uniformly at random and put it to the human; update the mixture; after n rounds deploy the mixture of a uniformly chosen round. This is the idea of asking the human again, with some probability, on the inputs that task decomposition produces, with all the asking done beforehand. Suppose that the human is in the class with prior weight 2^(−K) and that a run makes at most L calls. Then the divergence between the human's and the mixture's answer distributions, added up along the mixture's own run and then over all rounds, is at most L·K ln 2 in expectation (Theorem R1). So for a human who always gives the same answer to the same question, the deployed run is exactly the run that the human would have produced, except with probability at most L·K ln 2/n; and for any human the law of the deployed run is within √(2·L·K ln 2/n) in total variation, in expectation, of the law of the run that the human would produce (Theorem R2). The shift that decomposition produces costs nothing: the asked call is an unbiased sample of the calls that a deployed run makes, whatever the scaffold does with the answers (S4(i), Section 6.1). The human answers n single questions and is not needed again. For a person under a generic prior both bounds are empty (answer 10). For a finite class and a deterministic human, protocol and bound are known: it is the algorithm "Stagger" of Li and Zhang (2025), an instance of the reduction of Ross, Gordon and Bagnell (2011). The note adds the human who varies, a guarantee and a check that need no class, and the limits.

2. **A guarantee and a check assume nothing about the class or the prior** (Sections 3.3 and 3.5). *Coverage* (R7): for any region of inputs fixed in advance, the deployed run enters the region without the human ever having been asked a question in it with probability at most L/n, whatever the human is. In the researcher's terms this is what rehearsal does about the guard, the hypothesis that answers as the human does on everything seen and otherwise on inputs with a property that nothing seen had. Each property fixed in advance is met unasked with probability at most L/n; so with probability at least 1 − δ no property that a program of at most log₂(δn/L) bits decides is met unasked, the programs being those of a prefix-free language fixed in advance (Section 3.3). What the imitator does at an input that is met unasked is the prior's choice (N1), and one question in a region says nothing about the other inputs of the region. *Certificate* (V1): freeze an imitator, however it was obtained, and compare it with the human on m further calls drawn from its own runs. If no disagreement is recorded then, at confidence 1 − δ, a run of the imitator has in expectation at most L·ln(1/δ)/m *unfaithful* calls, that is, calls at which its answer differs from the answer that the human would have given. Such a certificate can be obtained only if the human nearly always gives the same answer on those inputs. For a human who varies, what can be certified without assuming a class is weaker: how often answers have each of a weighted list of properties and, if questions are put twice, a squared distance between the answer distributions (Pr7, Section 8.7).

3. **When a fixed fraction of the answers may be replaced without harm, the price does not grow with the length of the computation** (Section 3.4). If the human system reaches a good outcome even when any θ·L of its answers are replaced, then n = K ln 2/(θδ) questions keep the added failure probability below δ for a deterministic human, whatever L is (R3). For a human who varies the bound is √(K ln 2/(2n))/θ, and an example needs of the order of K/θ² questions. That the human system has this tolerance is assumed and never tested by the protocol. And where the tolerance comes from doing each critical step several times, 1/θ is of the order of the number of critical steps, and the price grows with that number, as it must (N8, Section 9.1).

4. **In the worst case over classes no protocol does much better, and these are theorems as well** (Section 4).
   * Where no question was asked and the answers elsewhere do not settle the matter, every behaviour is consistent with the data (N1).
   * There are a scaffold in which each of L calls can decide the outcome and a class of ⌈L/(2δ)⌉ + 1 candidates for which every protocol needs at least L/(4δ) − 3L/2 questions to keep the failure probability of a run below δ ≤ 1/6 for every candidate (N2). For δ ≤ 1/10 that is at least the L calls of one run: for one such run it is cheaper to let the human make the calls. Imitation saves human work only across many runs, and only if failed runs are accepted. On this class the number of human answers replaced for each answer spent is at most about four times the expected number of failed runs allowed in all, and rehearsal breaks even once about K ln 2 failed runs are allowed (N2d).
   * The factor K cannot be removed in general: for some classes of K bits every protocol needs of the order of K·L/(δ·log₂(L/δ)) questions (N2b), and under low stakes the same holds where the tolerance comes from replication (N8).
   * On other classes rehearsal is not the best protocol. Questions chosen where the candidates disagree save the logarithm in the worst case (N2c) and a factor of order L/δ on a class with a few rare informative inputs (Section 4.3).

5. **Copy or predictor** (Sections 5, 8.7 and 9.4). The imitator of answer 1 is a committee: a fresh draw from the posterior at every call. One program can take its place. For a deterministic human, with probability at least 1 − 2δ over the rehearsal and the draw, a single posterior draw made at the end has an unfaithful run with probability below 2L(K ln 2 + ln(1/δ))/n (R6(b)). For a human who varies a posterior draw fails, because single answers cannot tell the human from the human with the coins filled in (the example after R4; Pr7). The program that maximises the posterior under squared prior weights does not fail: after rehearsal, with probability at least 1 − 2δ, its law of runs is within squared Hellinger distance L·(28·K ln 2 + 40·ln(1/δ))/n of the human system's (R6(c)); from recorded runs of the human system the corresponding statement holds for every exponent above 1 and is false at exponent 1 (H2). This is the steeper prior of the researcher's notes. None of this makes the program a copy of the person. The data fix the human's behaviour on the inputs of one closed system and nothing else. And the model assumes that each answer is a fresh draw from the human's answer distribution (A1), under which no predictor can beat that distribution: a cleverer predictor is assumed away and not excluded (Section 9.4). Under low stakes a single member of the class can also be much worse than the committee: in the examples of R5 a member that goes wrong stays wrong for the rest of the run, while the committee draws afresh at every call.

6. **Outside the rehearsed system the price is a density ratio, or the human stays** (Section 6).
   * If the tasks of deployment are drawn from another law than the tasks of rehearsal, every bound is multiplied by the largest ratio of the two laws (S1). To be prepared for N possible laws costs a factor N in the worst case, and for all laws that a program of ℓ bits can sample at least about 2^ℓ and at most 2^(2ℓ+1) (S2, S3, N3). Where the law of rehearsal gives no mass, N1 applies.
   * In an *open* system, where inputs arrive in any way and nothing can be rehearsed, the idea of asking again works as it stands, and the human never leaves: if each arriving input is put to the human with probability q, the imitator of a deterministic human gives at most (1 − q)·K ln 2/q wrong answers in expectation over the whole unbounded run, whatever the inputs are (S5). The asking cannot always stop early. For a class of 2^K thresholds and one sequence of inputs, every learner that asks at most 2^(K−1) − 1 questions has an expected number of silent errors and unanswered abstentions which, multiplied by the number of questions plus one, is at least 3·2^K/16; and for every class of functions 2^b − 1 questions suffice if a bound b ≥ K is known (N7).

7. **Failing only obviously costs more than failing rarely** (Section 7). The rehearsed mixture answers everywhere, and its wrong answers are not marked. A rule that may abstain can do better in three ways, each with its limit.
   * *On the system that it rehearsed.* For a deterministic human, the rule "answer only when the mixture gives one answer probability at least 1 − η", when it answers the rehearsal runs itself, has at most L·K/(n·log₂(1/η)) wrong answers per run in expectation and aborts a run with probability at most L·K ln 2/(n·η) (Ab1): fewer silent errors by the factor log₂(1/η), at the price of aborts.
   * *On any inputs, never silently wrong.* The rule "give each answer with the least probability that a plausible candidate gives it, and abstain with the rest" produces the human's run, or a beginning of it followed by an abort, provided that the human is among the plausible candidates (Ab2). That proviso is a theorem only if the plausible set reaches down to the human's weight 2^(−K). The rule then refuses every unasked input that a test of fewer than about K bits separates from the asked ones, and it needs of the order of 2^K questions not to abort on the rehearsed system. With a smaller plausible set the rule is a *whitelist*: it refuses what differs in a simple way from what was asked and acts elsewhere, and its soundness is an assumption that the data cannot confirm. Something of the kind is forced: a rule that is never silently wrong for a class that can change one input at a time must ask or abstain at every new input (N4).
   * *On a batch of inputs known in advance, silently wrong at a small rate.* For a deterministic human of weight at least 2^(−b), with b known, a rule built from n questions drawn from one law answers any batch with at most a fraction ε of wrong answers, refuses only inputs in a set whose mass under that law is below (2b ln 2 + ln(1/δ))/(ε·n), and tells without the human whether the human must be asked again about the batch (Ab5; the algorithm is that of Goldwasser, Kalai, Kalai and Montasser 2020). Its price is linear in b, where the second way needs 2^K. But ε is a fraction of a batch and not a bound for one run, the batch must be known before any of it is answered, and off the law of its questions the rule may refuse everything.

   So nothing in this note acts on new inputs as they arrive, is never silently wrong and needs no assumption. What can be had on new inputs without a further assumption is a small rate of silent errors on batches.

8. **The property idea gives a second guarantee, cheaper and weaker** (Section 8). Call a property a *test*: a set of (question, answer) pairs, taken from a family with weights π. An imitator can be built from the tests alone, with no class of hypotheses. While the human still answers every question, and the imitator commits to its answer first, the following holds on every sequence of questions, whatever the human is: the number of the imitator's answers in each test B stays within √((n + 1)(2 ln(1/π(B)) + ln(n + 1) + 1.39)) of the number of the human's, and a kind of answer that the human never gives is given at most log₂(1/π(B)) + 2 times in total (Theorem Pr5, for a finite set of possible answers; the method is Blackwell's approachability and is not new). A property costs its own complexity, not the human's. Once the human has left, the guarantee is for the rehearsed system only: a property that the human always satisfies is violated at no more than L·(log₂(1/π(B)) + 2)/n calls of a deployed run, in expectation (Pr6; it needs the assumptions of Section 1 on the protocol's coins and on the human's answers at the asked calls). This is the exact sense in which "the imitator keeps the properties the data had" can be had without assuming that the human belongs to a class. It is not imitation. For most deterministic humans, in the sense of most functions on a large set of inputs, a fair coin matches every test frequency that a sample can resolve, and it differs from the human at half of the calls; tests of single answers do not control whole runs; and properties outside the family are not kept (Sections 8.2 and 8.5). And the Bayes mixture on ordinary data need not keep even a simple property at its own price. In a class with *memorisers*, hypotheses that are a table of the answers seen and answer badly elsewhere, and on some universal machines, it violates one with probability above 1/2 for about the first K/log₂(4u) questions, where u is the number of inputs and a memoriser spends about log₂(4u) bits on one question–answer pair (Pr2); for one fixed machine the question is open.

9. **Solomonoff induction on ordinary questions has no guarantee where it matters; on rehearsal questions it has R1** (Sections 4.1, 6.1 and 11). With questions drawn from a fixed law, the data do not constrain the mixture, or any other protocol, on the inputs that only decomposition produces (N1). In the toy system of Section 11 the mixture is wrong in 35% to 100% of the runs after 10,000 such questions, while 6400 rehearsal questions bring the rate below half a percent. The inductor is the same in both cases; what differs is where the questions come from.

10. **What this does not give** (Sections 9 and 10). Every bound on imitation is in K, and for a person under a generic prior K is of the order of 10⁹ bits. There the bounds are empty: at an hour per answer a working life pays for about 1.4·10³ bits at θ = δ = 0.1, and if one answer is a year of work it pays for less than one bit. Nothing here says how to obtain a prior that already holds the human within an affordable K, or how to tell that one has it. So the protocol works where the prior is already that close, K being the bits that it still lacks; or for properties, at their own price; or as a check of an imitator obtained in any way. Four further limits. The protocol imitates behaviour on the inputs of one closed system and says nothing about the same human elsewhere. Its failures are not marked (answer 7). In rehearsal the human reads questions that the imitator's own runs wrote, in the early rounds those of the prior; with the class of all programs nothing here bounds what such text contains or what reading it does (Section 9.5). And one person who answers n questions remembers them; rehearsal then needs a fresh person for each question, and what is imitated is a pool of people (Section 3.7). One bet changes the rate and leaves the factor K: if the human is separated from every other member of a class without patches, that is, a class in which no member is another member altered on a small set of inputs, then the error falls exponentially in n once n exceeds about 2L·K ln 2/γ, γ being the separation (I1, Section 9.2). If the human is not in such a class, the error is silent.
