## 6. Inputs from outside

Rehearsal draws the top-level input z, the *task*, from the law D₀; deployment may draw it otherwise, or from no law at all. Like R1, the bounds in K of this section are empty for a person under a generic prior (K of the order of 10⁹ bits, Section 9.1); those in 2^K are empty for any human of interest. Statements cited by number are those of `proofs/shift-abstain.md`.

### 6.1 Shift

**Theorem S4 (three kinds of shift)** (proved here, from R1, S1, S2, N1 and N3). Run Protocol R (Section 2) for n rounds with top-level law D₀ and deploy M^(T).

(i) *The shift that the system makes itself costs nothing.* For every set X₀ of inputs, the probability that the question of round T lies in X₀ is 1/L times the expected number of calls of the deployed run in X₀. This holds whatever the scaffold does with the answers: it may decompose a task, recurse, or write the input of one call from the answers to others. So Theorem R1 needs no factor for shift.

(ii) *A shift of the top-level law to D₀′ ≤ B·D₀ costs the factor B* (S1(a)). To be prepared for N laws with disjoint supports costs the factor N, and on some class every protocol pays it (S2, N3). For all laws that programs of at most ℓ bits sample under a fixed time bound, the price is 2^ℓ up to a factor polynomial in ℓ. Rehearsal on a mixture of them pays at most the factor 2^(2ℓ+1), and with other weights 2^ℓ times a polynomial in ℓ (S2). On some class no protocol does with much less than the factor 2^ℓ: at least 2^ℓ/(c·ℓ²) such laws have disjoint supports, c being a constant of the reference machine, and the number of questions grows by that factor (N3).

(iii) *Any other shift has no guarantee.* On a task to which D₀ gives no mass no run was rehearsed; where such a task leads to inputs that no question covered, N1 applies.

*Proof.* (i) The index J of the asked call is uniform and independent of the run, and the deployed run has the law of the run of round T. (ii) and (iii) are the cited statements (Theorem 2.5). ∎

Part (i) is the reduction of Ross, Gordon and Bagnell (2011) as used by Li and Zhang (2025). *What it does not say.* Part (i) equates expected numbers of calls. A kind of call that a run makes fewer than about L/n times in expectation is probably never asked, and there the prior fixes the answer.

**Proposition S1 (density ratio)** (proved here; (a) is the standard change of measure).

(a) Let E′ and E be expectations over a run with z drawn from D₀′ and from D₀. If D₀′ ≤ B·D₀, then E′[φ] ≤ B·E[φ] for an answerer fixed before the run and every function φ ≥ 0 of runs. So every bound of Section 3 on the deployed run (unfaithful calls, the distance of R2, a validated rate) holds with the factor B. If D₀′ ≤ B·D₀ fails on a set of tasks, add the D₀′-mass of that set times the largest value of φ.

(b) The *visit measure* of an answerer gives each set of inputs the expected number of calls of its run in that set. A bound for inputs that are dominated by the visits of the rehearsal runs, and not by the visits of the deployed imitator itself, loses a factor of order L, because the imitator of one round is then judged on inputs that the imitators of other rounds visit. Let d̄ be the average of the visit measures of M^(1), …, M^(n), and d′ ≤ B·d̄ a measure on inputs. Then

  E ∫ TV(P(·|x), M^(T)(·|x)) d′(dx) ≤ (2L − 1)·B·E[β(M^(T))],

where β(M^(T)) is the expected number of unfaithful calls of a run of M^(T) (Section 1). The factor 2L − 1 cannot be lowered below L/2.

*Proof.* (a) The expectation of φ given z does not depend on the law of z. (b) Proposition 2.3 and Example 2.2. ∎

In the example an early mixture errs at the first call and is thereby sent away from the input x₁ that the human system visits L − 1 times; so its error at x₁ never shows in its own runs. For L = 100, n = 10⁴ and a human of weight 1/L, a deployment that visits x₁ as the human system does meets 0.4975 unfaithful calls in expectation, where the factor B alone would allow 0.0463. So for visit measures the bound with B alone is false, for a human of weight 1/L by a factor of about L/(2 ln L): this is the compounding of errors of Ross and Bagnell (2010). *What it does not say.* S1 says nothing about tasks to which D₀ gives no mass.

**Proposition S3 (a property of tasks)** (proved here). Let Γ be a set of tasks with D₀(Γ) = p > 0. For a deterministic human, the deployed run on a task in Γ has at most L·K ln 2/(p·n) unfaithful calls in expectation. The factor 1/p is necessary for protocols that take each question from a run on a task drawn from D₀: if δ ≤ 1/4 and L/(2δ) is an integer, there are a scaffold and a class for which a failure probability of at most δ on the tasks of Γ, for every member (a run fails as in Section 4), needs n ≥ (L/(4δ) − L)/p. Proof: Proposition 2.4. *What it does not say.* The factor 1/p is the price of a property that was not singled out in advance. A property that is named in advance costs nothing: rehearse on D₀ conditioned on Γ. To be prepared for N unknown disjoint properties costs the factor N (N3).

### 6.2 Open systems

In an open system (Section 1) no scaffold can be run in advance, and inputs arrive in any way.

**Proposition S5 (asking at random, for ever)** (proved here; label-efficient prediction, Cesa-Bianchi, Lugosi and Stoltz 2005, applied to the regret bound of R1). After the input x_t of step t is fixed, a coin decides with probability q that the human answers and the pair joins the data; otherwise the current mixture M^(t) answers. Then E Σ_t KL(P(·|x_t) ‖ M^(t)(·|x_t)) ≤ K ln 2/q, the sum running over all steps, asked or not. For a deterministic human the mixture gives at most (1 − q)·K ln 2/q wrong answers in expectation over the whole unbounded run; an example comes within the factor 2 ln 2 of this. Proof: Theorem 3.1 and Example 3.2. *What it does not say.* It does not say which steps are wrong. The human never leaves: the questions go on at the rate q for as long as inputs arrive. And the bound is in K: for a person under a generic prior it says nothing before the run has more than about K ln 2/q steps.

**Theorem N7 (the asking cannot stop early)** (proved here). At each step a learner sees an input and either puts it to the human, or predicts an answer, or abstains; it sees the human's answer only when it asks. Sil counts its wrong predictions and Abs its abstentions; Qu is a bound on the number of its questions on every run.

(a) Let the class contain the N thresholds x ↦ 1[x ≤ i], 0 ≤ i < N, and show the inputs 1, …, N − 1 in order. Every learner with Qu ≤ N/2 − 1 has E[Sil + Abs]·(Qu + 1) ≥ 3N/16 for a uniformly drawn threshold as the human, hence for some threshold. If Qu + 1 divides N, some learner has E[Sil + Abs]·(Qu + 1) < N/2.

(b) For every class of functions, every human f in it and every known b ≥ K, *enumeration* is never wrong and asks at most 2^b − 1 questions, on input sequences of any length. Enumeration keeps the hypotheses of weight at least 2^(−b) that fit the answers; it asks where two of them differ and gives their common answer elsewhere.

*Idea.* (a) While every answer is 1, the learner's questions cut the thresholds into at most Qu + 1 blocks of neighbours. Inside a block of u thresholds the learner predicts the same for all of them, and its predictions are wrong at least ⌊u²/4⌋ times, summed over the members of the block; the squares of the block sizes add up to at least N²/(Qu + 1). (b) Every question refutes a kept hypothesis other than the human, and at most 2^b hypotheses have weight 2^(−b) or more. Proof: Theorem 3.3 and Corollary 3.6.

So a deterministic human can leave an open system: never later than after 2^b − 1 questions, if a bound b ≥ K is known. But for 2^K equally weighted thresholds as in (a) the human cannot leave before 2^K·min{1/2, 3/(16j)} − 1 questions on some run, if j silent mistakes and unanswered abstentions are tolerated in expectation. So the asking can stop, but in the worst case only after a number of questions that is exponential in K. For a person that is no bound, and only in this sense can the asking not stop. *What it does not say.* Part (a) is a worst case over one class and one sequence of inputs, and its learner may put to the human only the input that is shown: a learner that chooses its questions freely finds a threshold with K questions, by halving. For a human who is not deterministic see Section 7.5.

## 7. Failing obviously

An imitator may *abstain*, that is, refuse to answer; a run halts at its first abstention and is then *aborted*. A *silent error* is an answer that is neither the human's nor an abstention. Until Section 7.5 the human is a function f. A rule is *sound* for a function if every answer that it gives is that function's, and sound for a set of functions if it is sound for each of them. In this section A is the set of asked inputs, and c denotes constants of the reference machine. K(Q) is the length of a shortest program that decides the set Q. A *test* is here a decidable set of inputs (not a set of pairs of question and answer, as in Sections 3.5 and 8), and it has K(Q) bits. sep(x; A) is the least K(Q) over the tests Q that contain x and no element of A: the number of bits that it takes to separate x from the asked inputs. For an asked x there is no such test, and sep(x; A) is infinite. Numbers cited without a file refer to `proofs/shift-abstain.md`.

### 7.1 The threshold rule

**Theorem Ab1** (proved here; the Bayes-mixture form of the trade-off of Sayedi, Zadimoghaddam and Blum 2010). For η < 1/2 the rule Thr_η answers y if M_D(y|x) ≥ 1 − η and abstains otherwise.

(a) On any sequence of asked inputs, apply the rule to each input before the human answers; let it be wrong u times and abstain v times. Then u·log₂(1/η) + v·log₂(1/(1 − η)) ≤ K. So u ≤ K/log₂(1/η) and v ≤ K/log₂(1/(1 − η)) ≤ K ln 2/η; examples attain the first two bounds up to rounding. If η < w(f) the rule is never wrong, at any input.

(b) Rehearse with Thr_η answering the calls and one uniformly chosen call of each run, the aborting one included, put to the human. The run of the rule of a uniformly chosen round has at most L·K/(n·log₂(1/η)) wrong answers in expectation and is aborted with probability at most L·K ln 2/(n·η).

*Proof.* (a) As in the proof of R1, the product over the asked rounds of M^(t)(f(x_t)|x_t) telescopes and is at least w(f). The factor is at most η at a wrong round and below 1 − η at an abstention round. If η < w(f), then M_D(f(x)|x) ≥ w(f) > η, and no other answer reaches 1 − η. (b) The asked call is a uniformly chosen one of the L call slots. So round t is a wrong round with probability 1/L times the expected number of wrong answers of its run, and an abstention round with probability 1/L times its abort probability. Take expectations in (a). ∎

*What it does not say.* The counts of (a) are over asked rounds; an error at an input that is not put to the human teaches nothing. In (b) the rule itself must answer the rehearsal runs: if the mixture answers them and Thr_η is only deployed, the calls of the deployed run are not distributed as the asked calls. And both parts are bounds in K: for a person under a generic prior they are empty, like that of R1.

### 7.2 The minimum over a plausible set

For a set 𝒞 of hypotheses, the rule Min_𝒞 answers y with probability min_{ν∈𝒞} ν(y|x) and abstains with the remaining probability. Order the hypotheses by posterior weight, the heaviest first and ties in a fixed order, and let α ∈ (0, 1]. The *top set* keeps a hypothesis if its posterior weight is at least α times the sum of its own weight and the weights of the hypotheses ranked before it; it has at most 1/α members. The *ratio set* keeps the hypotheses of weight at least α times the largest; it contains the top set.

**Theorem Ab2** (the rule, the top set and the first clause of (b): Cohen, Hutter and Nanda 2022; the rest proved here, (a) from their domination at a single step). The human may be any sampler.

(a) *Dominance.* If P ∈ 𝒞, then on every scaffold, with inputs from anywhere, the run of Min_𝒞 is, in a coupling, the human system's run or an initial part of it followed by an abort. So every set of complete runs has at most its probability under the human system.

(b) P stays in both sets for ever with probability at least 1 − α/w(P) (Ville's inequality); a deterministic human with certainty if α ≤ w(f).

(c) *Coverage.* In the class of all programs, Min over the ratio set abstains with certainty wherever sep(x; A) ≤ log₂(1/α) − c. For programs of total functions it answers f(x) wherever sep(x; A) > K + K_D + log₂(1/α) + c, where K_D is the length of the shortest program consistent with the data.

(d) *Abort after rehearsal.* After n rounds of Protocol R freeze the top set 𝒞 and deploy Min_𝒞 on the same scaffold. Let δ ∈ (0, 1). For a deterministic human the run is aborted with probability at most

  Pr[f ∉ 𝒞] + δ + L·(K ln 2 + ln(1/(α·δ)))/(α·n) + L·(L − 1)·K ln 2/n.

For samplers it is aborted with probability at most

  α/w(P) + δ + (L/α)·√(2G/n) + L·(L − 1)·√(K ln 2/(2n)),  where G := ln S + ½·ln(1/(α·w(P))) + ln(1/δ)

and S := Σ_ν √w(ν) is assumed finite. The term ln S cannot be dropped: without it the bound is false in an example (Example 5.7(b)).

*Idea.* For (a), keep each answer of the human's run with probability min_{ν∈𝒞} ν(y|x)/P(y|x), and abort otherwise. For (c), take a plausible hypothesis and a test Q without asked inputs. The two *patches* of the hypothesis, which give one fixed answer on Q, or another, and agree with it elsewhere, fit the data as well as it does, cost about K(Q) bits more, and contradict each other on Q. Proof: Theorems 5.1, 5.3, 5.6 and Proposition 5.2.

*What it does not say.* P ∈ 𝒞 is a theorem only for α ≤ δ·w(P), or α ≤ w(f). Then by (c) the rule refuses every unasked input simpler than about K, and the bound of (d) is small only when n is of the order of L·K·2^K, for samplers of L²·K·4^K: empty for a person. With a moderate α the rule needs that the human is in the set. For the ratio set this says that the human's posterior weight is at least α times the largest, in other words that the human is within log₂(1/α) bits of the best explanation of the data. That is an assumption which the data cannot confirm.

*Comparison.* Cohen, Hutter and Nanda keep the demonstrator, who may have memory, and query with the leftover probability. They bound the expected sum of cubed query probabilities by |𝒜|·α⁻³·(24/w + 12) (their Theorem 1; 𝒜 the actions, w the demonstrator's prior weight), and events unlikely under the demonstrator with factors that grow with time (their Theorem 5). Here the query is an abort, domination is exact for whole runs, and the price moves into (d).

### 7.3 The novelty rule

**Proposition Ab3** (proved here; first stated as Observation B in this project's digest of the researcher's notes, `research/understand/notes.md`, Section 6.2). For programs of total functions, the rule Nov_s asks the human at x if sep(x; A) < s, or abstains there when nobody answers, and otherwise answers as the shortest program consistent with the data. (a) It asks at most 2^s − 1 questions on any input sequence, whatever the human. (b) If s > 2K + c it is never wrong. (c) Frozen, it refuses exactly {x : sep(x; A) < s}, for ever: with the s of (b), every unasked input simpler than about 2K. With a smaller s nothing here guarantees its answers.

*Proof.* (a) A question at x has a test of fewer than s bits that contains x and no input asked before. After the question that test contains an asked input and serves no later question. So the questions have different tests, and fewer than 2^s programs have fewer than s bits. (b) Let g be the shortest program consistent with the data, and let it be wrong at x. The set {g ≠ f} contains x and no asked input, and a program that decides it can be made from programs for g and f, of at most K bits each; so sep(x; A) ≤ 2K + c, and the rule answers only where sep(x; A) ≥ s. (c) is the definition of the rule. ∎

*What it does not say.* With the s of (b) the count of (a) is of the order of 4^K or more, which is no bound for any human of interest. And the rule is not computable, because sep is not.

### 7.4 The trade-off, rocks and whitelists

**Proposition N4** (standard: the example of singletons, Example 1 of Sayedi, Zadimoghaddam and Blum 2010 as Balsubramani 2015 reports it, in the model of Li, Littman and Walsh 2008 for learners that may say "I don't know"). A learner that is never silently wrong for every member of a class asks or abstains wherever a member that agrees with the human on A differs from the human. With the zero function as the human and the 2^d point functions in the class, that is every new input. Proof: Proposition 7.1. *What it does not say.* N4 limits a learner only for a class that contains such members; the point functions, which can change one input at a time, are the extreme case.

**Theorem Ab4** (proved here; the lower bound of row 3 follows from the exact result of Zhang and Chaudhuri 2016). Let the human be deterministic, of K bits, and let Sil, Abs and Qu be as in N7.

| setting | upper bound (a rule that achieves it) | lower bound (for every rule) |
|---|---|---|
| open system; each arriving input is put to the human with probability q; otherwise the rule Thr_η answers or abstains, and the human answers every abstention | E[Sil] ≤ ((1 − q)/q)·K/log₂(1/η), with at most K ln 2/η abstentions | 2^K thresholds, inputs in order, Qu ≤ 2^(K−1) − 1: E[Sil + Abs]·(Qu + 1) ≥ 3·2^K/16, where Abs counts the abstentions that nobody answers (N7(a)) |
| open system, no silent error | at most 2^b − 1 questions for a known b ≥ K (N7(b)) | the zero function among the point functions: every new input is asked or refused (N4) |
| the human's answer is shown after every prediction; at most k wrong predictions; K ≥ k + 1 | about K ln 2·2^(K/(k+1)) abstentions (Ab1(a)) | 2^K thresholds, rules without coins: (k + 1)·2^(K/(k+1))/e − k − 1 abstentions on some sequence |
| closed system | the bounds of Ab1(b) | the class and the scaffold of N2 with (1 − p_a)·L/(2·p_s) cells, this number being an integer: n ≥ (1 − p_a)²·L/(4·p_s) − L |

In the last row p_s bounds, for every member of the class, the probability of a *silent failure*: the deployed run gives an outcome that is neither declined nor the human system's. And p_a bounds the probability that the outcome is declined when the human is the zero function; n is counted under the zero function. Proof: Theorem 7.3. *What it does not say.* The last row is one closed system. Where the inputs that the questions missed are rare and recognisable, abstaining on them saves a factor of order p_a/p_s in questions (Example 4.5a).

**Rocks and whitelists.** The researcher's notes reject a caution that "just turns you into a rock" and accept "an effective whitelist" (his words, as this project's digest of his notes quotes them). Here the two words get an exact sense, which is ours. A frozen rule is a *rock* if it refuses every input that was not asked, or every unasked input below some complexity: it is safe because it does nothing new. It is a *whitelist* if it refuses only inputs that a short test separates from the asked ones, and answers everywhere else.

In the table the rules are frozen and sep stands for sep(x; A). The best explanation of the data is the shortest program consistent with them, of K_D bits, and a hypothesis is within r bits of it if its program is at most r bits longer. For programs of total functions, every rule that is sound for all hypotheses within r bits of the best explanation refuses every x with sep(x; A) ≤ r − c (Proposition 7.5). This forces the second column of rows 1, 2 and 4 (in row 2 with b − K_D for r); in rows 3 and 5 it is a property of the rule.

| rule | refuses every x with | refuses only x with | sound when |
|---|---|---|---|
| any rule that is sound for all programs of total functions | x not asked | (no bound) | always |
| enumeration, budget b | sep ≤ b − K_D − c | sep ≤ 2b + c | b ≥ K |
| Thr_η, η = 2^(−r) | sep < r − c | not known | posterior weight of f above η |
| Min, ratio set, α = 2^(−r) | sep ≤ r − c | sep ≤ K + K_D + r + c | f within r bits of the best explanation |
| Nov_r | sep < r | sep < r | r > 2K + c, or the shortest consistent program is right elsewhere |
| mixture | no x | no x | not guaranteed off A |

*Rocks.* With a parameter that makes soundness a theorem for all data (η < 2^(−K), α ≤ 2^(−K), r > 2K + c), rows 3 to 5 refuse every unasked input simpler than about K. One expects K for a person to exceed the complexity of every input ever written (this depends on the reference machine); then they are the rocks the researcher rejects, like row 1. Enumeration is sound once b ≥ K; its forced radius b − K_D shrinks as the data single out the human. *Whitelists.* With a moderate r the same rules refuse what a test of about r bits separates from A, and rows 4 and 5 nothing beyond the third column: the whitelists he accepts. Soundness is then the assumption in the last column, and its failure is silent. The exception is Thr_η on the system it rehearsed: there Ab1(b) bounds its wrong answers per run by L·K/(n·r) in expectation, without that assumption, and its abort probability by L·K ln 2·2^r/n. If the calls of a decomposition share a property of fewer than r − c bits that no asked input has, rows 1 and 3 to 5 refuse them all, and row 2 does if b − K_D ≥ r; the mixture errs at each with probability at least 2^(−r−c). For the class of all programs rows 2 to 6 are not computable.

**Between the rock and the bet: batches.** The rules above are never silently wrong, or they count their silent errors on asked inputs only, or, like Thr_η on the system it rehearsed, per run of that system. If the inputs to be answered are known as a batch before any of them is answered, more can be had: a small *rate* of silent errors on every batch, at a price that is linear in a bound on K.

**Proposition Ab5** (the rule, (a) and the first claim of (b) are those of Goldwasser, Kalai, Kalai and Montasser 2020, who treat classes of finite VC dimension with two answers; proved here for a countable class with any set of answers). Let the human be a function f of the class with weight at least 2^(−b), for a known b. Put to the human n questions drawn independently from a law P on inputs (in this block P is that law, and the human is f). Let V be the set of the functions of the class of weight at least 2^(−b) that fit the answers, and h one of them (to be definite, the first in a fixed order by non-increasing weight; the statements hold for any choice). A *batch* is a list z_1, …, z_m of inputs, in which an input may occur several times. Given a batch and ε ∈ (0, 1], the rule Bat_ε repeats one step: while some member g of V differs from h at more than ε·m positions of the batch that are not yet refused, it refuses every position at which g and h differ. Then it answers as h at the other positions.

(a) *Sound up to ε.* On every batch, chosen in any way after the questions, at most ε·m answers are wrong. This holds with certainty.

(b) *Few refusals where the questions came from.* Let γ := (2b ln 2 + ln(1/δ))/n. With probability at least 1 − δ over the questions, for every batch the refused inputs lie in a set of P-mass below γ/ε; the set depends on the batch. This bounds the chance that one further input, drawn from P independently of the batch, is refused. The inputs of the batch itself can be refused in far greater number, even if they are drawn from P: a batch with ε·m < 1 loses every input at which two members of V differ. But if the batch consists of m draws from P that are independent of one another and of the questions, and if ε ≥ 7.4·γ and ε·m ≥ b ln 2 + ln(1/δ), then on the event of the first claim nothing is refused, with probability at least 1 − δ over the batch.

(c) *A check, without the human, of whether the human is needed again.* If the rule refuses nothing, the whole batch is answered with at most ε·m errors, and no question about the batch was put. If it refuses, the human can instead answer at (b ln 2 + ln(1/δ))/ε or more positions of the batch, drawn uniformly and independently from 1, …, m, so that an input that fills many positions is drawn often. With probability at least 1 − δ, every function of weight at least 2^(−b) that fits all the answers is then wrong at no more than ε·m positions of the batch, and the first of them can answer the whole batch.

*Proof.* (a) When the rule stops, no member of V differs from h at more than ε·m of the positions that it answers, and the human is a member of V. (b) Each step refuses more than ε·m positions, so there are fewer than 1/ε steps. Two functions that differ on a set of P-mass above γ agree with each other at all n independent questions with probability below 2^(−2b)·δ, and there are fewer than 2^(2b) pairs of functions of weight at least 2^(−b). So with probability at least 1 − δ every member of V differs from h, which agrees with it at the questions, on a set of mass at most γ, and the fewer than 1/ε steps refuse a set of mass below γ/ε. The second claim of (b) and (c): Theorem 7.6, items 4 and 6. ∎

A count of whole sequences of steps, in the place of pairs, gives the bound (⌈1/ε⌉·b ln 2 + ln(2/δ))/n on the refused mass, with the same probability; for small ε that is about half of γ/ε (Theorem 7.6, item 3a).

*The price, and what it buys.* So n = (2b ln 2 + ln(1/δ))/(ε·ε′) questions buy a rule that is silently wrong on at most a fraction ε of any batch, with certainty, and that, with probability at least 1 − δ over the questions, refuses on every batch only inputs from a set of mass below ε′ under the law of its questions. The price is linear in b. That alone is not the gain. Without abstention, (b ln 2 + ln(1/δ))/ε questions from P already make every function of weight at least 2^(−b) that fits the answers wrong on a set of P-mass at most ε, with probability at least 1 − δ; and R1 has a price linear in K for the runs of a rehearsed system. What the refusals buy is that the rate ε holds on every batch, also on one that an adversary chose. The rules that are never silently wrong give a guarantee for every single input, and their known prices are exponential. Enumeration asks up to 2^b − 1 questions (N7(b)), and frozen after n questions from P it can still refuse nearly everything: with the zero function as the human among the 2^b − 1 point functions and P uniform, a set of mass at least 1 − n/(2^b − 1). For the frozen minimum rule over a set that is certain to contain the human, the bound of Ab2(d) is small only after a rehearsal of the order of L·K·2^K rounds. In the class of programs of total functions Bat_ε is a whitelist: it refuses only inputs with sep(x; A) ≤ 2b + c, as enumeration does. With ε·m < 1 it is enumeration with budget b, frozen: never wrong, and a rock below complexity b − K_D − c (row 2 of the table).

*What it does not say.* (i) ε is a fraction of the batch and not a bound for a run. If the batch consists of the calls of whole runs of at most L calls each, at most ε·m of these runs contain a wrong answer, and m is at most L times the number of runs; so a run drawn uniformly from the batch contains a wrong answer with probability at most ε·L. But nothing is said about a run that is singled out in advance, a run that contains a wrong answer may be wrong at every call, and a batch that is one run with related calls can be refused entirely. (ii) Off the law P the rule may refuse everything. And it keeps the rock in the form of a rate: in the class of programs of total functions, if a test of at most b − K_D − c bits contains no asked input, the rule answers at most 2ε·m positions of the batch inside it, wherever the batch comes from. So if calls that share a simple property which no asked input has, as the calls of a decomposition may, fill more than a fraction 2ε of the batch, all but 2ε·m of them are refused. (iii) The batch must be known before any of it is answered. The calls of fresh runs are such a batch if neither the scaffold's questions nor their number depend on the answers. The questions of the rehearsal of Section 2 are then independent draws from one law, that of the J-th call of a run for a uniformly drawn J (a round whose run is shorter yields no question). The first claim of (b) holds for this law: a run drawn afresh, independently of the batch, has in expectation fewer than L·γ/ε calls in the refused set. The second claim of (b) needs independent draws, which the calls of one run are not; for a batch of fresh runs of exactly L calls each it holds with ε times the number of runs in the place of ε·m (Remark 7.10(a)). For questions that depend on the answers there is only a form by stages (Theorem 7.6, item 5, and Remark 7.10(b)). For inputs that arrive one at a time nothing is proved. The obvious rule counts, for each member of V, the answered inputs at which that member differs from h, and refuses an input when a member that differs there has reached ε·m. It can be led by ε·m copies of one input to refuse every later input that was not asked (Example 7.9(c)); whether another rule for such inputs has the guarantees (a) and (b) is open. (iv) The human must be a function, and a bound b ≥ K must be known; if K > b the errors are silent. (v) For a person, with b ≥ K of the order of 10⁹ bits, the price is as far out of reach as that of R1.

### 7.5 A human who is not deterministic

Dominance (Ab2(a)) holds as stated. If the human has everywhere a preferred answer of probability at least 1 − ε₀ > 1 − η, then in expectation Thr_η gives another answer in at most K ln 2/kl(1 − ε₀ ‖ η) asked rounds and abstains in at most K ln 2/kl(ε₀ ‖ η), kl(a ‖ b) being the divergence in nats between coins of bias a and b (Proposition 4.7); it then imitates that answer, not the distribution. Where every answer has probability below 1 − η it abstains once the mixture is close to the human. No rule has both counts of Ab1(a) for all samplers: with a fair coin of weight 1/2 and the 2^N functions on N inputs, of weight 2^(−N−1) each, at most (N + 1)/4 expected silent errors against every function force at least (N − 1)/2 expected abstentions against the coin, which costs one bit (Proposition 4.8).
