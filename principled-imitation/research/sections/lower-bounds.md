## 4. What no protocol can do

Sections 2 and 3 are about one protocol; this section is about all of them. Here a *protocol* is any rule that chooses questions for the human, each from the answers so far and from coins of its own, and then hands over a *deployed system*. A deployed system is anything that produces, from the data, the z and ρ of a run and fresh coins, an outcome for that run, or *declines* to give one (Section 7 calls this abstaining). An imitator that answers the calls of a run one after another is a deployed system, and the bounds below hold also for systems that see z and ρ as a whole. All questions are put before the z and ρ of the deployed run are drawn; Proposition N2a is the one exception. n is the expected number of questions, and each statement says under which human it is counted; a lower bound on an expected number is also a lower bound on the largest number. For a deterministic human, a deployed run *fails* if the system declines or if its outcome differs from the outcome of the human system's run on the same z and ρ. A run of an imitator that fails is unfaithful, so every lower bound on the probability of failure is also a lower bound on the probability of an unfaithful run. Numbers refer to `proofs/lower-bounds.md`.

### 4.1 No coverage, no guarantee

**Theorem N1 (two humans that the questions did not tell apart)** (proved here; the standard two-point argument). Let P and P′ be samplers that agree at every input outside a set X₀ of inputs, and let γ be the probability that the protocol puts at least one question in X₀; it is the same for both humans. Let the task be one call at an input x, and let R and R′ be the laws of the deployed answer at x when the human is P and when the human is P′. Here "declined" counts as an answer, and the laws are over everything random: the protocol's coins, the human's answers and the system's coins. Then

  TV(R, P(·|x)) + TV(R′, P′(·|x)) ≥ TV(P(·|x), P′(·|x)) − γ.

If P and P′ are functions f and f′ with f(x) ≠ f′(x), the probability of a wrong or declined answer at x under f and the same probability under f′ add up to at least 1 − γ. And on every scaffold the laws of the whole deployed run under P and under P′ are within γ of each other in total variation.

So a protocol that asks nothing in X₀ deploys the same thing for both humans: if they are functions that differ at x, its answer at x is wrong or declined with probability at least 1/2 for one of them.

*Proof.* Let the protocol meet P and P′ with the same coins, and with answers that are equal wherever the two samplers agree. The two interactions then coincide until the first question in X₀. So the data, and the deployed answer or run, which is computed from the data with fresh randomness, differ with probability at most γ; hence TV(R, R′) ≤ γ. The display is the triangle inequality. For functions use R(f(x)) + R(f′(x)) ≤ 1 and R′(f′(x)) ≤ R(f′(x)) + γ. ∎

*The class of all programs.* N1 limits a protocol that is to serve both P and P′, so it matters which pairs a class contains. The class of all programs contains a partner for every human. Let X₀ be a decidable set of inputs that contains x, and write K(X₀) for the length of a shortest program that decides the set X₀. The program that answers as P does outside X₀ and gives no answer on X₀ is a hypothesis in the sense of Section 1. A shortest program for it is at most K(X₀) + c bits longer than one for P, where c stands, here and below, for a constant of the reference machine; so, with weights 2^(−length of program), its weight is at least 2^(−K(X₀)−c) times the weight of P. If the human always answers, the answer law of this partner at x is disjoint from the human's. N1 then applies with total variation 1, and with γ the probability that a question falls in X₀; γ = 0 if X₀ contains no input that the protocol can ask. (The least K(X₀) over the decidable sets that contain x and no asked input is the number sep(x; A) of Section 7.)

If only programs that always answer are admitted, a human who gives every string a positive probability at x has no partner with a disjoint answer law. There are then, for every N ≥ 2, partners at total variation at least 1 − 1/N from the human at x, for log₂N + 2·log₂log₂N further bits, and the right side of N1 is at least 1 − 1/N − γ (Proposition 1.8).

That the Bayes mixture over all programs keeps a posterior weight of at least 2^(−K(X₀)−c) on the programs that give no answer on X₀, as long as nothing is asked in X₀ and however much is asked outside it, was known; the same holds for any other computable behaviour on X₀, at the price of the bits that describe it (Paper 2 (see References), Proposition G.5; Theorem 1 of Cohen, Hutter, Bengio and Russell 2025 is a published relative for Bayesian imitators). N1 shows that this is not a defect of the mixture: every protocol is in the same position.

*What it does not say.* N1 limits a protocol only for a class that contains both members of the pair: in a class of two functions that differ at every input, one question anywhere shows which of them is the human. And it is a statement about one input. It does not say that deployed runs meet such inputs: after rehearsal a deployed run seldom enters a region, fixed in advance, in which nothing was asked (R7), and for a human in the class its calls are seldom unfaithful (R1).

### 4.2 Critical calls are not amplified

Call a call of a run *critical* if a change of its answer alone changes the outcome of the run.

**Theorem N2 (critical calls)** (proved here; for a scaffold of one call it is a known bound of active learning, see below). Let δ ≤ 1/6. There are a scaffold of L calls and a class of ⌈L/(2δ)⌉ + 1 functions for which every protocol whose deployed run fails with probability at most δ for every member has

  n ≥ L/(4δ) − 3L/2, and n ≥ L/(4δ) − L if L/(2δ) is an integer.

Here n is the expected number of questions under the member f₀ below, for which every call of the scaffold is critical. Fewer than L/(4δ) + 1 questions suffice.

*The class and the scaffold.* Put N := ⌈L/(2δ)⌉. The inputs are N *cells*, numbered 1, …, N. The class consists of the zero function f₀ and, for each cell i, the function f_i that is 1 at i and 0 at every other cell. The scaffold draws L cells independently and uniformly, asks each of them, and has the outcome 1 if some answer was 1 and the outcome 0 otherwise.

*Proof.* Give f₀ the weight L/(N + L) and each f_i the weight 1/(N + L), and fix the protocol's coins. Under f₀ the protocol asks a set A of cells. Under f_i with i ∉ A it gets the same answers and deploys the same system. Let U be the number of distinct cells of the deployed run outside A. If the system's outcome is not 0, the run fails under f₀; if it is 0, the run fails under each of the U functions f_i whose cell the run meets. As U ≤ L, the weighted average of the failure probabilities is at least E[U]/(N + L), and E[U] ≥ (N − n)·p, where p := 1 − (1 − 1/N)^L is the probability that the run meets a given cell. So n ≥ N − δ·(N + L)/p, and the two bounds follow (Corollary 2.2). For the converse ask ⌈N/2⌉ cells drawn at random; if an answer is 1 deploy the member that it shows, and otherwise deploy f₀. The failure is at most p/2 ≤ δ. ∎

With equal weights K = log₂(N + 1) ≤ log₂(L/(2δ) + 2) for every member, and by R1 rehearsal reaches failure δ on this class with L·K ln 2/δ questions. So the factor L/δ of that price is forced, and what the lower bound leaves open is the factor 4·K ln 2. For one call (L = 1) the bound is the star-number bound of active learning, with the same argument (Hanneke and Yang 2015, Theorem 3, crediting Dasgupta 2004, 2005).

*What it does not say.* N2 is about a guarantee for every member, not about an average over a prior: if a prior gives the functions f_i together a weight below 1/2, the protocol that asks nothing and deploys f₀ fails with probability below δ on average over that prior. Nor does N2 count critical calls. It counts the inputs on which critical calls can fall and which the class can change one at a time: a scaffold that asks one input L times has L critical calls and needs one question.

**Proposition N2a (the human stays for the run)** (proved here: Proposition 2.5). Take the class and the scaffold of N2 with ⌈L/√(2δ)⌉ cells. Even a protocol that is shown the L cells of the run before it asks, and that fails with probability at most δ for every member, has

  n ≥ L·(1 − √(2δ) − δ) − δ,

with n counted under f₀: for small δ nearly one question for each call. Asking the L calls gives failure 0. So on these classes a run costs about L answers of the human whatever is done, and the factor 1/(4δ) of N2 is the price of preparing the imitator before the task is known.

### 4.3 The factor K; targeted questions

**Theorem N2b (the factor K)** (proved here: Theorem 3.1 and Proposition 3.2). Let δ ≤ 1/20, let N := L/(2δ) be an integer, and let d ≥ 1. There are a scaffold of L calls and a class of 2^K functions of VC dimension d, with equal weights and K = d·log₂N, for which every protocol whose deployed run fails with probability at most δ for every member has

  n ≥ (1 − 10δ)·d·L/(8δ) = (1 − 10δ)·K·L/(8δ·log₂(L/(2δ))).

Here n is the expected number of questions when the member is drawn uniformly from the class. In the same expectation ⌈d/2⌉·(L/(4δ) + 1/2) questions suffice.

*The class and the scaffold.* The inputs are d *blocks* of N cells each. A member is 1 at exactly one cell of every block and 0 at the other cells; there are N^d = 2^K members. The scaffold draws a block uniformly and then L cells of that block independently and uniformly, asks them, and has the outcome 1 if some answer was 1 and the outcome 0 otherwise.

*Idea.* The blocks are d separate searches of the kind of N2, and what is learnt in one block says nothing about another. If a protocol would ask a fraction κ of the cells of a block as long as its answers there are 0, then on average over the place of the 1 it asks at least κ·N/2 questions in that block, and a run in that block still fails with probability at least p·(1 − κ − 2L/N), with p as in the proof of N2. For the upper bound, draw ⌈d/2⌉ of the blocks at random and search each of them until its 1 is found (Proposition 3.2). Proof: Theorem 3.1.

So for fixed L and δ the number of questions grows in proportion to d, the number of separate searches.

*What it does not say.* It is a worst case over a class that is built for it; it does not say that every class of 2^K functions is that hard. And in bits the bound is not of the order K·L/δ: it has the logarithm of L/(2δ) in the denominator. By the next statement no class of functions needs more than the order K·L/(δ·log₂(L/δ)).

**Proposition N2c (targeted questions)** (proved here: Theorem 3.3; the algorithm follows a known one). Let the class consist of functions, let the human be a member f, with K = log₂(1/w(f)), and let 0 < δ < 1 with L/δ ≥ 16. On every scaffold of horizon L the protocol below asks at most

  1 + 2.7·K·L/(δ·log₂(L/δ))

questions, and its deployed run is unfaithful with probability at most δ.

*Protocol AB* (named after its two kinds of step). Put η := δ·log₂(L/δ)/L. The protocol keeps the members that agree with all answers received so far. The *posterior weight* of an answer y at an input x is the share of the weight of these members that lies on the members with the answer y at x. Each step is of one of two kinds.

(A) If there is an input that occurs in some run of the scaffold answered by some member of the class, and at which no answer has a posterior weight above 1 − η, put such an input to the human.

(B) Otherwise let the *vote* answer one run of the scaffold on a fresh z and ρ; the vote is the function that gives at every input an answer of largest posterior weight. Then put one call of that run to the human, drawn as in Protocol R. Such a question, put to the human at random to check a run, is called an *audit*.

The protocol stops after n_B steps of kind (B), where n_B := max{1, ⌈L·K/(δ·log₂(1/η))⌉}. For all later runs it deploys the vote of one of these n_B steps, drawn uniformly.

*Idea.* The human is never refuted, so the weight of the members that remain never falls below 2^(−K). A step (A) removes at least the fraction η of that weight, whatever the answer. An audit at which the human's answer differs from the vote's, at an input of the kind named in (A), removes all but a fraction below η of it. So there are at most K ln 2/η steps (A) and at most K/log₂(1/η) such audits. If the run of a vote is not the human system's run on the same z and ρ, there is a first call at which the vote's answer is wrong. Up to that call the two runs agree, so its input occurs in a run answered by the human, and the audit falls on it with probability 1/L. Hence the probabilities that the run of the vote is not the human system's run add up, over the n_B steps (B), to at most L·K/log₂(1/η) in expectation. The deployed vote is that of a uniformly drawn one of these steps, so its run is unfaithful with probability at most L·K/(n_B·log₂(1/η)), which is at most δ by the choice of n_B. The questions are the n_B audits and the steps (A); for L/δ ≥ 16, log₂(1/η) is at least half of log₂(L/δ), and this gives the constant 2 + ln 2 < 2.7. Proof: Theorem 3.3.

The protocol follows the membership-query algorithm with which Bshouty (2017, Lemma 3) proves the bound of Hegedűs (1995) and Moshkov (1983), with prior weights and with audits of runs.

*What it does not say.* The count is an upper bound for every class of functions, not the number of questions that a given class needs. The proof uses that one answer refutes a member, so N2c needs a deterministic human. A question of kind (A) is a call that would occur if the earlier calls of a run had been answered as some member answers them; in general it is not a question of any run of the human system. So A2 is used more heavily than in Protocol R: the human must be a member as a function on all these inputs, also on those that follow answers which the human never gives. K enters only through the number n_B of audits, as it enters Protocol R only through the number of rounds. And the bound is in K: for a person under a generic prior, with K of the order of 10⁹ bits (Section 9.1), it is as empty as R1.

**Random questions against chosen ones** (proved here: Propositions 3.7 and 3.10). The questions of rehearsal are random calls of runs, and these are not always the best questions. Let δ ≤ 1/8. Take one *common* input and K *rare* inputs; the class of the 2^K functions with values 0 and 1 that are 0 at the common input, with equal weights; and a scaffold of L calls whose inputs are drawn independently, the common input with probability 1 − 4δ/L and each rare input with probability 4δ/(K·L), and whose outcome is the list of its answers. On this scaffold the question of a rehearsal round is one such draw, independent of the past. Every protocol whose questions are n such draws, independent of one another and of the answers, needs

  n ≥ 0.1·K·L/δ − 0.4

for failure at most δ for every member, whatever it deploys, while the K rare inputs, asked once each, give failure 0. So here random calls cost at least L/(10δ) − 0.4/K times as much as chosen questions. And R1 is sharp for rehearsal up to a factor of about 7: by R1, L·K ln 2/δ rounds suffice. Compared with the count of N2c, which holds for every class, the bound for random questions is the larger one only if L/δ > 2^27. On the classes of N2b there is no such gap: rehearsal has failure at most δ for every member after 2·d·L/δ rounds, which is within the factor 16/(1 − 10δ) of the lower bound of N2b (Proposition 3.10).

### 4.4 What low stakes requires

**Theorem N5 (tolerance of k faults)** (proved here: Proposition 5.6). Let k ≥ 0 and g ≥ 1, and let N := g/(2δ) be an integer. There are a scaffold of horizon L = g·(k + 1) + k and a class of N + 1 functions such that the human system of every member tolerates k faults with failure 0, in the sense of Section 3.4 with the runs that have the human system's outcome as the good ones, and yet every protocol whose deployed run fails with probability at most δ for every member has

  n ≥ (1 − 4δ)·(L − k)/(4·(k + 1)·δ),

with n counted under the zero function.

*The class and the scaffold.* The inputs are N *regions* of 2k + 1 inputs each. The class consists of the zero function f₀ and, for each region, the function that is 1 on that region and 0 elsewhere. The scaffold draws g regions independently and uniformly. In each of them it asks the inputs of the region one after another until k + 1 answers are 0 or k + 1 answers are 1, and that answer is the *value* of the region. The outcome is 1 if some region has the value 1, and 0 otherwise. A run that would need more than L calls, or more than the 2k + 1 inputs of a region, stops with the outcome 0.

*Idea.* Every member is constant on a region. So with the member's own answers a region ends after k + 1 calls, and k replaced answers can neither change the value of a region nor use up the L calls. For the bound, a question anywhere in a region shows the member on the whole region, and the regions drawn play the part of the cells of N2, with g in the place of L. Proof: Proposition 5.6.

By R3, L·K ln 2/((k + 1)·δ) questions suffice here, with K = log₂(N + 1). So tolerance of k faults divides the sufficient number by k + 1, and in this example the necessary number as well. What remains between the two numbers is the factor K, for which see N8 (Section 9.1), and constants.

*What it does not say.* The scaffold is an instrument for a lower bound. The inputs of a region are one question in 2k + 1 wordings, put to a deterministic human, who answers all of them alike: asking it k + 1 times guards against errors of the imitator and against nothing else. And it is not claimed that tolerance never lowers the price by more than the factor k + 1: a tolerant scaffold with few possible inputs is cheap, as after N2.

### 4.5 A human who is not deterministic

**Theorem N6 (a human who varies)** (proved here: Theorems 6.1 and 6.3; the method is Assouad's, for which see Yu 1997). Let a protocol ask its questions and then deploy an imitator, which is used from then on. Its *error* is the total variation between the law of a run answered by this imitator and the law of the human system's run, in expectation over the protocol's coins and the human's answers to its questions. For every K ≥ 1 there are classes of 2^K samplers with equal weights for which error at most ε for every member needs

(a) n ≥ K/(512·ε²) questions on the scaffold that makes one call at an input drawn uniformly from K inputs, if ε ≤ 1/16;

(b) n ≥ L·K/(8192·ε²) questions on the scaffold that draws one of K inputs uniformly and puts it L times, if ε ≤ 1/64.

Here the expected number of questions is at most n under every member.

*The classes.* There are K inputs, and the answers are 0 and 1. For each of the 2^K ways of giving every input a sign, the class has the sampler that answers 1 with probability 1/2 + b at the inputs with the sign plus and with probability 1/2 − b at the others. In (a) b = 4ε, and in (b) b = 16ε/√L.

*Idea.* The two-point argument of N1 is applied to each input: two members that differ in the sign of one input can be told apart only by questions at that input, and n questions leave few for each of K inputs. An imitator that has the wrong sign at an input errs there by at least b at one call, and by an amount of the order of b·√L over a run of L calls at that input; this is why b can be smaller by the factor √L in (b). Proof: Theorems 6.1 and 6.3.

Rehearsal reaches error ε with K ln 2/(2ε²) rounds on the scaffold of (a) (R2, the case of questions that do not depend on the answers, with L = 1), and with (1 + ε)·L·K ln 2/ε² rounds on every scaffold of horizon L (R2(b)). So the orders K/ε² and L·K/ε² are right, and there is no logarithm to save as in N2c. The constants are far apart: by the factor 512·ln 2/2 = 177 for one call and by about 5700 for runs.

*What it does not say.* The classes depend on ε. And the bounds are for an imitator that is trained once and then used. If a single run is all that is wanted, and its law is averaged over the training, no such bound holds: for one call at a fixed input, one recorded answer of the human, replayed, has exactly the human's law.

### 4.6 What imitation amplifies

**Corollary N2d (answers replaced for each answer spent)** (proved here: Proposition 7.2). Let the human be deterministic. A protocol asks n questions and then answers r runs of a scaffold of horizon L, each on a fresh z and ρ. Let F be a bound, valid for every member of the class, on the expected number of failed runs among the r, and put δ := F/r. The human would have given up to r·L answers, and the *amplification* r·L/n is the number of answers replaced for each answer spent.

(a) *The class and the scaffold of N2* with L/(2δ) cells, where L/(2δ) is an integer and δ < 1/4. Every protocol has r·L/n ≤ 4F/(1 − 4δ), with n counted under f₀.

(b) *A class and scaffold of N2b,* where L/(2δ) is an integer and δ ≤ 1/20. Every protocol has r·L/n ≤ 8F·log₂(L/(2δ))/((1 − 10δ)·K), with n counted for a member drawn uniformly.

(c) *Rehearsal,* for every class of functions, every scaffold and every member. With n = ⌈L·K ln 2/δ⌉ rounds it has r·L/n ≥ F/(K ln 2 + δ/L). If the human system tolerates k faults with failure 0, the same holds with K ln 2/(k + 1) in the place of K ln 2, in the number of rounds and in the bound.

*Proof.* (a), (b): from a deployed system for the r runs one gets a system for a single run that fails with probability at most F/r = δ for every member, by putting the run at a uniformly drawn place among r runs and filling the earlier places with runs on fresh inputs. Then n is bounded from below by N2b in (b), and in (a) by the bound n ≥ L/(4δ) − L of N2, which in this case holds for every δ < 1/4 (Corollary 2.2). (c): by R1 each run is unfaithful with probability at most δ; under tolerance use R3. ∎

So on the class of (a) a guarantee for every member saves no human work unless failed runs are allowed: if F < 1/4 − δ, the amplification is below 1, however many runs there are. Imitation saves work across runs, and the price is counted in failed runs. By (c) rehearsal breaks even, with amplification 1, once about K ln 2 failed runs are allowed in all. On the classes of (b) no protocol breaks even before (1 − 10δ)·K/(8·log₂(L/(2δ))) failed runs are allowed.

*What it does not say.* (a) and (b) are worst cases over classes that are matched to L and to the rate δ = F/r. For a fixed F the class grows with r; a fixed class of this kind never needs more questions than it has cells, and after that every further run costs nothing. (c) is in K. For a person under a generic prior, with K of the order of 10⁹ bits (Section 9.1), rehearsal breaks even after about 7·10⁸ failed runs, and on the classes of (b) no protocol does before about 7·10⁶ (at L = 1000 and δ = 0.01): there (c) is empty.

## For Section 6: lower bounds for inputs from outside

**Theorem N3 (N possible shifts)** (proved here: `proofs/lower-bounds.md`, Theorem 4.1). Let X₁, …, X_N be disjoint nonempty sets of inputs, and let the class consist of the zero function and the N functions that are 1 on one of these sets and 0 elsewhere. Under *shift i* the task is one call at an input drawn from a law on X_i. Let 0 < a ≤ 1/2. If the deployed answer is right with probability at least 1/2 + a under every shift and for every member, then n ≥ 2aN, with n counted under the zero function. And ⌈2aN⌉ questions suffice.

So every shift that is to be covered has to be paid for, in proportion to the advantage a over a fair coin. One question in a randomly chosen set X_i gives 1/2 + 1/(2N) under every shift; it is only for protocols without coins that "better than a coin under every shift" takes a question in every X_i. *In bits:* for every ℓ ≥ 1 there are 2^ℓ disjoint decidable sets, and laws on them, each described by a program of at most ℓ + 2·log₂ℓ + c bits, where c is a constant of the reference machine. For these sets N3 gives n ≥ 2a·2^ℓ. So to be prepared for every shift that a program of ℓ bits describes costs at least of the order of 2^ℓ questions, up to a factor polynomial in ℓ. *With critical calls:* let shift i be the scaffold of N2 on ⌈L/(2δ)⌉ cells of its own, and let the class consist of the zero function and the functions that are 1 at one cell. Then failure at most δ ≤ 1/6 under every shift and for every member needs n ≥ N·(L/(4δ) − 3L/2) (`proofs/lower-bounds.md`, Theorem 4.3).

*What it does not say.* N3 is a worst case over one class, which can change the answers on one set X_i and nowhere else. Like N1 it limits a protocol only for a class that contains such members.

**Proposition S2 (a mixture of task laws)** (proved here: `proofs/lower-bounds.md`, Proposition 4.4; the inequality is the standard domination by a mixture). Let D₁, D₂, … be finitely or countably many laws of the top-level input, with weights π_i > 0 of total at most 1, and run Protocol R with their mixture as the top-level law: z is drawn from D_i with probability proportional to π_i. Then every D_i is at most 1/π_i times the mixture. So by S1(a), when the deployed run draws z from D_i, every bound of Section 3 on the deployed run holds with the factor 1/π_i; for a deterministic human the deployed run is then unfaithful with probability at most L·K ln 2/(π_i·n). For N laws with weights 1/N the factor is N, and for N laws with disjoint supports there is a class on which no protocol avoids a factor N (N3).

*What S2 needs.* The mixture has to be sampled. A procedure that halts with probability one can do this if a sampler of D_i that halts with probability one can be computed from i, and if i can be drawn with probability proportional to π_i. An example is the family of all programs p of a fixed machine with coins, each stopped at a fixed computable time bound, where p is drawn by choosing its length ℓ with probability 2^(−ℓ−1) and then ℓ fair bits. Then π_p = 2^(−2ℓ−1), and a law that a program of ℓ bits samples within the time bound costs the factor 2^(2ℓ+1). The exponent 2ℓ comes from this way of drawing the length: if the length ℓ is drawn with probability 4/((ℓ + 1)·(ℓ + 2)·(ℓ + 3)), the factor is 2^ℓ·(ℓ + 1)·(ℓ + 2)·(ℓ + 3)/4 (`proofs/lower-bounds.md`, Remark 4.4a). By N3 there is a class on which all shifts of ℓ bits together cost at least of the order of 2^ℓ, up to a factor polynomial in ℓ.

The time bound cannot simply be dropped. No sampler that halts with probability one dominates, with a finite factor, the law of every sampler that halts with probability one. The universal mixture over all programs does, but no program that halts with probability one samples it; a procedure with an oracle for the halting problem does (`proofs/lower-bounds.md`, Proposition 4.4(c)). With ordinary computation the time bound can be made to grow. Take time bounds b(1) ≤ b(2) ≤ …; in round t draw p as in the example and run it for at most b(t) steps; if it has not stopped, the round asks nothing. Then for a deterministic human and for every law D that a program p_D samples while halting with probability one,

  Pr[the deployed run with z drawn from D is unfaithful] ≤ 2^(2|p_D|+1)·L·K ln 2/n + (1/n)·Σ_{t ≤ n} τ_D(b(t)),

where |p_D| is the length of p_D and τ_D(b) is the probability that p_D runs for more than b steps (`proofs/lower-bounds.md`, Proposition 4.6). If b(t) tends to infinity, the last term tends to 0 as n grows, at a rate that depends on the program p_D and is not uniform over programs.

*What it does not say.* S2 gives nothing for a law that the mixture does not dominate (S4(iii)). Its bounds are in K and are empty for a person under a generic prior, like those of Section 3. And whether some other protocol with ordinary computation has a bound of the last kind whose second term vanishes uniformly over programs is not decided here.
