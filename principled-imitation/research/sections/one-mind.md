### 5.1 One program from rehearsal

The imitator of Section 2 is a *committee*: at every call a hypothesis is drawn afresh from the posterior and gives the answer. The human is one program. Can a single *member* of H take the committee's place, for a whole run or for all runs? This subsection answers from single questions, as rehearsal asks them; Section 5.2 answers from recorded runs of the human system. As in Section 3, A1 to A3 hold and the deployment is closed. Statements cited by number alone are those of `proofs/one-mind.md`.

Every upper bound below that contains K says something only when the number of questions n exceeds a multiple of K. For a person under a generic prior K is of the order of 10⁹ bits (Section 9.1), and all these bounds are empty. What the subsection contributes is the comparison between the committee and a member.

**Theorem R4 (one posterior draw per run; proved here).** Let the human be a function f in H; the other hypotheses may answer at random. Rehearse as in Protocol R (Section 2), but in each round let one hypothesis, drawn from the posterior, answer every call of the run. Then

  Σ_{t=1}^{n} Pr[the run of round t is not the human system's run on the same (z, ρ)] ≤ (2L²/(L + 1))·K ln 2.

So let each deployed run be answered by one hypothesis drawn from the posterior of a uniformly chosen round T ≤ n, with T and the hypothesis drawn afresh for each run. The deployed run is then the human system's run on the same (z, ρ), except with probability at most 2L²·K ln 2/((L + 1)·n).

*Proof.* Fix a round t, the answers recorded before it, and (z, ρ); let x₁, x₂, … be the questions of the human system's run. Whoever answers a run on (z, ρ), its questions are x₁, x₂, … up to and including the call of its first wrong answer (Lemma 1.4). Let p_j be the probability that the run of a draw from the posterior first goes wrong at call j; the p_j add up to the probability u that this run is not the human system's. A hypothesis gives a wrong answer at x_j at least as often as its run first goes wrong at call j, so the mixture M^(t) is wrong at x_j with probability at least p_j. And with probability 1 − p₁ − … − p_{j−1} the run of round t reaches call j without a wrong answer and asks x_j there. Let m_t be the probability that M^(t) gives to a wrong answer at the question of round t, and m_t := 0 if none is asked. The asked call is uniform among L calls and independent of the run (A3(i)), so

  E[m_t] ≥ (1/L)·Σ_j p_j·(1 − p₁ − … − p_{j−1}) = (1/L)·(u − u²/2 + Σ_j p_j²/2) ≥ u·(L + 1)/(2L²),

because Σ_j p_j² ≥ u²/L (at most L of the p_j are positive) and u² ≤ u. As in the proof of R1, the product of the factors 1 − m_t over the rounds is at least w(f) = 2^(−K) on every path, so Σ_t m_t ≤ K ln 2. Take expectations and sum over t. The second claim is the average over T. Details: Theorem 2.1 and Corollary 2.2. ∎

The bound is less than twice the bound L·K ln 2/n of the committee (Section 3.2). For K > 1 the constant is exact up to the factor (1 − 1/L)(1 − 1/K), in a class that is very large (Proposition 2.5). Li and Zhang (2025, Appendix A) call the analysis of this variant an open question: in their terms it is state-wise annotation with *first-step mixing*, one policy drawn for a whole episode, where Protocol R has *each-step mixing*, a fresh draw at every step. For a deterministic human R4 is such an analysis. It bounds the probability that a run deviates. Their bound for Protocol R is in the cost of recovering from one deviation and rests on the expected number of deviating calls, which R4 does not bound.

*What it does not say.* R4 does not bound how many calls of an unfaithful run are unfaithful; by R5 it can be a quarter of them. And R4 has no version for a human who is not deterministic. Let the scaffold draw one of N questions and put it to the human three times (L = 3), and let the human err with probability ε ≤ ½ each time, independently. Let H contain the human with weight ½ (K = 1) and every function g of the question with weight ½·Π_z P(g(z)|z), the product being over the N questions z. Such a function is the human with the coins filled in: one outcome of the human's coin for each question. As long as no question has been asked twice, which is likely for about √N rounds, the posterior weight of the human stays ½; a posterior draw is then a function with probability ½, and a function gives the same answer three times. So in round t the law of the draw's run is at total variation at least (3/2)·ε(1 − ε)·(1 − t/N) from the human system's, while the law of the committee's run is within t/N of it (Example 3.1). With N large compared with n² this lasts for n rounds: for one posterior draw per run, no bound that depends on K, L and n alone tends to 0 as n grows. In this example it would help to put every call of the rehearsal runs to the human, because two different answers to one question refute every function; in a variant whose runs repeat no question it does not help (Proposition 3.2). Recorded runs of the human system do help (H1, Section 5.2).

**Proposition R5 (low stakes: a member against the committee or its vote; proved here, by examples).** On the scaffold and the two classes described below, a run answered by a member of the class can have at least L/4 unfaithful calls with probability at least a constant times L/n after n questions, while a run answered by the committee has two or more unfaithful calls with probability at most 2·(L/n)². So where a quarter of the answers may be wrong, a member fails as if no fault were tolerated, and the committee does not. Precisely:

(a) Class A, with K = 1, N = n ≥ 100 and L ≤ √n. After n rounds of rehearsal, answered as in Section 2 or as in R4, a run answered by one draw from the posterior has at least L/4 unfaithful calls with probability at least 0.11·L/n. A run answered by the committee has more than k unfaithful calls with probability at most (L/n)^(k+1)/(k + 1)!, for every k ≥ 0.

(b) Class B, with K = log₂(2n), N = 2n, n ≥ 16 and L ≤ n. Take any protocol that puts at most n questions to the human and then answers each run by one member of the class, chosen without seeing the top-level input of the run. For some human in the class, who may depend on the protocol, such a run has at least L/4 unfaithful calls with probability at least L/(8n). After at most n questions, however they were chosen, and for every human in the class, a run answered by the committee has more than k unfaithful calls with probability at most L/(2n²) + (3L/(2n))^(k+1)/(k + 1)!, for every k ≥ 0. The same holds for a run answered by the committee's *plurality vote*, which gives at each input the answer of largest posterior weight; the vote is one program, but not a member of the class.

*The scaffold and the classes.* The top-level input of a run is a list of L symbols, drawn independently and uniformly from N values. The input of call j is the j-th symbol together with a *flag*. The flag is empty until a call is answered 1, and from then on it is the symbol of that call. Answers are 0 or 1. The function g₀ answers 0 at every input. For each of the N values a, the *trap* g_a answers 1 at the symbol a with the empty flag and at every input whose flag is a, and 0 elsewhere; so in a run that it answers alone it answers 0 until a first occurs, and 1 at that call and at every later call. A trap is a function of its input alone, as every hypothesis is: the flag carries what it needs of the run so far. Class A consists of g₀, which is the human, with weight ½, and of the N traps with weight 1/(2N) each. Class B consists of the N traps with equal weights, and the human is one of them.

In (a), call a run good if fewer than L/4 of its answers are 1; this set of runs does not mention the human. The human answers 0 everywhere, so the human system tolerates ⌈L/4⌉ − 1 faults with failure 0 (Section 3.4), and the unfaithful calls of a draw are its answers 1. So the draw fails with probability at least 0.11·L/n, and failure δ needs n ≥ 0.11·L/δ questions, where R3 gives the committee failure at most K ln 2/(θn) with K = 1 and θ = ¼, whatever L. With traps slightly heavier than the human (N = 2n, K close to log₂(2n + 1), L ≤ n), the heaviest member that is *consistent* with the answers to any n questions, that is, gives each of them, fails in the same way, with probability at least (9/32)·L/n; ties are broken without sight of the run's input. Proof: Propositions 4.2, 4.3 and 4.6, Corollary 4.4 and Remark 4.5.

*What it does not say.* These are examples; nothing is said about classes in general. In (b) the count is of answers that differ from the human's, so a set of good runs that is defined by it refers to the human. Against a set of good runs that does not refer to the human, a member chosen without seeing the run's input fails exactly as often as the human system would if that member were the human (Corollary 4.4(c)); the examples of that kind are (a) and the one with heavier traps. Whether the vote of a committee uses the tolerance of faults in general, as it does in class B, is open.

**Proposition R6 (which program, and when; proved here).**

(a) *The heaviest consistent hypothesis must not answer the rehearsal runs.* Let H be a class of functions and f ∈ H the human. Call the hypothesis that answers the rehearsal runs the *driver*. Order H by non-increasing weight, and let the driver of each round be the first hypothesis in that order that is consistent with the answers recorded so far, that is, the heaviest consistent one, with ties broken by the order. Then the expected number of rehearsal runs that are not the human system's run is at most L·(i₀ − 1) ≤ L·(2^K − 1), where i₀ − 1 is the number of hypotheses ranked before f. The bound is attained: there is a class of L + 1 functions with equal weights and f ranked last in which every rehearsal run is unfaithful until the L other functions are refuted, and that takes L² rounds in expectation. In that class at most L·ln(L + 1) rehearsal runs are unfaithful in expectation when the committee answers them (R1), and at most (2L²/(L + 1))·ln(L + 1) when posterior draws do (R4).

(b) *After rehearsal, for a deterministic human.* Let H be a class of functions and f ∈ H the human, and rehearse as in Section 2 for n rounds. With probability at least 1 − 2δ, every consistent g in H with w(g) ≥ w(f), for instance the heaviest consistent member, satisfies

  Pr[the run answered by g alone is not the human system's run on the same (z, ρ)] < 2L·(K ln 2 + ln(1/δ))/n.

So does one hypothesis drawn from the final posterior and kept for good, with probability at least 1 − 2δ over the rehearsal and the draw.

(c) *After rehearsal, for a human who is not deterministic.* Let H be any class with P ∈ H, and rehearse as in Section 2 for n rounds. Let ν̂ maximise w(ν)² times the probability that ν gives to the recorded answers. With probability at least 1 − 2δ,

  H²(Q_ν̂, Q_P) < L·(28·K ln 2 + 40·ln(1/δ))/n, and hence TV(Q_ν̂, Q_P) < √(L·(56·K ln 2 + 80·ln(1/δ))/n).

Here Q_ν̂ is the law of a run in which ν̂ alone answers every call, and H² is the squared Hellinger distance of Lemma L4 (Section 3.2).

*Idea.* (a) A driver that goes wrong early in a run hides what the other hypotheses would answer later in that run. While a wrong hypothesis drives, each of its unfaithful runs refutes it with probability at least 1/L, namely when the asked call is the call of its first wrong answer; so each hypothesis ranked before f costs at most L unfaithful runs in expectation. Without a scaffold this is the standard gap between an arbitrary consistent learner, with up to |H| − 1 mistakes, and the halving algorithm, with at most log₂|H| (see Littlestone 1988; standard).

(b) Let u be the probability that the run answered by g alone is not the human system's. Then g is wrong at some question of the human system's run with probability u. So in a round in which the committee's run is the human system's run except with probability q, the question refutes g with probability at least (u − q)/L. With probability at least 1 − δ these q add up, over the rounds, to less than L·(K ln 2 + ln(1/δ)); this is R1 in a form with high probability. So a g with large u stays consistent for n rounds only with a probability that is exponentially small in n·u/L, and a union bound over the class with the weights w, which is the Occam bound, gives the claim. Both steps need a martingale, because the questions depend on the earlier answers.

(c) In each round the square root of the likelihood ratio of a hypothesis ν to P is multiplied by a factor whose mean is 1 − h, where h is 1/L times the expected sum of H²(ν(·|x), P(·|x)) over the calls x of the committee's run. So for a ν that is far from the human along the committee's runs, w(ν)/w(P) times that square root is small with high probability; and ν̂ does as well as P under the squared weights only if this product is at least 1. The products can be added up over the class, because the weights w add up to at most 1. Here the square is used: with the weights w themselves the sum would run over √w(ν), and these need not add up to a finite number. So ν̂ is close to the human at the calls of the committee's runs. Up to here this is the standard analysis of selection by description length with a doubled penalty, which for independent data is in Zhang (2006, Theorem 4.1) and, as Zhang reports, in Barron and Cover (1991). The committee's runs are close to the human system's, as in R2, and Lemma L4(a) with the triangle inequality for the Hellinger distance passes from the calls of those runs to the law of the runs that ν̂ answers alone.

Proof: Proposition 5.1 and Example 5.2 for (a); Theorem 5.5 and Corollary 5.6 for (b); Theorem 3.8 for (c).

*What it does not say.* R6 does not say which member is selected: nothing here separates the human's own program from another member whose runs on this scaffold have the same law (Section 9.4). Parts (b) and (c) are proved for rehearsal runs answered by the committee. When posterior draws answer them, (b) holds with 3 in the place of 2 (Proposition 5.7); for (c) nothing is proved, and the committee's own runs are then controlled only up to a factor 2^K (Proposition 3.5). In (c) the weights w themselves give no bound in terms of K, L, n and δ alone. A draw from the final posterior fails by the example after R4: as long as no question has been asked twice it is one of the functions with probability ½, and the runs of a function are at total variation at least 1 − (1 − ε)³ from the human system's. And the member of largest posterior weight under the weights w can be a function whose runs are at total variation 7/8 from the human system's. This happens with probability at least 0.98 − n²/(2N) in a class with K = 4, L = 3 and 2^(n+2) functions of N questions, in which the human is a fair coin (Example 3.6; the construction is that of Zhang 2006, Section 4.4). In a class in which the numbers √w(ν) have a finite sum, in particular in a finite class, the weights w do give a bound, which contains the logarithm of that sum (Remark 3.9).

**Squared weights are the steeper prior.** In the example after R4 the weights w cannot tell the human from the human with the coins filled in. The squared weights of R6(c) can: the runs of such a function are far in law from the human system's, and by R6(c) the runs of the selected member are close to it once n is large. This is the device that the researcher's notes propose for that purpose: a simplicity prior steeper than the universal one, for hypotheses with private coins, that is, hypotheses that answer every call with fresh coins, as the samplers of Section 1 do. Paper 2 (see References) makes it exact as the weights w^λ with λ > 1. For questions drawn independently from one law it proves that the programs which maximise w^λ times the likelihood are, with high probability, exactly the shortest programs that answer as the sampler does at almost every question, once the number of questions exceeds a bound in λ, the length of those programs, the confidence and a margin, which is the least distance from the sampler of the short programs that differ from it (its Theorem 6.3); at λ = 1 there is no such bound (its Proposition 6.6). R6(c) is the case λ = 2 for the questions of rehearsal, and H2 (Section 5.2) has every λ > 1 for recorded runs.
