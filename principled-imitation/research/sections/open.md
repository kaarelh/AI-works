## 13. Open questions

The questions are ordered by how much an answer would change the conclusions of Section 0. Each proof file ends with a longer list for its part.

1. **A prior under which K is affordable.** Every positive result on imitating the human's answers is in K, and the theorems hold for any prior fixed before the data. Nothing here says how to obtain a prior that puts weight 2^(−K) on a given person with K in the thousands and not in the billions, nor how to tell that one has it. With the certificate one can at least find out afterwards, for a human who is nearly deterministic on the inputs that matter.

2. **Classes without patches.** If the human is separated from every other member of a class, the posterior concentrates exponentially fast (I1), and the lower bounds of Section 4 do not apply, because they need a class that can change one place at a time. The factor K remains: I1 says nothing before n exceeds 2L·K ln 2/γ, γ being the separation. Is there a natural class of this kind that contains the humans one cares about? For functions on the integers the companion note on isolation in this repository (`ultranatural/note.md`, see References) has candidates; for samplers, and with a prior to fall back on when the human is outside the class, nothing is proved.

3. **Both prices in one imitator, for a human who varies.** For a deterministic human, the imitator built from tests pays at most K + 3 disagreements if the human is in the class and, in any case, at most log₂(1/π(B)) + 3 violations of each property that the human keeps (Pr5 with the halved weights of Section 8.4). For a human who varies, the tilted mixture trades the two against each other (Section 8.5). Is there one randomised imitator whose divergence from the human, summed over the questions, is of order K under A2, and whose counts of answers in each test stay within order √(n·(Λ(B) + ln n)) of the human's with no assumption?

4. **The factor K, exactly.** Over classes of K bits the least number of questions is of order K·L/(δ·log₂(L/δ)), reached by targeted questions that may lie off every run (N2c); rehearsal's own worst case is of order K·L/δ. A variant that asks only calls of its own runs and saves the factor ln(L/ln L) is in the proof file (`proofs/lower-bounds.md`, Proposition 3.9). For additive work, where a run is good if at most k of its L answers are wrong, the only lower bound here that contains K is n ≥ (1 − 2k/L − 2δ)·K, on the class of all functions from K inputs to {0, 1} (`proofs/assumptions.md`, Proposition 6.4). Whether the factor 1/(θδ) of R3 is ever needed for such work is open, and so is any lower bound with K under the hypotheses of Proposition R3a.

5. **Low stakes for a human who varies.** R3 costs of the order of K/θ² questions in an example, against K/θ for a deterministic human. When is K/θ enough, and must the number of questions grow as the allowed excess failure shrinks?

6. **The last round.** All bounds are for the mixture of a uniformly chosen round. For a deterministic human, the mixture after all n answers can have (L − 1)/(40·√L) times as many unfaithful calls in expectation as the bound L·K ln 2/n of a uniformly chosen round allows, in an example whose class contains samplers that are not functions (`proofs/rehearsal-core.md`, Proposition 6.3); and its probability of an unfaithful run exceeds that bound by at most a factor of about 2L·(1 + ln(n/(2L·K ln 2))) (Proposition 6.4 there). Whether a constant factor holds when all members of the class are functions is open. For recorded runs (H1), whether the last posterior alone has the bound is a problem that Grünwald and Kotłowski (2011) pose.

7. **One program for a human who varies.** A member chosen by squared weights after rehearsal by the mixture has a bound (R6(c)). Open: whether a posterior draw for each run can be supported by fewer answers than whole recorded runs of the human system; whether R6(c) holds when the rehearsal runs are answered by posterior draws and not by the mixture; and the general form of R5, that is, whether the plurality vote of the committee always inherits the fault tolerance that the committee uses.

8. **Abstention.** For the batch rule (Ab5): a version for inputs that must be answered as they arrive, with no answer of the human shown afterwards; a version for a scaffold whose questions depend on the answers; whether the rule can drive its own rehearsal as the threshold rule does in Ab1(b); and a version for a human who varies. For the other rules: a bound on what the threshold rule refuses (its width as a whitelist); whether the novelty rule needs s > 2K or s > K suffices; rehearsal driven by the minimum rule, which would remove the term L(L − 1) from Ab2(d); what replaces the count of abstentions for samplers (Section 7.5); computable versions of sep and of the plausible sets under time bounds.

9. **The reference machine.** Whether the mixture keeps a simple property at a fresh input, at a price in the property's complexity, is false on some universal machines (Pr2(c)) and open for any fixed one that would be called natural. No definition of natural is known that makes the question precise.

10. **Episodes.** When the unit put to the human is an episode with memory inside it, then for a deterministic human R1, R3 and V1(a) hold with episodes for calls (E1). For nested episodes and a human who varies the whole-run bound carries an extra factor, the smaller of four times the largest number of calls in one episode and half the number of episodes of a run; whether it is needed is open.

11. **Constants.** The best constant in Lemma L4(a) lies between 2.9639 and 2.9642. The constants of the lower bounds for a human who varies (N6) are about 10⁴ away from the upper bounds.
