**What may fail (details: Section 3.7).** A1 fails for a person who remembers, within a run the earlier calls and from one question of the protocol to the next the earlier questions; the recorded answers need not show it. A2 fails if no hypothesis of the class answers as the human does, and the bounds then compare the imitator with the best hypothesis on the runs that the imitator makes, not on those of the human system. A3 fails if a hypothesis can compute a coin of the protocol, for instance from a seed, or if the weights were chosen after answers had been seen; errors can then stay at a constant rate however many questions are asked.

### 3.7 The assumptions in practice

Sections 3.1 to 3.6 assume A1 to A3. This subsection takes the three in turn: how the assumption can fail, what the theorems still give, and whether the protocol's data show the failure. Numbers in this subsection refer to `proofs/assumptions.md`.

**A1: a human who remembers.** The protocol's data need not show that A1 fails. Let the human answer the second call of a run from memory of the first. In rehearsal and in validation the human answers single calls in separate sittings, and answers them as a human without memory would. The protocol then imitates the scaffold answered by a human without memory, and R1, R3 and V1 hold for that system; its runs can be at total variation 1/2 from the runs of the real human (Example 1.1).

*Memory inside a piece of work* can be kept if the piece of work is the unit that is put to the human. Call such a unit an *episode*: the human starts afresh, receives messages one at a time and answers each, and an answer may depend on the earlier messages and answers of the same episode and on nothing else of the past. If the human is reset between episodes in this way, then for a deterministic human R1, R3 and V1(a) hold with whole episodes in the place of calls (Theorem E1, Section 9.3).

*Memory from one question to the next* is another matter. A1 is also an assumption about the n questions of rehearsal: the answer to a question must not depend on the questions before it. One person who answers n questions remembers them. A1 for the rehearsal then needs a fresh person for each question, and what is imitated is a pool of people: the sampler "a member of the pool, drawn afresh at every call", whose human system is a system of strangers who pass notes to one another, and whose K is the price of the pool's answer distributions, not of one person's (Remark 1.1a). For one person who answers all the questions, what remains is Theorem E2 (Section 9.3).

*A human who knows that it is a rehearsal,* and who answers differently because no run depends on the answer, is imitated as such. The theorems hold for the human as that human answers in rehearsal, and a protocol all of whose questions are answered in that setting learns nothing about the same human in another (Proposition 1.15).

**A2: a human outside the class.** Without A2, R1 becomes an oracle inequality: for every ν in H,

  E[Σ_{calls x of the run of M^(T)} KL(P(·|x) ‖ M^(T)(·|x))] ≤ L·ln(1/w(ν))/n + (1/n)·Σ_{t=1}^{n} E[Σ_{calls x of a run of M^(t)} KL(P(·|x) ‖ ν(·|x))]

(Theorem 2.1; standard: it is the regret bound of Section 3.1 against an arbitrary member, and its right side has the form of the index of resolvability of Barron and Cover 1991). The second term is the divergence of ν from the human, summed along the *mixtures'* runs, not the human system's, and averaged over the rounds.

*What it does not say.* The second term does not shrink with n, and it can be infinite for a hypothesis whose runs are exactly the human system's. This is how the protocol behaves, not a weakness of the proof. In a class of two hypotheses with equal weights, one of which has exactly the human system's runs, that one is refuted with probability 0.44, at an input that only wrong answers reach; the imitator is then the other hypothesis for ever, and its run differs from the human system's half the time (Example 2.3). The protocol goes on as before when this happens. For a deterministic human the certificate shows the failure, since V1(a) does not assume A2; for a human who varies it is shown only by the listed tests of V1(b) and, with few possible answers, by questions put twice (Section 8.7).

**A3: the protocol's coins.** *What it is for.* A hypothesis may model how the data set was made. Two statements say what A3 does about such hypotheses (proved here: Theorem 3.2(b) and (c)).

First, a hypothesis loses weight at the rate at which it deviates from the human on the mixtures' runs, whatever it computes about the experiment. For ν in H let d_t(ν) be 1/L times the expected sum of H²(P(·|x), ν(·|x)) over the calls x of a run of M^(t). With probability at least 1 − δ, for all n at once, the posterior odds of ν against the human are at most

  (w(ν)/w(P))·δ^(−2)·exp(−2·Σ_{t=1}^{n} d_t(ν)).

The reason is A3(i) and (v): given the run, the asked call is a uniformly drawn call of a fresh run of the current mixture.

Second, a prior fixed first cannot favour a hypothesis that knows coins drawn afterwards. Let c* be a random element of the experiment, for instance the sequence of the asked indices, and let c ↦ ν_c send each of its values to a hypothesis, so that ν_{c*} is "the hypothesis that knows these coins". By A3(iii) the expected weight of ν_{c*} is at most the largest probability with which the map yields any one hypothesis. For a map that is one-to-one this is the probability of the likeliest value of the coins: L^(−n) for the asked indices of n rounds, and 2^(−ℓ), however large n is, if all the coins come from a seed of ℓ bits. That weight has still to be set against the human's 2^(−K) (Section 10, test 3).

*How it fails.* If the asked call is computed from the run with a seed of ℓ bits, a hypothesis that holds the seed is never refuted, and the deployed run has at least (L − 1)/(2^ℓ + 1) unfaithful calls in expectation for every n, in a class with K = 1 (Example 3.3). Rehearsal runs taken from a generator with a seed leave a floor of the same kind, although parts (i) to (iv) of A3 then hold: in an example with a seed of three bits and K = 1 the deployed answer is wrong with probability at least 1/18 for every n (Example 3.3a). Under the priors of these examples the floors are small for a long seed. Under a prior that favours the holder of the seed they are not, and this is the difference between a seed and physical randomness: by the second statement above, no prior fixed in advance can favour a hypothesis that knows coins drawn afresh. A prior that has seen the rehearsal data can memorise them: if half of its weight sits on a table of those data that gives one fixed answer b at every other input, and if inputs never repeat, the imitator answers b at a new input with probability at least 1/2 (Example 3.6).

The remedies are matters of design, and the designer can check each of them: physical randomness for every coin of the protocol, that is, for the asked index, for the z and ρ of every rehearsal and validation run and for the imitator's draws; inputs that carry nothing, such as a date, which a rehearsal input could not carry; and a prior fixed before the first coin is drawn.

## 9. What remains a bet

The theorems of Sections 3 to 8 are conditional. This section says what they rest on, and what that costs in the researcher's units. Numbers refer to `proofs/assumptions.md`. A closed deployment and A1 to A3 are assumed unless stated otherwise.

The bets are the following. The theorems assume them, and the protocol's own data do not confirm them, with the one exception named under the first.

1. *The prior.* That a prior can be had under which the human costs an affordable K. Every bound on imitation is in K, and nothing in this note says how to obtain such a prior, or how to tell in advance that one has it (Section 9.1; Section 13, question 1). Afterwards, for a human who is nearly deterministic, the certificate tells whether a given imitator is good enough (V1(a)); for a human who varies there is only the slow form of Section 8.7.
2. *Tolerance of faults.* That the human system reaches a good outcome when some of its answers are replaced. R3 assumes this. The protocol never observes a run of the human system, so it never tests it (Section 9.5).
3. *Rare inputs.* That what the prior says is acceptable at the inputs that rehearsal met less often than about once in n rounds (Section 9.2).
4. *A human without memory.* That the human answers as A1 says: without memory of earlier calls and of earlier questions, and as the same human would answer if a run depended on the answer (Sections 3.7 and 9.3).
5. *A class without patches.* A fifth bet, which one may add, is on the form of the class. It changes the rate and leaves the factor K (I1, Section 9.2).

A3 is not a bet of this kind. Its parts are matters of design, which the designer can arrange and check (Section 3.7), and so are the three conditions of a closed deployment when the scaffold calls nothing outside itself.

### 9.1 The budget

For an excess failure δ with a fraction θ of the calls replaceable, R3 needs n = K ln 2/(θδ) answers for a deterministic human and K ln 2/(2θ²δ²) otherwise. For the former and θ = δ = 0.1:

| human time | n | K paid for |
|---|---|---|
| a working life of 10⁵ hours, an hour per answer | 10⁵ | 1443 bits |
| 50 years, a year per answer | 50 | 0.72 bits |
| 10⁴ people for a year, a year per answer | 10⁴ | 144 bits |
| 10⁶ people for 2000 hours, an hour per answer | 2·10⁹ | 2.9·10⁷ bits |

*K for a person* is not known. Landauer's estimate of the learned content of a person's long-term memory, about 10⁹ bits (1986; not read in the source), suggests its order under a prior that knows nothing of the individual. It is not a bound on K, which could be smaller, since only the answers on the inputs of the closed system count (Section 1), or larger, since what is innate is not in that figure. This is what "of the order of 10⁹ bits" means wherever the note says it. At K = 10⁹, θ = δ = 0.1 and an hour per answer the count is 6.9·10¹⁰ answers, or 3.5·10⁷ years of 2000 hours. The researcher expects that an episode able to do research may take a year. With a year per answer one person's bounds are empty, and the certificate alone takes 300 human-years.

*More people* help less than their number suggests, for two reasons. The rows with many people imitate a pool and not a person (Section 3.7). And questions that are answered at the same time are all drawn with the same mixture, so that the bound counts batches of answers in the place of answers (Proposition 6.3). The exception is a scaffold whose questions do not depend on the answers: there one batch does the work of n rounds (Proposition 6.5). At the other end stands a scaffold that is a chain, each call's input being the list of the earlier answers: for a human whose answers along the chain are long random strings, every protocol needs as many batches, one after another, as the chain has links, except with the small probability of guessing an answer (Remark 6.6). With a year per answer, twenty years allow twenty batches one after another, whatever the number of people; for a scaffold as deep as a chain the bound of R3 then pays for 0.29 bits at θ = δ = 0.1.

*What a prior built from knowledge can buy.* The theorems hold for every prior that is fixed before the protocol's coins are drawn. So a prior built from all that is known about people may be used, and K is then the number of bits that this prior still lacks. But no prior makes K small for many candidates at once: under every prior at most 2^k candidates have K ≤ k, and the average of K over any N candidates is at least log₂ N (Proposition 2.4). So unless the prior happens to favour the human in question, K is at least the logarithm of the number of candidate humans that the prior, with all knowledge built in, cannot tell apart on the inputs of the closed system. And for some sets of 2^K candidates no protocol, with whatever prior, needs fewer questions than N2b and the next theorem say. That a given prior has a small K for a given person is the first bet of the list above.

**Theorem N8 (the factor K under low stakes; proved here).** Tolerance of faults that comes from replication does not remove the factor K. For all integers d ≥ 1, g ≥ 1 and k ≥ 0 and every δ < 1/10 for which N := g/(2δ) is an integer, there are a scaffold of L = g·(2k + 1) calls and a class of 2^K functions, K = d·log₂N, such that every member's human system tolerates k faults with failure 0, and yet every protocol whose deployed run fails with probability at most δ for every member needs, in expectation over a uniform member,

  n ≥ (1 − 10δ)·K·g/(8δ·log₂(g/(2δ)))

questions ("protocol" and "fails" as in Section 4). Rehearsal with equal weights needs at most 2g·K ln 2/δ + 1 (R3).

*The class and the scaffold.* The inputs are d blocks of N cells, each cell in 2k + 1 copies; a member is 1 on one cell of each block and 0 elsewhere. The scaffold draws a block and g independent uniform cells of it, asks every copy of each drawn cell, and outputs 1 if most copies of some drawn cell answered 1.

*Idea.* The blocks are d separate searches, as in N2b; copies add calls, not information. Proof: Theorem 6.1.

The count depends on L and k only through g = L/(2k + 1), the number of independent critical units of a run. So "whatever L" in R3 is no amplification of such work: with k = θL tolerated faults a run has fewer than 1/(2θ) units, and the lower bound is the one for g units that are not replicated.

*What it does not say.* It is a worst case over one family of classes and scaffolds. A scaffold that asks one input many times has many critical calls and needs one question.

**Additive work** (proved here: Proposition 6.4). The factor K is also forced where no single answer is critical, though without the factor 1/(θδ). Let a run make L calls at independent uniform inputs and be good if at most k of its answers differ from the human's; every member's human system then tolerates k faults with failure 0. On the class of all 2^K functions from K inputs to {0, 1}, a protocol whose run is good with probability at least 1 − δ for every member needs

  n ≥ (1 − 2k/L − 2δ)·K

questions in expectation over a uniform member, and K questions suffice. For k/L = δ = 0.1 the bound is 0.6·K: fifty answers cannot serve a class of more than 2^83 such candidates. The bound of R3 for this class is about 69·K at these values, so the factor 1/(θδ) is not needed here. Whether it is ever needed for additive work is open.

### 9.2 Rare situations, and isolation

Inputs that runs meet with probability far below 1/n are probably never asked. There the imitator does what the prior says, and R1 holds for two priors that disagree there (Proposition 7.1).

If the human were itself a draw from the prior, no theorem would be needed. After any number of questions, none included, a run answered by one draw from the posterior then has exactly the law of a run of the human system, the probabilities being taken over the prior as well (Proposition 7.7; this is the defining property of posterior sampling, which goes back to Thompson 1933). The theorems of this note are of another kind: they hold for every human in the class, also for one to whom the prior gives little weight. What the imitator does in a situation that rehearsal never met is in every case the prior's guess.

A universal prior keeps a doubt at each new kind of input. For a deterministic human, the mixture over all programs fails to give the human's answer at an unasked input x with probability at least 2^(−sep(x; A)−c), where A is the set of asked inputs and sep(x; A) is the length of a shortest program for a decidable set that contains x and no asked input (Sections 4.1 and 7.4). A prior without patches has no such doubt. The idea is that of the companion note on isolation in this repository (`ultranatural/note.md`; see References); here are its definitions with runs in the place of samples.

Let H be a class of functions, and for functions g and g′ let D(g, g′) be the probability, over z and ρ, that the run answered by g differs from the run answered by g′. Fix a scale γ in (0, 1]. A member g′ is a *patch* of a member g if 0 < D(g, g′) < γ: the two differ, but only where runs seldom go. A member is γ-*separated* if no member is a patch of it, that is, if D(g, g′) is 0 or at least γ for every member g′; a class is *without patches* at scale γ if all its members are separated. A member is γ-*isolated* if every patch of it in H has smaller weight. The *patch-free prior* at scale γ is w restricted to the isolated members, and these form a class without patches, since of two isolated members that were patches of each other each would be lighter than the other (Lemma 7.3).

**Theorem I1 (proved here; it is Theorem 9 of the companion note, with runs for samples).** Let H consist of functions, and let the human be a function f in H of weight 2^(−K) that is γ-separated. Rehearse as in Protocol R (Section 2), but in each round let one hypothesis drawn from the posterior answer the whole run. After n rounds the expected posterior weight of the members whose runs differ from the human's is at most

  2^K·(1 − γ/(2L))^n ≤ 2^K·e^(−γn/(2L)).

So a run answered by one draw from the posterior after the last round is the human system's run on the same z and ρ except with probability at most 2^K·e^(−γn/(2L)), and n ≥ (2L/γ)·(K ln 2 + ln(1/δ)) rounds bring this down to δ. No random round is needed: the bound is for the posterior after the last round.

*Idea.* Fix a round, and compare the hypothesis g that answers its run with a second hypothesis g′ drawn independently from the same posterior. On a given z and ρ a hypothesis leaves the human system's run at its first answer that differs from the human's, if it has one. If g′ has such an answer and it comes no later than that of g, the run of g reaches the call at which g′ first errs; the question lands on that call with probability 1/L and then refutes g′. Of the two draws each is the earlier one with the same probability, and a member whose runs differ from the human's leaves the human's run with probability at least γ. So a round refutes, in expectation, at least the fraction γ/(2L) of the prior weight of the surviving members whose runs differ from the human's. That weight is at most 1 at the start, and the human's weight 2^(−K) is never removed. Proof: Theorem 7.4.

*What it does not say.* The bound is below 1 only when n exceeds (2L/γ − 1)·K ln 2. So the bet changes the rate and not the price: the failure probability δ enters through ln(1/δ) where R4 has the factor 1/δ, the factor L·K stays, and the factor 1/γ comes in. For a person under a generic prior the bound is as empty as R1. Nor does the theorem say that a person is separated. Separation depends on the reference machine: for independent samples and two possible answers, exchanging the answers on a decidable set Q of small measure gives a machine at most K(Q) + c bits away for which the altered function is the isolated one and f is its patch (the companion note, Proposition 8(5)). The lower bounds of Section 4 do not contradict I1; their classes consist of patches of one another.

*The price of the bet* (proved here: Proposition 7.5). Let the human be an isolated member altered on a set of inputs that runs meet with probability ε, and let no member be closer to the human than the unaltered one. Then every imitator that answers each run with one member of the class has a run that differs from the human system's with probability at least ε, for every n. The data do not show it until a question lands on the altered set: until then every answer of the human is an answer of the unaltered member, and the posterior is the one that I1 describes for that member. After that the loss shows in the likelihood of the data, and the class may be left without a member that fits (Example 7.6). Mixing the full prior into the patch-free one with a share q restores every theorem in K, at the price of log₂(1/q) more bits (Proposition 7.5(c)); whether the exponential rate survives this is open.

No data decide between the two bets. For every set of inputs that the questions miss, a human and the same human altered on that set produce the same data (Proposition 7.1). The universal prior keeps a doubt at every new kind of input and learns at the rate 1/n; the patch-free prior has no doubt, learns at an exponential rate, and is wrong on a patch until a question lands on it.

### 9.3 Memory, and a human who changes

**Theorem E1 (episodes as the unit asked; proved here).** Let the scaffold group its calls into *episodes*, at most L_e in a run, the input of a call being the earlier messages and answers of its episode together with the new message. Let the human be reset between episodes and deterministic, each answer being a function of that input, and let this function be in H with weight 2^(−K). Rehearse with episodes in the place of calls: in each round an episode number is drawn uniformly from 1, …, L_e; the human carries out the episode of that number, as one episode, if the run has one; and the mixture answers all other calls, conditioning within an episode on its own earlier answers of that episode. Deploy the mixture of a uniformly chosen round. Then the deployed run has in expectation at most L_e·K ln 2/n *unfaithful episodes*, that is, episodes with an answer other than the human's, and it is the human system's run on the same z and ρ except with probability at most L_e·K ln 2/n. R3 and V1(a) hold with episodes in the place of calls: in R3 the human system has to tolerate answers replaced in at most k episodes of a run, and in a validation round the human carries out one uniformly drawn episode of a run of the imitator, whose own answer to each message is drawn alongside for comparison.

*Idea.* It is R1 with the episode as the unit. A human who is reset between episodes is, for the law of every run, a sampler whose input is the page of the episode so far (Lemma 1.3). A mixture that conditions on its own earlier answers of the episode is one posterior draw for each episode (Lemma 1.6). And up to its first answer that differs from the human's, the imitator's episode shows the page that the human's own episode would show. Proof: Theorems 1.7 and 1.8.

*What it does not say.* n counts whole episodes carried out by the human, however long each is. For a person under a generic prior the bound is as empty as R1, and each of the n answers is now a whole episode. Single messages shown to a fresh reader do not replace whole episodes: a human who has done work in an earlier message that is not on the page answers a later message otherwise than a reader of the page does, and the system that the protocol then imitates can be at total variation 1 from the human system (Example 1.10). The reset between episodes is an assumption of the same kind as A1, and one person violates it in the same way (Section 3.7). For a human who is not deterministic only weaker forms hold: a divergence form of R1 (Theorem 1.7); for whole runs, E[H²(Q_M, Q_P)] ≤ 2·L_e·K ln 2/n for the deployed mixture M if the calls of each episode are consecutive, and with min{4m_e, L_e/2} in the place of 2 if episodes are nested or interleaved, m_e being the largest number of calls of an episode (Theorem 1.9, Proposition 1.9a). A form of R3 for such a human is missing.

**Theorem E2 (a human who changes; proved here from the standard regret bound).** Drop A1 and A2, and let the human of round t be a sampler P_t that may depend in any way on what happened before; this covers one person who remembers the earlier questions. For every ν in H,

  E[Σ_x KL(P_T(·|x) ‖ M^(T)(·|x))] ≤ L·ln(1/w(ν))/n + (1/n)·Σ_{t=1}^{n} E[Σ_x KL(P_t(·|x) ‖ ν(·|x))],

the sums over x running over the calls of the rehearsal runs of rounds T and t. Proof: Theorem 1.11.

So the mixture of a uniformly chosen round is close, along the run of that round, to the human of that round, up to the price of ν divided by n and the average divergence of the best fixed ν from the moving human. *What it does not say.* Nothing about the human after the last question: two humans who agree during the rehearsal and part ways afterwards give the same data (Proposition 1.14). The second term does not shrink with n while the human keeps moving, and the first is the price of ν in bits, which for a person is as large as in R1. Under a drift that is linear in time, longer rehearsal can be worse: the bound of Corollary 1.12 is smallest at a finite n, and in an example the error itself first falls and then rises.

### 9.4 Copy or predictor

Is the imitator a copy of the human, or a predictor of the human? The note's answer has four parts.

*What the data fix.* Two humans whose answer distributions P(·|x) agree on the inputs that the scaffold can produce have the same human system, and every protocol that asks only such inputs produces the same law of data, and the same deployed imitator, for both (Proposition 7.8). So the data fix the human's answer distributions on those inputs, to the accuracy of R1, and nothing else: not the mechanism, and not the behaviour on other inputs.

*A better predictor.* Under A1 the answer to x is a fresh draw from P(·|x), whatever else is known. So no predictor, however it models the world, gains on the human's own answer distributions in expected log-likelihood, and its likelihood exceeds theirs by a factor c with probability at most 1/c (Proposition 7.9; standard: the likelihood ratio is a supermartingale, and the second statement is Ville's inequality, Ville 1939). A predictor that beats the sampler exists exactly when A1 is false. A1 assumes such a predictor away; it does not show that there is none.

*The sampler with coins filled in.* Take a sampler, and the same sampler with an answer drawn once and for all for every input. The two give the same law of data to every protocol that asks no input twice, and they differ at an input that is asked twice (Proposition 7.10; Pr7(a) in Section 8.7). So single answers cannot tell them apart, and a draw from the posterior can be such a filled-in sampler with constant probability (the example after R4 in Section 5.1; H2 at λ = 1 in Section 5.2). What selects the sampler is a steeper prior. From recorded runs of the human system, with weights w^λ for any λ > 1, a program drawn from the posterior has a law of runs close to the human system's (H2); after rehearsal, the program that maximises the posterior under squared weights has (R6(c)). Weights w^λ with λ > 1 are the device that the researcher's notes propose, together with private coins, for telling the sampler from the sampler with its coins filled in; Paper 2 (see References) develops it.

*The deployed object.* The imitator of R1 is a committee: a hypothesis drawn afresh from the posterior at every call (at every episode in E1, at every run in R4 and I1). One program can take its place (R6(b) and R6(c), H2). Nothing in the theorems makes that program resemble the human inside, and nothing constrains it off the inputs of the closed system. The robustness of recovering the sampler that the researcher named as the ideal theorem is obtained in the sense of H2 and R6(c), for the law of a run on one scaffold, and no further.

### 9.5 The objections

Section 8 of `proofs/assumptions.md` classes twenty-two objections to the claim "rehearsal is a human-imitation protocol that works exactly as far as any protocol can". Seven are fatal to a part of the claim.

* *K.* For a person under a generic prior the number of answers that the theorems ask for is out of reach (Section 9.1), and the factor K cannot be removed in general (N2b, N8). This is fatal to "works from a reasonable number of answers", read as a theorem about a person.
* *Critical steps.* For some scaffolds and classes no protocol amplifies work whose single steps decide the result, whether the steps are replicated or not (N2, N8).
* *Long episodes.* If an episode that can do research takes a year, as the researcher expects, the budget is not met for scaffolds whose questions depend on earlier answers (Section 9.1). It is met only if scaffolds of short calls can do research, which no theorem about the learner can show.
* *Not a person.* The protocol imitates the human inside one computation fixed in advance, and an imitator that is certified for that computation is not certified for another use. It is not an upload.
* *Silent failures.* The unfaithful calls of the deployed mixture are not marked. The rules of Section 7 that are never silently wrong on new inputs pay for it. With a parameter that makes their soundness a theorem they are *rocks*, in the researcher's word rules that are safe because they refuse whatever is new: they refuse every unasked input that a test of fewer than about K bits separates from the asked ones. With a milder parameter they are *whitelists*, rules that refuse what differs in a simple way from what was asked and act elsewhere, and their soundness is then an assumption that the data cannot confirm. Something of the kind is forced: for a class that can change one input at a time, a rule that is never silently wrong must ask or abstain at every new input (N4). Two things short of this are theorems. On the system that it rehearsed, the threshold rule trades silent errors for aborts (Ab1(b)). And for a deterministic human, a known bound b on K, questions drawn independently from one law and inputs known as a batch in advance, there is a rule that is silently wrong on at most a fraction ε of the batch and that, except with a small probability over the questions, refuses only inputs in a set of small mass under the law of its questions, at a price linear in b (Ab5, Section 7.4). That rule does not act on inputs that arrive one at a time, its ε is a fraction of the inputs of a batch and not a bound for a run, off the law of its questions it may refuse everything, and for a person its price is as far out of reach as that of R1. So the objection is fatal to "fails only obviously" in the strict sense: nothing in this note acts on new inputs one at a time, is never silently wrong and needs no assumption.
* *The human reads what the imitator wrote.* In rehearsal the human reads questions that the imitator's own runs wrote, in the early rounds those of the prior, and nothing here bounds what such text contains or what reading it does. The paragraph after this list says more.
* *A human who varies.* The certificate that needs no K is lost. The certificate of V1(a) is valid for every human, but it is issued only if the human is nearly deterministic on the inputs visited; and without A2 one answer per input cannot certify that a randomised imitator has few unfaithful calls (Section 3.5). With questions put twice and few possible answers there is a certificate at the slow rate m^(−1/4) (Section 8.7).

**The human reads what the imitator wrote.** A rehearsal question is a call of a run that the current mixture answered. So its text is built from the mixture's earlier answers, and in the early rounds these are the prior's. For a deterministic human at most L·K ln 2 questions in expectation follow an unfaithful call (Proposition 1.16); for K of the size of Section 9.1 this bound does not exclude that every question of an affordable rehearsal is of that kind. Such a question need not be one that the human system would ever ask, and it may be nonsense; the human answers it all the same, and it counts as one of the n answers. With the class of all programs the text may be written by any program, and nothing here bounds what reading it does to the human. If it changes the human, A1 fails and E2 is what remains. If it can harm the reader, the protocol is not safe; harm to the person who reads the output is one of the failures that the researcher's notes name. The objection is fatal to the combination of three things: the class of all programs, a scaffold too large for the human to carry out whole runs, and a reader who must not be harmed. The remedies are a restricted class, which strengthens A2, and for small scaffolds recorded runs of the human system (H1), in which the human reads only what the human wrote.

Of the other fifteen objections, five are answered by the theory as it stands. They are: hypotheses that model the experiment or act with intent (test 3 of Section 10); a guarantee that is weaker by the factor 1/q on a set of runs of probability q, which the human system pays as well; the slack of an additive guarantee, which R3 already gives to an adversary; the limits of reuse (N2d); and the comparison with one run carried out by the human (test 5).

Eight can be answered at a price. A1 is false of people: memory inside a piece of work is met by asking whole episodes (E1), and memory from one question to the next by a pool of people, one for each question, which is then what is imitated (Section 3.7). A2 can fail while the protocol goes on: for a deterministic human the certificate shows it, for a human who varies only the listed tests do. A3 can fail in practice: physical randomness for every coin of the protocol and a prior fixed before the first coin are matters of design. The deployed round is often an early one: a round drawn from the second half of the rehearsal keeps every bound up to the factor 2. The human of rehearsal may answer otherwise than the human whose answers would be used: what the human is told is part of the design. The mixture over all programs is not a computation: a class of programs that always halt gives one, and A2 for it is a stronger assumption. And the claim as first worded, "exactly as far as any protocol can", was false: on one class questions chosen freely save a factor of order L/δ (Section 4.3), and in an example a late round saves a factor of order 1/δ; the paragraph "What survives" replaces it.

Two lie outside the theorems: whether the human system does anything useful, and whether it tolerates faults, which R3 assumes and the protocol never tests.

*What survives.* For a computation that we can run in advance, a human without memory who is one of the weighted candidates, and fresh coins throughout: n answers to single calls of the imitator's own runs give, for the mixture of a uniformly chosen round, at most L·K ln 2/n unfaithful calls per run in expectation if the human is deterministic, and at most L·√(K ln 2/(2n)) otherwise (R1, Section 3.4). There are scaffolds and classes of 2^K candidates on which every protocol whose run fails with probability at most δ ≤ 1/20 for every candidate needs, in expectation over a uniform candidate, at least K·L/(16δ·log₂(L/δ)) answers (N2b), and at least K·g/(16δ·log₂(g/δ)) when faults are tolerated by replication and a run has g critical units (N8). A given imitator of a deterministic human can be certified at a cost that does not contain K, if it is good enough to pass (V1(a)). Two guarantees do not assume that the human is among the candidates. A region of inputs fixed in advance is entered unasked with probability at most L/n (R7). And each listed property that the human always keeps is violated, in expectation, at no more than L·(log₂(1/π(B)) + 2)/n calls of a deployed run of the imitator built from tests (Pr6; it assumes A3, and A1 at the asked calls). The claim offers nothing outside that computation, where Sections 6 and 7 name the prices; nothing at rates below about 1/n; and nothing for a human other than the one who answered. For a person under a generic prior the bounds in K are empty (Section 9.1); what does not contain K is the certificate, R7 and the guarantee for listed properties.

## 10. The seven tests

The tests are seven questions that this project's digest of the researcher's notes (`research/understand/notes.md`, Section 5) derives from his objections to his own schemes. The verdicts of the table are for Protocol R (Section 2) in a closed deployment; the last paragraph of this section puts the imitator built from tests (Pr6) to the same questions. Numbers refer to `proofs/assumptions.md`, whose Section 5 has the details.

| test | verdict |
|---|---|
| 1 guard | met in frequency: a property fixed in advance is met unasked with probability at most L/n, and what is done there is the prior's choice; all properties at once only at the price K; not met outside a closed deployment |
| 2 machine | the rates are the scheme's; K, rare inputs and every route to a small K are the machine's |
| 3 referent | passed under A3 as far as R1 is not empty; for a person under a generic prior R1 is empty, and the test is then not passed |
| 4 copy | not passed |
| 5 budget | not passed |
| 6 rock | not a rock; its failures are silent |
| 7 horizon | outside the theorems |

**1. Guard.** *Name an input property that no training input has, in particular one that task decomposition creates. What does the scheme do there, and with what probability?* For each property of inputs fixed in advance, the probability that the deployed run meets it although no question had it is at most L/n (R7). N properties cost the factor N; so with probability at least 1 − 2^k·L/n every property with a description of at most k bits, in a fixed prefix-free language, that an input of the deployed run has was already the property of an asked input (Corollary 3.2′). Decomposition creates no property that escapes this bound: rehearsal runs go to full depth, and the question of round T is a uniformly drawn call of a run that has the law of the deployed run (S4(i)). A kind of call that runs make less often than about once in n rounds is still probably never asked. The statement is false for all properties at once, and for a property chosen with the data in view: when inputs seldom repeat, the deployed run meets the property "not yet asked" with probability near 1. Such properties are covered only by R1, at the price K. And what the scheme does at an input that is met unasked is the prior's choice (N1); the test is met in frequency, not at the input. For the mixture after the last round, kept for good, the bound fails in an example by a factor of about 0.4·√L (Example 3.2b). Outside a closed deployment the test is failed: a date in the inputs, or a real task that is not a draw from the rehearsed law, leaves the guard unrefuted, and what follows is again the prior's choice (Examples 3.4, 3.5). The exception "now" of the researcher's notes, a theory that is the same as the best one except for a clause about the next moment, is excluded by condition C3 of a closed deployment; it is not answered.

**2. Machine.** *Is the answer to test 1 a fact about the scheme, or about the reference machine?* Three things are facts about the scheme and mention no machine: the rate 1/n; R7; and the certificate V1(a), which can be issued only for a human who is nearly deterministic on the inputs visited. The constant K is the machine's: a change of machine by a bits changes K by at most a, and for a person K is not known on any machine. So is the behaviour at inputs that rehearsal met less often than about once in n runs and therefore probably never asked. For every decidable set Q of inputs and every computable behaviour b on Q there is a universal machine, at most K(Q, b) + c bits from the given one (K(Q, b) being the length of a shortest program for the pair), on which the mixture, after any data that do not meet Q, gives the answers of b at any finitely many inputs of Q probability at least one half (Paper 1 (see References), Proposition 8.2, for the mixture over programs that compute functions and for the one-tape version). And for every scheme whose only bias is the machine's and for two possible answers, a machine sep(x; A) + c bits away exchanges the two answers at an unasked input x (`ultranatural/note.md`, Theorem 3; see References). R1 holds on both machines (Proposition 7.1). Lastly, every route to a small K is a choice of machine: a prior built from knowledge (Section 9.1), or the restriction to isolated hypotheses (I1, Section 9.2).

**3. Referent.** *Does a hypothesis that models how the data set was made do as well as the intended one?* Under the five parts of A3 the asked call is an unbiased sample of a fresh run of the current mixture. Two statements follow (Section 3.7). A hypothesis loses weight at the rate at which it deviates from the human on the mixtures' runs, whatever it computes about the experiment. And a prior fixed first gives the hypothesis that knows the realised coins, on average, at most the probability of guessing them. That weight must be set against the human's 2^(−K). Call a *table of the data* a hypothesis that answers as recorded on the asked inputs and otherwise elsewhere. Under a prior that charges a table about as many bits as its questions carry, the tables outweigh the human until the questions carry more than K bits, and until then the imitator can answer unasked inputs as the tables do (Remark 3.2a). So the test is passed as far as R1 is not empty. Where R1 is empty it can fail, and for a person under a generic prior R1 is empty. Hypotheses that act with intent are covered in the same way and to the same extent. They cannot break R1, which holds for every class and prior, and they cannot aim at the calls that are not asked, because the asked call is drawn after the run. What they can choose is where the remaining unfaithful calls fall and what they say; this is why R3 counts those calls as an adversary's. The test does not cover what the human knows about the experiment (Section 3.7).

**4. Copy.** *Does anything in the theorems distinguish the sampler from a better predictor of it, or from the sampler with coins filled in?* From a better predictor: no. Under A1 there is none, and A1 is an assumption; the case that the researcher has in mind, a predictor that does better by reasoning about the world around the human, is assumed away and not treated. From the sampler with coins filled in: only by questions put twice, or by a steeper prior. With one answer per input the two cannot be told apart (Pr7(a)), and a draw from the posterior can then be a filled-in sampler with constant probability (the example after R4; H2 at λ = 1). Weights w^λ with λ > 1, the steeper prior of the researcher's notes, select the sampler: from recorded runs for every λ > 1 (H2), and after rehearsal for λ = 2 and the program that maximises the posterior (R6(c)). Both statements are about the law of a run on one scaffold. The robustness of recovering the sampler that the researcher named as the ideal theorem is obtained in this sense and no further (Section 9.4). The deployed object of R1 is the Bayes predictor of the human.

**5. Budget.** *How many human answers, of what length, on which questions? Could humans produce them in a few hours each, or does the protocol need human-years per datum?* The answers are n single calls of the imitator's own runs. In the early rounds these runs are written by the prior, so a question need not be one that the human system would ever ask (Section 9.5). The number of answers is of the order of K/(θδ) for a deterministic human whose system tolerates a fraction θ of faults, and of K/(θδ)² for a human who varies (Section 9.1). The factor K is forced in the worst case over classes: for critical calls (N2b), under tolerance by replication (N8) and, without the factor 1/(θδ), for additive work (Section 9.1). For a person under a generic prior K is out of reach at any length of answer. The comparison with the human's own work says the same. For one run and a deterministic human, R3 and the certificate together ask for (K ln 2 + ln 20)/(θδ) answers at confidence 0.95. At θ = δ = 0.1 that is fewer than the L answers of one run of the human system only if L exceeds 2.4·10³ for K = 30 bits, and 7.0·10⁴ for K = 10³ bits. What is cheap is only the check of a given imitator of a deterministic human: 300 short answers at θ = δ = 0.1 and confidence 0.95. For a human who varies no such certificate exists; with few possible answers and questions put twice there is a slower one (Section 8.7). And a certificate does not produce an imitator.

**6. Rock.** *Does the scheme still act on the inputs that matter, or does it abstain everywhere new?* It acts everywhere in the closed system and never abstains. The price is paid in the other thing that was asked for, failures that show: its unfaithful calls are not marked, and they must be taken as an adversary's. The theory has the opposite corner too. The minimum rule (Ab2) fails only visibly on any input if the human is in its plausible set. Where that is a theorem the rule refuses, for the class of all programs, every unasked input that a test of fewer than about K bits separates from the asked ones: it is a *rock*, in the researcher's word a rule that is safe because it refuses whatever is new. With a moderate plausible set it is a *whitelist*, a rule that refuses what differs in a simple way from what was asked and acts elsewhere, and its soundness is an assumption that the data cannot confirm. Between the two stands the batch rule (Ab5, Section 7.4). For a deterministic human, a known bound b on K, questions drawn independently from one law and inputs known as a batch in advance, a rule that is silently wrong on at most a fraction ε of the batch is a theorem, at a price linear in b. Except with a small probability over the questions, the inputs that it refuses lie in a set of small mass under the law of the questions, so that an input drawn afresh from that law is seldom refused. The rule has three limits. It does not act on inputs that arrive one at a time. Its ε is a fraction of the inputs of a batch and not a bound for a run: in a batch of whole runs it allows a wrong answer in a fraction ε·L of the runs. And off the law of its questions it may refuse everything. Nothing in this note acts on new inputs one at a time, is never silently wrong and needs no assumption.

**7. Horizon.** *Granting perfect per-call imitation, does the scaffold do anything?* No theorem about the learner can say. Three points weigh against. R3 needs a human system that survives arbitrarily replaced answers, and the protocol never tests this. Where that tolerance comes from replication it need not lower the number of answers needed for each critical unit (N8). And the budget is met only by short calls, while the researcher expects that an episode able to do research may take a year.

**The imitator built from tests on the same questions.** The imitator of Section 8.4, rehearsed as in Pr6, has no class of hypotheses, and its verdicts differ. Guard and budget are passed for listed properties and for nothing else: a deployed run violates a listed property that the human always keeps at no more than L·(log₂(1/π(B)) + 2)/n calls in expectation, whatever kinds of input it meets, at the property's own price and not at the price K (for a test of weight 2^(−20) and L = 10, 2200 answers bring the expected number of violating calls of a run to 0.1). This bounds an expected number of calls of a run: at a kind of input that runs meet less often than about once in n rounds, a listed property that the human always keeps can still be broken nearly every time the input comes (Section 8.4, after Pr6). R7 holds for this imitator as for the mixture, since the proof of R7 uses nothing about the imitator (`proofs/rehearsal-core.md`, Proposition 5.6). The machine enters only through the weights π, which are ours to choose and which are the machine's if they are 2^(−length of a program for the test); and the referent test does not arise, since there is no hypothesis that could model the making of the data. On the other three tests this imitator does no better than the mixture, and on one it does worse: it is not a copy and not even a sampler, since its answer is a choice that balances counts; it acts everywhere, and its failures are as silent as the mixture's; and tests of single calls do not control whole runs (Section 8.5).
