### 8.3 An imitator built from tests alone

An imitator can be built from tests alone, with no class of hypotheses. While the human still answers every question, the number of its answers in each test of a weighted family stays close to the number of the human's, on any sequence of questions and whatever the human is (Theorem Pr5). Rehearsed as in Protocol R of Section 2, it has a bound for the runs that it answers after the human has left (Theorem Pr6). Numbers in Sections 8.4 and 8.5 refer to `proofs/property-online.md`.

*The setting.* Tests are here sets B of pairs (question, answer), in a countable family 𝓑 fixed in advance, with weights π(B) > 0 of total at most 1 and prices Λ(B) = ln(1/π(B)) as in Section 8.2. In round t the imitator first fixes a *rule* a_t: X → Y, its answer to every possible question. Then a question x_t and the human's answer y_t arrive; they may depend in any way on the past and on a_t. The *increment* of B in round t is g_t(B) := 1[(x_t, a_t(x_t)) ∈ B] − 1[(x_t, y_t) ∈ B]. The *book* of B is S_n(B) := Σ_{t≤n} g_t(B): the number of rounds up to n in which the imitator's answer was in B, minus the number in which the human's was. V_n(B) ≤ n is the number of rounds t ≤ n with a non-zero increment.

**Theorem Pr5 (matching all tests on any sequence; proved here).** Let Y be finite. Under Rule NH, which is stated below and uses no randomness, on every sequence of questions and answers, for every n and every test B:

(i) S_n(B)² ≤ (V_n(B) + 1)·(2Λ(B) + 1.39 + ln(V_n(B) + 1)), hence |S_n(B)| ≤ √((n + 1)·(2Λ(B) + 1.39 + ln(n + 1)));

(ii) if the human's answer was never in B, the imitator's was in B in at most log₂(1/π(B)) + 2 rounds; if it was always in B, the imitator's was outside B in at most as many.

Nothing is assumed about the human: not A1, not A2.

**Rule NH.** Put G_v(s) := (v + 1)^(−1/2)·exp(s²/(2(v + 1))). Before round t let the test B have the book S = S_{t−1}(B) and the count V = V_{t−1}(B). Its *slope* is

  c_t(B) := ¼·(G_{V+1}(S + 1) − G_{V+1}(S − 1)) + σ·2^(V−2),

where the last term is present only if V ≥ 1 and all non-zero increments of B before round t had one sign σ ∈ {+1, −1}. The rule a_t(x) is an answer y that minimises the *score* Σ_B π(B)·c_t(B)·1[(x, y) ∈ B]; if several answers do, any of them may be taken. The slope has the sign of the book, so the rule avoids tests that have so far held more of its answers than of the human's, and seeks tests that have held fewer. The letters name the two terms of the slope: N for a normal mixture, since G_v(s) is the mean of exp(Z·s − Z²·v/2) for a standard normal variable Z, and H for halving, since the second term doubles with every further increment of the same sign.

*Idea.* Give each test a *potential* u ≥ 0: a function of its increments so far that starts at 1 and grows in a round by at most the slope times the increment. The rule minimises the score over all answers, and the human's answer is one of them; so the imitator's answer scores no more than the human's, and the difference of the two scores is Σ_B π(B)·c_t(B)·g_t(B). Hence the sum Σ_B π(B)·u, which converges absolutely, never grows, and u ≤ 1/π(B) for every test at every time. The potential has two parts. The first is ½·G_V(S); it gives (i). The second stays at ½ at the first non-zero increment and doubles at each later one while all have one sign, and it is 0 once they do not; it gives (ii). Proof: `proofs/property-online.md`, Lemmas 1.3 and 1.4, Theorem 3.3.

*What it does not say.* Pr5 is about rounds in which the human answers; what an imitator of this kind does once the human has left is the subject of Pr6. The bounds count answers over n rounds: divided by n, (i) says that the frequency of answers in B differs from the human's by at most about √((2Λ(B) + ln n)/n). Nothing is said about any single answer, or about tests outside the family (Section 8.5).

*The researcher's objection* (Section 8.2) was that properties which held so far can contradict one another at a new question, so that no answer keeps them all. Pr5 does not ask the imitator to keep them all, and the rule has no notion of which to keep. It gives its answer; the human's answer then shows which property the human breaks; and a book records only differences between the imitator's answers and the human's. A property that the human keeps in every round is broken by the imitator in at most log₂(1/π(B)) + 2 rounds in all, B being the test "the answer breaks the property"; a round in which the imitator broke a property that the human kept is one of these. A property that the human breaks at the new question is from then on matched only in the sense of (i) (Remark 3.3a). All this uses the human's answer in every round. What is left of it once the human has gone is said after Pr6.

*Lower bounds (proved here).* (ii) is optimal up to the 2: among 2^r tests of weight 2^(−r), every rule gives, against some human, r answers in a test that this human never satisfies (Proposition 3.5; the argument is standard). In (i) the order √n is forced in general: for a family of ⌈√n⌉ tests, chosen for n, every rule lets some book reach (√2 − 1)·√n within n rounds on some sequence (Proposition 3.6). For a fixed finite family it is not forced: another rule keeps the books bounded in n, by a bound that may be exponential in the size of the family (Proposition 3.9). Whether the logarithms in (i) are needed is open.

*A class as tests.* Let H be a class of functions with weights w, as in Section 1. Halve the weights of the tests and add, with the weights w(ν)/2, the tests "the answer is not ν(x)", one for each ν in H. If the human is a function f in H, then (ii) gives at most K + 3 rounds in which the imitator's answer differs from the human's (Corollary 3.4). The tests that were there before keep the bounds of Pr5 with π(B)/2 in the place of π(B), that is with log₂(1/π(B)) + 3 in (ii), whether or not the human is in H. Up to the 3, K + 3 is the bound of the halving algorithm (see Littlestone 1988; standard). For a person it is empty, like the bound of Theorem R1.

*The rule with one rate.* The simplest rule of this kind has one parameter, a rate λ > 0. It minimises Σ_B π(B)·sinh(λ·S_{t−1}(B))·1[(x, y) ∈ B] and has |S_n(B)| ≤ ln(2/π(B))/λ + n·λ/2, by the potential cosh(λ·S) (Theorem 2.1, where λ is written η). For λ = 1/√n, with n fixed in advance, the bound is √n·(ln(2/π(B)) + 1/2). This rule has no bound of the kind of (ii). With this λ and a family of two tests, however its ties are broken, there are a deterministic human who never satisfies a test of weight 1/(2n) and questions on which the rule gives at least ½·√n·ln n answers in that test within n rounds (Examples 2.3 and 2.5); Rule NH gives at most log₂ n + 3.

**Theorem Pr6 (rehearsal with this imitator; proved here).** Let Y be finite. Rehearse as in Protocol R of Section 2, with the rule a_t of Rule NH in the place of the mixture, entering each asked call in the books, and deploy, for each run afresh, the rule of a uniformly drawn round. The human answers at most n single calls and is then not needed. Assume A3, and A1 at the asked calls; A2 is not assumed. For a rule a let d_B(a) be the expected number of calls of a run answered by a whose answer is in B, minus the expected number of calls of the same run at which the human's answer would be in B. Then Δ_n(B) := (1/n)·Σ_{t≤n} d_B(a_t) is this difference for a deployed run, given the rehearsal. For every test B:

(a) |E Δ_n(B)| ≤ L·√((1 + 1/n)·(2Λ(B) + 1.39 + ln(n + 1))/n);

(b) if the human never gives an answer in B, a deployed run has in expectation at most L·(log₂(1/π(B)) + 2)/n calls with an answer in B; if the human's answer is always in B, it has in expectation at most as many calls with an answer outside B;

(c) with probability at least 1 − δ over the rehearsal, for all tests at once, |Δ_n(B)| ≤ (L/n)·(|S_n(B)| + √(2n·ln(2/(π(B)·δ)))); the right side can be read off the record of the rehearsal.

*Proof.* The asked call is the J-th, with J uniform on {1, …, L} (a shorter run asks nothing, and its round leaves the books as they are), so it is each of the L positions with probability 1/L, and by A1 its answer is a draw from P; so E[g_t(B) | past] = d_B(a_t)/L and E S_n(B) = (n/L)·E Δ_n(B); (a) and (b) follow from Pr5. For (c), S_n(B) − (n/L)·Δ_n(B) is a martingale with increments in intervals of length 2; apply the Azuma–Hoeffding inequality (standard; Hoeffding 1963) with failure probability π(B)·δ and sum over B (Theorems 6.2 and 6.3). ∎

So if "y is a correct solution of x" is a test, the answers of a deployed run are correct as often as the human's answers to the same questions would be, up to (a); and if the human's answers are always correct, a deployed run has in expectation at most L·(log₂(1/π(B)) + 2)/n incorrect answers, by (b). The price is that of the test and not that of the human. To bring the bounds below γ calls per run takes n of order L²·(2Λ(B) + ln n)/γ² in (a), and n = L·(log₂(1/π(B)) + 2)/γ in (b).

*What it does not say.* The comparison is with the answers that the human would give to the imitator's own questions, not with the human system, whose runs may ask other questions. The bounds are averages over the rounds, and a round must be drawn afresh for each run: against a human who answers one question with a fair coin, every single rule, kept for good, is off by 1/2 (Example 6.5). And they bound expected numbers of calls of a run, not what is done at one call. In particular, when two listed properties contradict each other at a question, the human is no longer there to show which one to break. In an example with one call per run there are two listed properties and a question, which a run asks with probability q, to which no answer keeps both. The human keeps both at the other question, and at this one keeps the second and breaks the first. Under Rule NH (ties to the least answer) the rule of every round, up to the first round in which this question is asked, answers it so that the second property is broken. So a deployed run that asks this question breaks a property that the human always keeps with probability at least 1 − n·q/2, while the expected number of such calls of a run is at most 1/n, within the bound of (b) (Example 6.5a). The answer to a kind of question that runs ask less often than about once in n rounds is thus not controlled by (b); under Theorem R1 the mixture's answer to such a question is not controlled either.

### 8.4 What this does not give

* **Tests outside the family.** The guarantee reaches as far as the family and no further. With the single test "the answer is 1", the human f(x) = x mod 2 and the questions 1, 2, 3, …, Rule NH (ties to the least answer) keeps its book within 1 and gives the wrong answer in every round (Example 7.1). The family may hold many properties at once, for instance every property that some program of a fixed prefix-free language of halting programs decides, each at the price of the length of its program (Section 8.2); so the property to be kept need not be picked in advance. But whatever the weights, at most 2^k tests have log₂(1/π(B)) ≤ k, and a property that no short program decides has no cheap test. For a deterministic human f, the property that always fits, "the answer is the one this human gives", enters as the test "the answer is not f(x)" of "A class as tests" in Section 8.4, and its price there is the human's own, K + 1 bits. For a human who varies there is no such test: a set of pairs that contains every answer the human may give to a question is also kept by an imitator that always gives one and the same of these answers (Remark 7.2a).
* **Runs.** Tests of single calls do not control what happens across the calls of a run. If a scaffold puts one question twice to a human who answers with a fair coin, the deployed imitator, which answers a whole run by one rule, gives the same answer twice. Its run is then at total variation at least 1/2 from the human system's for every n, although the frequency of every test of single calls is matched as in Pr6 (Proposition 7.3). In the toy system of Section 11, a tree of seven additions in which the human errs at each addition with probability 0.1, the rule with one rate gets single additions right as often as the human does, and the sum at the root of the tree in 62% of the runs, against 48% in the human system.
* **A choice, not a sample.** The rule's answer is a choice that evens out the books, not a draw from a model of the human. Over the rounds, its answers to a fixed question need not resemble draws from P(·|x): in the example of the first bullet they are 0 and 1 in turn at every question, while the human is a function. A randomised version *tilts* a base sampler R_t, such as the mixture: it answers x with y with probability proportional to R_t(y|x)·exp(−κ·sinh(λ)·Σ_B ω_t(B)·1[(x, y) ∈ B]), where κ > 0 is the strength of the tilt, λ is a rate as in Section 8.4, and the numbers ω_t(B), of absolute sum below 1, are computed from the books so far. For this imitator the book of B counts, in each round, the probability that its answer is in B in the place of the indicator. If the human's answers are draws from P, the expected absolute value of each book is at most the bound of the rule with one rate, ln(2/π(B))/λ + n·λ/2, plus the base's expected cumulative divergence from the human, E Σ_{t≤n} KL(P(·|x_t) ‖ R_t(·|x_t)), divided by κ·λ (Theorem 7.6, where λ and κ are η and β). With the mixture as base, in rehearsal under A1 to A3, and with λ = 1/√n, this is at most √n·(ln(2/π(B)) + 1/2 + K ln 2/κ), and the tilted sampler's own divergence from the human, summed over the n asked questions, is at most 2K ln 2 + 1.39·κ² in expectation (Corollary 7.7). In these bounds the two prices trade against each other: with κ = 1 the divergence stays within about twice the mixture's K ln 2 and every test bound contains K ln 2; with κ = K ln 2 the test bounds are free of K up to the summand 1 and the bound on the divergence is of order K². *Open:* one imitator with the mixture's bound under A2 and the test bounds without it, for a human who varies. For a deterministic human, "A class as tests" in Section 8.4 has both.
* **Against the mixture.** Each has guarantees that the other lacks. *A cheap property that the mixture breaks a number of times proportional to K.* Let there be finitely many questions, one bad answer and A good ones, and let the human be any function that never gives the bad answer. Let the class consist of the human, with weight 2^(−K), and of *tables*, with total weight 1/2: a table holds each question independently with probability p, with one of the A good answers drawn uniformly, and gives the bad answer to every question that it does not hold. Then the mixture gives the bad answer with probability at least (1 − p)/2 at each of the first (K − 1)/log₂(A/p) questions that are new to it. Rule NH, with the test "the answer is the bad one" at weight 1/2 in its family, gives the bad answer in at most 3 rounds in all, against every human who never gives it (Proposition 8.1; compare Pr2(b) in Section 8.6). *Where the mixture is better.* It is better for tests that nobody listed: in the example of the first bullet, with the class of the two functions x mod 2 and 1 − (x mod 2) at weight 1/2 each, the mixture is wrong in half a round in expectation (Proposition 8.2). It is also better for whole runs (Theorem R2), and it is a sampler. *Frequencies of tests, for a human who varies.* Both match them at order 1/√n: Rule NH up to the factor √(ln n), by Pr6(a), and the mixture under A2. After one rehearsal no imitator built from n answers does better: for one of two humans its rate at one test is off by 1/(4√n) or more with probability at least 0.21 (Proposition 6.7; Le Cam's two-point argument, standard: Le Cam 1973). The mean of Δ_n(B) over rehearsals, which Pr6(a) bounds, can still be of order 1/n (Example 6.5). The rate K ln 2/n of the mixture is a rate for the divergence, not for the frequencies of tests.
* **Computation.** A rule that minimises the score up to 2^(−t) in round t is computable for decidable tests, computable weights with a computable tail bound and, for each question, a known finite set of candidate answers that contains the human's, such as answers of bounded length; Pr5 then holds with π(B)/2 in the place of π(B) (Propositions 4.1 and 4.2). With an unbounded set of answers no rule that always halts has the bounds of Pr5, even for a single decidable test and a deterministic human; the human of this example is not computable (Proposition 4.4). Weights that can only be approximated from below were not examined.
* **Outside the closed system.** Pr6 is about a closed deployment: runs of the rehearsed scaffold on top-level inputs drawn from the rehearsed law D₀. If deployment draws its top-level inputs from another law that is at most a constant multiple of D₀, and nothing else changes, the bound (b) of Pr6 holds multiplied by that constant, as in S1(a), because it counts something that cannot be negative. The bounds (a) and (c) do not carry over, even then: differences at different inputs can cancel under D₀ and not under the other law (Remarks 6.4, item 5). At inputs that the rehearsal runs do not reach, no question was asked, and no protocol is constrained there (Theorem N1).

*Attribution.* The method is not new. Its step is Blackwell's approachability condition (1956), as Abernethy et al. (2011, Theorem 3 with Lemma 1) state it, with an exponential potential; and with two answers and a human who always gives the answer that the imitator does not give, the game is Spencer's balancing game (1977), as Bárány (2024) describes it. The nearest published forms are online multivalid prediction (Gupta et al. 2022, Theorem 3.2; its tests see the forecast), defensive forecasting (Vovk et al. 2005, Theorem 1) and, for finitely many tests and one rate, Lee et al. (2022, Theorem 2.1). Rule NH's potentials are of the kind used by expert algorithms without a learning rate (Chernov and Vovk 2010; Luo and Schapire 2015) and by the halving algorithm. The statements on forecasts that pass many checking rules at once are asymptotic. As Perchet (2013) reports them: for countably many rules that see the forecast, the statement is that of Sandroni et al. (2003; his Proposition 3.2); for rules that do not see it, the statement is his Theorem 3.2, of which a weaker version first appeared in Lehrer (2001). We found no published statement of Pr5 in this form.
