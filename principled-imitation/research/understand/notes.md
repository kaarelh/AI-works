## Summary: what the notes say, in ten points

1. **He has posed this problem since about 2023 (the cloning note links to a tweet of March 2023) and still counts it open.** The standing form: with unbounded compute, cheap data and short human tasks, "specify a plan that clearly works … you should make a compelling case that your thing works" (`ai/research from hypercomputer/what are the approaches to getting novel research papers given a lot of compute?.md`). Of the concrete candidates, including "solomonoff on question-answer pairs", he says "i don't think we actually have anything here that works" (same note).
2. **The obstruction he returns to most is one if-statement.** "if input has property P, then output 0. else, do the program" costs about C(P) bits whatever the amount of data (`philosophy/prediction/solomonoff function induction gives at least some const probability to bad behavior on ood inputs.md`). And "there will be a simple property distinguishing test from train if you want to do task decomposition" (`ai/learning/solomonoff induction/solomonoff function induction.md`).
3. **He knows the same floor applies to properties that are simple only given the learned program** ("if this internal variable is set 0, then the if statement triggers", same note) and asks for a positive result when no simple distinguishing property exists.
4. **The property idea is recorded as a plan, not a definition.** "define a property inductor … it arguably captures the intuitive idea of "please continue like this" better than SI" (`ai/learning/solomonoff induction/solomonoff induction is not the paragon of generalization.pdf`). He also records its defeater: "there are other properties which, if preserved, contradict this property … solomonoff induction just isn't property-preserving in general" (`philosophy/prediction/scientific reports from a predictive model/could one do science with solomonoff on text?.md`).
5. **Copy, not outside predictor.** He wants "really a clone (in terms of its internals)" as opposed to "thinking about the model from the outside and trying to predict it" or "some possibly deceptive outer loop playing the training game", while holding that "what matters is that the trained model generalizes correctly on test data + out-of-distribution (or at least in a reasonable way …)" (`ai/human imitation/notes on human imitation.md`).
6. **Task decomposition is both the reason for the shift and itself in doubt.** His standing question is how long a memory-reset human episode T must be for research to be possible with a non-galactic number of calls; his guesses run from "A few weeks is probably not enough, and a year is probably enough" (`ai/alignment general/quick takes oct 2024.md`) to "T = 10 days will not work … T=1000 days very likely works" for proving the minimax theorem starting from middle-school mathematics (`.../at which point does imitation become powerful?/conversation with rio.md`). Long episodes make each datum expensive.
7. **His most worked-out scheme makes every prompt in-distribution by making prompts random,** then shows "some random subset to solomonoff function induction", optionally with task decomposition; his verdict is "idk. on outside view, probably not", because "if the shortest program looks at you having made the data set, then you are probably screwed" (`philosophy/prediction/scientific reports from a predictive model/a better attempt at getting science out of solomonoff.md`).
8. **Two tools he proposed are now partly theorems in this repository:** a steeper simplicity prior with private randomness per input (to get "simple model + noise", the sampler and not its filled-in coins), and time-bounded and circuit versions of the induction. He says of the first: "I think it's very unlikely this steeper penalization is anywhere close to a full solution to the philosophical problem here" (`/home/user/AI-works/solomonoff-operator-induction/research/prior/hanni-lesswrong-comment-thread.md`).
9. **Three small results in the notes are directly usable in a protocol:** a target f can be taught with at most K(f) well-chosen examples (`philosophy/prediction/which input-output pairs does solomonoff function induction need to see to generalize correctly?.md`); a random stopping time makes the event that a fixed property holds up to the stopping point and fails just after it improbable, for any property (`philosophy/philosophy of understanding/induction in mathematics.md`); and a simple property keeps posterior probability at least 2^(−C(P)) of being preserved (`ai/learning/solomonoff induction/solomonoff function induction.pdf`).
10. **He distrusts worst-case conservatism but accepts whitelists.** "knightian uncertainty conservatism seems stupid/useless — it just turns you into a rock … but being conservative about the parts that are used in your system is not stupid" (`ai/alignment general/a kind of conservatism that isn't stupid.md`). Any abstaining protocol will be read against this.

### 2.1 Predicting text: a pretrained language model, or Solomonoff induction on a sequence

* **What.** Train a predictor on human text and prompt it; in the ideal version, "enter all existing human scientific papers in sequence into a solomonoff inductor, and then "title: A detailed description of how to cure alzheimer's. date: Feb 2025"" (`philosophy/prediction/scientific reports from a predictive model/could one do science with solomonoff on text?.md`).
* **Hope.** Samples from the distribution of human text are research.
* **Where it fails.**
  * The prompt is not what it seems: "if you imagine seeing it on the internet, what would follow would totally not be an ASI giving honest best superhuman answers, but probably an excerpt from a sci-fi story"; at best "an honest human's best response to the question given some not-too-large amount of thinking (maybe 1 hour)"; and "it could also just be sort of out of distribution … so the model might just produce some hard-to-predict nonsense" (`ai/human imitation/email to yoshua about trott's plan.md`).
  * The property-preservation argument has a defeater of its own shape, and the data are "nowhere near physically or conceptually to an alzheimer's cure. eg, if there's pointing into a universe with this data set on a hard drive, then an alzheimer's cure is nowhere near" (`could one do science with solomonoff on text?.md`). The same note lists what could come out instead of a proof (§3, F5, F6, F8, F19).
  * Sequence induction "doesn't give you a proof of the riemann hypothesis after a bunch of interleaved statements and proofs" (`philosophy/philosophy of thinking/some ideal inductions.md`).
  * A trained predictor of a mind is not that mind: `ai/alignment general/model trained on human vs human.md` (quoted under D3); "one major issue is that the predictor is asked to track all sorts of nonsense, whereas Alice is focused on what matters" (`philosophy/prediction/scientific reports from a predictive model/could one get scientific reports from a predictive model in some limit?.md`).
  * Practical: "you are training the model to represent the entire human distribution — this might be a bunch harder than just getting it to be good enough to solve the problem one way"; "there is a theoretical upper bound not exactly at human capabilities, but not that far from it" (`ai/human imitation/difficulties with getting science from human imitation (in time).md`).
* **Unresolved.** Whether errors compound harmfully (`ai/human imitation/argument against.md`, four lines of the note):
  > "basically, eps error … compounds"
  > "counter: sure, you won't get a copy of the human, but does the error compound in a direction that's worse? like it might just be the same researcher exploring a different path genuinely, who cares"
  > "counter counter: the researcher has a goaly bit that keeps it on track"
  > "counter counter counter: insofar as that is a thing that makes sense, the predictive system / stolen model will also have such a goaly bit"

### 2.2 Solomonoff function induction on question–answer pairs (the main object)

* **What.** "you have a bunch of input-output pairs. you have the usual prior on all programs. solomonoff function induction := you think of the input-output pairs as being generated by picking a random program from this prior and generating using it, and so you update on seen pairs as you should" (`ai/learning/solomonoff induction/solomonoff function induction.md`). Hypotheses are samplers; "one should use different bits of randomness from the universal semimeasure's infinite read tape for sampling on different inputs" (same note).
* **Hope.**
  * A dominance theorem ("Thm 1 (template)"): the inductor loses to any computable sampler by at most a constant in log loss. He regards the version for samplers that see only x as "easy to prove".
  * In distribution it works: "i want to understand if the in-distribution case works at least" (shortlist above).
  * Some out-of-distribution cases plausibly work: labels of proved and refuted formal sentences generalise because "(sth equivalent to) searching for a proof/disproof is plausibly the simplest thing that works"; sentences labelled with shortest proofs; "suppose we have correctly counted the lengths of odd strings. then plausibly we will generalize correctly to all strings" (`philosophy/prediction/some examples where function solomonoff generalizes well ood.md`).
  * Few well-chosen examples suffice: "you can always learn f by showing the function value on K(f) inputs (like, if you choose well)" (`philosophy/prediction/which input-output pairs does solomonoff function induction need to see to generalize correctly?.md`; statement and proof in §4, Q7).
  * A human-like inductor sits inside the mixture: "a human with the right prompt thinking for a long time could generalize well, and that's computable … of course, it might just have much lower weight than stuff which doesn't generalize … but maybe one can say: ok but across many ood generalizations, solomonoff can't lose to this humanlike inductor by that much!" (`philosophy/prediction/does solomonoff generalize well out of distribution?.md`).
* **Where it fails.**
  * The guard: "solomonoff function induction doesn't work that well when there is a simple property distinguishing test from train — in that case we can just have an if statement checking the property followed by output 0 if it is in the test setting (vs do some legit thing in the train setting), for some small const extra program length. this sorta makes me sad about various concrete proposals for the riemann hypothesis proof problem, because there will be a simple property distinguishing test from train if you want to do task decomposition" (`solomonoff function induction.md`, "added later").
  * The guard relative to the learned program: "you don't just have counterexamples with simple properties, but also properties which are simple given a program that does well on train, which could be complicated (eg if this internal variable is set 0, then the if statement triggers, with a choice of variable such that it never triggers in train and also the variable is high complexity to calculate)" (same place).
  * His summary in the September 2025 draft: "regardless of how much train data has been seen, good ood generalization to strings with some property has at least a const probability of happening but also at least some const probability of not happening, at least for a loose meaning of 'const'" (`ai/learning/solomonoff induction/solomonoff function induction.pdf`, §3).
  * Machine dependence, noticed and left open: "maybe good behavior has a better const. but hmm it does mean there's a programming language in which it doesn't have a better const? well the programming language is specific to that property tho, which is maybe weird" (`philosophy/prediction/solomonoff function induction gives at least some const probability to bad behavior on ood inputs.md`).
  * Easy-to-hard: "your ideal (bounded) solomonoff-style predictor will learn to do whatever is the simplest-easiest thing that works on the cases it is presented / but doing that thing probably doesn't work on the hard cases..." and "there is a connection between you finding yourself in a bad situation when providing data for hard cases and the AI just not handling them how you'd want to have them handled" (`philosophy/prediction/a tension in trying to get good generalization from easy cases.md`). And: "hypothesis (weakly held): for solomonoff, ood generalization is a thing, but easy-to-hard generalization is not. … maybe it's more like: true ood generalization is not a thing for solomonoff. there is "fake ood generalization", which is where you've already seen many "ood generalizations" of the same kind"; "whenever there were helpful properties that made the task easier on train data compared to test data, you're fucked(?)"; "whenever there is another generalization which is ood compared to all previous ones, you might just get fucked. and maybe you probably do?" (`does solomonoff generalize well out of distribution?.md`).
  * It reproduces the teacher: "roughly speaking solomonoff would be making the same mistakes the person would make" (`philosophy/philosophy of thinking/beating solomonoff induction at grokking a notion.md`). For imitation this is the wanted behaviour on the inputs seen; it matters for D13 wherever what the person means and what the person would say come apart.
  * It cannot use what the choice and order of inputs reveal: "there are computable predictions which make use of information about which x's had their outputs revealed previously to make predictions, which isn't something solomonoff function induction can natively do" (`solomonoff function induction.md`; his two constructions are in §4, Q4).
  * Which inputs are asked matters: "eg giving a random string as the "question" is probably very stupid" (`which input-output pairs does solomonoff function induction need to see to generalize correctly?.md`).
* **Unresolved in the notes.** A positive converse ("if there isn't a simple property distinguishing test from train, then solomonoff function induction works well?"); a mutual-information form of the history-aware theorem; whether the Kolmogorov and semimeasure versions agree (§4, Q4–Q6).
* **Established in this repository since then** [read in source: `/home/user/AI-works/solomonoff-operator-induction/README.md` and `/home/user/AI-works/ultranatural/README.md`; details in `repo.md`]: dominance on every consistent sequence; i.i.d. risk O(K(f)/n); the two versions are not equivalent; the floor with the separation complexity sep(x; A) and its machine dependence (the "grue theorem").

### 2.3 Function induction with task decomposition, and the random-prompt scheme

* **What.** "you can try to combine with task decomposition to get longer-term scientific progress out" (shortlist). The one scheme he writes out is in `philosophy/prediction/scientific reports from a predictive model/a better attempt at getting science out of solomonoff.md`:

> "* you get n great generalist researchers …
> * … [rooms held in quantum superposition for a year]
> * we put our $n$ researchers in $n$ different such rooms, with the task to try to work on some research question(s) given to them in a prompt. we use quantum randomness to generate a random text prompt of arbitrary length. the text will with extremely high probability be complete nonsense. let's say that a researcher should really try to see if there is anything sensible in there and work on that if there is, but if they can't find anything after spending like a week, it's fine if they just spend their year working on whatever they want. by the end of the year, they should produce a new text file. …
> * now you collect this input->output data set from the rooms
> * you show some random subset to solomonoff function induction, and then ask it to give the output on a legit question you're interested in
> * you can try to combine this with task decomposition: you have the initial prompt ask the scientist to eventually output a prompt to a next scientist. for example, they could be improving a library of textbooks"

  The rooms are isolated (quantum superposition, or light-years apart) so that a predictor cannot "learn too much about the work other scientists actually did in a universe from the work some scientists did in that universe, because that would make prompts that were not actually given in that universe more weirdly out of distribution".
* **Hope.** No legitimate prompt is off the training distribution's support, including the prompts that a decomposition produces: "you could also do this with better prompts … i think this is a distraction if one wants to get task decomposition working, because one needs generalization to prompts you wouldn't have given anyway in that case."
* **Where it fails.** Under the heading "does this work?": "idk. on outside view, probably not. i don't really understand this well on inside view atm. but one observation: if the shortest program looks at you having made the data set, then you are probably screwed, because overwhelmingly, the quantum mass in which you write a legitimate question there has the answer nowhere to be found around it … you want the shortest program to look at the broader randomized process you are instantiating..." Also the guard of §2.2: `solomonoff function induction gives at least some const probability to bad behavior on ood inputs.md` says its proposition "is relevant eg to the attempt to get science from solomonoff with random prompts".
* **Unresolved.** Whether decomposition works at all for research (§2.13).
* **Tension between two notes** [my own derivation]. This scheme trains on random strings; the teaching note calls a random string as a question "probably very stupid". The first note is about support, the second about how much each datum teaches. A protocol has to answer both.

### 2.4 A steeper simplicity prior, private randomness, and related ways not to learn the wrong thing

* **What.** `philosophy/philosophy of thinking/beating solomonoff induction at grokking a notion.md` asks how a human can "not make the mistakes another person would make", and lists ways to get this:
  * "an even stronger simplicity prior than solomonoff. eg if there are explainable mistakes on a simple model, you want the simple model that doesn't predict the mistakes. this will have inf log loss but let's just do a version of the simple hypothesis with noise, and then penalize the likelihood term less. have people not already considered this for solving the model + data split problem? does this attempt to solve the model data split problem introduce some pathologies?" The pathology in sequence prediction ("it'll be better to drop bits and take the likelihood loss") "is not present in the function case, if we don't get randomness in the universal semimeasure way (like if we make the randomness not shared between different inputs — each input has to sample its own random bits)".
  * "alternatively: just set abs bound on model complexity, rest has to be likelihood. this feels bad because if you get the bound wrong you get some nonsense"; or look for the point where the graph of remaining entropy against hypothesis length changes slope.
  * "simplicity prior defined in terms of existing understanding".
  * "you specify properties of the thing or notion sometimes" (§2.5).
  * "some sort of t complexity bound might help."
  * "also as a human one can just ask: what is it that this person is trying to teach me. what is this person trying to point at."
* **Hope.** In the function case the "good model" (simple model plus independent noise) is "a vertex of the convex hull of the set of attainable (hypothesis complexity, expected neg log likelihood) tuples", so some penalty picks it; for imitation, the sampler is picked and not "the sampler with some bits specified" (quotation (e) in §1.1).
* **Where it fails or is unresolved** (his words, in the LessWrong thread copied at `/home/user/AI-works/solomonoff-operator-induction/research/prior/hanni-lesswrong-comment-thread.md`): "Maybe I'm just failing to construct the right "universal hypothesis" for this example?"; "I can see some other examples where this functional setup still doesn't work nicely"; "The example here is definitely somewhat cherry-picked"; the coefficient must grow with the data ("in the bayesian picture we will need to pick a stronger prior when the data set is larger in this example"); "I think it's very unlikely this steeper penalization is anywhere close to a full solution to the philosophical problem here."
* **Established in this repository since then** [read in source: `/home/user/AI-works/solomonoff-operator-induction/README.md`, item 6]: exact recovery of a sampler with private coins for λ > 1; a toy imitation theorem with matching limits ("data identify behaviour, not mechanism; an entangled person cannot be copied by any procedure; a coupling with a short description defeats every λ/n").

### 2.5 Property induction and property preservation

**No note defines the property inductor.** What the notes contain:

* **The plan** (`ai/learning/solomonoff induction/solomonoff induction is not the paragon of generalization.pdf`, August 2025, section "plan"):
  > "• a human can totally handle new situations well even when SI outputs would not
  > • formally, there's a trivial case where we have some computable criterion that outputs are supposed to meet. and you can just make an inductor whose outputs keep meeting the computable criterion, when those of SI might not. one should construct a concrete example here where SI keeps taking Ls
  > • ok but you could say this is sort of silly. i'd say it is mathematically silly, but nevertheless philosophically important to understand!
  > • define a property inductor. this sometimes works well, and feels less silly. it arguably captures the intuitive idea of "please continue like this" better than SI. its approximations are computable things which can do better than SI. construct an example where it does better than SI
  > • Is it possible to beat SI at providing binary labels to data? if we care about individual labels being good in a log loss additive way, then no i guess
  > • is this just AIXI with some utility function?"

  and under "mess": "so, we are interested in examples where computable things keep beating solomonoff. i guess they must not be predicting stuff well then? … why can't we just keep doing ood shifts, and keep seeing solomonoff fail? well maybe we could, but then a computable thing which is getting things right must also keep failing at prediction". The abstract's thesis: "generalization quality is often importantly different from the ability to predict raw data."
* **Two meanings of generalising well** (`ai/learning/solomonoff induction/solomonoff function induction.pdf`, §3): "generalization in some specific way", and "to produce more outputs with some property (that often cannot be explicitly defined). … we're quite happy with any correct proof being provided in this case. Really, the first notion is a special case of the second, just with the property that the input-output pairs are those from a particular function." Then the two-sided "result" (§4, Q5).
* **The argument and its defeater** (`could one do science with solomonoff on text?.md`): quoted under D13 and in point 4 of the summary; the example of a counter-property is "that the papers are written by ppl from before 2025".
* **A property verifier inside the mixture** (`does solomonoff generalize well out of distribution?.md`): "you can run a compute bounded SI inside solomonoff that is additionally conditioning on the property verifier (like, for the property of interest). you will need to code the sequence so far, but this can be done at its kolmo complexity, right? should think thru the details carefully here. if the property is simple, i think this comes close to implying kolmo doesn't do that poorly on that property?"
* **Properties as what a notion is made of** (`beating solomonoff induction at grokking a notion.md`): "eg [concrete] and [abstract] make a partition of things maybe, but [alice would think this is concrete] and [alice would think this is abstract] might not"; "1+1=2 won't be true if you accidentally assign 1->rabbit and 2->chicken from a demonstration"; integrals were used for two centuries because "we hypothesized an object with some properties and these properties turned out to be those of a real thing, and in fact to pin it down uniquely! though of course this leaves the following important question: how did we identify this set of properties as important?"
* **Which properties project** (`philosophy/epistemology/induction/grue-bleen and UTMs.md`): either grue "has higher complexity" in a reasonable language, or "we have a sense of which patterns continue, and which patterns fail to continue … a meta-pattern that lets us point at good patterns … can the UTM just be seen as a making concrete / capturing of noticed patterns on patterns?", or we understand the mechanism. And `philosophy/epistemology/induction/the complexity of 'now'.md` (the file name has typographic quotes): "for any theory T, there is a theory T' which is the same except with an exception clause for 'the next moment'. if that were constant complexity, would be fucked because always at least const prob on opposite then. so 'the next measurement' must be not const bits".
* **Simplest object with given properties** (`philosophy/philosophy of thinking/analogy/likening a situation to a mathematical situation.md`): "a good method for finding the right mathematical object is to list some properties and try to just find the simplest thing which satisfies these properties … sometimes a unique mathematical object is actually forced by a categoricity result"; with the question in §4, Q15.
* **Properties as verifiers or questions** (`philosophy/prediction/predicting specific things.md`): "for each property, there could be a verifier. or in some broader sense there could be a question. you predict the answer to that question / new properties are added when they help answer question."

**What he hopes.** That "continue like this" can be made an inductor whose outputs keep the properties the data had, correctness of answers among them, and that this is closer to what a human does than raw prediction.

**Where it fails or is unresolved.**
* Preservation of one property can contradict preservation of another (the defeater).
* The trivial version needs the criterion in hand; the interesting case is a property that "often cannot be explicitly defined".
* What he asked next, and what this repository found [read in source: `/home/user/AI-works/ultranatural/README.md`]: tallying the properties a label would break gives the same window as SOI's own margin, because "One guard, "if the input is like x, do something else", breaks all of them at once for about sep(x; A) bits"; and laws of the whole function do pin addition down, "but only because large numbers are expensive to name" (the task text's summary of `equational.md`).

**My reading of the intended type** [my own derivation]. A property inductor keeps a posterior over properties R ⊆ X × Y (deciders, or verifiers), updates it on the pairs seen as positive examples (§2.6), and outputs at a new x some y with (x, y) in the likely R. The "trivial case" is R known. Read this way, property induction is the generative form of the verifier route of §2.11, and its two open parts are the prior over properties and the choice among the y that satisfy R.

### 2.6 Positive example induction

**No note defines it under that name.** The nearest content is a size-principle calculation and a generative-versus-classifier comparison.

* **What** (`could one do science with solomonoff on text?.md`, "an attempt at a concrete calculation"): "we have a data set $E$ of examples, with each example being a question asked in $1900$ understanding together with a textbook answering that question. Let $G$ be the set of all good question+answer pairs. Let's say the point is to learn $G$\[^4\]. To hopefully make things easier, let us assume that $E$ is a random subset of $G$. … we say we have a prior on which property $G$ we might be shown samples of, and then we update on the samples we see being $E$. This sets our probability that $G=g$ to $0$ for any $g$ such that $E$ has an element outside $g$. For any $g\supseteq E$, the probability is proportional to the prior times a factor which depends on $|g|$, with larger candidates $g$ losing more probability. … The likelihood which the actual $G$ gets is then $|G|^{-|E|}$, whereas the likelihood of any other $G'$ containing all of $E$ is $|G'|^{-|E|}$. At what point do we expect the correct $G$ to have the highest posterior probability?" A footnote adds that "really the point is to generate/complete some particular strings in $G\setminus E$".
* **The same idea as a generative hypothesis** (`ai/learning/good paper generator given math oracle?.md`): "specify the set with the property P …, then put a pointer inside this set. what's the complexity of this generative thing? i guess we need to spend complexity(P) + num-train-data-pts times the avg of log (size(P)) over questions in train data". The alternative, "train a good-textbook-classifier somehow, and then just generate the alphabetically first thing this classifier maps to 1", has "the obvious question … how one is supposed to get negative examples. / tentatively: the generative model case makes more sense".
* **Hope.** Positive examples alone, with a penalty on large sets, identify the set of good answers; "presumably something starts working here at some amount of data. but how much data is needed?"
* **Unresolved.** His question above; "can we say something about what happens for generic properties P?"; the "friendly" assumption that training answers are uniform over all good answers.
* **Two remarks** [my own derivation]. (i) For imitation the set is the set of answers the human might give; learning it is learning the support, or a high-probability set, of P(·|x). (ii) The size penalty acts only where data fall. A set equal to G except on inputs with an unseen property Q is consistent with every sample and loses no likelihood, so it keeps posterior weight about 2^(−K(Q)) times a term for the alternative: the guard of §2.2 again. This agrees with the grue theorem as stated in `/home/user/AI-works/ultranatural/README.md` ("every scheme whose inductive bias comes from the reference machine alone").

### 2.7 Solomonoff axiom induction

* **What** (`ai/learning/solomonoff induction/solomonoff axiom induction.md`): "you have say mathematical statements. you are told that some are true, some are false … you get all the axiom systems from one can prove these … and not their negations …, with some weights". A new statement gets "a p(true/false/independent)".
* **Hope.** Hypotheses of the kind a scientific theory is: "A scientific theory is much less like a program that prints (or predicts) an observation sequence than it is like a theory in the sense used in logic" (`philosophy/philosophy of science/some disanalogies between solomonoff induction and science.md`). A third verdict comes for free.
* **Where it stands.** He argues that "solomonoff function induction with consistent assigners only is equivalent to the version of solomonoff axiom induction that requires proofs" (§4, Q12), so it is a restriction of function induction, not a new power. Without the consistency requirement he thinks function induction can beat it by more than a constant (on suitable coherent data ""always accept" gets everything right here and is very simple, but axiom induction will suck"). Fixes he lists: "we should be fine with only generating a very small subset of observations", "we should be fine with making some mistakes", "a metric on values".
* **Bearing on imitation** [my own derivation]. It applies to labels of sentences, hence to the verifier route. Its hypotheses are closed under inference, so a deviant label at a sentence the data entail is impossible; an exception must be consistent with the data and costs what it costs to state. That is the mechanism of the equational inductor, not an escape from the floor at inputs the data do not constrain.

### 2.8 Circuit induction

* **What** (`ai/learning/circuit solomonoff/circuit occam learning.md`): order Boolean functions by the description length of their smallest circuit; "guess next outputs … with the simplest function compatible with the data so far". The Bayes version over circuits is in `ai/learning/circuit solomonoff/for circuit induction, regret and complexity are polynomially related.md`.
* **Hope.** A computable stand-in for the ideal inductor with the same guarantees; a bridge to neural networks ("NN-bayes" as circuit induction: `ai/learning/NN bayes generalization/`, `ai/learning/open problems for bridging parametrized bayes and solomonoff (comment on lucius's dialogue).md`); and the original cloning question (§4, Q1), where the circuit is "the model being stolen, i.e. Eliezer".
* **Where it fails or is unresolved.** In distribution he has a theorem. Out of distribution: "I don't know if an out-of-distribution learning result is also true in this setting. My guess is that this is probably "morally true", at least for "generic ways to order inputs" but I can't prove any such result"; he has a counterexample "for a specific weird circuit language, picking a specific weird input ordering" (§4, Q2, Q3). `ai/learning/circuit solomonoff/ood min circuit induction.md` ends: "somehow you need the simplest circuit to not generalize correctly but most circuits to survive... seems weird".
* **Related practical thread.** With real learners, data must make the target shallow: "if you fail to give dense data for any structure in the brain, then the structure "looks deep" to the ML algorithm you're using, and then it is probably not going to be "learned properly"" (`ai/mind uploads with ML (and prediction in particular)/msgs to samuel on ML mind upload difficulty.md`); "you have a deep boolean circuit … you can try to "model steal" it with ML by training on its input output pairs / this will suck … but! if you give intermediate states like every 3 layers of the circuit and use ML to predict each next intermediate state from the previous one, then you can win … the theme: making stuff shallow lets ML win" (`some good questions in conceptual alignment.md`).

### 2.9 Time-bounded universal induction

* **What** (`ai/learning/solomonoff induction/polytime solomonoff (response to lucius).md`): a mixture over all programs, each cut off after p(n)·log log n steps and started late, with constant regret against every O(p(n))-time predictor (§4, Q13).
* **Hope.** A computable inductor with a dominance theorem; and a way to use the gap between time and description length (`philosophy/prediction/playing t-complexity against k-complexity.md`): the simplest process P′ matching a bounded process P "might be easier to run", or "maybe the process $P'$ has higher t-complexity but lower k-complexity. and maybe it in fact generalizes "correctly" to things which are not in the time bound of P / example: you get humans to provide provable/disprovable labels to mathematical statements, in cases where given mathematical literature your math researchers can figure it out in 1 hour. it seems plausible this generalizes correctly to statements we couldn't prove/disprove in 1 hour?"
* **Where it stands.** The construction is claimed with a proof; the easy-to-hard tension of §2.2 is the caution. For imitation there is a consequence [my own derivation]: under a pure simplicity prior the simplest explanation of a bounded human's answers may be an unbounded idealisation of the human (proof search, in his example). That may be welcome for capability and is not a copy.

### 2.10 Induction from similar cases; random stopping

* **What** (`philosophy/philosophy of thinking/some ideal inductions.md`): for a new question with "a bunch of questions … already answered", after dismissing sequence induction ("i think this probably doesn't give you a good answer") and function induction ("i think this is a good idea, but it also fails in that way"): "maybe we can think of induction as being from similar cases. then we need to bring into attention some similar questions … one way to do this would be to use some sort of learned similarity between questions. this prompts: where does the similarity come from? / one way to do this would be to think of the question as a special case of some class of questions, and to look at other known answers of questions in this class. this prompts: how" (the note stops there).
* **A sound special case** (`philosophy/philosophy of understanding/induction in mathematics.md`): for a fixed property P of natural numbers, "say one goes in with a commitment to check to some random n and then conclude for n+1 … one gets a failure, ie a wrong inference, iff the statement is true up to n and then false. having fixed P, there is at most a unique such n, so the probability of this is at most the max over n of the stopping probability at n, which we can make arbitrarily small!" And on reference classes: "my guess is that one can in principle always cook up classes in an adversarial fashion which let you convince me of any falsehood this way. however, maybe this mostly goes away once one starts to prefer simpler classes?"
* **Hope.** An induction justified "by pointing at a big class of which that inference is one example, and arguing that most inference in this class are correct" (same note).
* **Unresolved.** Where similarity or the class comes from. The random-stopping argument is his one statement in which randomising where the checking stops gives a guarantee with no assumption about the property; §6 uses it.

### 2.11 Verification instead of, or on top of, imitation

* **What.** "you could try to just use solomonoff on human labels to get a verifier"; "some sort of debate" (shortlist). The folder `ai/verification and generation/` studies what can be verified at all.
* **Hope.** If outputs can be checked, failures are obvious (D12) and a generator need not be trusted. For mathematics this works: "you're asking for $x$ such that $\phi(x)$ … someone could hand me some $x$ and a supposed proof of $\phi(x)$, and i could verify" (`ai/verification and generation/cases of verification.md`). A verifier learned from labels of proved and refuted statements plausibly generalises (§2.2).
* **Where it fails.**
  * Soft things have no known verification protocol: physics-olympiad solutions, philosophical answers, technologies ("is this really a mind upload of me that runs at 100x speed?"), plans (`cases of verification.md`).
  * "it is easy to verify that proposed gadget X indeed does some desired thing F … but it is hard to verify that it only does F, and not also anything else that is bad … this sounds a lot more like "there is no bad thing Y such that Y would happen" which has a coNP flavor" (`ai/verification and generation/x verification is easy; only x verification is hard?.md`).
  * A learned or human verifier can be optimised against: "training against a verifier, if it works, probably completely fucks everything" (`ai/verification and generation/why verification is sorta tough (where we're confused)?.md`); "hacking humans is a scientific problem we can state. it is surely easier than many other scientific problems we can state?" (`ai/getting research from a misaligned AI/humans getting hacked?.md`).
  * A total computable labeller of provable against refutable sentences does not exist: "there is no hypothesis which assigns 1 to each provable statement and 0 to each disprovable statement" among total computable ones (`philosophy/prediction/a predictive model assigning truth-values to sentences.md`; he cites the consistent guessing problem). So good verifiers must be allowed not to answer.
  * A verifier needs negative examples (§2.6).
  * Conservative decision rules on top of an uncertain estimator: "unless the system is basically perfect anyway, this is just impossible to satisfy without paralysis. And if the system is basically perfect anyway, then it's probably good enough to work with the vanilla decision rule" (`ai/saifh/Thoughts on conservatism.md`); the bit-counting version is in `ai/saifh/a criticism of knightian decision rules for safe general intelligences.md` (§3, F20).

### 2.12 Simulation, and richer data than (x, y)

* **Simulation** (`ai/research from hypercomputer/research from simulation.md`): with the laws of physics and unbounded compute, "set up an initial field configuration of earth except that magically everyone has disappeared except alignment researchers who are told to figure out some way to AI-x-derisk our non-simulation world … i think the biggest problem (assuming hypercomputer and the laws of physics) is: how the fuck do you create the initial field configuration."
* **Extraction from a world model** (the "cursed" list): find in the inductor's best program "the guys it "takes to exist"" and run them forward. He cannot write this down.
* **Brain data** (`ai/mind uploads with ML (and prediction in particular)/`): predict scans; the obstacle is density of data ("My guess is that an MRI is really far from probing "densely enough" for ML to "find most of the structures correctly""), with toy problems: learn a cellular automaton from states every 10^6 steps, or from space and time averages; recover a Turing machine from "some noisy measurements of its computational state" (`turing machine prediction.md`). Counter-argument recorded from a friend: "if you do any low-level thing, then high-level behaviors are not going to emerge, because you're missing some low-level dynamics that create them" (`sam's counterarguments.md`). His reply is a hope about the target's own robustness: "maybe there are circuits in the brain which reduce noise already … the human's ability to act goal-directedly: if you have a circuit in the brain tracking that you're making progress toward something, even a noisy version of it might help keep the system on track" (`some reasons for optimism about getting research from a scan-predictor.md`).
* **Bearing on the present problem** [my own derivation]. Under unbounded compute the depth obstacle disappears, but the data type remains a design choice: answers only, or also intermediate work (a scratchpad). The robustness remark is the notes' only suggestion for how per-step closeness could become closeness of a long process: the human corrects small errors.

### 2.13 Humans consulting copies of themselves: how long must an episode be?

* **What.** The question behind task decomposition, in `.../at which point does imitation become powerful?/a statement of the question for matt and damiano.md`: "Suppose that you have a machine M that lets you sample from the distribution you get when you take any current human, give them any prompt …, and give them time T to produce some output … How large does T need to be for it to be possible to get research progress (e.g. on alignment or on the riemann hypothesis) with a modest/realistic number of calls to M?"

## 4. The precise mathematical questions and conjectures in the notes

For each: the statement as he gives it, its status in the notes, and where I know it, its status in this repository. Statuses in this repository are [read in source] from the two READMEs named in §0 or from `repo.md` in this folder (a teammate's digest whose labels I cite; I did not open the paper sections myself except where I say so).

**Q1. The cloning question for random circuits** (`ai/human imitation/notes on human imitation.md`).
> "Take a random boolean circuit $C$; it implements some function $f\colon \{0,1\}^n\to \{0,1\}$. (We will probably need to pick the circuit to have some reasonably small size, like at most the size at which one starts to get all boolean functions.) Split the input space into train and test data (maybe even drawn from a different distribution to model the out-of-distribution case). Is it the case that with high probability (i.e. for most $C$), any circuit $C'$ with associated function $f'$ which agrees with $f$ on the train data will either also agree with $f$ on test data or be much larger? (Ideally, putting some complexity prior on circuits, almost all the probability mass conditional on seeing the input-output behavior on train data would be on $C$.)"

Status in the notes: open; "seems like the obvious OOD generalization problem to write down". He points to compressed sensing as "a sort of very strong version of this for a particular (silly) model class … their thm exactly says that the secret vector can be recovered as the simplest one with the given input-output behavior with just a small num of data pts / i wonder if this can be made a special case of some more general thm for a much larger func class", and links a 1978 paper of Pearl (identified in Appendix A).

**Q2. Mistakes of Occam circuit learning on an arbitrary ordering** (`ai/learning/circuit solomonoff/circuit occam learning.md`).
* *Theorem he states (in distribution).* "Fix an arbitrary distribution on the input space $\{-1,1\}^d$ (eg uniform). After seeing $1000000 \cdot C(f)$ input-output pairs, with probability $>0.99$ (on which input-output pairs we happen to sample), the simplest surviving function gets $>0.99$ test accuracy (measured against that same distribution)." Proof sketch by a union bound over the simpler circuits.
* *Claim with a "physicist's proof" (any ordering).* "Fix some ordering of all $2^d$ possible inputs. Apply this learning algorithm online, counting mistakes. The number of mistakes will be at most $1000000 \cdot C(f)$." "My question is whether this sort of thing can be given a rigorous proof or a counterexample."
* *His counterexample for a contrived language:* a circuit language in which each single memorised exception is simpler than the constant function, probed in order of complexity: "we keep guessing wrong for like $2^{C(0)}$ times".
* *A related existence question* (`ood min circuit induction.md`): inputs x_1, …, x_n and circuits C_1, …, C_n of non-decreasing size with C_i agreeing with f on x_1, …, x_{i−1} and disagreeing at x_i, n superpolynomial in the circuit complexity of f. His answer: "this is possible" by exceptions of growing complexity, "but also this isn't the right question", since those are not the simplest consistent circuits.
* Status in this repository: for Bayes over circuits the number of mistakes is polynomial in the circuit size on every ordering, but Occam learning of the conjunction of d variables makes at least 2^d − d − 2 mistakes on a simply described ordering, for every tie rule; "It is the selection rule, not the prior, that fails" (`repo.md`, item C4, citing Paper 1, Theorems 9.8 and 9.10). So his claim holds for the mixture and fails for the simplest-consistent rule he stated it for.

**Q3. Regret and circuit complexity are polynomially related** (`ai/learning/circuit solomonoff/for circuit induction, regret and complexity are polynomially related.md`).
* (a) "for circuit induction, expected regret m when learning the function f implies there is a circuit of size O(m log m * inputdim) implementing f". Sketch: split the log loss by whether the mistake probability exceeds 1/2; bound the large ones by adding a memorised exception to every surviving circuit. He notes an unrepaired gap ("i think the issue is not serious, just mildly annoying"), and adds: "i now think the statement … is true for NN bayes as well".
* (b) Under i.i.d. uniform inputs, "the expected number of errors is at most $poly(C(f),d)$".
* Open, in his words: "Without any assumption on the distribution, proving this sort of regret bound becomes equivalent to the out-of-distribution case, which idk how to rigorously prove (also idk if the statement is even true)."
* Status in this repository: as under Q2.

**Q4. Dominance, and what the order of the inputs gives away** (`ai/learning/solomonoff induction/solomonoff function induction.md`; the same text is §2 of the PDF).
* *"Thm 1 (template)":* "For a given class C of predictors, each predictor P in C loses to "the solomonoff inductor for C". That is: on any data distribution, that solomonoff inductor does worse than P in log loss by at most a constant (depending on $P$ but not the distribution)." Easy, he says, for computable samplers that see only x.
* *Two constructions against a version for predictors that see the data so far.* (i) "let $x_1, x_2, \ldots$ be a deterministic sequence of increasingly long random strings, and make a function which has input-output pairs $(x_1,0),(x_2,x_1),(x_3,x_2),(x_4,x_3),\ldots$, and make the data set reveal them in precisely that order": a trivial predictor has zero loss; "I'm pretty sure Solomonoff induction gets unbounded loss on this sequence. I don't actually have a proof". (ii) "you give 0 to everything except one string you've skipped among all strings of a certain length (repeat for each length). the predictor that sees the data set can easily tell which one it is and give that 1. but solomonoff has nothing inside doing that — if it had, we could compress these incompressible strings!"
* *His "Thm 1" for a simple reveal law:* "Let C be the set of computable samplers which get to see the revealed input-output data set so far when sampling the output on the next input … Suppose the data-generating process samples a function $f\colon X\to Y$ and then reveals a sequence of input-output pairs, with the sequence of inputs sampled from a distribution $\Delta$. Then each predictor P in C can only beat solomonoff function induction in expected log loss by at most the complexity $C_\Delta$ of sampling from the reveal sequence distribution $\Delta$ plus a constant $C_P$ (depending on the predictor but not the data-generating process)." Proof sketch: an internal simulator and Jensen's inequality.
* *Open, in his words:* "can thm 1 be improved to a version that talks about the algorithmic mutual information of $\Delta$ and $f$ instead of the complexity of $\Delta$?"; "could also prove equivalence of universal semimeasure and kolmogorov versions? if they are in fact equivalent".
* Status in this repository: "The dominance theorem for computable reveal laws in the notes holds pathwise for deterministic laws. For randomised laws it holds in expectation, under finiteness assumptions and with an extra K(n) + K(A) term; the sketched proof in the notes does not work as written"; "Kolmogorov version versus semimeasure version: not equivalent"; if the hypotheses also see the list of previous inputs, the loss is at most that of sequence induction plus a constant (`/home/user/AI-works/solomonoff-operator-induction/README.md`, items 2 and 3).

**Q5. The two-sided "result" on property-abiding generalisation** (`ai/learning/solomonoff induction/solomonoff function induction.pdf`, §3, stated without proof):
> "• the probability of P-abiding generalization is at least 2^{−C(P)}
> • if no input with property Q is seen during training, then probability of P-abiding generalization is at most 2^{−C(Q)} times 2 to the negative complexity of the smallest string not satisfying P on the given input times a const"

* *Reading* [my own derivation]. The second bullet, taken literally, bounds the probability of abiding from above by a small number, which contradicts the first. His gloss ("at least a const probability of happening but also at least some const probability of not happening") shows the intent: the probability of *violating* P at an input with the unseen property Q is at least a constant times 2^(−C(Q)) times 2^(−C(z)), z a simplest violating output.
* Status in this repository: the project proved a two-sided theorem of this kind with K(P, Q) in the first bound and showed that two natural pointwise forms fail (`repo.md`, item D3, citing Paper 1, Theorem 8.3 and Proposition F.3; I opened Proposition F.3 in `/home/user/AI-works/solomonoff-operator-induction/paper/sections/app-ood.tex`: with P = "the label is 0" and data labelled 1 on all of {0,1}^d but an incompressible x*, the probability of label 0 at x* is at most 2^(−d+c) although K(P) = O(1)).
* *A form of the first bullet that needs no Q* [my own derivation; unrefereed]. Assume the data themselves abide by P, which is the case of a property that has held so far. Let P ⊆ X × Y be decidable with a decider of complexity K(P). For each hypothesis p let p^P be p with its P-violating outputs suppressed (a sampler with private coins redraws until the output abides; a deterministic program diverges instead). The map p ↦ p^P costs K(P) + c bits, and p^P gives data D ⊆ P at least the probability p gives it. Hence, for the coin-free mixture W and for Solomonoff's per-query mixture M_pq, and for every D ⊆ P, the hypotheses that never output a P-violating label on any input have posterior weight at least 2^(−K(P)−c), and the posterior probability of a violating label at any next input is at most 1 − 2^(−K(P)−c). This is consistent with Proposition F.3, whose data violate P.
* *A hindsight bound of the same kind* [my own derivation; unrefereed]. For M_pq and any data D_n ⊆ P (realisable or not), let v_t(p) be the probability that hypothesis p outputs a P-violating label at x_t. Then the posterior mean of Σ_{t≤n} v_t(p), under the posterior given all of D_n, is at most (K(P) + c)·ln 2. Proof: M_pq ≥ 2^(−K(P)−c)·Σ_p 2^(−|p|) p^P(D_n), and p^P(D_n) = p(D_n)·Π_t (1 − v_t(p))^(−1); divide by M_pq(D_n) and apply Jensen's inequality to the logarithm. So a property that has held on n inputs is, on those inputs, violated by the posterior with average probability O(K(P)/n). I did not get the corresponding statement for the next, unseen input with K(P) in place of K of the target: the obstacle is hypotheses that start violating only at inputs of a new kind, the guard again.

**Q6. The floor, its logarithmic term and its machine dependence** (`philosophy/prediction/solomonoff function induction gives at least some const probability to bad behavior on ood inputs.md`).
> "Proposition. suppose there is some simple property such that no input with that property showed up in training. then you have at least corresponding const probability of just outputting 0 on all inputs with that property"

with the checks he asks for: "is there some log term here that one is actually losing? we wouldn't get a const in that case. probably you can avoid that with the universal semimeasure?" and the machine question quoted in §2.2. Also the converse he asks for (`solomonoff function induction.md`): "can we show something like: if there isn't a simple property distinguishing test from train, then solomonoff function induction works well? … we could maybe prove that it works well if there isn't a simple distinguishing property given such a program, but maybe that's less interesting?"

Status in this repository: the floor holds with no logarithmic term; a universal machine K(Q, b) + c away makes the bad behaviour on Q have posterior probability at least 1/2; "no simple separating property ⇒ small error" fails for complex targets and holds, given the labelled sample, with a factor depending on the target; a positive statement for properties simple given a program fitted to the training data only is open (`repo.md`, items D1, D2, D5). In `/home/user/AI-works/ultranatural/note.md`, Theorem 1 [read in source]: for a total computable f consistent with the data and x new, every property true so far that excludes (x, f(x)) has complexity at least sep(x; A | f) − c, where sep(·| f) is separation complexity given a program for f. Consequence [my own derivation]: every total computable g consistent with the data and with g(x) ≠ f(x) has K(g) ≥ sep(x; A | f) − 2c, because the graph of g is such a property. This is a positive converse in exactly the relative form of which he says "maybe that's less interesting?": if no test that is simple *given f* separates x from the inputs seen, all simple consistent hypotheses agree with f at x.

**Q7. Teaching** (`philosophy/prediction/which input-output pairs does solomonoff function induction need to see to generalize correctly?.md`).
> "Proposition. for the purposes of this statement, let's say f has been learned by a predictor when on every input the output of f is more probable than every other output. you can always learn f by showing the function value on K(f) inputs (like, if you choose well). (here, K(f) denotes the kolmogorov complexity of the shortest program computing f)
> Proof. suppose f(x) is not the unique top guess on x. then show f(x). note that since f(x) wasn't the top guess, this conditioning raises the probability of f by at least a factor of 2. if given the new posterior you can find another such input, then repeat. since the probability of f starts out at at least 2^{-K(f)}, this can't happen more than $K(f)$ times."

Status in the notes: proved as quoted, for a deterministic target with prior weight at least 2^(−K(f)). Two limits [my own derivation]: "learned" means the top guess is right at every input, with no margin, so the floor of Q6 still stands at every input that a simple test separates from the K(f) teaching inputs; and choosing the teaching inputs requires knowing f. The practical question he attaches is open: "you have a human giving outputs on some inputs, eg to scientific questions after thinking for 3 months. you want to know which inputs you need to get data on to get good generalization."

**Q8. Is there out-of-distribution generalisation that is not of an already-seen kind?** (`philosophy/prediction/does solomonoff generalize well out of distribution?.md`). The weakly held hypothesis quoted in §2.2; the claim to check that an inductor conditioned on a simple property verifier sits inside the mixture at a cost of about the verifier's complexity; and the question whether "across many ood generalizations, solomonoff can't lose to this humanlike inductor by that much" says more than "if you set things up such that you're no longer ood compared to the previous stuff, then you can win". Status in the notes: open. In this repository the "novelty budget" answers the counting part: with sep_k the separation complexity of the k-th input from the earlier ones, the error at step k is at least 2^(−sep_k−c) and Σ_k 2^(−sep_k) ≤ 1, "each simple property surprises only once" when labels are revealed (`repo.md`, item D4, citing Paper 1, Proposition 8.5).

## 5. What he would count as success

**The standard, in his words.**
* "The point here is to specify a plan that clearly works (as opposed to a thing you might merely assign >50% chance of working after thinking about it for a few hours but still feel really confused about) — you should make a compelling case that your thing works." (`ai/alignment general/some good questions to ponder to understand alignment/some good questions in conceptual alignment.md`, question 6)
* The draft's form of it: a protocol that "just really works (modulo having very much compute, but not modulo keeping the human in the loop producing data forever), ideally for which we could prove some theorem that convinces us it really works"; "such and such a learning protocol works with high probability for producing a close enough P' with only decently few queries to P", true "when there is a distribution shift between training and deployment".

**What the result must deliver** (from §1.2).
1. On inputs where P very likely gives a correct solution, P′ gives one too; where P would not behave bizarrely under a shift to inputs with a simple property, neither does P′; or P′ "basically only fails obviously", and obviously without asking the human (draft; D1, D12, D13).
2. The guarantee covers the inputs the scheme itself produces under task decomposition (D9).
3. The data are affordable by his rules: short human tasks or existing data; few queries; no long human work laundered through memorisation (D5, D6).
4. Unbounded compute may be used; a secure server may be assumed (D5).
5. The scheme is general, "not … fine-tuned to this case in particular" (D8).
6. Preferably the result says something about being a copy (D3). Here he has already said what would satisfy him: "the ideal theorem here would be some sort of robustness of recovering the sampler, at least in a toy setting" (quotation (e) in §1.1).

**What he accepts as partial success.** A toy theorem with honest scope. From the same message: "we are mostly not claiming to have solved the practical motivating problems … we are just hoping to provide some useful ideas for a toolkit for handling these problems, and we think at least some toy cases of the problems are solved by these ideas (assuming that's your conclusion from the work you've done; of course don't say this if you think this is false)." And from the shortlist: "i want to understand if the in-distribution case works at least".

**What he does not accept.**
* A scheme that is plausible but not understood ("idk. on outside view, probably not", of his own best scheme).
* A guarantee bought by worst-case caution that makes the system inert (F20, F21).
* A scheme whose difficulty has moved into an unexamined step (F29), or that is tuned to its example (F28), or that needs the human indefinitely (draft).
* For verification-based variants: training the generator against the verifier (§2.11).

**The tests he is likely to apply to any proposal** [my own derivation, from the pattern of his objections to his own schemes].
1. *The guard test.* Name an input property that no training input has, in particular one that task decomposition creates. What does the scheme do there, and with what probability? (F1–F3)
2. *The machine test.* Is the answer to test 1 a fact about the scheme, or about the reference machine? ("there's a programming language in which it doesn't have a better const")
3. *The referent test.* Does a hypothesis that models how the data set was made do as well as the intended one? (F8–F10)
4. *The copy test.* Does anything in the theorem distinguish the sampler from a better predictor of it, or from the sampler with coins filled in? (F13–F15)
5. *The budget test.* How many human answers, of what length, on which questions? Could humans produce them in "a few hours" each, or does the protocol need human-years per datum? (D5, D6, §2.13)
6. *The rock test.* Does the scheme still act on the inputs that matter, or does it abstain everywhere new? (F20)
7. *The horizon test.* Granting perfect per-call imitation, does the scaffold do anything? (§2.13) This one is outside a theorem about the learner, and he knows it.

**The kind of mathematics he writes and reads** [my own derivation]. Dominance statements against an explicit comparison class with a constant "depending on $P$ but not the distribution" (Q4, Q13); explicit counterexamples to the natural stronger statement (Q2, Q4); two-sided bounds (Q5). He writes down when a constant makes a statement empty: "losing by a constant in $\log$ loss on the entire sequence is "not too much" because you can lose a constant in log loss by just being silly … on some finite initial sequence … (That said, the constant $c_P$ could of course be very large.)" (`ai/learning/solomonoff induction/polytime solomonoff (response to lucius).md`, Remark 2). A theorem whose constant is K(P) for a human will meet that remark.

## 6. What the notes imply for the design of a protocol

Everything in this section is **[my own derivation]** unless tagged otherwise, and is unrefereed. It is here because the summary asked of me is "the findings that matter most for designing an imitation protocol with a provable guarantee". §6.3 relates the notes to the lead's candidate theory at `/home/user/AI-works/principled-imitation/research/candidate-theory.md` as it stood when I read it (a first working draft, marked there as unchecked).

### 6.1 Six constraints the notes put on any protocol

1. **Without labels at inputs of a new kind, no protocol is right there, and task decomposition makes inputs of a new kind.** This is F1–F3 with the project's quantification by sep(x; A). So the human's answers must reach the deployment distribution somehow: by asking during deployment-like runs, by training prompts that cover it, or by a class of hypotheses that forbids patches. The notes develop a version of the second (§2.3). Of the first they have only the early remark about consulting the human "at critical junctures" and the coin-flipping aside (§2.14). The third is the subject of the project's ultranatural notes, not of his.
2. **Coverage by prompts chosen in advance is the route he tried, and it is exponentially expensive.** See Observation D.
3. **Abstention must be the whitelist kind.** A rule that abstains whenever some surviving hypothesis disagrees abstains on every new input (F20, F21). A rule that abstains only on inputs that a simple test separates from what has been asked is the conservatism he accepts ("an effective whitelist generated by looking at what stuff your body makes itself by default"), and the number of its abstentions can be counted (Observation B).
4. **A guarantee on behaviour is all that data can give;** a statement about being a copy needs extra structure. In the notes the extra structure is private randomness with a steeper prior (§2.4); in the project's second paper it is that plus an incompressibility condition, with matching impossibility results.
5. **The human's episodes are long and few.** If T is of the order of a year (§2.13), each human answer costs about a human-year: a thousand answers is a thousand human-years, and a human-run tree with more than a handful of nodes is out of reach. Any theorem that needs the human system itself to be run many times is restricted to small scaffolds.
6. **The constant will be K(P) or worse, for a human.** The notes do not estimate it. They do contain the idea that would shrink it: a "simplicity prior defined in terms of existing understanding" (§2.4), that is, complexity of P given everything else that is known, which is a choice of reference machine and therefore falls under the machine test of §5.

### 6.2 Four observations

Setting for A: a stateless sampler P in a countable class with prior weights w, Bayes predictive M_D from the data D gathered so far (Solomonoff's per-query mixture when the class is all samplers; then ln(1/w(P)) ≤ (K(P) + c)·ln 2).

**Observation A (asking at random gives an average guarantee under any shift).** Inputs x_1, x_2, … arrive in any way that may depend on the whole past, including the system's own outputs. At step t a coin c_t with Pr[c_t = 1] = q is flipped independently of the past and of x_t. If c_t = 1 the human is asked, answers y_t ∼ P(·|x_t), and (x_t, y_t) joins D. If c_t = 0 the system answers with a draw from M_D(·|x_t). Then

  E Σ_t KL( P(·|x_t) ‖ M_{D_{<t}}(·|x_t) ) ≤ ln(1/w(P)) / q,

the sum running over all steps, asked or not. For a deterministic target f, the expected number of unasked steps at which the drawn answer differs from f(x_t) is at most (1 − q)·ln(1/w(f))/q.

*Proof.* On the asked steps the answers are draws from P at adaptively chosen inputs, so the usual Bayes regret bound gives E Σ_t c_t·KL_t ≤ ln(1/w(P)) (the lead's L3; `repo.md`, items A6 and A7, for the project's versions). KL_t is determined before c_t is flipped, so E[c_t·KL_t] = q·E[KL_t]. For deterministic f, the probability of a wrong draw is 1 − p_t ≤ ln(1/p_t) = KL_t, with p_t the predictive probability of f(x_t). ∎

*What it gives.* For T ≥ ln(1/w(P))/ε and q = ln(1/w(P))/(εT), the average over T steps of the expected KL is at most ε, with ln(1/w(P))/ε expected questions in total, whatever T and whatever the shift, because the asked inputs are an unbiased sample of the deployment inputs. This is the formal content I can attach to "requerying the human with some probability on the … deployment distribution". The random stopping argument of `philosophy/philosophy of understanding/induction in mathematics.md` (Q14) is the same device in its simplest form.

*What it does not give.* (i) Which steps are wrong: a single unasked input of a new kind is still wrong with probability at least 2^(−sep−c). (ii) Closeness of whole transcripts: summing total variations over T steps, with Pinsker's and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequalities, gives only sqrt(T·ln(1/w(P))/(2q)), which exceeds 1 in every case of interest. So a scaffold that needs every call to be right gains nothing; one that tolerates an ε fraction of wrong calls gains everything. Whether the human's own robustness to small errors (§2.12, "a goaly bit that keeps it on track") can be turned into such tolerance is not addressed in the notes. (iii) The human must stay reachable at random times through deployment, against D6, unless the random asking is confined to a training phase run on the scheme's own induced distribution and the learner is then frozen. For that variant the result to check is the reduction of Ross, Gordon and Bagnell (§6.4).

**Observation B (asking on novelty: the number of questions does not grow with deployment).** Deterministic target f, total computable hypotheses as in `/home/user/AI-works/ultranatural/note.md`. Rule: at input x, if sep(x; A) < s, where A is the set of inputs asked so far, ask the human and add x to A; otherwise answer with the simplest total hypothesis consistent with the answers so far. Then:
1. at most 2^s − 1 questions are ever asked, on any input sequence of any length;
2. if s > 2K(f) + c′, every unasked answer equals f(x).

*Proof.* (1) An asked x has a separating test Q with K(Q) < s that was disjoint from A before the question and is not after it; fewer than 2^s tests have complexity below s. (2) By the consequence stated under Q6, a consistent g with g(x) ≠ f(x) has K(g) ≥ sep(x; A | f) − 2c ≥ s − K(f) − 3c, which exceeds K(f) and hence the complexity of the simplest consistent hypothesis. ∎

*Reading.* The rule asks on the inputs that a test of fewer than s bits separates from what has been asked, which are the inputs where the guard argument gives a wrong label weight above about 2^(−s), and nowhere else: it is the "whitelist" of F20. The count is exponential in s and so in K(f), which is hopeless as a number; but it counts kinds of novelty met, not inputs, and it is finite without any assumption on the deployment sequence. sep is not computable; the unbounded compute he grants covers that only if it includes a halting oracle. A variant with time-bounded tests would be computable; I have not worked out what it guarantees. If abstentions are not followed by a human answer (the draft's failing obviously without querying), A never grows and the rule abstains on every input of every new kind for ever: under task decomposition that may be all of them. This is the precise sense in which D12 and D9 pull against each other.

**Observation C (never wrong costs exponentially many questions in the worst case).** Take the zero function and the 2^d point functions on {0,1}^d. A learner that is never wrong on this class must ask at every new input until it has met the exceptional one, since answering 0 unasked is wrong for one member of the class and answering 1 is wrong for all the others: up to 2^d questions, for functions of complexity at most d + O(log d). So in Observation B the factor 2^s cannot be removed in general, and the three regimes are: never wrong, with exponentially many questions; wrong on an ε fraction, with ln(1/w)/ε questions (Observation A); wrong with probability at least 2^(−sep−c) at each new kind of input, with no questions. (`repo.md`, item C3, reports the identification form of this from Paper 1: "every query strategy, adaptive or randomised, needs at least (2^d − 1)/2 queries in expectation over a uniform a to identify a".)

**Observation D (why his random-prompt scheme is the expensive way to cover the deployment distribution).** If training prompts are drawn from d and the deployment prompts from d′ with d′ ≤ B·d pointwise, any bound on the average per-call loss under d transfers to d′ with a factor B. For uniformly random prompts of ℓ bits and a deployment distribution uniform over N meaningful prompts of that length, B = 2^ℓ/N. Drawing prompts from the universal distribution m in place of the uniform one gives B ≤ 2^(K(d′)+c) for every computable d′ [from memory, not checked: the dominance property of the universal discrete semimeasure; m is a semimeasure and can be sampled only in the limit]. For the distribution induced by a scaffold run by the human, the only general bound on K(d′) is about K(P) plus the complexity of the scaffold and of the top-level tasks, so the factor this argument guarantees is of order 2^(K(P)). Asking on the induced distribution itself replaces this factor by 1/q. This is the difference between his two remarks, "giving a random string as the "question" is probably very stupid" and "one needs generalization to prompts you wouldn't have given anyway".

### 6.3 Where the notes bear on the lead's candidate theory

* **A1 (stateless human).** The notes treat learning across and within episodes as essential to research (D10, F24) and episodes as long. Statelessness is the draft's own toy assumption; the price is that T must be long, which feeds constraint 5.
* **A2 (realisable in the universal class).** He himself hesitates: "i guess this human will be given by a probabilistic turing machine? or do we need to be more quantum than that?" (`philosophy/prediction/does solomonoff generalize well out of distribution?.md`). The size of K(P) is constraint 6.
* **Theorem A (record N runs of the human system, then imitate the system).** I did not check its proof; my point is about its hypothesis. It needs the human to run the deployment scaffold itself N times. By constraint 5 that confines it to scaffolds a few calls deep, and F26 forbids the obvious way round. What the researcher wants from task decomposition is trees no human team could run ("making 15 internal calls each episode limits you to depth like log_{16} 10^{12} = 10"). So the weight falls on the shift from small human-run scaffolds to large ones, which is his easy-to-hard tension (F17) and the guard F2: depth of the tree is a simple property no training call has.
* **Theorem C (coverage).** Its consequence (ii), a training distribution that mixes all simple distributions, is the notes' random-prompt scheme in a better form; Observation D prices it.
* **Theorem D (limits).** Parts (b) and (c) are F1 and Observation C; the notes agree and add the relative form F3.
* **Theorem E (abstention with dominance).** It will face the rock test. Observation B is the count of how often such a rule abstains when each abstention is answered by the human; without answers it abstains on every new kind of input.
* **Theorem F (properties).** The two derivations under Q5 give statements with K(R) in place of K(P): a floor 2^(−K(R)−c) on the posterior weight of hypotheses that never violate R, and a hindsight bound (K(R) + c)·ln 2 on the posterior's total violation probability over the inputs already seen. Both hold for data that abide by R whether or not they are realisable. A bound with K(R) at the *next* input I could not get, and Proposition F.3 of Paper 1 shows one natural form is false.
* **"Hypotheses that model the data-collection process" (§8 of the candidate theory).** These are F8–F10. Under a regret bound they are harmless where data arrive, because they are just other samplers; where data do not arrive they are guards. The researcher's worry about them is therefore the same worry as F1, not a separate one.
* **"The meaning of upload" (§8 of the candidate theory).** D3 and F13–F15; the notes offer the steeper prior with private coins and nothing else, and call the philosophical problem "not remotely solved".

### 6.4 Outside work on asking the demonstrator during deployment

I did not read these papers in this session; the bibliographic data and the one-line descriptions are **[read in source: web search result, not the work itself]**. They are listed because the notes do not mention them and they are the closest formal work I know to §6.2.

* Michael K. Cohen, Marcus Hutter and Neel Nanda, "Fully General Online Imitation Learning", *Journal of Machine Learning Research* 23(334):1–30, 2022; arXiv:2102.08686. A Bayesian imitator that takes, for each action, the minimum probability over the top posterior models and asks the demonstrator with the remaining probability; no resets; events unlikely under the demonstrator are bounded in probability under the imitator; the query probability tends to 0 "in mean cube".
* Lihong Li, Michael L. Littman and Thomas J. Walsh, "Knows What It Knows: A Framework For Self-Aware Learning", *ICML 2008*, pp. 568–575 (journal version in *Machine Learning*, 2011). Learners that must either be right or say "I don't know"; the enumeration algorithm says it at most |H| − 1 times for a finite class H.
* Nicolò Cesa-Bianchi, Gábor Lugosi and Gilles Stoltz, "Minimizing Regret with Label Efficient Prediction", *IEEE Transactions on Information Theory* 51(6):2152–2162, 2005. Prediction with expert advice when only a limited number of labels may be asked for; Observation A is of this type.
* Stéphane Ross, Geoffrey J. Gordon and J. Andrew Bagnell, "A Reduction of Imitation Learning and Structured Prediction to No-Regret Online Learning", *AISTATS 2011*, PMLR 15:627–635; arXiv:1011.0686. A no-regret learner trained on the distribution its own policy induces yields a policy that is good under that distribution.
