# Imitation and prediction with occasional queries to the demonstrator ("asking for help"): what is proved

*A digest for the "principled human imitation" problem. Question asked of this digest: which protocols that query the demonstrator only occasionally have provable guarantees, what exactly is bounded, how the query bound depends on the prior weight (complexity) of the true demonstrator, and what survives if the demonstrator is withdrawn after a finite phase.*

**Conventions.** w(·) is a prior weight on a countable class. For the universal class ln(1/w(P)) = K(P)·ln 2, so *"polynomial in 1/w" means "exponential in description length"* and *"logarithmic in 1/w" means "linear in description length"*. A **silent error** is an output that differs from what the demonstrator would produce (or carries more probability than the demonstrator gives it) and is not flagged by a query or an abstention. T is a time horizon, N a finite class size, d a VC or Littlestone dimension.

**Four feedback models.** The literature differs most in when the true answer is seen; results do not transfer between models.

* **F1, full feedback:** the true label is revealed after every round (mistake-bound model; Sayedi et al.; Zhang–Chaudhuri; Neu–Zhivotovskiy; Goel et al.; Yu–Blanchard). For imitation this means the human labels every deployment input for ever.
* **F2, feedback only on request:** the label is seen only when the learner queries/abstains (KWIK; selective sampling; label-efficient prediction; Cohen–Hutter–Nanda; Kosoy; Plaut et al.). The human must stay available, but is used rarely.
* **F3, feedback only when predicting** (apple tasting; "censored" abstention). Not our case.
* **F4, no feedback after a finite phase** (PAC; selective classification; PQ-learning; offline behaviour cloning). This is the researcher's target ("not modulo keeping the human in the loop producing data forever").

**Bottom line (details and tags below).**

1. The closest existing result is Cohen–Hutter–Nanda (2022): act with the *minimum* probability over a posterior top set, ask the demonstrator with the remaining probability. It bounds the probability of every event in terms of its probability under the demonstrator, for stochastic demonstrators in a countable class, on arbitrary non-resetting input processes. Its query bound is E Σθ_q³ ≤ |A|·α^(−3)·(24/w + 12) with α < w, that is 2^(4K(P)), and the demonstrator is needed for ever (§2.1).
2. No paper found combines a finite query phase, a query complexity logarithmic in 1/w(P), a guarantee under shift, and stochastic demonstrators (§4).
3. This is not an accident. For "never silently wrong" on arbitrary inputs the number of queries is Θ(1/w) in the worst case (exactly n − 1 for n thresholds), and the same order of training samples is needed even with i.i.d. inputs to keep the abstention rate low. With feedback only on request and silent mistakes allowed, (silent mistakes) × (queries) ≳ horizon/32 for thresholds, matched up to ln(1/w) by random audits (§3.1, §3.4, §5).
4. A logarithmic dependence is proved only with one of: labels on every round; per-input error on a distribution the training distribution covers; a metric that makes novelty recognisable and bounds its damage (Plaut et al.); knowledge of the typical input distribution or of the unlabelled test inputs (Goel et al.; PQ-learning of Goldwasser et al., which is the one finite-phase result under arbitrary shift with abstention) (§2.4, §2.8).
5. If the demonstrator is withdrawn, the top-set construction keeps exact domination: every non-abort event of any scaffold run has at most its human-system probability, and the total-variation distance to the human system equals the abort probability, which is at most the human-driven average of the top set's disagreement. What is lost is control of the abort rate off the training distribution (§3.2).
6. Cohen–Hutter–Nanda's bounds hold conditionally with the *posterior* weight after any training phase, so the exponential is in the bits of the demonstrator that training does not pin down, not in K(P) (§3.3).
7. Relation to the candidate theory (version 2 of `candidate-theory.md`). Its Theorem A is, in substance, Foster–Block–Misra's log-loss behaviour cloning bound. Its rehearsal Protocol R is their log-loss DAgger (the Bayes mixture drives the rollouts and is trained on the inputs it induces) with one uniformly chosen call per rollout put to the human, which is the label-efficient device of Cesa-Bianchi–Lugosi–Stoltz. Its abstention rule Ab1 reproduces the Sayedi–Zadimoghaddam–Blum trade-off; its Ab2 is the Cohen–Hutter–Nanda top set (§2.5, §2.6, §2.9, §3.2).
8. The top-set rules are the "Knightian" conservatism the researcher's notes object to ("turns you into a rock"). The literature's reply is that each ask is answered, so a kind of novelty is paid for once; the count is the number of hypotheses of posterior weight ≥ α, at most 1/α, and the lower bounds say this count cannot be made polynomial in K(P) for this guarantee (§3.1, §4).

## 2. Paper by paper

### 2.1 Cohen, Hutter, Nanda: *Fully General Online Imitation Learning*

**Citation.** Michael K. Cohen, Marcus Hutter, Neel Nanda. "Fully General Online Imitation Learning." *Journal of Machine Learning Research* 23 (2022), paper 21-0618, pp. 1–30. https://www.jmlr.org/papers/v23/21-0618.html ; arXiv:2102.08686 (v2 of 4 Oct 2022, the JMLR text, read in TeX source).

**Protocol** [read].

* Time steps t = 0, 1, 2, … . Finite action set A, finite observation set O. A history element is h_t = (q_t, a_t, o_t), where q_t ∈ {0,1} records who chose the action (1 = demonstrator).
* The environment μ(o_t | h_<t, q_t, a_t) is an arbitrary stochastic, history-dependent kernel; it **never resets**. The demonstrator π^d(a | h_<t) is an arbitrary stochastic history-dependent policy.
* Model class Π: a finite or countable set of policies with prior weights w(π) > 0 summing to 1 (Example 1 there: programs computing a policy in linear time, with w ∝ 2^(−length)).
* Posterior, updated only on demonstrator-chosen actions: w(π | h_<t) ∝ w(π)·∏_{k<t: q_k=1} π(a_k | h_<k).
* **Top set.** Order the models by posterior weight, π_1, π_2, … ; Π^α(h_<t) := {π_n : w(π_n | h_<t) ≥ α·Σ_{m≤n} w(π_m | h_<t)}. It contains every model of posterior weight ≥ α and has at most 1/α members.
* **Imitator.** It takes action a itself with probability min_{π′∈Π^α(h_<t)} π′(a | h_<t). With the remaining probability θ_q(h_<t) := 1 − Σ_a min_{π′} π′(a | h_<t) it **queries**: the demonstrator picks the action, which is executed and added to the data.

**Assumptions** [read]. Realisability, π^d ∈ Π, throughout. For Theorems 4 and 5 also *fairness*: neither μ nor π^d depends on the query record q. A parameter α that must be set below w(π^d) ("α should be set a few orders of magnitude below w(π^d) … w(π^d) will have to be estimated").

**Theorems** [read]. P^i denotes the law of the whole interaction when the imitator acts, P^d the law when the demonstrator picks every action, E := {∀t: π^d ∈ Π^α(h_<t)}, and s_α := |A|·α^(−3)·(24·w(π^d)^(−1) + 12).

1. *Theorem 1 (Limited Querying).* E_{P^i}[Σ_{t≥0} θ_q(h_<t)³] ≤ s_α.
2. *Theorem 2 (Top Models Contain Truth).* P^i(E) ≥ 1 − α/w(π^d). (Proof: 1/w(π^d | h_<t) is a non-negative supermartingale.)
3. *Theorem 3 (Predictive Convergence).* For α < w(π^d): E_{P^i}[Σ_t (Σ_a |π^i_α(0,a | h_<t) − π^d(a | h_<t)|)³ | E] ≤ s_α/(1 − α/w(π^d)).
4. *Theorem 4 (KL Bound).* For fair μ, π^d and α < w(π^d), with both laws restricted to the first t action–observation pairs: KL_t(P^i(·|E) ‖ P^d(·|E)) ≤ s_α^(1/3)·t^(2/3)/(1 − α/w(π^d))² − log(1 − α/w(π^d)). So KL_t/t → 0, but KL_t is **not bounded**.
5. *Theorem 5 (Preserving Unlikeliness).* Fix t, an event B on the first t action–observation pairs, D := B ∩ E. For fair μ, π^d: P^i(D) ≤ t²·s_α / ( log(t²·s_α/(27·P^d(B))) − 3·log log(1 + t^(2/3)·s_α^(1/3)/(3·P^d(B)^(1/3))) )³. Equivalently, from the proof: P^d(B) ≥ P^i(D)·exp(−t^(2/3)·s_α^(1/3)·P^i(D)^(−1/3)).
6. *Top Model Convergence* (Theorem 6 by my count of the shared counter; a pure sequence-prediction result). For a countable class M of measures with prior w, truth μ ∈ M: E_μ Σ_t Σ_x [μ(x | x_<t) − min_{top models} ν(x | x_<t)]² ≤ α^(−3)·(24/w(μ) + 12), and E_μ Σ_t [1 − Σ_x min_{top models} ν(x | x_<t)]² ≤ |X|·α^(−3)·(24/w(μ) + 12). It rests on *SMAP convergence*: the posterior-weighted mixture ρ^stat_n of the top n models satisfies E_μ Σ_t Σ_x (ρ^stat_n − μ)² ≤ 6/w(μ) + 3.

**The mechanism behind Theorems 4 and 5** [read]. If π^d ∈ Π^α then the imitator's own-action probabilities are **dominated**: π^i_α(0, a | h) ≤ π^d(a | h). Adding the queried part gives π^i_α(a | h) ≤ (1 + θ_q(h))·π^d(a | h), so the likelihood ratio of a t-step trajectory is at most ∏_{k<t}(1 + θ_q(h_<k)).

**What is and is not bounded.**

* The bound is on the sum of **cubes** of the query probability, not on the number of queries. The proof of Theorem 4 gives E Σ_{k<t} θ_q ≤ t^(2/3)·s_α^(1/3) [read]; nothing better is proved for the expected count. For a class of *deterministic* policies θ_q ∈ {0,1}, so Theorem 1 bounds the expected total number of queries by s_α [own, immediate].
* Theorem 5 is weak: it is non-trivial only when P^d(B) is below roughly exp(−t^(2/3)·s_α^(1/3)), and the authors say "we do not prove a nice bound on the ratio" [read].

**Dependence on the prior weight** [read]. Since α < w(π^d) is required, "the bounds in this theorem and Theorem 1 are effectively quartic in w(π^d)^(−1)", that is, **2^(4K(P))** for a universal prior. The authors: "Error bounds in Bayesian prediction and MAP prediction tend to be Θ(log(w(truth)^(−1))) and Θ(w(truth)^(−1)) respectively, so theoretically, our case resembles the MAP one"; on the factor α^(−3): "The cubic dependence on α is unfortunate … we are unsure whether this dependence can be improved"; on the whole: "We do not know if a better rate is possible under additional assumptions" and "the quartic dependence on w(π^d)^(−1) in the worst case is a weakness of our approach". No lower bound is proved. Their informal remedy: "By pre-training with N consecutive demonstrator queries and calling the posterior at that point the new 'prior' for the purposes of our analysis, the 'prior' on w(π^d) could usually be made quite large, unless most demonstrator models behave extremely similarly for the first N steps." (Made precise in §3.3 below.)

**Inputs.** Arbitrary: no i.i.d., no Markov, no reset assumption. This also covers a demonstrator with memory (the researcher's footnote), which a stateless-sampler model does not.

**Must the demonstrator stay available?** Yes [read]: "The obvious downside is that the training never ends. The agent can always make queries for more data, but importantly, it does this with diminishing probability."

**If the demonstrator is withdrawn after a finite phase.** Not treated in the paper. [own] Freeze the posterior at the withdrawal time T₀ and let the imitator *abstain* (halt) with the probability with which it would have queried. On the event {π^d ∈ Π^α at time T₀} (probability ≥ 1 − α/w(π^d) by Theorem 2) the domination π^i_α(0,a | h) ≤ π^d(a | h) still holds at every later history, hence, for fair μ and π^d and every event B on the continuation: P[B and no abstention | h_<T₀] ≤ P^d[B | h_<T₀], with factor **1** (better than Theorem 5). What is lost is every control on how often it abstains: Theorem 1's proof needs the data from the queries. In a universal class the frozen imitator abstains with probability close to 1 at every history lying in a decidable set Q that contains no phase-1 history and has K(Q | π^d) + O(log|A|) ≤ log₂(posterior weight of π^d / α): for each action a the model "π^d, except always a on Q" has the same likelihood as π^d and is still in the top set, and together these models leave no action with positive minimum probability. See §3.2 for the same statement in the toy model, with an accounting of the abort probability.

**Toy example in the paper** [read]. 3^12 = 531,441 models, α = 10^(−14); 486.75 ± 52.63 queries in 2^15 steps over 20 seeds.

### 2.2 The pessimism line: Cohen–Hutter 2020 and descendants

**(i) Cohen and Hutter: *Pessimism About Unknown Unknowns Inspires Conservatism*.** Michael K. Cohen, Marcus Hutter. *Proceedings of the 33rd Conference on Learning Theory (COLT 2020)*, PMLR 125:1344–1373. https://proceedings.mlr.press/v125/cohen20a.html ; arXiv:2006.08753 (v1, read in TeX source).

*Protocol* [read]. General reinforcement learning: finite actions, observations, rewards in [0,1]; history-dependent environment μ in a countable class M with prior w; mentor policy π^m in a countable class P with prior w′; discount γ. M^β_t := the smallest set of top-posterior world-models whose posterior mass exceeds β. The agent follows π^β_t := argmax_π min_{ν∈M^β_t} V^π_ν(h_<t). It defers to the mentor when a Thompson-sampled estimate X_t = V^{π̂_t}_{ν̂_t}(h_<t) (π̂_t, ν̂_t sampled from the two posteriors) exceeds the pessimistic value Y_t = max_π min_{ν∈M^β_t} V^π_ν(h_<t) plus positive noise Z_t, or when Y_t = 0 (the "zero condition").

*Assumptions* [read]. μ ∈ M, π^m ∈ P; observed rewards ≥ ε_r > 0. For the safety theorem, M is the class FC_FG of all world-models computable within a time class F and a space class G (closed under addition, F ⊇ O(t)).

*Theorems* [read].
* *Mentor-Level Performance (corollary of "Exploiting Surpasses Exploring").* liminf_t [V^{π^β}_μ(h_<t) − V^{π^m}_μ(h_<t)] ≥ 0 with probability 1.
* *Limited Querying.* The query probability θ_t → 0 with probability 1. **No rate**: "Ideally, we would have finite bounds instead of merely asymptotic results. Unfortunately, to our knowledge, no finite performance bounds have been discovered for agents in general environments, except for on-policy prediction error."
* *Probably Respecting Precedent.* Let E be a set of (history, action) pairs decidable in C_{(F/t)G}, and μ_E the world-model equal to μ until E happens and giving reward 0 for ever afterwards; c_E := w(μ_E)/w(μ). Then P[for all t: if E has not happened by time t−1, then E does not happen at t, or the mentor chose a_t] ≥ 1 − (1−β)/(c_E·w(μ)).
* *Don't Do Anything I Wouldn't Do (corollary).* If "π^m(a_t | h_<t) = 0" is decidable in that class, then as β → 1 the probability that the agent never takes an action the mentor would never take tends to 1.
* *Diverging from the Mentor.* With a mentor flipping a fair coin and reward 1 for heads, 1/2 for tails, the agent's long-run frequency of heads exceeds 1/2 with probability 1, for every β: the agent is statistically distinguishable from the mentor. (It is a reward maximiser, not an imitator.)

*Dependence on the prior.* The pessimism level must satisfy 1 − β < δ·w(μ_E): polynomial in 1/w, appearing in the size of the top set, not in a query count (there is none). The authors add: "the factor of w(μ) is less of a bother than it appears; if the agent's lifetime were preceded by N mentor-led actions, and the posterior after that became the new prior, the 'prior' on μ could practically be made quite large" [read].

*If the mentor is withdrawn.* Not treated. [own] The proof of the precedent theorem shows that when μ_E is in the top set and E is unprecedented, either the pessimistic action avoids E or the zero condition holds and the agent defers. Without a mentor the zero condition has no safe resolution, so the theorem needs either a mentor for ever or a designated halt action.

*Relevance to imitation.* (a) The credible-mass top set M^β is the Bayesian counterpart of Cohen–Hutter–Nanda's α-top set (see §3.5). (b) The safety statement is about *unprecedented* events, the same object as the project's separating properties.

**(ii) Cohen, Hutter, Bengio, Russell: *RL, but don't do anything I wouldn't do*.** *Proceedings of the 41st Conference on Uncertainty in Artificial Intelligence (UAI 2025)*, PMLR 286:821–836 [venue from a search result]. arXiv:2410.06213 (v1, read in TeX source). Two results bear directly on query-free Bayesian imitation.

* *Theorem 1 (Little constraint in novel situations)* [read]. Let ξ be the Solomonoff-type Bayes mixture conditioned on a history whose first k actions came from a trusted policy (a "Bayesian imitation"). There is a constant d such that for every utility U_m and every event E: if E is unprecedented and occurs at time t, then for every v < V*_{ξ,U_m}(x_<2t) there is a policy π with V^π_{ξ,U_m}(x_<2t) > v and KL_{x_<2t, m}(π ‖ ξ) < [d + K(U_m) + K(E) + K(v·ξ(x_<2t))]/log 2. The bound does not depend on k: "regardless of how safe the demonstrator's policy is". *Proposition 2*: the complexity of the simplest unprecedented event yet to occur grows more slowly than every computable function tending to infinity. This is a published sibling of the project's floor and grue results (weight 2^(−K(Q,b)−c) for behaviour b on an unseen simple set Q, however much data).
* *Theorem (Tight KL constraint with approximate imitator)* [read]. With ν_α(x | x_<t) := min over the α-top set of ν′(x | x_<t) (the "pessimistic Bayesian imitator" of Cohen–Hutter–Nanda), on the event that the truth μ is always in the top set: {π : KL(π ‖ ν_α) ≤ b} ⊆ {π : KL(π ‖ μ) ≤ b}, because ν_α ≤ μ pointwise. "The downside is that there may be no policy with small KL divergence to the semi-distribution ν_α … we should allow an optimizer … to refuse to pick an action if need be … Ideally, if human demonstrators are on hand, the optimizer should ask for help". The authors state that no tractable approximation is known.

**(iii) Bengio, Cohen, Malkin, MacDermott, Fornasiere, Greiner, Kaddar: *Can a Bayesian Oracle Prevent Harm from an Agent?*** arXiv:2408.05284 (v3, read in TeX source; "Accepted at UAI 2025" per the arXiv listing). Non-i.i.d. part [read]: for a countable family of theories with prior P and true index i*, (a) with probability ≥ 1−δ, inf_t P(i* | Z_1:t) ≥ δ·P(i*) (Ville); (b) if α < δ·P(i*), then with probability ≥ 1−δ, for all t and all contexts x_t: P(H_t = 1 | Z_1:t, τ*, x_t) ≤ max over the α-top set of P(H_t = 1 | Z_1:t, τ_i, x_t). I.i.d. part: the same with the posterior-weighted maximiser, for all large t, **without a rate**. No query mechanism; the guardrail rejects actions. Same 1/prior dependence in α.

**(iv) Ebtekar and Cohen: *Golden Handcuffs make safer AI agents*.** arXiv:2604.13609 (v1, April 2026, "preliminary version", read in TeX source). A Bayes-optimal reinforcement learner whose subjective reward range is extended to include −L; it yields to a mentor when V*_ξ(h_<t) ≤ −1 and also follows mentor rollouts at random times with frequency t^(−1/3) [read].
* *Theorem (trigger count)* [read]: if the true rewards are never negative, E[#{t : V*_ξ(h_<t) ≤ −1}] ≤ (L/(1−γ))·ln(1/w_μ). **Logarithmic in the prior weight of the truth**, linear in L. It rests on a lemma of the form E Σ_t P_ξ(an event that never happens under the truth occurs s steps ahead | h_≤t) ≤ s·ln(1/w_μ), which is the mixture regret bound applied to a never-occurring event.
* *Corollary (safety)* [read]: if L ≥ 2^(K(E)+C), the optimising policy never triggers a decidable event E before a mentor does.
* Reading: the exponential has moved from the complexity of the *truth* to the complexity of the *event to be avoided* (L ≥ 2^(K(E)+C)), and the number of safety deferrals is L·ln(1/w_μ)/(1−γ). This is the only result in this line where the truth's complexity enters logarithmically; the guarantee is for a named class of events (complexity ≤ log L − C), not for all events.

**(v)** Cohen, Catt, Hutter, "Curiosity Killed or Incapacitated the Cat and the Asymptotically Optimal Agent", *IEEE Journal on Selected Areas in Information Theory* 2:665–677, 2021 (arXiv:2006.03357): a "mentee" that explores by deferring to a mentor reaches mentor-level reward with query probability → 0, asymptotically, without rates [mem; bibliographic data read in Plaut et al.'s bibliography].

### 2.3 Kosoy: *Delegative Reinforcement Learning*

**Citation.** Vanessa Kosoy. "Delegative Reinforcement Learning: learning to avoid traps with a little help." SafeML Workshop at ICLR 2019. arXiv:1907.08461 (v1, read in TeX source).

**Protocol** [read]. A finite MDP (finite states S, actions A, reward a function of the state, geometric discount γ) that never resets and may contain traps. Each round the agent takes an action a ∈ A or the delegation action ⊥; on ⊥ an *advisor* takes an action b ∈ A, which has its usual effect and is observed. Hypotheses: N pairs (T^k, υ^k) of a transition kernel and an advisor policy, **uniform prior**. Algorithm: posterior sampling in episodes of length T; follow the sampled hypothesis's optimal policy π^{*J}; **delegate whenever some hypothesis with non-discarded posterior says the advisor would never take the intended action** (υ^k(a | s) = 0); hypotheses whose posterior falls below η are discarded.

**Assumptions** [read]. The true (MDP, advisor) is one of the N hypotheses. In each hypothesis the advisor is *ε-sane*: its support contains only actions that do not lose long-run value (no trap is entered), and it takes a Blackwell-optimal action with probability > ε. γ close to 1. The algorithm is not anytime (parameters depend on γ).

**Theorem 1** [read]. There is a constant C such that for all ε, η ∈ (0,1) and T ≥ 1 there is a policy π† with
* (1/N)·Σ_k Reg_k(γ) ≤ C·( η·N + t̄/T + √((1−γ)·T·ln N/η) + ((1−γ)·T·ln N/η²)·(1/ε + |A|) ), where regret is normalised to [0,1] and t̄ is an average bound on the derivative of the value functions in γ;
* for all K: (1/N)·Σ_k Pr_k[#delegations > K] ≤ C·( η·N + (ln N/(K·η))·(1/ε + |A|) ).

**Corollary 1** [read]. For γ ≥ 1 − (t̄+1)³·min(ε, 1/|A|)/(N²·ln N) and Ξ := (N⁶·ln N·(1/ε + |A|)·(t̄+1))^(1/4), for every hypothesis k: Reg_k(γ) ≤ C·Ξ·(1−γ)^(1/4), and Pr_k[#delegations > K] ≤ C·( Ξ·(1−γ)^(1/4) + (1/K)·(N⁶·(ln N)³·(1/ε + |A|)³/(1−γ))^(1/4) ).

**Why the delegation count is bounded** [read, proof outline]. A delegation happens only when a hypothesis of posterior ≥ η says the advisor takes the action with probability ≥ ε while another of posterior ≥ η says it never does; so the advisor's answer "provides an amount of information about the environment that can be bounded below in terms of η and ε", and the total information is at most ln N. Result: expected number of delegations O(ln N/(η·ε)).

**Dependence on the prior.** The total information is ln N, but the guaranteed gain per delegation is only of order η·ε and η must be below 1/N (the error term is η·N). Making the right-hand side of the second bound of Theorem 1 at most δ takes η of order δ/N and then K of order **N·ln N·(1/ε + |A|)/δ²** [own arithmetic from the theorem]: linear, up to a logarithm, in 1/(prior weight). The paper notes that a worst-case (non-Bayesian) regret bound costs "another factor of N".

**Inputs.** Arbitrary trajectories of one non-resetting finite MDP.

**Must the advisor stay?** The number of delegations is finite with high probability and of order (1−γ)^(−1/4), small compared with the effective horizon 1/(1−γ). But they occur at times chosen by the agent, anywhere in the run. **Withdrawal at a fixed time** is not treated; the algorithm has no fallback when "no action is certainly safe" [read: the else-branch of the algorithm delegates].

**Relevance.** Not an imitation result: the advisor is used for safety, and the agent optimises reward. What transfers is the delegation rule (act only where every plausible hypothesis gives the advisor's action positive probability) and the information-gain accounting, which makes the origin of the 1/prior factor explicit.

### 2.4 Plaut, Zhu, Russell and the JMLR follow-up

**Citations.**
* Benjamin Plaut, Hanlin Zhu, Stuart Russell. "Avoiding Catastrophe in Online Learning by Asking for Help." *Proceedings of the 42nd International Conference on Machine Learning (ICML 2025)*, PMLR 267:49537–49568. https://proceedings.mlr.press/v267/plaut25a.html ; arXiv:2402.08062 (v6, read in TeX source).
* Benjamin Plaut, Juan Liévano-Karim, Hanlin Zhu, Stuart Russell. "Safe Learning Under Irreversible Dynamics via Asking for Help." arXiv:2502.14043 (v4 of 11 Sep 2026, "Accepted to JMLR" per the arXiv listing; read in TeX source). Earlier arXiv title: "Asking for Help Enables Safety Guarantees Without Sacrificing Effectiveness".

**Protocol (ICML paper)** [read]. Horizon T. Each round the agent sees x_t ∈ X ⊆ R^n and either takes an action y_t from a finite set Y or queries; on a query the mentor's action π^m(x_t) is shown and taken. The mentor policy π^m: X → Y is **deterministic** and stationary. Payoff μ_t(x_t, y_t) ∈ [0,1] is "the chance of avoiding catastrophe on that time step"; **payoffs are never observed**. Multiplicative regret R^× := log ∏_t μ_t(x_t, π^m(x_t)) − log ∏_t μ_t(x_t, y_t); additive regret R^+ likewise with sums. Goal: *total* regret → 0 ("subconstant") with o(T) queries.

**Assumptions** [read]. (1) *Local generalisation*: for some L and all x, x′, t: |μ_t(x, π^m(x)) − μ_t(x, π^m(x′))| ≤ L·‖x − x′‖. The agent needs a nearest-neighbour distance oracle for the metric, not L. (2) π^m ∈ Π, where either Π has VC dimension d and the inputs are σ-smooth (each x_t drawn from a distribution of density ≤ 1/σ relative to uniform, chosen adaptively), or Π has Littlestone dimension d and the inputs are fully adversarial. (3) μ_t(x, π^m(x)) ≥ μ₀ > 0.

**Algorithm** [read]. Hedge over a finite cover of Π, updated only on query rounds, with an independent query probability p = 1/√(εT) each round ("label-efficient" Hedge, analysed by Russo et al. 2024); **plus** "ask for help if out-of-distribution": query whenever the nearest previously queried input *with the same proposed action* is farther than ε^(1/n).

**Theorems** [read].
* *Positive result (parametrised).* For Y = {0,1}, T ≥ 1, ε ∈ [1/T, (μ₀/2L)^n]: E[R^×] ∈ O((dL/(σμ₀))·T·ε^(1+1/n)·log(T + 1/ε)); E[R^+] ∈ O((dL/σ)·T·ε^(1+1/n)·log(T + 1/ε)); E|Q_T| ∈ O(√(T/ε) + (d/σ)·T·ε·log(T + 1/ε) + E[diam(x)^n]/ε).
* *Positive result (tuned).* With ε = T^(−2n/(2n+1)): E[R^×] ∈ O((dL/(σμ₀))·T^(−1/(2n+1))·log T), E[R^+] ∈ O((dL/σ)·T^(−1/(2n+1))·log T), E|Q_T| ∈ O(T^((4n+1)/(4n+2))·((d/σ)·log T + E[diam(x)^n])). Many actions: an extra factor |Y|. A separate result for X ⊆ R and mentors with at most K segments, fully adversarial inputs: R^× ≤ 4LKT/(g(T)²·μ₀) with |Q_T| ≤ (diam(x) + 4)·g(T).
* *Negative result (no dimension assumption).* "Any algorithm with sublinear queries has unbounded worst-case regret": sup_{μ,π^m} E[R^×], sup E[R^+] ∈ Ω(L·√(T/(sup E|Q_T| + 1))), even for i.i.d. uniform inputs on [0,1], two actions, and a time-independent payoff. *Corollary*: even if the mentor never causes catastrophe, any algorithm with sublinear queries has worst-case probability of avoiding catastrophe tending to 0 as T → ∞. (The displayed formula has a sup over (μ, π^m) where the text says "in the worst case"; I read it as an infimum.)
* *Without local generalisation (appendix).* X = [0,1], i.i.d. uniform inputs, Π = indicator functions of intervals (VC dimension 2): any algorithm with sublinear queries has lim_{T→∞} sup E[R^×] = ∞. The construction: payoff 1[y = π^m(x)] and a hidden interval of width 1/f(T) with |Q_T| ≪ f(T) ≪ T.

**JMLR follow-up** [read]. Three reductions.
* *First reduction (full feedback → queries on request).* If a full-feedback algorithm has regret ≤ R(T, σ) with R concave in T, then running it on the queried rounds only and querying each round independently with probability k/T gives expected k queries and regret ≤ (T/k)·R(k, σ). With realisability: R = d for Littlestone dimension d, and R = O(d·log T + 1/σ) for VC dimension d with σ-smooth inputs, hence mistakes O((T/k)·(d·log k + 1/σ)). The paper says the reduction "is based on standard ideas from" Cesa-Bianchi, Lugosi and Stoltz (2005), "who proved a similar result but only for a single algorithm".
* *Second reduction (add out-of-distribution queries).* If Γ makes ≤ k queries and ≤ R mistakes, the wrapped algorithm has R^+ ≤ L·ε·R, R^× ≤ L·ε·R/μ₀, and O(k + R + |A|·E[diam^n]/ε^n) queries. Tuned: R^+ ∈ O(L·T^(−1/(2n+2))·(d·log T + 1/σ)), queries O(T^((2n+1)/(2n+2))·(d + 1/σ + E[diam^n])). (In the fully adversarial case with Littlestone dimension d the term 1/σ is absent.)
* *Third reduction (to MDPs).* The same algorithm has regret O(L·T^((2n+1)/(2n+2))·(d·log T + 1/σ)) against the mentor in multichain MDPs with infinite state spaces and no resets, with the same query bound.

**Dependence on complexity.** Logarithmic: d is a VC or Littlestone dimension (≤ log₂ N for a finite class). The price is (i) the metric assumption, which bounds the cost of a silent error by L times the distance to a queried input with the same action, and (ii) a query count that is sublinear but **unbounded** in T, with exponent tending to 1 as the dimension n of the input space grows.

**Must the mentor stay?** Yes; the query count grows with T. No statement for a fixed query budget and T → ∞. By the negative result, with Q queries the worst-case regret is Ω(L·√(T/(Q+1))) for unrestricted mentor classes; for classes of finite dimension the paper gives no fixed-budget bound.

**Relevance.** Deterministic mentors only; the guarantee is about a payoff, not about matching a distribution. The structural lesson: a logarithmic dependence is obtained once there is a *recognisable notion of novelty* (distance to queried inputs) that also bounds the damage of a wrong answer.

*Also:* Sarah Liaw, Benjamin Plaut, "Learning When Not to Learn: Risk-Sensitive Abstention in Bandits with Unbounded Rewards", arXiv:2510.14884 ("accepted to AISTATS 2026" per the arXiv listing): no mentor, an abstain action with reward 0, Lipschitz rewards, i.i.d. inputs; sublinear regret for a caution-based rule, and two impossibility theorems (an incautious first commit can cost infinite expected regret; if all inputs are far out of distribution no algorithm has sublinear regret) [read, theorem statements only].

### 2.5 KWIK and the mistakes–abstentions trade-off (deterministic, adversarial inputs)

**KWIK.** Lihong Li, Michael L. Littman, Thomas J. Walsh. "Knows What It Knows: A Framework for Self-Aware Learning." ICML 2008, pp. 568–575; journal version with Alexander L. Strehl, *Machine Learning* 82:399–443, 2011 [bibliographic data confirmed by a search result and by other papers' bibliographies; the papers themselves were not reachable].
* *Protocol* [mem]. A target h* in a known class H. Each round the adversary picks x_t; the learner outputs a prediction or ⊥; a prediction must be ε-accurate; **only after ⊥** the learner observes (a possibly noisy sample of) h*(x_t). Requirement: with probability ≥ 1−δ all predictions are accurate and the number of ⊥ is at most B(ε, δ).
* *Results.* For a finite deterministic class, the enumeration algorithm (answer when the version space agrees, otherwise ⊥) makes no mistake and at most |H|−1 abstentions [read, as described in Zhang–Chaudhuri]. A single coin's bias is KWIK-learned with O(ε^(−2)·log(1/δ)) ⊥'s [mem].
* *Stochastic hypotheses.* Carlos Diuk, Lihong Li, Bethany R. Leffler, "The Adaptive k-Meteorologists Problem and Its Application to Structure Learning and Feature Selection in Reinforcement Learning", ICML 2009, pp. 249–256: given k probabilistic predictors one of which is correct, KWIK bound O((k/ε²)·ln(k/δ)) [mem], with "a matching lower bound" [snippet of the abstract]. So for stochastic hypotheses too, the zero-silent-error query count is **linear in the number of hypotheses**.

**With k mistakes allowed.** Amin Sayedi, Morteza Zadimoghaddam, Avrim Blum. "Trading off Mistakes and Don't-Know Predictions." *Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems 23* (NIPS 2010). Feedback F1: the true label is revealed after every round. For a finite class, at most k mistakes with at most (k+1)·|H|^(1/(k+1)) abstentions [read, as described in Zhang–Chaudhuri].

**Exact characterisation.** Chicheng Zhang, Kamalika Chaudhuri. "The Extended Littlestone's Dimension for Learning with Mistakes and Abstentions." COLT 2016, PMLR 49:1584–1616. arXiv:1604.06162 (v3, read in TeX source). Model [read]: each round the adversary shows x_t, the learner outputs −1, +1 or ⊥, "the adversary then reveals an outcome y_t" (F1). A (k, m) bound: at most k mistakes and at most m non-trivial rounds (mistakes plus abstentions).
* *Theorem (Optimality of SOA.DK)* [read]. If ELdim(H, k) = m then the algorithm SOA.DK achieves a (k, m) bound on every H-realisable sequence, and some adversary prevents every deterministic algorithm from achieving (k, m−1).
* *Theorem* [read]. ELdim(H, k) ≤ sup{t : C(t, ≤k+1) ≤ S(H, t)}, with S the tree shattering coefficient (≤ |H|; ≤ C(t, ≤d) for Littlestone dimension d). Hence ELdim(H, k) ≤ (k+1)·|H|^(1/(k+1)).
* *Theorem (thresholds)* [read]. For n distinct thresholds, ELdim(H, k) = max{t : C(t, ≤k+1) ≤ n}. For k = 0 this is **n − 1**: zero mistakes needs |H|−1 abstentions, exactly.
* *Theorem (unions of ≤ l singletons on an infinite domain)* [read]. ELdim = ∞ for k ≤ l−1 and = l for k ≥ l. So for singletons (Littlestone dimension 1), zero mistakes needs **unboundedly many** abstentions, while one mistake needs one non-trivial round.
* *Theorem (ELdim(H, d) = d for Littlestone dimension d)* [read]; non-realisable case under an "l-bias" assumption: fewer than l (respectively l + d) mistakes forces unboundedly many abstentions.

**Abstention at a price.** Gergely Neu, Nikita Zhivotovskiy. "Fast Rates for Online Prediction with Abstention." COLT 2020, PMLR 125:3030–3048. arXiv:2001.10623 (v2, read in TeX source). Prediction with N experts, F1, abstention costs c < 1/2 per use: expected regret ≤ log N/(2(1−2c)) ∧ √(T·log N/2), with a matching lower bound Ω(log N/(1−2c) ∧ √(T·log N)) [read]. Logarithmic in N and independent of T, agnostic, but the labels are shown every round. (Corinna Cortes, Giulia DeSalvo, Claudio Gentile, Mehryar Mohri, Scott Yang, "Online Learning with Abstention", ICML 2018, PMLR 80:1059–1067, arXiv:1703.03478: abstention as a feedback-graph bandit problem; abstract read only.)

**Reading for imitation.** The (k+1)·|H|^(1/(k+1)) trade-off needs F1: the learner must learn that it erred. With feedback on request only, a silent mistake teaches nothing, and the relevant trade-off is the one of §3.4. The candidate's Ab1 (answer when the mixture gives one label probability ≥ 1 − η) is the mixture form of the same trade-off, counted on the questions that are put to the human: with N = 2^K equally weighted hypotheses, k = K/log₂(1/η) wrong answers against K·ln 2/η = ln N·N^(1/k) abstentions, to be compared with (k+1)·N^(1/(k+1)) [own arithmetic]; Zhang–Chaudhuri's threshold theorem shows that this shape cannot be improved in general.

### 2.6 Selective sampling and label-efficient prediction (feedback on request)

* Nicolò Cesa-Bianchi, Gábor Lugosi, Gilles Stoltz. "Minimizing Regret with Label Efficient Prediction." *IEEE Transactions on Information Theory* 51(6):2152–2162, 2005 (COLT 2004). With N experts, T rounds and at most m label queries, a forecaster that queries at random and updates exponential weights on the queried rounds has expected regret of order T·√(ln N/m), and no forecaster does better up to constants [mem; the form T·√(log N/m) for upper and lower bounds also appears in a snippet of Audibert and Bubeck's minimax table]. Agnostic and adversarial, so regret per round does not vanish unless m grows.
* The realisable version is the first reduction of Plaut et al. (§2.4) [read]: k random queries give ≤ (T/k)·d mistakes for Littlestone dimension d; for a finite class with the halving algorithm, **mistakes × queries ≤ T·log₂N**. For a stochastic demonstrator and the Bayes mixture the same device gives Σ_t E KL(P ‖ mixture) ≤ ln(1/w)/q at audit rate q on any input process (Observation A in `notes.md`, X4 in `repo.md`). The candidate's Protocol R uses the device inside a training phase: one uniformly chosen call per rehearsal run is put to the human, which is what turns the regret bound on asked inputs into its Theorem R1 on all the calls of the imitator's own runs.
* Matteo Russo, Andrea Celli, Riccardo Colini Baldeschi, Federico Fusco, Daniel Haimovich, Dima Karamshuk, Stefano Leonardi, Niek Tax. "Online Learning with Sublinear Best-Action Queries." arXiv:2407.16355 (abstract read): with k best-action queries, regret Θ(min{√T, T/k}) under full feedback and Θ(min{T/√k, T²/k²}) when feedback comes only with the queries. As restated by Plaut et al. [read]: Hedge with query probability p has expected regret ≤ log|Π|/p².
* Nicolò Cesa-Bianchi, Claudio Gentile, Luca Zaniboni. "Worst-Case Analysis of Selective Sampling for Linear Classification." *JMLR* 7:1205–1230, 2006. A Perceptron that queries the label with probability b/(b + |margin|) has an expected mistake bound of the same form as the Perceptron's on every individual sequence, counting mistakes on unqueried rounds too [mem; snippets confirm "mistake bounds that hold for individual sequences", the query probability b/(b + |r_t|), and, for the 2004 conference version, a bound with the term (2b+1)²/(8b)·‖u‖²/γ²]. The expected number of queries is Σ_t E[b/(b + |p̂_t|)] [snippet of a survey]; it is not bounded uniformly over sequences [mem].
* Ofer Dekel, Claudio Gentile, Karthik Sridharan. "Selective Sampling and Active Learning from Single and Multiple Teachers." *JMLR* 13:2655–2697, 2012: a linear noise model with adversarially chosen instances; regret and number of queries bounded in terms of the dimension, log T and the number of small-margin rounds [mem, not checked; I do not vouch for the exact form].
* **Ayush Sekhari, Karthik Sridharan, Wen Sun, Runzhe Wu. "Selective Sampling and Imitation Learning via Online Regression."** NeurIPS 2023 [venue: mem]. arXiv:2307.04998 (v1, read in TeX source). A noisy expert whose label distribution is φ(f*(x)) with f* in a class F; the learner competes with the expert's most likely action.
  * *Theorem (adversarial contexts)* [read]: with probability ≥ 1−δ, Reg_T = Õ(inf_ε {ε·T_ε + Reg(F;T)/ε}) and number of queries N_T = Õ(inf_ε {T_ε + Reg(F;T)·E(F, ε/4γ; f*)/ε²}), where Reg(F;T) is the regret of an online regression oracle (O(log|F|) for finite F), E the eluder dimension, T_ε the number of rounds with margin below ε. For finite F the eluder dimension is at most |F| − 1 [read].
  * *Theorem (i.i.d. contexts)* [read]: the eluder dimension is replaced by a disagreement coefficient.
  * *Lower bound* [read]: any algorithm with regret ≤ 64·ζ·T/s on all instances of margin ζ/2 makes Ω(s/(40·ζ²)) queries on some instance, s the star number. *Corollary*: there is a class with |F| = √T such that any algorithm making fewer than √T queries has regret ≥ √T on some instance.
  * *Imitation learning* [read, statement of contributions]: episodic MDP of horizon H, T episodes, queries to a noisy expert at visited states; the same form of bounds, with T_ε counting small-margin steps of the *expert's* trajectory; and an exponential-in-H separation between offline demonstrations and interactive queries when the expert's feedback is noisy.
  * Dependence: regret is logarithmic in |F|; the query count is logarithmic in |F| **times the eluder dimension**, which is as large as |F|−1 for unstructured classes and small (≈ dimension) for linear ones.

### 2.7 Apple tasting (feedback only on one kind of prediction)

David P. Helmbold, Nick Littlestone, Philip M. Long. "Apple Tasting." *Information and Computation* 161(2):85–139, 2000 [bibliographic data: mem]. The label is seen only when the learner predicts 1. As described in Raman et al. [read]: a full-feedback learner with at most M₊ false positives and M₋ false negatives converts into an apple-tasting learner with expected mistakes ≤ M₊ + 2·√(T·M₋); for any class, expected mistakes O(min{T, √(T·ln|H|/ln(1 + T/ln|H|)), |H|}), matched for "amply splittable" classes.

Vinod Raman, Unique Subedi, Ananth Raman, Ambuj Tewari. "Apple Tasting: Combinatorial Dimensions and Minimax Rates." COLT 2024, PMLR 247:4358–4380. arXiv:2310.19064 (v3, read in TeX source). Realisable case [read]: (1/8)·min{max{√((W(H)−1)·T), L(H)}, T} ≤ minimax expected mistakes ≤ AL_W(H) + 2·√((W(H)−1)·T), W the effective width, L the Littlestone dimension; a trichotomy Θ(1), Θ(√T), Θ(T). For singletons over the natural numbers, L = 1 and yet the expected mistakes scale with T. Agnostic case: between √(L·T/8) and 3·√(L·T·ln T).

Relevance: one-sided feedback already destroys constant mistake bounds for classes of Littlestone dimension 1; the Littlestone dimension stops being the right complexity once feedback is partial.

### 2.8 Abstention when test inputs are shifted or adversarial

**PQ-learning (no feedback after training).** Shafi Goldwasser, Adam Tauman Kalai, Yael Tauman Kalai, Omar Montasser. "Beyond Perturbations: Learning Guarantees with Arbitrary Adversarial Test Examples." *Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems* 33:15859–15870, 2020. arXiv:2007.05145 (v3, read in TeX source).
* *Protocol* [read]. n labelled examples from a distribution P, labels from f in a class C of VC dimension d; n **unlabelled test inputs** from an arbitrary Q (or, transductively, an arbitrary test set chosen by a white-box adversary after seeing the training data and the learned classifier). Output: a selective classifier h|_S that may reject.
* *Theorem (PQ guarantees)* [read]. For all n, δ, f ∈ C and distributions P, Q: with probability ≥ 1−δ, err_Q ≤ 2ε* and rej_P ≤ ε*, where ε* = √(8d·ln(2n)/n) + 8·ln(16/δ)/n. More generally any trade-off err_Q ≤ O(ε), rej_P ≤ Õ(d/(n·ε)).
* *Theorem (transductive)* [read]. With probability ≥ 1−δ over training x and genuine test points z from P: for **every** modified test set x̃, err_x̃ ≤ ε* and the rejection rate on the genuine points ≤ ε*, ε* = √((2d/n)·log 2n) + (1/n)·log(1/δ).
* *Lower bounds* [read]. For every d there are a class of VC dimension d and distributions P, Q with E[rej_P + err_Q] ≥ K·√(d/n) for every learner; transductively K·√(d/min{m, n}).
* What it is: a finite labelled sample, **arbitrary** test distribution, errors rare on the test distribution, rejections rare on the training distribution, complexity entering as a VC dimension. What it is not: it handles deterministic binary labels only; it needs the unlabelled test inputs (in batch); the rejection rate on Q is not bounded (it can be 1 if Q is far from P), which is the intended "obvious failure".
* Adam Kalai, Varun Kanade, "Efficient Learning with Arbitrary Covariate Shift", arXiv:2102.07802 (ALT 2021 [venue: mem]; read in TeX source): PQ-learnability is equivalent to *reliable* learnability (learning with one-sided error, Kalai–Kanade–Mansour 2012).
* Ancestors [mem]: Ronald L. Rivest, Robert Sloan, "Learning Complicated Concepts Reliably and Usefully", AAAI 1988 (never err, say "I don't know" rarely); Ran El-Yaniv, Yair Wiener, "On the Foundations of Noise-free Selective Classification", *JMLR* 11:1605–1641, 2010 (the consistent selective strategy abstains on the disagreement region of the version space: zero error with certainty in the realisable i.i.d. case, coverage bounds for particular classes and distributions, and classes for which perfect selective classification has vanishing coverage).

**Sequential prediction with injected inputs (full feedback).**
* Surbhi Goel, Steve Hanneke, Shay Moran, Abhishek Shetty. "Adversarial Resilience in Sequential Prediction via Abstention." *Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems* 36:8027–8047, 2023. arXiv:2306.13119 (v2, read in TeX source). *Protocol* [read]: a distribution D and f* in a class F are fixed; each round an adversary either injects an arbitrary input or lets nature draw x_t ~ D; the learner outputs 0, 1 or ⊥ and then **sees the clean label f*(x̂_t)**. Error = number of wrong predictions on any round + number of abstentions on non-injected rounds; abstaining on injected rounds is free. *Theorem* [read]: if the learner **knows the marginal D**, then for VC dimension d, E[misclassifications] ≤ d²·log T and E[abstentions on clean rounds] ≤ 6d. *Theorem* [read]: for VC dimension 1 without knowledge of D, ≤ 2·√(T·log T) and ≤ √(T·log T).
* Jialin Yu, Moïse Blanchard. "Distribution-Free Sequential Prediction with Abstentions." arXiv:2602.17918 (v2, February 2026, read in TeX source). Unknown D. *Theorem* [read]: for VC dimension d and every α ∈ [0, 1/3], against oblivious adversaries, MisErr ≲ T^(3α) and E[AbsErr] ≲ d²·log^(5/3)(T)·T^(1−α). ***Lower bound*** [read]: there is a class of VC dimension 1 such that for every T, every learner and every α ∈ [0,1], some oblivious adversary forces E[MisErr] ≥ T^α/32 or E[AbsErr] ≥ T^(1−α)/2. So without knowledge of the clean distribution, **(misclassifications) × (abstentions on clean rounds) ≳ T/64**, even with labels shown every round.
* Ezra Edelman, Surbhi Goel. "Reliable Abstention under Adversarial Injections: Tight Lower Bounds and New Upper Bounds." arXiv:2602.20111 (v1, February 2026, abstract read): a matching Ω(√T) lower bound on the combined error for VC dimension 1 without distribution access, "establishing a sharp separation between the two information regimes".

Reading: with abstention, a logarithmic dependence under arbitrary injected inputs is possible **if the learner knows what typical inputs look like** (the clean marginal, or unlabelled test inputs in PQ-learning); without that knowledge a polynomial-in-T price is forced even for the simplest classes.

### 2.9 Imitation learning without queries on request (boundary of this digest)

* Stéphane Ross, J. Andrew Bagnell, "Efficient Reductions for Imitation Learning", AISTATS 2010; Stéphane Ross, Geoffrey J. Gordon, J.   * *Proposition (log-loss behaviour cloning)* [read]: for any, possibly stochastic, expert π* in a finite class Π, maximum likelihood over n expert trajectories gives, with probability ≥ 1−δ, D²_H(P^{π̂}, P^{π*}) ≤ 2·log(|Π|/δ)/n, where P^π is the law of a whole trajectory. Horizon-free; no interaction.
  * *Theorems* [read]: for a deterministic expert J(π*) − J(π̂) ≤ 4R·D²_H (R the range of total reward), so regret ≤ 8R·log(2|Π|/δ)/n; for stochastic experts regret ≲ √(σ²·log(|Π|/δ)/n) + R·log n·log(|Π|/δ)/n; lower bounds c·H/n (deterministic) and c·√(σ²/n) with constant probability (stochastic), **for offline and online imitation alike**, with |Π| = 2.

The policy π̂^i is the exponential-weights (Bayes) mixture under log loss, layer by layer, and the output is the uniform mixture of π̂^1, …, π̂^n. *Proposition (regret for log-loss DAgger)*: for π* ∈ Π finite, with probability ≥ 1−δ, the online estimation error (1/n)·Σ_i Σ_h E^{π̂^i}[D²_H(π̂^i_h(x_h), π*_h(x_h))] is at most (2/n)·Σ_h log(|Π_h|·H/δ): the mixture is close to the expert **on the states its own rollouts visit**. *Proposition (regret for DAgger)*: J(π*) − J(π̂) ≲ √(σ̄²·Est) + μ̃·Est in general and ≲ μ·Est for a deterministic expert (μ a recoverability parameter).

With a prior on a countable class the same argument gives ln(1/w) in place of log|Π| for the Bayes mixture; for a single selected hypothesis one needs a penalised likelihood, as in the candidate's Theorem B. The candidate's **Protocol R and Theorem R1 are log-loss DAgger** with a stateless class (one mixture for all calls), a countable class with a prior, and one uniformly chosen call per rollout annotated in place of all of them. (Per human answer the rate is the same: with a stationary class, n answers give a per-run error of order L·ln(1/w)/n either way; the subsampling replaces human answers along whole rollouts by more rollouts, which cost only compute.) What Foster et al. add to the candidate: a passage from the per-call Hellinger error on the imitator's own states to a bound on a payoff that is not the crude sum over calls (variance and recoverability terms), and lower bounds showing that for general classes interaction does not improve on recorded expert runs. What the candidate adds: the single-call audit, which matters when a run of the human system is unaffordable.

### 2.10 What withdrawal of the demonstrator means in the models of §2.5–§2.9 [own, immediate]

* **Zero-silent-error rules under feedback on request** (KWIK, top sets). The rule "answer only where all surviving hypotheses agree" stays sound for ever with the hypotheses that survive at withdrawal; every later ⊥ is an abstention that nobody answers. Nothing bounds how many there are: the disagreement region of the surviving set is abstained on for ever.
* **Rules that tolerate mistakes and learn from feedback** (the mistakes–abstentions trade-offs under full feedback, label-efficient audits, selective sampling). After withdrawal there is no further learning. The predictor frozen at that time keeps whatever error rate it has on the inputs still to come; on an adversarial continuation this can be every round, since all the mistake and regret bounds are sums over rounds whose feedback arrives. With i.i.d. inputs the frozen predictor has error rate of order ln(1/w)/n after n labels, hence about T·ln(1/w)/n silent errors over T further rounds.
* **PQ-learning, selective classification, behaviour cloning.** Built for withdrawal. Their guarantees are per test point or per run and relative to the training distribution; none bounds a total over an unbounded deployment.

## 3. Consequences for a finite query phase (my own derivations)

Everything in this section is **[own]**: short derivations from the statements above, in the setting of `candidate-theory.md` (samplers ν(y|x), prior w, a scaffold Π with horizon L; I write Q^Π_S for the law of a run answered by S, which is Q_S there, and d^Π_P for the visit measure d_P of the human-driven run). None has been refereed. Relation to what the project already has: §3.1 is the prior-weight form of Observation B/C in `notes.md` and of N4 and Ab3 in the candidate; §3.2 extends the candidate's Ab2; the upper bound of §3.4 is Observation A in `notes.md`; the lower bound of §3.4, §3.3 and §3.5 are, as far as I can see, not in the project's files.

### 3.1 Deterministic targets: weighted enumeration

Let H be a countable class of functions with prior w, target f with w(f) ≥ α. Keep V := {h : w(h) ≥ α, h consistent with all queried pairs}. On input x: if all of V agrees, output the common value; otherwise query. Then no output is ever wrong, and at most ⌊1/α⌋ − 1 queries are made, on every input sequence. (Each query removes a member of V; |V| ≤ 1/α; f ∈ V always.) By Zhang–Chaudhuri's threshold theorem this is tight: n equally weighted thresholds need n − 1 queries. So for the guarantee "never silently wrong, arbitrary inputs", the query complexity is **Θ(1/w)** in the worst case over classes; Cohen–Hutter–Nanda's α^(−3)·w^(−1) is an upper bound for the stochastic generalisation. My conjecture, not a result: its α^(−3) is not optimal (for deterministic classes 1/α suffices; for k stochastic predictors the k-meteorologists bound is k/ε² up to logarithms [mem]).

### 3.2 A frozen top set gives exact domination, and all the discrepancy is in the abort probability

*Setting.* Samplers ν(y|x), P ∈ H, data D_n = ((x_i, y_i))_{i≤n} with x_i chosen by any rule that fixes x_i before y_i ~ P(·|x_i). C_α(D) := {ν : posterior weight w_D(ν) ≥ α} (at most 1/α members). Deployed object: S(y|x) := min_{ν∈C_α(D)} ν(y|x), and with the remaining probability θ(x) := 1 − Σ_y S(y|x) the call **aborts** the run with a visible flag.

*Claims.*
1. Pr[for all n: P ∈ C_α(D_n)] ≥ 1 − α/w(P). (Ville's inequality for 1/w_{D_n}(P); this is Cohen–Hutter–Nanda's Theorem 2.)
2. On {P ∈ C_α(D)}: S(·|x) ≤ P(·|x) for every x, hence for every scaffold Π and every set B of transcripts, Pr[run of Π with S lies in B and does not abort] ≤ Q^Π_P(B). Writing Q̂ for the law of the run with all aborted runs mapped to one symbol ⊥: **TV(Q̂, Q^Π_P) = Pr[abort]**. The imitation *is* the human system except for a visible abort.
3. Pr[abort] ≤ E_{Q^Π_P}[Σ_j θ(x_j)] = ∫θ dd^Π_P: the abort probability is governed by the **human-driven** query distribution of the deployment scaffold. (The non-aborted prefix law is dominated by the human-driven one.)
4. max_{ν∈C_α} TV(ν(·|x), P(·|x)) ≤ θ(x) ≤ Σ_{ν∈C_α} TV(ν(·|x), P(·|x)). (The lower bound uses P ∈ C_α; the upper bound does not.)
5. *On-distribution.* If the training inputs are i.i.d. from μ, answers are fresh per query, and Σ_ν √w(ν) =: S_½ < ∞ (or the class is truncated in advance to the at most 1/α₀ hypotheses of prior weight ≥ α₀, with α₀ ≤ w(P); then S_½ ≤ α₀^(−1/2) by Cauchy–Schwarz), then with probability ≥ 1−η every ν ∈ C_α(D_n) has average Hellinger gap E_{x~μ}[1 − Σ_y √(ν(y|x)·P(y|x))] < ε², where ε² = [ln S_½ + ½·ln(1/(α·w(P))) + ln(1/η)]/n. Hence E_{x~μ} θ(x) ≤ √2·ε/α, and for a deployment scaffold with d^Π_P ≤ B·L·μ: Pr[abort] ≤ √2·B·L·ε/α. (The summability condition is the one in the candidate's Theorem B; the truncation is the device attributed to Kosoy in §2.3.)

*Proof sketch of 5.* ν ∈ C_α(D) forces the likelihood ratio ν(D)/P(D) ≥ α·w(P)/w(ν); E√(likelihood ratio) = (1 − gap)^n ≤ e^(−n·gap); Markov and a union bound; then E_μ TV ≤ √(2·gap) and |C_α| ≤ 1/α.

*Reading.* (i) Domination needs no assumption on the deployment inputs at all; this is the precise sense of "only fails obviously". (ii) The price is in the abort rate: making it small even on the training distribution takes n of order ln(1/(αw))·(B·L/(α·γ))² for abort probability γ, with α < δ·w(P): **polynomial in 1/w(P)**. For deterministic targets the sharper count is n ≈ ln(1/α)/(α·γ), and n ≳ 1/w is necessary in the worst case (class: the zero function and N singletons under the uniform distribution; until most points are seen the version space disagrees on all unseen points). So exact domination is exponentially expensive in description length even without shift; this is a property of the guarantee, not of the online setting. (iii) In a universal class the worst case 1/α is not reached by "patches" of P alone: those in C_α are the ones with K(patch | P) ≤ log₂(w_D(P)/α) + O(1), at most about w_D(P)/α of them (1/δ if α = δ·w_D(P)). The exponential comes from hypotheses that are comparably simple to P and agree with it on the sample.

### 3.3 Cohen–Hutter–Nanda after a training phase: the bound depends on the posterior weight

Their theorems hold for an arbitrary countable class, prior, environment and demonstrator. Conditional on the history h_<n of any first phase (for instance n consecutive queries), the continuation is the same imitator started from the prior w(· | h_<n) in a shifted environment. (Shifting can make distinct models coincide; I checked that the proofs go through for an indexed family of models, which the paper does not state.) Hence, for every α:

E[Σ_{t≥n} θ_q(h_<t)³ | h_<n] ≤ |A|·α^(−3)·(24/w(π^d | h_<n) + 12), and Pr[for all t ≥ n: π^d ∈ Π^α | h_<n] ≥ 1 − α/w(π^d | h_<n).

So "pre-training" replaces w(π^d) by the **posterior** weight W_n := w(π^d | h_<n), and α may be taken just below δ·W_n. This is their informal remark made exact, with two caveats. (a) E[1/W_n] ≤ 1/w(π^d) only: W_n is large with high probability exactly when the first phase refutes the competitors, and it stays small if comparably simple models agree with π^d on everything the first phase shows. Exactly, W_n = w(π^d)/m_n with m_n := Σ_π w(π)·(likelihood ratio of π to π^d on the first-phase data), the prior mass that survives the first phase. The online bound is polynomial in 2^(K_off)/δ with K_off := log₂(1/W_n) = K-type cost of π^d minus the cost log₂(1/m_n) of its first-phase behaviour: the number of bits of the demonstrator that the training phase does not pin down. (b) W_n is not observable, so setting α needs an assumption of the form "after training the truth holds posterior weight ≥ c".

Interpretation: on-distribution behaviour is paid for logarithmically (ln(1/w) of regret during training); what is paid for polynomially in 1/weight is only the information about the demonstrator that training inputs do not reveal, and the queries are spent exactly where that information matters (simple novel situations). By §3.1 this second price cannot be avoided for domination-type guarantees.

### 3.4 Feedback on request, silent mistakes allowed: mistakes × queries ≈ horizon

*Upper bound* (label-efficient reduction; Plaut et al.'s first reduction [read]; X3 in `repo.md`): auditing each round independently with probability p and predicting by weighted majority over the hypotheses consistent with the audited labels, a deterministic target of prior weight w incurs at most log₂(1/w)/p expected silent mistakes on any input sequence. Over a horizon T, with Q = p·T expected queries: **M·Q ≤ T·log₂(1/w)**.

*Lower bound.* Domain {1, …, n}; hypotheses h_s(x) = 1[x ≤ s], s ∈ {0, …, n}, uniform prior 1/(n+1) (VC dimension 1, description length log₂(n+1)); inputs presented once each in increasing order (fixed in advance, all distinct); labels seen only on queries. For every learner making at most Q queries on every run, with s uniform:

E[silent mistakes] ≥ (n − Q)²/(4·(Q+1)·(n+1)), hence ≥ n/(32·(Q+1)) for Q ≤ n/2.

*Proof.* It suffices to treat deterministic learners. Until the first query answered 0 all answers are 1, so the query positions q_1 < … < q_m (m ≤ Q; put q_0 = 0, q_{m+1} = n+1) and the predictions between them are fixed. If s lies in the block [q_j, q_{j+1}), the g_j points strictly between the two queries are predicted without information on where s is in the block; summing over the g_j + 1 positions of s in the block, the number of mistakes is ≥ Σ_{i≤g_j} min(i, g_j + 1 − i) ≥ g_j²/4. Sum over blocks, Σ_j g_j = n − m, at most Q+1 blocks, Cauchy–Schwarz. ∎ (I also checked the inequality by exhaustive search over query positions for n ≤ 12, Q ≤ 4; the optimum there has M·(Q+1) ≈ n/3.)

So on a worst-case input order, **M·(Q+1) ≥ T/32** for a class of T+1 hypotheses (about log T bits): O(1) silent mistakes need Ω(T) queries; polylog(T) queries leave T/polylog(T) silent mistakes. The same product law is proved in an easier model (labels always shown, abstentions on injected inputs free) by Yu–Blanchard, and Sekhari et al.'s corollary (|F| = √T, fewer than √T queries, regret ≥ √T) and Plaut et al.'s Ω(L·√(T/(Q+1))) are of the same kind.

*Consequence.* "Finitely many queries, then a bounded total number of silent errors over an unbounded deployment on arbitrary inputs" is impossible already for thresholds. A finite-phase theorem must either (i) bound the error **per run** on a deployment distribution that the training distribution covers, or (ii) trade silent errors for visible abstentions (§3.2), or (iii) assume structure that makes novelty recognisable (a metric, a known input distribution, rigidity).

### 3.5 The prior-averaged version of the top set

Let M^β(D) be the smallest set of top-posterior hypotheses with posterior mass > β (Cohen–Hutter's set). If P is drawn from the prior and then D from P, Pr[P ∈ M^β(D)] > β, at every fixed data size, by the definition of the posterior. So with β = 1−δ the domination of §3.2 holds with probability ≥ 1−δ over (P, D) **with no condition relating a threshold to w(P)**. The frequentist statement for a fixed P needs 1−β < δ·w(P) (Cohen–Hutter's (1−β)/(c_E·w(μ)), Cohen–Hutter–Nanda's α/w(π^d), Kosoy's η·N). This is the Bayesian reading of the candidate's Ab2 with a moderate threshold (version 1 of the candidate asked, under its Theorem E, for a prior-averaged version in which the threshold need not be below w(P)); the cost is that M^β can be much larger than C_α, so the abort rate is larger, and the statement is an average over the prior, which the machine test in the notes digest will not accept as a guarantee for one particular human.

## 4. (a) Which result is closest to the researcher's problem, and what exactly it leaves open

**Closest: Cohen, Hutter, Nanda (2022).** It is the only work that has all of: a stochastic demonstrator; a countable class with a prior that can be "all programs" (the setting of Solomonoff operator induction); no i.i.d. or reset assumption, so it covers whatever input distribution a task decomposition driven by the imitator induces, and a demonstrator with memory; a guarantee on the probability of **every event** on whole trajectories and not on a bounded loss; and a failure mode that is only "ask". Its central device, act with the minimum probability over a posterior top set, is what gives "does not do anything the human would not" as a theorem.

What it leaves open, item by item:

1. **The human stays in the loop for ever.** Only E Σθ_q³ is bounded. The expected number of queries by time t is bounded only by t^(2/3)·s_α^(1/3). Nothing is said about a finite budget or a withdrawal time. With a frozen posterior, domination survives with factor 1, but the abort rate is uncontrolled off the training distribution; to make it small on the training distribution my bound needs about 1/w(P)² training queries, and at least 1/w(P) are necessary in the worst case (§3.2).
2. **Exponential constants.** s_α = |A|·α^(−3)·(24/w + 12) with α < δ·w: order 2^(4K(P)). A factor 1/w is necessary for this type of guarantee (§3.1); whether α^(−3) is necessary is open (the authors say so). After a training phase the relevant weight is the posterior one (§3.3), which helps only if the human is nearly the simplest model consistent with their training-phase behaviour.
3. **α must be set from an estimate of w(π^d)**, or of the posterior weight; with α too large there is no guarantee, and nothing detects this.
4. **The event bound is weak.** Theorem 5 is polylogarithmic in 1/P^d(B) and degrades like t²; KL_t grows like t^(2/3). A statement of the form "P^i(B) ≤ (1+ε)·P^d(B) + ε" at a query cost is not proved.
5. **No use of benign structure.** The analysis treats every step as potentially novel. It does not show that few queries suffice when deployment resembles training, and has no notion of a training distribution, coverage or density ratio.
6. **Realisability and fairness.** No misspecified version; the environment and the human must not react to who answered.
7. **Not an upload.** The deployed object is a minimum over a changing set of models plus queries, not one program; nothing is claimed about mechanism.
8. **Finite action alphabet, uncomputable in the universal case** (computable for linear-time programs, intractable).
9. **No lower bounds** in the paper.

**Second closest, for a different reason: Plaut et al. (2025; JMLR).** It is the one F2 result with a complexity dependence of logarithmic type and total (not average) regret → 0. It leaves open: stochastic demonstrators and distributional closeness; a bounded query count (queries grow like T^((2n+1)/(2n+2)) to T^((4n+1)/(4n+2))); any structure other than a known metric with Lipschitz payoffs; the fixed-budget regime.

**Closest for "finite labelled phase, arbitrary shift, fail only by abstaining": PQ-learning (Goldwasser et al. 2020).** Error ≤ 2ε on an arbitrary test distribution and rejection ≤ ε on the training distribution from Õ(d/ε²) labelled examples, with a matching lower bound. It leaves open: stochastic labels (samplers); sequential or adaptively generated test inputs (it needs the unlabelled test inputs in advance, and in a task decomposition the inputs of later calls depend on the answers of the imitator being built); countable classes with a prior (an Occam version looks routine but I have not seen it); compositions of many calls.

**Closest for "no shift, no queries at deployment, whole-system closeness": Foster–Block–Misra (2024).** Their behaviour-cloning proposition is the candidate's Theorem A for finite classes, and their log-loss DAgger is the candidate's Protocol R with every call annotated (§2.9). For the present problem they leave open: countable classes with a prior (routine); audits of single calls (the candidate's addition); anything outside the distribution the rollouts visit; abstention.

**The gap, stated once.** No paper found gives, for a stochastic demonstrator in a countable class with a prior: (i) a finite query phase, (ii) query complexity logarithmic in 1/w(P), (iii) a guarantee on inputs outside those the training phase visits, and (iv) failures only by visible abstention at a bounded rate. The lower bounds of §5 show that these cannot all hold. The literature gives up one of them in three ways, each with a theorem of the right shape for a subcase:

* **Give up (iii): closed systems.** Train on the distribution the imitator itself induces (DAgger; the candidate's rehearsal). Proved for finite classes with every call annotated (Foster et al.); the candidate's R1–R3 extend this to a prior and to single-call audits. A different deployment scaffold then costs a density-ratio factor (the candidate's S1).
* **Give up the bounded rate in (iv): domination.** A top set frozen after training never gives an output more probability than the human does, on any input, and aborts visibly otherwise (§3.2; the candidate's Ab2). Its abort rate is small only where training covers, and only after a number of answers polynomial in 1/w(P).
* **Give up (i) or (ii): ask for help.** Cohen–Hutter–Nanda online; bounded in terms of the posterior weight after a training phase (§3.3). The count is exponential in the bits of the human that training does not pin down, and this is forced (§3.1).

A fourth shape exists only for deterministic binary labels: **PQ-learning**, with error on the actual deployment inputs and rejection on the training distribution both of order √(d/n), given the unlabelled deployment inputs. A version for samplers, with ln(1/w) in place of the VC dimension, would be new [own remark]. Its requirement is natural here in one respect: the deployment tasks are known in advance even when they are too many to put to the human. The obstacle is the circularity noted above (the inputs of later calls depend on the imitator), which would call for an iterated construction of the DAgger kind.

**On the researcher's suggestions.**

1. *"Requery the human with some probability on the induced deployment distribution"* has two formal versions. **During deployment** it is the label-efficient reduction: log₂(1/w)/p expected silent errors for a function, ln(1/w)/p total expected KL for a sampler, at audit rate p on any induced distribution, for as long as auditing continues; it cannot be stopped without assumptions (§3.4). **In a training phase on the induced distribution** it is DAgger, which is the candidate's rehearsal; this is the version that meets "not modulo keeping the human in the loop forever", and it is a closed-system guarantee.
2. *"Task decomposition"* enters none of the theorems above as a help. Its roles in this literature are to define the closed system whose induced distribution is rehearsed, and to be the source of the horizon factor L, which Foster et al. replace by variance and recoverability parameters of the task.
3. *"Properties that have held so far keep holding."* The results of this form are those of the pessimism line: an event that has never happened is not first caused by the agent, with probability ≥ 1 − (1−β)/w(μ_E) (Cohen–Hutter), or surely once a penalty exceeds 2^(K(E)+C) (Ebtekar–Cohen). Both pay exponentially in a description length and both need a mentor to fall back on. The negative counterpart is Cohen–Hutter–Bengio–Russell: a Bayesian imitator without queries gives no such guarantee after a simple unprecedented event.

**The "rock" objection.** The digest of the researcher's notes records his objection to worst-case rules over a set of plausible hypotheses: they forbid everything new ("it just turns you into a rock"). The top-set rules of §2.1 and §2.2 are such rules, and §2.1 (withdrawal) and §3.2 make the objection quantitative: a frozen top set aborts on every input that a simple test separates from the training inputs. The ask-for-help papers answer in the only way available to them: every abstention becomes a question, each kind of novelty is paid for once, and the number of kinds that matter is at most the number of hypotheses of posterior weight ≥ α. That number is exponential in bits, and by §3.1 no rule with this guarantee does better. Plaut et al. escape by assuming that novelty is measured by a known metric and harms in proportion to it; Ebtekar–Cohen by fixing in advance the complexity of the events to be avoided; the candidate's rehearsal by confining deployment to a closed system.

## 5. (b) Known lower bounds: which dependences are unavoidable

Ordered from the strongest guarantee downwards. "Class size" stands for 1/(prior weight) under a uniform prior. The candidate's N1–N4 and Observation C in `notes.md` are lower bounds of the same family derived inside the project; the list below is what is published, plus items 2 and 4, which are my own.

1. **Never silently wrong, arbitrary inputs: Θ(class size) queries.** For n thresholds exactly n − 1 abstentions are needed and suffice; for singletons on an infinite domain (Littlestone dimension 1) no finite number suffices [read: Zhang–Chaudhuri 2016, threshold and union-of-singletons theorems; KWIK upper bound |H|−1 as described there]. For k probabilistic predictors, a matching lower bound to O((k/ε²)·ln(k/δ)) is claimed by Diuk–Li–Leffler [snippet/mem]. Hence any imitator with a domination or zero-error guarantee on arbitrary inputs needs a number of queries **linear in 1/w** in the worst case; a logarithmic dependence is impossible for this guarantee [own inference from the cited theorems].
2. **The same guarantee with i.i.d. inputs still needs Ω(1/w) samples for low abstention** in the worst case (zero function plus N singletons under the uniform distribution) [own; a standard example in the selective-classification literature, mem].
3. **k mistakes with full feedback:** ELdim(H, k) non-trivial rounds are necessary and sufficient; ≤ (k+1)·|H|^(1/(k+1)), with equality in order for thresholds [read: Zhang–Chaudhuri]. In the non-realisable case fewer than l mistakes under an l-bias assumption forces unboundedly many abstentions [read].
4. **Feedback on request, silent mistakes counted:** M·(Q+1) ≥ T/32 for T+1 thresholds on a fixed input order [own, §3.4]. Published relatives: |F| = √T and fewer than √T queries force regret ≥ √T, and in general Ω(s/ζ²) queries for star number s [read: Sekhari et al. 2023]; regret Ω(L·√(T/(Q+1))) with Q queries for unrestricted mentor classes, even with i.i.d. inputs and Lipschitz payoffs, and unbounded regret for intervals (VC dimension 2) without the Lipschitz assumption [read: Plaut–Zhu–Russell 2025].
5. **Full feedback, abstention free on injected inputs, unknown clean distribution:** E[MisErr] ≥ T^α/32 or E[AbsErr] ≥ T^(1−α)/2 for a class of VC dimension 1 [read: Yu–Blanchard 2026]; combined error Ω(√T) [abstract: Edelman–Goel 2026]. With the clean distribution known, d²·log T and 6d are achievable [read: Goel et al. 2023]. So knowledge of the input distribution is worth the difference between logarithmic and polynomial.
6. **Finite training set, arbitrary test distribution, abstention allowed:** E[rej_P + err_Q] ≥ K·√(d/n), against d/n without shift [read: Goldwasser et al. 2020]. Shift costs a square root even with abstention and unlabelled test data.
7. **Agnostic, feedback on request:** regret of order T·√(ln N/m) with m queries is optimal [mem: Cesa-Bianchi–Lugosi–Stoltz 2005]. With one-sided feedback, expected mistakes Ω(√T) in the realisable case unless the effective width is 1 [read: Raman et al. 2024].
8. **Stochastic demonstrators, any amount of interaction:** regret ≥ c·√(σ²/n) with constant probability for a class of two policies, offline or online; c·H/n for deterministic experts [read: Foster–Block–Misra 2024]. Tabular: ≳ |S|·H²/N even with active queries at visited states [abstract: Rajaraman et al. 2020]. Interaction does not buy a better rate in general.
9. **No queries at all (pure Bayesian imitation):** for every model ν, the variant that follows ν until an event E happens and a near-optimal policy for a utility U afterwards has prior weight ≥ 2^(−Δ)·w(ν), Δ = K(U) + K(E) + K(v·ξ(history)) + d, and the same posterior ratio for as long as E has not happened; hence a policy within KL distance Δ bits of the Bayesian imitator is near-optimal for U after E, however much demonstrator data there is [read: Cohen–Hutter–Bengio–Russell 2025, Theorem 1 and its proof outline; the project's floor and grue results are the general form]. Hence some query, abstention or structural assumption is necessary for any guarantee at simply-describable novel inputs.
10. **Single-hypothesis prediction:** total error bounds of order 1/w for MAP/MDL-type predictors against ln(1/w) for mixtures, sharp in general [mem: Poland and Hutter, "Asymptotics of Discrete MDL for Online Prediction", *IEEE Transactions on Information Theory* 51(11):3780–3795, 2005; cited to this effect by Cohen–Hutter–Nanda, read]. Relevant because a top-set imitator behaves like a MAP predictor.
11. **Multiplicative accuracy:** a claim that guaranteeing catastrophe probability ≤ (1+ε) times the demonstrator's needs at least order 1/ε queries appears in an Alignment Forum comment [snippet only; not read, not verified].
12. **Not lower bounds, but stated limits:** Cohen–Hutter–Nanda prove no lower bound and call the quartic dependence a weakness [read]; Kosoy's bound is polynomial in N with no lower bound [read]; Cohen–Hutter's agent provably differs from its mentor in distribution for every β [read].

**Summary of the landscape.** A dependence logarithmic in 1/w is available (a) with labels on every round, (b) for per-input error on a covered distribution, (c) under a metric or a known input distribution that makes novelty recognisable, (d) for the count of deferrals when the events to be avoided are themselves simple and one pays 2^K(event) elsewhere (Ebtekar–Cohen). A dependence linear in 1/w is forced for zero-silent-error and domination guarantees on arbitrary inputs. In between, with feedback on request and arbitrary inputs, silent errors and queries trade off as M·Q ≈ T up to a factor ln(1/w).
