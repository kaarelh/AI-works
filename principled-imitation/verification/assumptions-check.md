**Lemma 0.1 (regret against any member, answers produced in any way). CORRECT.** Recomputed: (ln u)⁺ ≤ u/e, so the negative parts of Z_t and V_t have conditional mean at most 1/e; if the right side of (0.1) is finite, Pr[a_t = 1, ν(y_t|x_t) = 0] = E[a_t·p_t{ν = 0}] = 0 and likewise Pr[a_t = 1, p_t(y_t) = 0] = 0, so ξ(D_t) ≥ w(ν)ν(D_t) > 0 almost surely and the product of the predictive probabilities telescopes to ξ(D_n)/W; Z_t ≤ ln(W/w(ν)) + Σ_s V_s + Σ_{s≠t} Z_s⁻ is an integrable bound. ### Section 1 of the file (A1)

**Example 1.1 (memory inside a run). CORRECT.** The four claims were recomputed: the data of single calls in separate sittings are independent fair bits; the run of the real human system is uniform on {(0,0), (1,1)} and the run of P_cold is uniform on four points, so the total variation is 1/2; V1(b) passes because every test has the same expected frequency under the imitator P_cold and under the recorded answers.

**Lemma 1.3 (an episodic human is a sampler on visible histories). CORRECT.** P^vis is a function of the visible history alone and sums to 1 over η. One step is left to the reader in the sentence after the lemma: for episodes that are nested or interleaved, take e := (z, ρ, the coins of all other episodes); the messages to episode i are functions of e and of the earlier answers of episode i, e is independent of the coins of episode i, and the history h_j is a function of (e, earlier answers of episode i), so the tower property gives Pr[y_j = η | h_j] = P^vis(η | x_j).

**Lemma 1.6 (the episodic mixture is one posterior draw per episode). CORRECT.** The likelihood of h_j given ν_e = ν factorises into Π_{i<k} ν(y_i|v_i) and a factor that does not contain ν, because the other episodes use independent draws. Checked exactly on a nested scaffold (Section 6, part E-A: 200 random classes, largest deviation 1.4·10⁻¹⁶).

**Theorem 1.7 (R1 with episodes). CORRECT.** Lemma 0.1 is applied to the sequence of the human's messages; the data before the k-th message of the human's episode are D_{t−1} + pairs(v), so the mixture of the lemma is M̄^(t)(·|v); the chain rule turns the inner sum into KL(Q_{M̄^(t)[i←P]} ‖ Q_{M̄^(t)}). (a): Pinsker and Jensen. (b): the coupling by the coins U_{e,k} is correct; the hybrid run does not use U_{i,·}, so the union bound over the calls of episode i is legitimate; 1 − c ≤ −ln c. With one call per episode the statement is R1. The proof gives more than the statement says: by (0.2) the first display is an *equality*, Σ_t E Σ_i KL(Q_{M̄^(t)[i←P]} ‖ Q_{M̄^(t)}) = L_e·(ln(W/w(P^vis)) − E ln(1/w_{D_n}(P^vis))), the analogue of Theorem 2.1(b). Exact check of this equality on a nested scaffold with a stochastic human, n = 3: three instances, agreement to eight digits (Section 6, part E-B). A point that the statement relies on silently: for a deterministic episodic human, P^vis is an arbitrary convention at visible histories that the human cannot produce (those that contain an answer other than its own). Nothing depends on the convention: an episode is unfaithful at its *first* deviating call, whose history is one the human can produce; the hybrid run visits only such histories; and in Theorem 1.8(b) a rule that has replaced one answer of an episode may replace all later ones.

**Theorem 1.8 (certificate and low stakes with episodes, deterministic human). CORRECT.** (a) The mismatch event of a validation round is {j* < ∞} for i = I; exact check: |Pr[mismatch | I = i] − Pr[episode i of a run of C is unfaithful]| ≤ 3.3·10⁻¹⁶ over 2000 random instances (Section 6, part E-C). **Theorem 1.9 (whole runs, episodic human who is not deterministic). CORRECT; (b) can be sharpened, and one sentence about the numerical evidence is wrong.** (a) Theorem 2.1(c) of `hellinger-runs.md` applies with X_0 = (z, ρ) and X_i the answers of episode i; constants 4·(1/2) = 2 and √(2·2) = 2. ### Section 3 of the file (A3, the referent test)

**Theorem 3.2 (the referent test under A3). (a), (b), (c) CORRECT as mathematics; the reading of (c) is a GAP.**

(a) E[1_{A_t}·p_t] = Pr[A_t] − Pr[A_{t+1}] and p_t ≥ v_t/L, so Σ_t E[1_{A_t} v_t] ≤ L. Exact check on a chain scaffold for all subsets Q: the maximum over Q is 0.2948 for L = 3, n = 10 (bound 0.3000), 0.1000 for n = 30 (bound 0.1000), 0.0800 for L = 8, n = 100 (bound 0.0800) (Section 6, part X-E). The statement is for one fixed Q; see the entry for test 1 on "at once".

(b) E[R_t | 𝓕_{t−1}] = R_{t−1}(1 − d_t(ν)) exactly; exact check: E[√(ν(D_n)/P(D_n))·Π_t (1 − d_t)^(−1)] = 1.000000000 and E[G_n] = 0.997, 0.995 for two members (Section 6, part X-D).

(c) Σ_ν w(ν)·Pr[ν_{c*} = ν] ≤ max_ν Pr[ν_{c*} = ν]. Correct. The gap is in what is concluded from it. The reading says that the hypothesis that knows the realised coins "has negligible prior weight", and test 3 and objection O11 rest on this. What matters is its weight *relative to the human's*, 2^(−K). For a deterministic human the posterior odds of ν_{c*} against the human are w(ν_{c*})/w(f), and (c) bounds their expectation by 2^K·max_ν Pr[ν_{c*} = ν]. This is small only if the min-entropy of what the hypothesis must know about the experiment exceeds K bits. For the sequence J_1, …, J_n alone that is n·log₂L > K. In the regime of objection O1 (K of the order of a description of a person, n affordable) the inequality goes the other way: a hypothesis of the form "the record of this experiment, garbage elsewhere" that is simpler than the human by more than the entropy of the questions has *more* posterior weight than the human, and the imitator then does on unasked calls what that hypothesis does. R1 is not contradicted, because its bound L·K ln 2/n is then above L. Fix for the reading: "(c) bounds the expected weight of the hypothesis that knows the coins by the probability of guessing them; it is outweighed by the human once the entropy of the protocol's choices exceeds K bits, and not before." This is the researcher's F9 ("if the shortest program looks at you having made the data set") in exact form, and the honest verdict on the referent test is: passed exactly as far as R1 is not empty.

## 5. Additions by the referee

### 5.1 Episodes: the distance √(H²) is subadditive over episodes, for any interleaving

**Proposition A.** Let Π be an episodic scaffold (Definition 1.4 of the file; episodes may be sequential, nested or interleaved), and let C and P be samplers on visible histories. For a set S of episode numbers let Q_S be the law of the run in which the episodes with number in S are answered by P and all others by C; so Q_∅ = Q_C, Q_{{i}} = Q_{C[i←P]} and Q_{{1,…,L_e}} = Q_P. Then:

1. (*Cut and paste.*) H²(Q_S, Q_T) = H²(Q_{S∩T}, Q_{S∪T}) for all S, T.
2. √(H²(Q_C, Q_P)) ≤ Σ_{i ≤ L_e} √(H²(Q_C, Q_{C[i←P]})).
3. H²(Q_C, Q_P) ≤ L_e·Σ_i H²(Q_C, Q_{C[i←P]}) ≤ (L_e/2)·Σ_i KL(Q_{C[i←P]} ‖ Q_C); for L_e = 2, H²(Q_C, Q_P) ≤ 2·Σ_i H²(Q_C, Q_{C[i←P]}) ≤ Σ_i KL(Q_{C[i←P]} ‖ Q_C).

*Proof.* (1) With respect to (D₀ ⊗ λ) ⊗ counting measure the run τ = (z, ρ, y_1, …, y_len) has under Q_S the density q_S(τ) = Π_{i∈S} p_i(τ)·Π_{i∉S} c_i(τ), where p_i(τ) := Π_{j: e_j = i} P(y_j|x_j) and c_i(τ) := Π_{j: e_j = i} C(y_j|x_j) (an empty product is 1). This holds although the episode number e_j of a call depends on the history, because the answerer of call j is determined by e_j. Hence √(q_S·q_T) = Π_{i∈S∩T} p_i·Π_{i∉S∪T} c_i·Π_{i∈SΔT} √(p_i c_i), an expression that depends on (S, T) only through S ∩ T and S Δ T. The pair (S ∩ T, S ∪ T) has the same intersection and the same symmetric difference. Integrate. (2) d(Q, Q′) := √(H²(Q, Q′)) is 1/√2 times the L₂ distance of the root densities, so it is a metric. For disjoint A and B, by (1), d(Q_∅, Q_{A∪B}) = d(Q_A, Q_B) ≤ d(Q_∅, Q_A) + d(Q_∅, Q_B). Induct over the episodes. (3) The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, (a + b)² ≤ 2(a² + b²), and H² ≤ KL/2. ∎

*Consequences for Theorem 1.9(b).* The constant 4m_e may be replaced by min(4m_e, L_e/2): under the assumptions of Theorem 1.7, E[H²(Q_{M̄^(T)}, Q_{P^vis})] ≤ min(4m_e, L_e/2)·L_e·K ln 2/n for arbitrary episodes. For two episodes (a parent and a child, or two workers who exchange messages any number of times) E[H²] ≤ 2K ln 2/n, with no factor m_e. Without squaring, (2) and Jensen's inequality give E[TV(Q_{M̄^(T)}, Q_{P^vis})] ≤ L_e·√(K ln 2/n) for arbitrary episodes.

*What it does not say.* Whether H²(Q_C, Q_P) ≤ c·Σ_i H²(Q_C, Q_{C[i←P]}) holds with an absolute constant for any number of interleaved episodes. The identity (1) is the "cut-and-paste" property that transcripts of communication protocols with private coins have (Section 7); the question is the multi-party one.

*Check.* Section 6, part E-D: on five scaffolds the largest values found by hill climbing are 1.0000 for √(H²)/Σ_i √(H²_i) (bound 1) and 1.64 to 1.80 for H²/Σ_i H²_i (bound 2 for two episodes).

### 5.2 The guard statement: all simple properties at once; the last mixture

**Proposition B (union bound for Theorem 3.2(a)).** Under A3(i) and A3(ii), for every finite family 𝒬 of sets of inputs,

  Pr[for some Q ∈ 𝒬 the deployed run of M^(T) meets Q and no question of rounds 1, …, T − 1 lay in Q] ≤ |𝒬|·L/n.

In particular, with probability at least 1 − 2^k·L/n every property of complexity at most k that some input of the deployed run has was already the property of an asked input. The statement is false for the family of all sets: if no input occurs twice, Q := {inputs that were not asked before round T} is met with probability one.

*Proof.* Theorem 3.2(a) for each Q, and at most 2^k sets have a description of at most k bits. ∎

So the guard test is passed simultaneously for properties of up to log₂(n/L) − log₂(1/η) bits, with probability 1 − η. Beyond that complexity each single property is still met unprepared with probability at most L/n, but some property of that size will be.

**Example C (the guard statement fails for the last mixture).** Scaffold: a chain of L calls with inputs x_1, …, x_L; call j + 1 is made only if call j was answered 1. The human is f ≡ 1. Take the limiting case in which the mixture answers the first link that has not yet been asked wrongly with certainty. (A class with members g_j, "1 everywhere except 0 at x_j", j < L, and weights w(g_j) proportional to ε^j, w(f) proportional to ε^L, comes within 2ε of it at every call; its K is about L·log₂(1/ε).) Then link ℓ + 1 is asked, and learned, with probability 1/L per round, and after link L − 1 is learned every run reaches x_L. For Q := {x_L} the exact values of Pr[the run of M^(n+1) meets Q and no question of rounds 1, …, n lay in Q], maximised over n and divided by L/(n + 1), are 1.32 for L = 10, 2.55 for L = 40 and 5.06 for L = 160, at n ≈ L²: about 0.4·√L. For the round T of the same chain the sum over all rounds is exactly L, as Theorem 3.2(a) allows. (Section 6, part X-E.) The reason is that the time at which the run first reaches Q has a standard deviation of order L^(3/2), and the last mixture is deployed at a fixed time.

### 5.3 Parallel questions

**Proposition D.** Let the inputs of Π not depend on the answers (next_j is a function of (z, ρ) and j). Draw n pairs (a_t, x_t) independently, each as (1[J ≤ len], x_J) for a fresh (z, ρ) and a uniform J; put all questions to the human at once (A1); let M_t be the mixture of the answers to the questions 1, …, t − 1 in this fixed order, and deploy M_T for T uniform on {1, …, n}. Under A2, Σ_{t ≤ n} E Λ(M_t) ≤ L·ln(W/w(P)), as in R1.

*Proof.* Lemma 0.1 of the file for the questions in the order t gives Σ_t E[a_t·KL(P ‖ M_t)(x_t)] ≤ ln(W/w(P)). The pair (a_t, x_t) is independent of the earlier answers, and for a scaffold of this kind E[a·g(x)] = (1/L)·E_{Q_B} Σ_j g(x_j) for every answerer B and every g ≥ 0, because the inputs of a run do not depend on who answers. So E[a_t·KL(P ‖ M_t)(x_t) | answers before t] = Λ(M_t)/L. ∎

So Proposition 6.3 of the file is an upper bound that is far from the truth for such scaffolds: one batch does the work of n rounds. The number of sequential batches that a scaffold needs is governed by the depth to which its inputs depend on earlier answers; the chain of the file's remark has depth L. (Check: Section 6, part X-F.)

### 5.4 Example 2.3 with unequal weights

In Example 2.3 let w(ν₁) = 2^(−K₁) and w(ν₂) = 1 − 2^(−K₁). Then ω_k = w(ν₂)2^(−k)/(w(ν₁) + w(ν₂)2^(−k)) ≥ 1/2 for k ≤ K₁ − 1, each of the first K₁ factors 2/(2 + ω_k) is at most 4/5, and Pr[ν₁ is refuted] ≥ 1 − (4/5)^(K₁). Exact values: 0.444, 0.656, 0.904, 0.987, 0.9998 for K₁ = 1, 2, 5, 10, 20 (Section 6, part N-A). So without A2 a member that is exact on the human system and costs K₁ bits is destroyed with probability tending to 1 as K₁ grows, and the imitator is then, for ever, a member that fails half of the time.
