**In brief.** (1) A1 does three jobs (data, target, identity) and its failure can be invisible in the protocol's data. If the human is reset between episodes and remembers inside them, the theorems hold with episodes as the unit asked, for a deterministic human; a weaker form holds otherwise. One person who answers all the questions of rehearsal violates A1 by design; the remedy is a pool, and then the pool is what is imitated. A human who changes is tracked only as well as the best fixed sampler, and longer rehearsal can be worse. (2) Without A2 the bound is an oracle inequality measured on the mixtures' runs, and the mixture can destroy a member that is perfect on the human system; with A2 the price is exactly the information gained about the human. There are classes for which the factor K is forced, also under low stakes (Theorem 6.1 for replication; Proposition 6.4, in a weaker form, for additive work). The certificate with no mismatch is valid without A2 and for every human, but it can be issued only for a human who is nearly deterministic on the visited inputs. (3) A3 as the proofs use it has five parts, and each has an explicit failure. Under them each guard fixed in advance is covered at the rate L/n, and all hypotheses about the experiment together at the price K; where K exceeds the entropy of the protocol's questions, tables of the data can dominate the posterior. (4) A deployment is closed if it is a rehearsal run that happens to count. (5) Of the seven tests: guard is passed for each property fixed in advance and for all together only at the price K; referent is passed as far as R1 is not empty; the scheme is not a rock, at the price of silent failures; the rates are facts about the scheme and K is the machine's; copy and budget are not passed; horizon is outside the theorems. (6) With an hour per answer one working life pays for about 1.4·10³ bits of K; with a year per answer, for less than one bit. Checking a given imitator is cheap for a deterministic human only. (7) What happens in rare situations is the prior's choice; a patch-free prior gives an exponential rate if its bet is right and a silent, certain error if it is wrong. (8) What survives of the claim is stated at the end of Section 8.

## 0. Setting, conventions, and two lemmas used throughout

### 0.1 Notation

The notation is that of `research/outline.md`. Logarithms: ln is natural, log₂ is binary. E denotes expectation.

* **Samplers, class, mixture.** X and Y are countable sets of strings. A *sampler* is a probability kernel S(·|x) on Y; a *function* f: X → Y is the sampler that answers f(x) with probability one. The human is a sampler P. H is a countable class of *hypotheses* ν, each a conditional semimeasure (ν(y|x) ≥ 0, Σ_y ν(y|x) ≤ 1; the missing mass stands for "no answer"), with weights w(ν) > 0 and W := Σ_ν w(ν) ≤ 1. When P ∈ H, K := log₂(1/w(P)). A *data list* D is a finite sequence of pairs (x_i, y_i); ν(D) := Π_i ν(y_i|x_i); ξ(D) := Σ_ν w(ν)·ν(D), so ξ(∅) = W. If ξ(D) > 0 the *posterior* is w_D(ν) := w(ν)ν(D)/ξ(D) and the *mixture* is M_D(y|x) := Σ_ν w_D(ν)ν(y|x) = ξ(D + (x, y))/ξ(D), where D + (x, y) is D with one pair appended. If ξ(D) = 0 put M_D := M_∅.
* **Divergences.** For a probability p and a sub-probability q on Y: KL(p ‖ q) := Σ_y p(y) ln(p(y)/q(y)) ∈ [0, ∞] (it is at least ln(1/q(Y)) ≥ 0); TV is total variation; H²(p, q) := 1 − Σ_y √(p(y)q(y)). For kernels, KL(S ‖ S′)(x) abbreviates KL(S(·|x) ‖ S′(·|x)), and likewise for TV and H².
* **Scaffold, runs.** A scaffold Π of horizon L draws a top-level input z ~ D₀ and its own randomness ρ ~ λ, then for j = 1, 2, … computes the input x_j := next_j(z, ρ, y_1, …, y_{j−1}) ∈ X of the j-th *call* or stops; it makes at most L calls; out(·) is its outcome. A *run* is τ = (z, ρ, y_1, …, y_len(τ)), where len(τ) ≤ L is its number of calls; h_j := (z, ρ, y_1, …, y_{j−1}) is the history before call j. An *answerer* A is a family of distributions A_j(·|h_j) on Y together with the symbol "no answer", which the scaffold treats as one more answer; a sampler S is the answerer that looks only at x_j. Q_A is the law of the run answered by A, and Q_P is the *human system*. For a function g, run_g(z, ρ) is the run in which g answers every call.
* **Protocol R (rehearsal).** For t = 1, …, n: (1) M^(t) := M_{D_{t−1}}; (2) a run τ_t of Π is generated on a pair (z_t, ρ_t) with M^(t) answering every call (a draw from the mixture at every call), its inputs being x_{t,1}, …, x_{t,len(τ_t)}; (3) J_t is drawn from {1, …, L}; if J_t ≤ len(τ_t) the question x_t := x_{t,J_t} is put to the human, the answer y_t is received and D_t := D_{t−1} + (x_t, y_t); otherwise D_t := D_{t−1}. We write a_t := 1[J_t ≤ len(τ_t)]. *Deployment:* T is drawn uniformly from {1, …, n}, and Π is run with M^(T).
* **σ-fields.** 𝓕_t is generated by everything realised up to the end of round t; 𝓖_t by everything realised before the human's answer of round t, the state of the human before that answer included (so 𝓕_{t−1}, τ_t, J_t and that state are 𝓖_t-measurable, 𝓖_1 ⊆ 𝓖_2 ⊆ …, the data D_{t−1} are 𝓖_t-measurable and the answer y_t is 𝓖_{t+1}-measurable).
* **Two functionals.** For a kernel C and a sampler P: Λ(C) := E_{Q_C} Σ_{j ≤ len} KL(P ‖ C)(x_j) and β(C) := E_{Q_C} Σ_{j ≤ len} TV(P, C)(x_j), both along C's own runs. β(C) is the least expected number of *unfaithful calls* of a run of C over all ways of coupling C's answers with shadow answers of the human (`proofs/rehearsal-core.md`, Lemma 3.2).

### 0.2 The assumptions, as the proofs use them

The note states A1 to A3 in words. The proofs use the following.

* **A1 (stateless human, fresh coins).** There is one sampler P such that, whenever a question x is put to the human, Pr[answer = · | everything realised before the answer] = P(·|x). The object to be imitated is Q_P for the same P.
* **A2 (the human is in the class).** P ∈ H.
* **A3 (the protocol's coins), in five parts.**
  (i) *The asked call is uniform given the run:* J_t is uniform on {1, …, L} conditionally on 𝓕_{t−1}, the run τ_t and the state of the human before round t.
  (ii) *Deployment is a further sample:* T is uniform on {1, …, n} and independent of everything else, and the deployed run uses a pair (z, ρ) ~ D₀ ⊗ λ and coins of the imitator that are independent of everything before.
  (iii) *The class is fixed first:* H and w are chosen before any coin of the protocol or of the human is drawn (they are not random).
  (iv) *The human is shown what the imitator was shown:* the question put to the human is the string x_{t,J_t}, and nothing that the human sees distinguishes a rehearsal question from the same string inside a run.
  (v) *Every run uses fresh coins:* given 𝓕_{t−1}, the run τ_t of round t (and likewise every validation run) is a run of the current imitator on a pair (z_t, ρ_t) ~ D₀ ⊗ λ and with draws of the imitator that are independent of everything before; so its conditional law is Q_{M^(t)}.

Part (v) was missing from the prover's list. The attack report found it (finding M3), with the counterexample that is Example 3.3a below; the prover's file had it only in the words "a run of Π is generated" of the protocol. The note's own wording of A3 ("the bits of each run, and which call is put to the human … are fresh: independent of the human's coins and not predictable by any hypothesis") names the bits of each run. Its clause "not predictable by any hypothesis" is A3(i) and A3(v) in words: given what a hypothesis can compute from its input, the asked index and the run are fresh. Section 3 discusses the wording. Each statement below says which parts it uses. Inequality (0.4) of Lemma 0.2 is about the realised runs and needs (i) and (iv) only; every statement about a functional of the frozen mixture (Λ(M^(t)), β(M^(t)), "a run of M^(t)") needs (v) as well.

### 0.4 Two lemmas

**Lemma 0.1 (regret against any member, for any way of producing the answers). PROVED.** Let rounds t = 1, …, n be given with: an indicator a_t ∈ {0, 1} ("a question is asked") and an input x_t ∈ X, both 𝓖_t-measurable; if a_t = 1 an answer y_t ∈ Y is produced in any way, and D_t := D_{t−1} + (x_t, y_t); otherwise D_t := D_{t−1}. Let p_t := Pr[y_t = · | 𝓖_t] be the conditional law of the answer (a random distribution on Y, defined on {a_t = 1}). Let H, w be any class with weights. Then for every ν ∈ H,

  Σ_{t ≤ n} a_t·ln(p_t(y_t)/M_{D_{t−1}}(y_t|x_t)) = ln(W·Π_{t: a_t = 1} p_t(y_t)/ξ(D_n))  almost surely.  (0.2)

Nothing is assumed about the human (no A1), about the class (no A2) or about how the questions are chosen.

*Proof.* If the right side of (0.1) is infinite there is nothing to prove. Otherwise E[a_t·KL(p_t ‖ ν(·|x_t))] < ∞ for every t, so almost surely on {a_t = 1} the distribution p_t gives no mass to {y : ν(y|x_t) = 0}, and Pr[a_t = 1, ν(y_t|x_t) = 0] = E[a_t·p_t({y : ν(y|x_t) = 0})] = 0. In the same way Pr[a_t = 1, p_t(y_t) = 0] = 0. Hence almost surely ν(D_n) > 0, so ξ(D_t) ≥ w(ν)ν(D_t) > 0 for all t ≤ n, and on {a_t = 1} we have M_{D_{t−1}}(y_t|x_t) = ξ(D_t)/ξ(D_{t−1}). The product of these factors over the asked rounds telescopes to ξ(D_n)/W, which is (0.2), and ξ(D_n) ≥ w(ν)ν(D_n). With Z_t := a_t ln(p_t(y_t)/M_{D_{t−1}}(y_t|x_t)) and V_t := a_t ln(p_t(y_t)/ν(y_t|x_t)) this gives, almost surely,

  Σ_t Z_t ≤ ln(W/w(ν)) + Σ_t V_t.  (0.3)

For a probability p and a sub-probability q on Y, Σ_y p(y)·(ln(q(y)/p(y)))⁺ ≤ Σ_y p(y)·q(y)/(e·p(y)) ≤ 1/e, because (ln u)⁺ ≤ u/e. So the negative parts of Z_t and V_t have conditional expectation at most 1/e given 𝓖_t, the conditional expectations are E[Z_t | 𝓖_t] = a_t KL(p_t ‖ M_{D_{t−1}}(·|x_t)) and E[V_t | 𝓖_t] = a_t KL(p_t ‖ ν(·|x_t)), and V_t is integrable. ## 1. A1: the stateless human

### 1.1 What A1 does, and that its failure can be silent

A1 does three things at once. (a) *Data:* every recorded answer is a draw from P at the asked input, whatever was asked before. (b) *Target:* the human system is the law of runs in which every call is answered by a fresh draw from the same P, so that "the answer the human would give at the imitator's input" is defined. (c) *Identity:* the P of rehearsal, the P of validation and the P of the target are one kernel. A human with memory can break each of the three, and the protocol's own data need not show it.

**Example 1.1 (memory inside a run: every theorem holds, for the wrong system). NEW.** The scaffold has two calls and no randomness: call 1 has the input "a", call 2 the input "b", answers are bits, and the outcome is 1[y_1 = y_2]. The human answers "a" with a fair bit U. Asked "b" later *in the same sitting*, the human repeats U. Asked "b" in a sitting that did not contain "a", the human answers with a fresh fair bit. In the real human system one human does both calls, the run is (U, U), and the outcome is 1 with probability one. In rehearsal and in validation the human answers single calls, each in a new sitting. Then:

1. The recorded answers are independent fair bits at both inputs. A1 holds for the sampler P_cold with P_cold(·|a) = P_cold(·|b) = Bernoulli(1/2), and R1, R3 and V1 are true statements about the system Q_{P_cold}, in which the two answers are independent.
2. Validation passes: for every set B of pairs (question, answer) the frequencies of B under the imitator and under the human's recorded answers agree in expectation, so V1(b) certifies the imitator.
3. The deployed run of the exact imitator P_cold has outcome 1 with probability 1/2: its law is at total variation 1/2 from the real human system.
4. No function of the data of any protocol that asks single calls in separate sittings distinguishes this human from a human who is P_cold in every sitting: the two produce the same law of data.

So this failure of A1 cannot be detected from inside the protocol, and what the theorems guarantee when it occurs is the imitation of the system of *cold* humans. (Reworded in the second round. The sentence read "A1 cannot be tested from inside the protocol", and the note said that the protocol's data "cannot show that A1 fails". Other failures of A1 can show: a human who changes can leave a class of functions without a consistent member, Example 1.13. What the example proves is that a failure need not show.) The repair that the note names (the input carries what must be remembered: call 2 gets the input ("b", "a", y_1)) works when reading a record puts the human in the state that living through it would have produced. Subsection 1.2 treats the case in which it does not.

**Remark 1.1a (A1 across the questions of rehearsal: one person, or a pool). ADDED (attack report, findings m7 and S2).** A1 is also an assumption about the n questions of rehearsal: the answer to question t must not depend on questions 1, …, t − 1 or on their answers. One person who answers n questions remembers them. In the first closed system of Section 4.2 (one fixed research question, rehearsed on that question itself) every rehearsal question is a piece of work on the real task, written in part by the imitator, and the person who answers question t has answered t − 1 of them. A1 then fails by the design of the protocol, not by an accident of psychology. What remains true for one person is Theorem 1.11 (the human P_t of round t may depend on everything before round t), with a second term that nothing makes small. The design that makes A1 true for rehearsal is a *pool*: a fresh person for every question. The researcher's rules allow it ("ability to use a vast quantity of current humans to do fairly short monitoring tasks", `research/understand/notes.md`, Section 1.1(c)). Its price is in what is imitated. The sampler P of A1 is then "a member of the pool, drawn afresh at every call"; the human system Q_P is a system of strangers who pass notes to one another, which is the "bureaucracy" that the researcher doubts (same digest, F23); and K is the complexity of the pool's kernel, not of a person.

### 1.2 Episodes: the human is reset between units, and remembers inside a unit

In this subsection hypotheses are samplers (probability kernels), to avoid bookkeeping for unanswered calls.

**Definition 1.2 (episodic human).** An *episodic human* consists of a probability space of private coins ζ and maps φ_k(ζ; o_1, …, o_k) ∈ Y, k = 1, 2, …. An *episode* is one use: fresh coins ζ are drawn (the human is *reset*), messages o_1, o_2, … arrive one at a time, and the human answers y_k := φ_k(ζ; o_1, …, o_k). Whatever the human remembers or learns inside the episode is a function of ζ and of the messages so far. The *visible history* before the k-th answer is v_k := (o_1, y_1, …, o_{k−1}, y_{k−1}, o_k). An environment is *blind* if each message o_k is a function of the earlier answers y_1, …, y_{k−1} of this episode and of a random element e that is independent of ζ.

For a sequence (o_1, η_1, …, o_k, η_k) let g(o_1, η_1, …, o_k, η_k) := Pr_ζ[φ_i(ζ; o_1, …, o_i) = η_i for all i ≤ k] (with g of the empty sequence equal to 1), and for a visible history v = (o_1, η_1, …, o_{k−1}, η_{k−1}, o_k) with g(o_1, η_1, …, o_{k−1}, η_{k−1}) > 0 let

  P^vis(η | v) := g(o_1, η_1, …, o_k, η)/g(o_1, η_1, …, o_{k−1}, η_{k−1}),

and let P^vis(·|v) be an arbitrary fixed distribution for the other v. P^vis depends on the human alone.

**Lemma 1.3 (an episodic human is a sampler on visible histories). NEW.** In an episode with a blind environment, Pr[y_k = η | e, y_1, …, y_{k−1}] = P^vis(η | v_k) almost surely, for every k.

*Proof.* Fix a value of e. Then o_i = ψ_i(e; y_1, …, y_{i−1}) for some functions ψ_i, and for every sequence η_1, …, η_k the event {y_i = η_i for all i ≤ k} is the event {φ_i(ζ; o_1, …, o_i) = η_i for all i ≤ k} with o_i := ψ_i(e; η_1, …, η_{i−1}). Because ζ is independent of e, its probability given e is g(o_1, η_1, …, o_k, η_k). Divide the value for k by the value for k − 1. ∎

So for an episodic human inside a scaffold (which is blind: it sees answers only, and different episodes use independent coins), the law of every run is the law of the run answered by the sampler P^vis. In detail, for episodes that may be nested or interleaved (the check report asked for this step): for episode i take e := (z, ρ, the coins of all other episodes). The messages to episode i are functions of e and of the earlier answers of episode i; e is independent of the coins of episode i; and the history h_j before a call j of episode i is a function of e and of the earlier answers of episode i. By the lemma and the tower property, Pr[y_j = η | h_j] = P^vis(η | x_j). *The target is still a human system Q_{P^vis} of a sampler.* What A1 loses is access.

**Definition 1.4 (episodic scaffold; episodic access).** An *episodic scaffold* is a scaffold Π with a rule that gives every call j of every run an *episode number* e_j ∈ {1, …, L_e}, a function of the history h_j, such that (a) numbers are given in order of first appearance, and (b) the input of a call is the visible history of its episode: x_j = (o) for the first call of an episode, and x_j = (x_{j′}, y_{j′}, o) otherwise, where j′ is the preceding call of the same episode and o is the scaffold's next message. L_e is the *episodic horizon* (the largest number of episodes of a run) and m_e the largest number of calls of one episode. The episodes are *sequential* if the calls of each episode are consecutive; in general they may be nested (an episode waits for sub-episodes) or interleaved. In the human system every episode is carried out by the human after a reset, and by Lemma 1.3 the human system is Q_{P^vis}.

**A1′ (episodic access).** The protocol can open an episode and feed it messages one at a time; the k-th answer of an episode with visible history v is a draw from P^vis(·|v), given everything realised before it. The protocol has no other access to the human. In particular it can show a visible history v to a freshly reset human only as the text of an opening message; the answer is then a draw from P^read(·|v) := P^vis(·|(v)), the reaction to *reading* v. A human has *transparent memory* if P^read(·|v) = P^vis(·|v) for every visible history v that the scaffold can produce. Under transparent memory A1′ is A1 with visible histories as inputs, which is the note's "whatever must be remembered travels in the input".

**Definition 1.5 (episodic mixture).** For a visible history v = (o_1, y_1, …, o_k) let pairs(v) be the list of the k − 1 question–answer pairs ((o_1), y_1), ((o_1, y_1, o_2), y_2), … that v contains. The *episodic mixture* after data D is M̄_D(·|v) := M_{D + pairs(v)}(·|v).

**Lemma 1.6 (the episodic mixture is one posterior draw per episode). NEW.** M̄_D is a kernel on visible histories, and the run of Π answered by M̄_D has the law of the run in which, at the first call of every episode, a hypothesis is drawn from the posterior w_D independently of everything else and answers all calls of that episode.

*Proof.* In the second procedure let ν_e be the hypothesis of episode e, and let j be the k-th call of episode e, with visible history v = x_j. The history h_j depends on ν_e only through the earlier answers y_1, …, y_{k−1} of episode e, whose joint probability given ν_e = ν and everything else is Π_{i<k} ν(y_i|v_i). So the conditional law of ν_e given h_j is proportional to w_D(ν)·Π_{i<k} ν(y_i|v_i), which is the posterior w_{D + pairs(v)}, and the conditional law of y_j given h_j is Σ_ν w_{D+pairs(v)}(ν)·ν(·|v) = M̄_D(·|v). A run whose answers have these conditional laws has the law Q_{M̄_D}. ∎

**Protocol R^ep (rehearsal with whole episodes).** For t = 1, …, n: M̄^(t) := M̄_{D_{t−1}}; an index I_t is drawn uniformly from {1, …, L_e}; a run of Π is generated in which the calls of episode number I_t are put to the human, in order and as one episode, and every other call is answered by M̄^(t); the pairs (x_j, y_j) of the human's episode are appended to the data. Deployment: T uniform on {1, …, n}, and Π is run with M̄^(T). The human carries out n episodes and no run.

For a sampler C on visible histories and i ≤ L_e, C[i←P] denotes the answerer that answers the calls of episode i by P^vis and all other calls by C. For a function f, an episode of a run of C is *unfaithful* if at one of its calls C's answer differs from f; N_ep(C) is the number of unfaithful episodes of a run of C.

**Theorem 1.7 (R1 with episodes in place of calls). NEW.** Assume A1′, that P^vis ∈ H with K := log₂(1/w(P^vis)), and A3 with I_t in place of J_t. Then

  Σ_{t ≤ n} E[Σ_{i ≤ L_e} KL(Q_{M̄^(t)[i←P]} ‖ Q_{M̄^(t)})] ≤ L_e·K ln 2.

**Theorem 1.8 (the certificate and low stakes with episodes; deterministic human). NEW.** Let the human be an episodic human whose kernel P^vis is a function f, and let C be any sampler on visible histories, however obtained (no A2).

(a) *Certificate.* In a validation round draw I uniformly from {1, …, L_e}, generate the run in which episode I is carried out by the human and the other calls are answered by C, and at each message of the human's episode draw alongside the answer that C would give at the same visible history; record a *mismatch* if one of these differs from the human's answer. The rounds are independent and each has a mismatch with probability ρ_e := E[N_ep(C)]/L_e. If m rounds show no mismatch, then with confidence 1 − δ, E[N_ep(C)] ≤ L_e·ln(1/δ)/m.

(b) *Low stakes.* Say that the human system *tolerates m faulty episodes with failure δ₀* (for a set G of good runs) if for every rule that at each call sees the history and the human's answer and may replace the answer, and that in every run replaces answers in at most m episodes, the run is in G with probability at least 1 − δ₀. Then the run of C is in G with probability at least 1 − δ₀ − E[N_ep(C)]/(m + 1). For C = M̄^(T) under the assumptions of Theorem 1.7 this is at least 1 − δ₀ − L_e·K ln 2/((m + 1)·n).

*Proof.* (a) With the coins of the proof of Theorem 1.7(b), the answer that C would give at the k-th message is F_C(x, U_{I,k}), and a mismatch is the event {j* < ∞} for i = I. It was shown there that this is the event that episode i of the run of C (same coins) is unfaithful. So the probability of a mismatch is (1/L_e)·Σ_i Pr[episode i of a run of C is unfaithful] = ρ_e. If ρ_e > ln(1/δ)/m, the probability of no mismatch in m rounds is (1 − ρ_e)^m < δ. (b) With the same coins, the run of C is the run of f under the rule "replace f(x) at the k-th call of episode e by F_C(x, U_{e,k})". Change the rule so that, once m episodes contain a replacement, it replaces nothing in any other episode. The changed rule is admissible, and its run coincides with the run of C unless N_ep(C) > m, which has probability at most E[N_ep(C)]/(m + 1) by Markov's inequality. ∎

(b) Fix i and take the coins U of the proof of Theorem 1.7(b). Let X_0 := (z, ρ, (U_{e,k})_{e ≠ i}) and, for k = 1, …, m_e, let X_k be the k-th answer of episode i (a padding symbol if there is none). Given X_0 and X_<k the input v_k of the k-th call of episode i, if it exists, is determined, since every other call is a function of X_0 and of the answers of episode i so far. In the run of C, X_k = F_C(v_k, U_{i,k}) has the conditional law C(·|v_k); in the hybrid run it has the law P(·|v_k). Let 𝐂 and 𝐏 be the laws of (X_0, …, X_{m_e}) in the two runs, 𝐂_k and 𝐏_k the laws of X_≤k, and h_k := H²(P, C)(v_k). Writing densities with respect to a common dominating measure,

  1 − H²(𝐏_k, 𝐂_k) = ∫ √(d𝐏_{k−1}·d𝐂_{k−1})·(1 − h_k) ≤ ∫ ½(d𝐏_{k−1} + d𝐂_{k−1})·(1 − h_k) = 1 − ½E_𝐏[h_k] − ½E_𝐂[h_k],

so E_𝐂[h_k] ≤ 2H²(𝐏_k, 𝐂_k) ≤ 2H²(𝐏, 𝐂) ≤ KL(𝐏 ‖ 𝐂), and KL(𝐏 ‖ 𝐂) = E_𝐏 Σ_k KL(P ‖ C)(v_k) = KL(Q_{C[i←P]} ‖ Q_C) by the chain rule. Hence E_{Q_C}[Σ_{j in episode i} H²(P, C)(x_j)] ≤ m_e·KL(Q_{C[i←P]} ‖ Q_C). Sum over i and apply L4 at the level of calls (Corollary 2.3 of `proofs/hellinger-runs.md`): H²(Q_C, Q_P) ≤ 4·E_{Q_C} Σ_j H²(C, P)(x_j). ∎

**Proposition 1.9a (cut and paste for episodes; the factor m_e is not needed for two episodes). ADDED (check report, Section 5.1, Proposition A; the proof was re-derived by the editor and the identity tested on 300 random scaffolds, Section 9, part 11, block 9).** Let Π be an episodic scaffold (Definition 1.4; the episodes may be sequential, nested or interleaved), and let C and P be samplers on visible histories. For a set S of episode numbers let Q_S be the law of the run in which the episodes with number in S are answered by P and all others by C; so Q_∅ = Q_C, Q_{{i}} = Q_{C[i←P]} and Q_{{1, …, L_e}} = Q_P.

1. (*Cut and paste.*) H²(Q_S, Q_T) = H²(Q_{S∩T}, Q_{S∪T}) for all sets S, T.
2. √(H²(Q_C, Q_P)) ≤ Σ_{i ≤ L_e} √(H²(Q_C, Q_{C[i←P]})).
3. H²(Q_C, Q_P) ≤ L_e·Σ_i H²(Q_C, Q_{C[i←P]}) ≤ (L_e/2)·Σ_i KL(Q_{C[i←P]} ‖ Q_C). For L_e = 2: H²(Q_C, Q_P) ≤ 2·Σ_i H²(Q_C, Q_{C[i←P]}) ≤ Σ_i KL(Q_{C[i←P]} ‖ Q_C).

*Proof.* (1) With respect to the product of D₀ ⊗ λ and counting measure, the run τ = (z, ρ, y_1, …, y_len) has under Q_S the density q_S(τ) = Π_{i ∈ S} p_i(τ)·Π_{i ∉ S} c_i(τ), where p_i(τ) := Π_{j: e_j = i} P(y_j|x_j) and c_i(τ) := Π_{j: e_j = i} C(y_j|x_j) (an empty product is 1). This holds although the episode number e_j of a call depends on the history, because the answerer of call j is determined by e_j. Hence √(q_S·q_T) = Π_{i ∈ S∩T} p_i·Π_{i ∉ S∪T} c_i·Π_{i ∈ SΔT} √(p_i·c_i), an expression that depends on the pair (S, T) only through S ∩ T and the symmetric difference S Δ T. The pair (S ∩ T, S ∪ T) has the same intersection and the same symmetric difference. Integrate, and use H² = 1 − ∫√(q·q′). (2) d(Q, Q′) := √(H²(Q, Q′)) is 1/√2 times the L₂ distance of the root densities, hence a metric. For disjoint sets A and B, by (1) with S = A and T = B, d(Q_∅, Q_{A∪B}) = d(Q_A, Q_B) ≤ d(Q_∅, Q_A) + d(Q_∅, Q_B). Induct over the episodes. (3) The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality; for two episodes (a + b)² ≤ 2(a² + b²); and H² ≤ KL/2. ∎

*Consequences for Theorem 1.9(b).* Under the assumptions of Theorem 1.7 and for arbitrary episodes, E[H²(Q_{M̄^(T)}, Q_{P^vis})] ≤ min(4m_e, L_e/2)·L_e·K ln 2/n. For two episodes (a parent and a child, or two workers who exchange any number of messages) E[H²] ≤ 2K ln 2/n, with no factor m_e. Without squaring, part 2, TV ≤ √(2H²), H² ≤ KL/2 and Jensen's inequality give E[TV(Q_{M̄^(T)}, Q_{P^vis})] ≤ E Σ_i √(KL(Q_{M̄^(T)[i←P]} ‖ Q_{M̄^(T)})) ≤ L_e·√(K ln 2/n) for arbitrary episodes. *What it does not say:* whether H²(Q_C, Q_P) ≤ c·Σ_i H²(Q_C, Q_{C[i←P]}) holds with an absolute constant c for any number of interleaved episodes. This is open (Section 10.1). The identity of part 1 is the property that transcripts of communication protocols with private coins have (Section 11).

**Which of R1, R3, V1 hold with "call" replaced by "episode".** For a deterministic human all three hold, for sequential and for nested episodes, with L replaced by L_e, with K the weight of the human as a function on visible histories, with n and m counting human *episodes*, and with one posterior draw per episode in place of one per call (Theorems 1.7(b), 1.8). One clause of R1 changes: the note's R1 also bounds the unfaithful calls "summed over all n rehearsal runs"; in Protocol R^ep the rehearsal runs contain an episode of the human, and Theorem 1.7(b) bounds, summed over the rounds, the unfaithful episodes of a run answered by the round's mixture throughout. For a human who is not deterministic, R1 holds in the form of Theorem 1.7: the divergence is measured along the episode as the *human* plays it inside the imitator's run, not along the imitator's own messages. Whole-run closeness holds with the constant 2 for sequential episodes and with min(4m_e, L_e/2) for arbitrary ones (Theorem 1.9, Proposition 1.9a); a version of R3 for an episodic human who is not deterministic is missing. V1(b) has an analogue for tests of whole episodes: compare the frequency of a set of transcripts of the episode I in runs of C with its frequency in runs in which the human plays episode I, by Hoeffding's inequality and a union bound as in V1(b). The prover's file asserted this without a statement; both reports marked it as not proved. It is not used in the note and is not stated as a theorem here.

**Example 1.10 (single messages asked in mid-episode: reading is not living). NEW.** Let the scaffold consist of one episode of two messages. The human of the human system answers the first message with "done" and privately draws a fair bit b; the second message asks for the bit, and the human answers b. A freshly reset human who is shown the text (o_1, "done", o_2) answers "?", having drawn no bit. So P^vis(·|(o_1, "done", o_2)) is uniform on {0, 1} and P^read(·|(o_1, "done", o_2)) is the point mass at "?". If rehearsal puts single calls to the human (Protocol R on the scaffold with calls as units), every answer to a second message is a draw from P^read. A1 holds for the sampler P_cold that equals P^vis on opening messages and P^read on longer histories; R1, R3 and V1 are true of Q_{P_cold}; and TV(Q_{P_cold}, Q_{P^vis}) = 1. The data of single cold calls are the same for this human and for a human with transparent memory and the kernel P_cold. Protocol R^ep, which asks the episode as a whole, learns P^vis. The same happens whenever work done during an earlier message is not on the page: a human who answered "I have a proof" after long thought can write the proof down; a reader of that sentence cannot.

### 1.3 A human who changes during rehearsal

**Theorem 1.11 (a moving human: regret against the best fixed sampler). NEW.** Under the hypotheses of Lemma 0.2 (no A1, no A2; the human of round t is a random kernel P_t that may depend on everything that happened in earlier rounds), for every ν ∈ H and for T uniform on {1, …, n},

  E[Σ_{j ≤ len(τ_T)} KL(P_T ‖ M^(T))(x_{T,j})] ≤ L·ln(W/w(ν))/n + (1/n)·Σ_{t ≤ n} E[Σ_{j ≤ len(τ_t)} KL(P_t ‖ ν)(x_{t,j})].

*Proof.* Lemma 0.2 divided by n. ∎

*What it says.* The mixture of a uniformly random round is, along its own run, close to *the human of that round*, up to the complexity of ν over n and the average divergence of the best fixed sampler ν from the moving human along the same runs. A human with memory across rehearsal questions is covered (P_t may depend on the earlier questions and answers). *What it does not say.* Nothing about the human of any other date, in particular not the human of the day of deployment; and the second term does not shrink with n when the human keeps moving. The left side is along the rehearsal run of round T; it is the same for a fresh deployed run of M^(T) when P_T does not depend on τ_T (A3(ii), A3(v)). The comparison with the best *fixed* sampler is a property of Protocol R with a static class, not of every protocol: a prior over sequences of hypotheses that switch a few times (M. Herbster and M. K. Warmuth, "Tracking the best expert", *Machine Learning* 32:151–178, 1998; named by the attack report from memory, not opened by anyone in this project) would compare with the best human that changes a few times, at a price per change. That variant is not worked out here.

**Corollary 1.12 (bounded drift; rehearsing longer can make the guarantee worse). NEW.** Let Y be finite with s elements. For η ∈ (0, 1) and a sampler ν let ν^η := (1 − η)·ν + η·(uniform distribution on Y), and let H^η := {ν^η : ν ∈ H} with w(ν^η) := w(ν). Let P° ∈ H be a sampler (the human at a reference date), K° := log₂(1/w(P°)), and suppose TV(P_t(·|x), P°(·|x)) ≤ γ_t for all x and t, almost surely; γ̄ := (1/n)Σ_t γ_t. Run Protocol R with the class H^η under the hypotheses of Lemma 0.2. Then, with κ := K° ln 2/n + ln(1/(1 − η)) + γ̄·(1 + ln(s/η)),

  E[Σ_{j ≤ len(τ_T)} KL(P_T ‖ M^(T))(x_{T,j})] ≤ L·κ  and  E[Σ_{j ≤ len(τ_T)} TV(P°, M^(T))(x_{T,j})] ≤ L·(γ̄ + √(κ/2)).

For a drift that is linear in time, γ_t = γ·t, we have γ̄ = γ(n + 1)/2, and κ is decreasing in n up to n* := √(2K° ln 2/(γ·(1 + ln(s/η)))) and increasing afterwards.

*Proof.* Let p, ν be distributions on Y, a := TV(p, ν) and q := ν^η. Write p = (1 − a)·r + a·r′ with r := min(p, ν)/(1 − a) and r′ := (p − ν)⁺/a (if a ∈ {0, 1} only one part is present). KL(· ‖ q) is convex, r/q ≤ 1/((1 − a)(1 − η)) on the support of r because r ≤ ν/(1 − a) and q ≥ (1 − η)ν, and r′/q ≤ s/η because q ≥ η/s. So KL(p ‖ q) ≤ (1 − a)·ln(1/((1 − a)(1 − η))) + a·ln(s/η) ≤ ln(1/(1 − η)) + a·(1 + ln(s/η)), using (1 − a)·ln(1/(1 − a)) ≤ a. Hence KL(P_t ‖ (P°)^η)(x) ≤ ln(1/(1 − η)) + γ_t(1 + ln(s/η)) for all x, and Theorem 1.11 with ν = (P°)^η gives the first bound. For the second, TV(P°, M^(T)) ≤ TV(P°, P_T) + TV(P_T, M^(T)) ≤ γ_T + √(KL(P_T ‖ M^(T))/2) at every input, and E Σ_{j ≤ len} √(k_j/2) ≤ √(L·E Σ_j k_j/2) ≤ L·√(κ/2) by the Cauchy–Schwarz and Jensen inequalities. The last claim is the derivative of K° ln 2/n + γ(n + 1)(1 + ln(s/η))/2. ∎

A bound that grows with n does not prove that the error grows. Section 9, part 7, shows that it does in an example: the distance between the deployed mixture and the human of the first round falls from n = 10 to about n = 410 and then rises; at n = 2000 it is two and a half times its least value. (The prover's five values of n put the turning point "near n = 300"; the exact computation over all n in the second round, Section 9, part 13, gives n = 414 for the human of the first round and n = 410 for the human of round T.) The note's sentence after Theorem E2, "longer rehearsal can be worse", rests on the corollary for the bound and on this example for the error.

**Example 1.13 (without smoothing, one changed answer destroys a class of functions). NEW.** Let H = {f, g} be two functions with f(x₀) ≠ g(x₀), and let the human answer like f in rounds t < t₀ and like g afterwards. If x₀ is asked once before t₀ and once after, both members have likelihood 0, ξ(D) = 0 and the posterior is undefined. If this happens with positive probability (x₀ is reached by runs on both sides of t₀), the right side of (0.4) is infinite for both members, so nothing is contradicted. A class meant for a human who may change has to give every answer positive probability, as H^η does.

**Proposition 1.14 (the date is a guard). PROVED.** Let a *moving human* be a sequence (P_t)_{t ≥ 1} of samplers, a question at time t being answered by a fresh draw from P_t. Let two moving humans (P_t) and (P′_t) satisfy P_t = P′_t for t ≤ n, while P_{n+1}(·|x) and P′_{n+1}(·|x) have disjoint supports for some x. Then for every protocol whose questions are asked at times 1, …, n and every way of answering x at time n + 1, for one of the two humans the answer lies in the support of that human's P_{n+1}(·|x) with probability at most 1/2.

*Proof.* All questions are answered by the same kernels under both humans, so the law of the data, and of the answer at x, is the same. The two supports are disjoint, so the two probabilities of landing in the respective support sum to at most 1. ∎

So what a protocol can promise about the human *after* the last question rests on an assumption about how the human moves; the theorems promise something only about the humans who answered.

### 1.4 A human who knows that no run depends on the answer; a human who reads what the imitator wrote

**Proposition 1.15 (the human of rehearsal is the only human the protocol sees). NEW.** Let the human's answer depend on the input x and on a *context* c that the human perceives and that is not part of x: the answer to x in context c is a fresh draw from P(·|x, c). Let all questions of rehearsal and validation be answered in one context c_reh.

(a) Every theorem that assumes A1 holds with P := P(·|·, c_reh), and the human system it is about is Q_{P(·|·, c_reh)}: the system of humans who answer as in rehearsal.

(b) Let c′ ≠ c_reh, and let P, P′ be two humans with P(·|·, c_reh) = P′(·|·, c_reh). For every protocol whose questions are answered in context c_reh, the law of the deployed run is the same for both; so it is at total variation at least TV(Q_{P(·|·,c′)}, Q_{P′(·|·,c′)})/2 from one of the two systems of humans in context c′.

(c) Consider the variant of Protocol R in which the human's answer to call J_t is put into the run in place of the mixture's answer and the run continues from it. Assume A3(i), (iv), (v), and let the human P_t of round t be fixed by 𝓕_{t−1} (under A1, P_t = P). Then for every ν ∈ H,

  Σ_{t ≤ n} E[E_{Q_{M^(t)}} Σ_j KL(P_t ‖ M^(t))(x_j)] ≤ L·ln(W/w(ν)) + Σ_{t ≤ n} E[E_{Q_{M^(t)}} Σ_j KL(P_t ‖ ν)(x_j)],

the inner expectations being over runs answered by M^(t) throughout. Under A1 and A2 this is R1, unchanged. (The prover's statement was "the bound (0.4) … holds for this variant unchanged". Inequality (0.4) allows P_t to depend on τ_t and sums along the realised rehearsal run, which in the variant contains a human answer; the check report asked for the two restrictions.)

*Proof.* (a) A1 holds for P(·|·, c_reh). (b) The data have the same law under P and P′; then the triangle inequality. (c) The index J_t is independent of the run (A3(i)), so it may be drawn first. The calls 1, …, J_t of the run of round t are then generated as in a run answered by M^(t) throughout: whether the call J_t exists, and its input, are functions of (z_t, ρ_t) and of the mixture's answers to the calls before it. So the pair (a_t, x_t) has the same conditional law given 𝓕_{t−1} as in Protocol R, and by A3(v), E[a_t·g(x_t) | 𝓕_{t−1}] = (1/L)·E_{Q_{M^(t)}}[Σ_{j ≤ len} g(x_j)] for every nonnegative g fixed by 𝓕_{t−1}. Apply Lemma 0.1 with p_t = P_t(·|x_t), and then this identity with g = KL(P_t ‖ M^(t)) and with g = KL(P_t ‖ ν). ∎

*Reading.* If the human answers a rehearsal question differently because no run depends on it, the object that the protocol imitates is still well defined: it is the system in which every call is answered by the human as that human answers in rehearsal. By (b), a protocol all of whose questions are answered in the context of rehearsal learns nothing about the human of another context: that human is a counterfactual on which its data are silent. The prover's file concluded that "no other human system is available to any protocol that takes the human out of the loop". Both reports found this too strong, and it is: (b) is about protocols that never produce the other context. A protocol can produce it, by not letting the human tell which answers are used (for example a first phase in which the human's answers enter runs that count, the questions having the same form as those of rehearsal). What holds for such a design is not worked out here. Part (c) is not such a design: the run that receives the answer is a rehearsal run whose outcome is used for nothing, so a human who answers differently when nothing depends on the answer is not helped by it. What (c) shows is only that feeding the answer back into the run does not disturb the bound (compare the mixed roll-outs of Ross, Gordon and Bagnell, Section 11). So what the human is told is part of the definition of the object that is imitated, and it is fixed by the designer, not by a theorem.

**Proposition 1.16 (the human reads questions that the imitator helped to write). PROVED (observation of `research/understand/alignment-proposals.md`, Section 6.1).** Under A1, A2, A3 and for a deterministic human, call the question of round t *tainted* if an earlier call of the run τ_t was unfaithful (an untainted question is the question that the human system would have asked at that position on the same (z, ρ)). The expected number of rounds with a tainted question, over the whole of rehearsal, is at most L·K ln 2.

*Proof.* The question of round t is tainted only if τ_t has an unfaithful call, which has probability at most the expected number of unfaithful calls of τ_t; by R1 these expectations sum to at most L·K ln 2. ∎

The bound does not grow with n, but it is of the size of the whole error budget, and the first rounds are driven by the prior. It is an upper bound: when it exceeds n it says nothing, and it never says that many questions are tainted (in the block classes of the lower bounds nearly all questions are untainted; Section 8, O8). A1 assumes that reading such a question does not change the human. If it does, P_t moves and Theorem 1.11 is what remains.

## 2. A2: the human is in the class

### 2.1 The theorems without A2

**Theorem 2.1 (L3 and R1 without A2; the exact price with A2). PROVED ((a) is the form recorded in `proofs/rehearsal-core.md`, Remark 4.4, and `proofs/hellinger-runs.md`, Appendix A; (b) is not in the candidate theory. Both are standard: (a) is the regret bound for Bayes mixtures in the form of an index of resolvability, (b) the chain rule for the divergence between the law of the data under the truth and under the mixture; Section 11).** Assume A1.

(a) For questions chosen as in Lemma 0.1 and every ν ∈ H,

  Σ_{t ≤ n} E[a_t·KL(P ‖ M_{D_{t−1}})(x_t)] ≤ ln(W/w(ν)) + Σ_{t ≤ n} E[a_t·KL(P ‖ ν)(x_t)].

Under Protocol R with A3(i), A3(iv) and A3(v), for every ν ∈ H,

  Σ_{t ≤ n} E Λ(M^(t)) ≤ L·ln(W/w(ν)) + Σ_{t ≤ n} E[E_{Q_{M^(t)}} Σ_j KL(P ‖ ν)(x_j)],

so E Λ(M^(T)) ≤ inf_{ν ∈ H} {L·ln(W/w(ν))/n + ā_n(ν)}, where ā_n(ν) := (1/n)·Σ_t E[E_{Q_{M^(t)}} Σ_j KL(P ‖ ν)(x_j)] is the divergence of ν from the human summed along the runs of the mixtures, averaged over the rounds.

(b) If also A2 holds, then for questions chosen as in Lemma 0.1,

  Σ_{t ≤ n} E[a_t·KL(P ‖ M_{D_{t−1}})(x_t)] = ln(W/w(P)) − E[ln(1/w_{D_n}(P))],

and, if the members of H are samplers, this number is the divergence KL(ℙ_P ‖ ℙ_w) between the law ℙ_P of everything the protocol sees when the human is P and its law ℙ_w when the human is drawn from w/W before the first question. Under Protocol R with A3(i), A3(iv) and A3(v), Σ_t E Λ(M^(t)) = L·(ln(W/w(P)) − E[ln(1/w_{D_n}(P))]).

*Proof.* (a) Lemma 0.1 with p_t = P(·|x_t) (A1), and Lemma 0.2 with P_t = P. (b) By (0.2), Σ_t a_t ln(P(y_t|x_t)/M_{D_{t−1}}(y_t|x_t)) = ln(W·P(D_n)/ξ(D_n)) = ln(W/w(P)) + ln w_{D_n}(P) almost surely; the summands are integrable with conditional expectations a_t·KL(P ‖ M_{D_{t−1}})(x_t) (proof of Lemma 0.1). For the second description: the protocol chooses its questions by the same rule whoever the human is, so the density of (coins of the protocol, D_n) under a human ν′ is a factor that does not depend on ν′ times ν′(D_n); under a human drawn from w/W it is the same factor times ξ(D_n)/W. The logarithm of the ratio is ln(W·P(D_n)/ξ(D_n)). For Protocol R use (0.5) and A3(v). ∎

*What (a) gives.* An oracle inequality: the deployed imitator does, on its own runs, as well as the best trade-off between the complexity of a member and its divergence from the human. This is the form of the guarantee of Ross, Gordon and Bagnell for training on the learner's own distribution: their bound is relative to "the true loss of the best policy in hindsight", ε_N := min_π (1/N)Σ_i E_{s∼d_{π_i}}[ℓ(s, π)], an average over the state distributions d_{π_i} of the learner's iterates (S. Ross, G. J. Gordon, J. A. Bagnell, "A Reduction of Imitation Learning and Structured Prediction to No-Regret Online Learning", AISTATS 2011, PMLR 15:627–635; the prover quoted it from the digest `research/understand/imitation-theory.md`, Section 3.2; the author of the attack report read the arXiv version 1011.0686v3 and found the definition verbatim; not opened by the editor). The trade-off between the complexity of a member and its divergence, min_ν {complexity/n + divergence}, is the index of resolvability of A. R. Barron and T. M. Cover, "Minimum complexity density estimation", *IEEE Transactions on Information Theory* 37(4):1034–1054, 1991 (named by the attack report, which saw the definition quoted in a search result; the paper was not opened by anyone in this project).

*What (a) does not give.* (1) The approximation term ā_n(ν) is measured where the *mixtures* go, not where the human system goes. A member that is exact on every input of the human system can have ā_n(ν) = ∞, and then the bound says nothing about it; Example 2.3 shows that this is how the protocol behaves, not a defect of the proof. (2) The term does not shrink with n. (3) If every member gives probability zero to some answer that the human gives at an input the mixtures reach, the right side is infinite for every ν and the posterior can become undefined.

*What (b) says.* The price paid by rehearsal is exactly L times the information that the answers carry about which member the human is: the prior surprise K ln 2 minus the expected surprise ln(1/w_{D_n}(P)) that remains. In the limit the price is L·(ln(W/w(P)) − lim_n E ln(1/w_{D_n}(P))). It reaches L·ln(W/w(P)) exactly when E ln(1/w_{D_n}(P)) tends to 0, that is, when the posterior weight of P tends to 1 almost surely (the two are equivalent: 1/w_{D_n}(P) converges almost surely, being a nonnegative supermartingale under the human P, and Ville's inequality bounds E sup_n ln(1/w_{D_n}(P))). That needs a class in which no second member has the kernel of P on the asked inputs, and L·ln(W/w(P)) equals L·K ln 2 only if W = 1. (The prover's sentence, "the bound L·K ln 2 is attained in the limit exactly when the posterior weight of P tends to 1", had neither condition; both reports asked for them.) For a deterministic human the amount spent so far, ln(W/ξ(D_n)) = Σ_t a_t·(−ln M^(t)(y_t|x_t)), is the cumulative log loss of the mixture and can be read off the data; the amount that remains cannot.

**Remark 2.1a (a floor for classes of functions: the unfaithful calls are draws from the refutable part of the prior). ADDED (attack report, Section 6, part E; proof re-derived by the editor).** Let H consist of functions, let the human be a function f ∈ H, and assume A3(i), (iv), (v). Then under Protocol R

  Σ_{t ≤ n} E[number of unfaithful calls of τ_t] ≥ L·Σ_{g ∈ H} w(g)·Pr[g is refuted within n rounds].

*Proof.* A function g that is consistent with the data D has posterior weight w(g)/ξ(D) ≥ w(g). At a call of τ_t with input x the mixture's draw differs from f(x) with probability at least the sum of w(g) over the unrefuted g with g(x) ≠ f(x). So, with N_t(g) the number of calls of τ_t at whose input g differs from f, E[number of unfaithful calls of τ_t | 𝓕_{t−1}] ≥ Σ_{g unrefuted before round t} w(g)·E[N_t(g) | 𝓕_{t−1}]. By (0.5) with the indicator of {x : g(x) ≠ f(x)}, the question of round t lies in that set with conditional probability N_t(g)/L given the run, and the first question in it refutes g; so for g unrefuted before round t, E[N_t(g) | 𝓕_{t−1}] = L·Pr[g is refuted in round t | 𝓕_{t−1}]. Sum over t and g. ∎

So for classes of functions the sum that R1 bounds by L·K ln 2 is at least L times the prior weight of the members that rehearsal refutes: R1 is tight up to the ratio of K ln 2 to that weight (in the first instance of the attack report's check the floor, the exact sum and the bound are 1.0652, 1.0654 and 1.5211; `proofs/code/assumptions_attack_core.out`, part E).

**Proposition 2.2 (A2 is needed only on the inputs the scaffold can produce). NEW.** Let X_Π ⊆ X contain every input that occurs in some run of Π under some answers. Let [P]_Π := {ν ∈ H : ν(·|x) = P(·|x) for all x ∈ X_Π}, w_Π := w([P]_Π) and K_Π := log₂(1/w_Π). If w_Π > 0 (P itself need not be in H), then Theorem R1 holds with K_Π in place of K, and so does every statement derived from R1 through the bound Σ_t E Λ(M^(t)) ≤ L·K ln 2.

*Proof.* Every question of Protocol R lies in X_Π, so ξ(D_n) ≥ Σ_{ν ∈ [P]_Π} w(ν)ν(D_n) = w_Π·P(D_n). The proof of Lemma 0.1 uses the member ν only through the inequality ξ(D_n) ≥ w(ν)ν(D_n); replace it by this one. ∎

K_Π ≤ K, and it can be far smaller: in the class of all programs, K_Π is at most the length of the shortest program whose kernel agrees with the human on X_Π, whatever the human does elsewhere. The quantity that the theorems charge for is the complexity of the human's behaviour *inside the closed system*.

**Example 2.3 (without A2, the member that imitates the human system exactly is destroyed where only the mixture goes). NEW.** X = {a, b}, Y = {0, 1}. The scaffold asks a; if the answer is 1 it asks b; then it stops (L = 2). The human is the function f ≡ 0, so the human system makes one call, at a, with answer 0. H = {ν₁, ν₂} with w(ν₁) = w(ν₂) = 1/2, where ν₁ answers 0 at a and 1 at b, and ν₂ answers a fair bit at a and 0 at b. The human is not in H. The run law of ν₁ *is* the human system: Q_{ν₁} = Q_f. The run law of ν₂ is at total variation 1/2 from it. Under Protocol R:

* While ν₁ is alive and a has been asked k times, the posterior weight of ν₂ is ω_k := 1/(1 + 2^k). In a round, with probability 1/2 the call a is asked (k grows by one); with probability ω_k/4 the mixture answers 1 at a and the call b is asked: the human answers 0 and ν₁ has likelihood zero from then on; otherwise nothing is asked.
* Hence Pr[ν₁ is never refuted] = Π_{k ≥ 0} 2/(2 + ω_k) = 0.556481, and Pr[ν₁ is refuted] = 0.443519 (Section 9, part 1: exact product, exact recursion, simulation 0.4432 ± 0.0011).
* After ν₁ is refuted the imitator is ν₂ for ever. So the run of the mixture after n rounds differs from the human system with probability tending to 0.2218, not to 0, although H contains a member whose runs are exactly the human system's.
* Theorem 2.1(a) is not violated: for ν₁ the right side is infinite (KL(f ‖ ν₁)(b) = ∞, and the mixtures reach b with positive probability); for ν₂ it is 2 ln 2 + n ln 2.

So without A2 the protocol can end, with probability bounded away from zero, at the worst member of the class while the best is perfect on the human system. Proposition 2.2 shows what would have saved ν₁: agreement with the human on *all* of X_Π, the inputs that wrong answers lead to included.

*With unequal weights the exact member is destroyed almost surely* (ADDED; check report, Section 5.4; recomputed by the editor, Section 9, part 11, block 7). Let w(ν₁) = 2^(−K₁) and w(ν₂) = 1 − 2^(−K₁). Then ω_k = w(ν₂)·2^(−k)/(w(ν₁) + w(ν₂)·2^(−k)) ≥ 1/2 for k ≤ K₁ − 1, each of the first K₁ factors 2/(2 + ω_k) is at most 4/5, and Pr[ν₁ is refuted] ≥ 1 − (4/5)^(K₁). Exact values: 0.4435, 0.6556, 0.9037, 0.9874, 0.9998 for K₁ = 1, 2, 5, 10, 20. So without A2 a member that is exact on the human system and costs K₁ bits is destroyed with probability tending to 1 as K₁ grows, and the imitator is then, for ever, a member whose run differs from the human system's half of the time.

### 2.2 Why the factor K cannot be avoided, and what a prior built from knowledge changes

**Proposition 2.4 (K is the number of candidates the prior takes seriously). PROVED; (c) quotes `proofs/lower-bounds.md`, Theorem 3.1 (N2b in the note).**

(a) For every class with weights and every k, at most 2^k members have log₂(1/w(ν)) ≤ k.

(b) For every finite set S of members, (1/|S|)·Σ_{ν ∈ S} log₂(1/w(ν)) ≥ log₂|S|.

(c) For all integers m_b ≥ 1 and L ≥ 1 and every δ ≤ 1/20 with N := L/(2δ) an integer there are a set S of 2^K functions, K = m_b·log₂N, and a scaffold with L calls such that every protocol (any way of choosing questions, any computation, any deployed answerer, any prior) whose deployed outcome is right with probability at least 1 − δ for every member of S asks at least (1 − 10δ)·K·L/(8δ·log₂(L/(2δ))) questions in expectation when the human is drawn uniformly from S.

(d) All theorems of the note hold for every prior that satisfies A3(iii). If the prior is the posterior w_{D′} of a prior w after earlier answers D′ of the same human, obtained under A1, then its K is K′ = K − log₂(w_{D′}(P)/w(P)), and the theorems hold conditionally on D′.

*Proof.* (a) Σ_ν w(ν) ≤ 1. (b) By Jensen's inequality the left side is at least log₂(|S|/Σ_{ν ∈ S} w(ν)). (c) is the cited theorem. (d) The proofs use nothing about w but Σ w ≤ 1 and that it is not random; the second sentence is the definition of the posterior. ∎

*Reading.* A prior built from existing knowledge can make K small for the humans it favours. It cannot do so for many: by (a) and (b) the average of K over any 2^k candidates is at least k under every prior. And (c) says that this is not a weakness of the Bayes mixture: there are sets of 2^K candidates, and scaffolds, for which some candidate costs of the order of K·L/(δ·log(L/δ)) questions under every protocol. (The prover wrote "when 2^K candidates remain possible, some of them cost …". That is false for other sets: 2^K functions that differ at one input, with 2^K answers there, cost one question. Both reports asked for the quantifier.) So "K" in the theorems can be replaced by "the logarithm of the number of humans that the prior, with all knowledge built in, still cannot tell apart on the inputs of the closed system" (Proposition 2.2 for the last clause), and by nothing smaller in the worst case over classes. Theorem 6.1 below gives the corresponding lower bound under low stakes for tolerance by replication, and Proposition 6.4 a weaker one for additive work.

### 2.3 The certificate: valid without A2 and for every human, issued only for a nearly deterministic one

**Proposition 2.5 (V1(a) without its hypotheses). MODIFIED (the hypothesis "deterministic human" of V1(a) is removed; A2 is not used, as the note says).** Let A be any answerer, frozen. Let the human satisfy A1 during validation, for some sampler P that need not belong to any class. In each of m validation rounds a run of A is generated, J is drawn uniformly from {1, …, L} (A3(i)), and if the run has a J-th call the human answers its input; let V = 1 if this happened and the human's answer differs from the answer A gave at that call, and V = 0 otherwise. Then V_1, …, V_m are independent with one mean ρ′, and

  β(A)/L ≤ ρ′ = (1/L)·E_{Q_A} Σ_j (1 − Σ_y A_j(y|h_j)·P(y|x_j)),

where β(A) = E_{Q_A} Σ_j TV(A_j(·|h_j), P(·|x_j)) is the expected number of unfaithful calls of a run of A. Hence:

(a) if no mismatch occurs, then with confidence 1 − δ, β(A) ≤ L·ln(1/δ)/m, and Theorem R3 applies with this β;

(b) in general, with confidence 1 − δ, β(A)/L ≤ V̄ + √(ln(1/δ)/(2m)), where V̄ is the observed frequency of mismatches;

(c) ρ′ = β(A)/L if the human is deterministic; for every human ρ′ ≥ (1/L)·E_{Q_A} Σ_j (1 − max_y P(y|x_j)), whatever A is.

(d) (ADDED; both reports.) The certificate of (a) is issued with probability (1 − ρ′)^m ≤ exp(−m·ρ′). For the exact imitator A = P the mismatch rate is ρ′ = (1/L)·E_{Q_P} Σ_j (1 − Σ_y P(y|x_j)²), the probability that two independent answers of the human to the asked input differ.

*Proof.* A is frozen (A3(v) for the validation runs) and the human answers with fresh coins, so the rounds are independent and identically distributed. Given the run, the human's answer to x_j is a draw from P(·|x_j) that is independent of the answer y_j ~ A_j(·|h_j) used in the run; the two differ with probability 1 − Σ_y A_j(y|h_j)P(y|x_j) ≥ 1 − Σ_y min(A_j(y|h_j), P(y|x_j)) = TV(A_j(·|h_j), P(·|x_j)). Average over the uniform J. (a) If β(A) > L ln(1/δ)/m then ρ′ > ln(1/δ)/m and Pr[no mismatch] = (1 − ρ′)^m < δ. (b) Hoeffding's inequality. (c) For a deterministic human P(·|x) is a point mass and the two expressions coincide; in general Σ_y A(y)P(y) ≤ max_y P(y). (d) The rounds are independent with mismatch probability ρ′; for A = P put A = P in the formula for ρ′. ∎

**What the proposition does and does not give (rewritten by the editor; finding M1 of the attack report and correction 1 of the check report).** The certificate with no mismatch is *valid* for every human. It can be *issued* only for a human whose answers are almost determined by the input on the inputs that A visits, and this is the hypothesis that matters:

* For a human who gives the answer f(x) with probability 1 − e and one other answer with probability e, the exact imitator has ρ′ = 2e(1 − e). With the m = 300 rounds that the budget of Section 6.2 uses (θ = δ = 0.1, confidence 95%), the exact imitator passes with probability 1 for e = 0, 0.55 for e = 0.001, 0.0025 for e = 0.01 and 1.4·10⁻²⁶ for e = 0.1 (Section 9, part 11, block 1). Answers that are sentences have a probability of agreeing with their own repetition that is close to zero, and then ρ′ is close to 1 for every imitator.
* Part (b) certifies β(A)/L ≤ V̄ + √(ln(1/δ)/(2m)), and V̄ tends to ρ′. Theorem R3 needs β/(θL) < 1. For e = 0.1 (the human of the lead's toy computation, `results/toy_property.out`) ρ′ = 0.18 > θ = 0.1: the exact imitator certifies nothing, for every m.
* The problem statement models the human as a probabilistic program. For that human, without A2, a certificate from single answers that the number of unfaithful calls is small does not exist (`proofs/rehearsal-core.md`, Proposition 13.4). What can be certified without A2 is: the frequencies of tests fixed in advance (V1(b); `proofs/rehearsal-core.md`, Theorem 13.1(b1)), which say nothing about how the calls of a run hang together; and, with questions put twice and answers from a small set, a squared distance of the answer laws (Remark 2.6; Pr7 in the note). Under A2 a bound on the number of unfaithful calls can be certified at the rate √(K/m) (`proofs/rehearsal-core.md`, Theorem 13.1(b3)), and then K is back.
* A certificate is a test of a given imitator, not a way to obtain one: it is passed with probability (1 − ρ′)^m, so the imitator must already have a mismatch rate well below 1/m (ρ′ = 0.001 passes 300 rounds with probability 0.74, ρ′ = 0.01 with probability 0.05). For a deterministic human in the class, rehearsal produces such an imitator from n ≥ m·K ln 2/δ′ questions with probability 1 − δ′ (`proofs/rehearsal-core.md`, Corollary 13.2), which contains K.

So Proposition 2.5 removes a hypothesis from the validity of V1(a), as the summary table says, and it is not an improvement of V1 for a human who is not deterministic.

**Remark 2.6 (what repeated questions certify for a human who is not deterministic). NEW.** The note says of such a human that "each input is seen once, so P(·|x) is not observable at any single x" and that only frequencies of tests can be estimated. Under A1 the human can be asked the same input twice, and this gives more. For a frozen sampler A and the input x of a uniformly chosen call of a run of A, let ρ_AP be the probability that A's answer differs from a human answer, ρ_PP the probability that two independent human answers to x differ, and ρ_AA the probability that two independent answers of A to x differ (computable without the human). Then

  E_x[Σ_y (A(y|x) − P(y|x))²] = 2ρ_AP − ρ_AA − ρ_PP,

because for a fixed x the three probabilities are 1 − Σ_y A·P, 1 − Σ_y P² and 1 − Σ_y A². So 2m human answers (two per chosen call) estimate the mean squared L₂ distance between A and the human on A's own inputs to within O(1/√m), with no family of tests. The L₂ distance is weak for answers spread over many values, and repeated questions need A1 in full: a human who remembers the first answer repeats it.

*Attribution and use (ADDED; attack report, finding w4 and Section 4, item 9; check report).* This is the collision estimator of the ℓ₂ distance from the literature on testing distributions (T. Batu, L. Fortnow, R. Rubinfeld, W. D. Smith and P. White, "Testing closeness of discrete distributions", *Journal of the ACM* 60(1), Article 4, 2013; the attack report saw in the arXiv preprint 1009.5397 that the ℓ₂ distance can be tested with a number of samples that does not depend on the size of the domain and that a small ℓ₂ distance does not give closeness in ℓ₁; the paper was not opened by the editor). On an answer set of s elements TV ≤ (√s/2)·‖A − P‖₂, so the remark certifies β(A)/L ≤ (√s/2)·√(2ρ_AP − ρ_AA − ρ_PP) for small answer sets, with no K, at a rate of order m^(−1/4) in the certified total variation; for long answers it certifies nothing. The forms that the note uses are in other parts: `proofs/property-batch.md`, Theorem 6.2 and Proposition 6.4 (Pr7), and `proofs/rehearsal-core.md`, Proposition 13.5 (each audited input put to the human r times: β(A)/L is at most the mean recorded distance plus ½·√((s − 1)/r) + √(ln(1/δ)/(2m))).

## 3. A3: the protocol's coins, and the referent test

### 3.1 What a hypothesis about the making of the data set can and cannot do

The referent test asks whether a hypothesis that models how the data set was made does as well as the intended one. The researcher's form of the worry: "if the shortest program looks at you having made the data set, then you are probably screwed" (`research/understand/notes.md`, F9). Two kinds of object must be kept apart.

**Definition 3.1.** (a) A *guard* is a member of H of the form "the human on X ∖ Q, something else on Q", for a set Q ⊆ X. A hypothesis that models the making of the data set and wants to fit the data and deviate elsewhere is a guard whose Q is the set of inputs that, by its model, are not put to the human. As a member of H it is a kernel in x alone: whatever it computes about the world or the experiment, its answer at a call depends on the string x. (b) A *family indexed by the coins* is a map c ↦ ν_c from the possible values of some random element c* of the experiment (for example the sequence of asked indices J_1, …, J_n, or the whole data set) into H; ν_{c*} is "the hypothesis that knows how this data set was made".

**Theorem 3.2 (the referent test under A3). NEW.**

(a) *No fixed property is new at deployment* (R7 of the note). Assume A3(i), A3(ii) and A3(v); the human may be any process. For every set Q ⊆ X that is fixed before the protocol starts (it must not depend on the realised data),

  Pr[the run of round T has a call with input in Q, and no question of rounds 1, …, T − 1 lay in Q] ≤ L/n,

and the same holds for the deployed run of M^(T) in place of the run of round T. In particular, for a deterministic human f and a function g ∈ H, the probability that g is still unrefuted before round T and the deployed run reaches an input at which g differs from f is at most L/n, whatever the weight of g.

(b) *A fixed hypothesis loses weight at the rate at which it deviates on the mixtures' runs.* Assume A1, A3(i), A3(iv), A3(v). For ν ∈ H let d_t(ν) := (1/L)·E_{Q_{M^(t)}}[Σ_j H²(P, ν)(x_j)], an 𝓕_{t−1}-measurable number. For every δ ∈ (0, 1), with probability at least 1 − δ, simultaneously for all n,

  ν(D_n)/P(D_n) ≤ δ^(−2)·exp(−2·Σ_{t ≤ n} d_t(ν)),

so the posterior odds of ν against P are at most (w(ν)/w(P))·δ^(−2)·exp(−2Σ_{t ≤ n} d_t(ν)) when P ∈ H.

(c) *A prior fixed in advance cannot anticipate fresh coins.* Assume A3(iii). For every family c ↦ ν_c indexed by a random element c*, E[w(ν_{c*})] ≤ max_ν Pr[ν_{c*} = ν].

*Proof.* (a) Let A_t be the event that no question of rounds 1, …, t − 1 lay in Q; A_t ∈ 𝓕_{t−1}. Let v_t := Pr[τ_t has a call with input in Q | 𝓕_{t−1}] and p_t := Pr[a_t = 1, x_t ∈ Q | 𝓕_{t−1}]. By (0.5) with g = 1_Q, p_t is 1/L times the conditional expected number of calls of τ_t with input in Q, so p_t ≥ v_t/L. Since 1_{A_{t+1}} = 1_{A_t}·(1 − 1[a_t = 1, x_t ∈ Q]), E[1_{A_t}·p_t] = Pr[A_t] − Pr[A_{t+1}]. Hence Σ_{t ≤ n} E[1_{A_t}·v_t] ≤ L·Σ_t (Pr[A_t] − Pr[A_{t+1}]) ≤ L. T is uniform and independent, so the probability in question is (1/n)·Σ_t E[1_{A_t}·v_t] ≤ L/n. Given 𝓕_{T−1} the deployed run has the law Q_{M^(T)} (A3(ii)), which is the conditional law of τ_T (A3(v)), so the same computation applies to it. For the last sentence take Q := {x : g(x) ≠ f(x)}: the first question in Q refutes g.

(b) Let R_n := √(ν(D_n)/P(D_n)) (defined almost surely). On {a_t = 1}, E[√(ν(y_t|x_t)/P(y_t|x_t)) | 𝓖_t] = Σ_y √(ν(y|x_t)P(y|x_t)) = 1 − H²(P, ν)(x_t), so by (0.5) and A3(v), E[R_t | 𝓕_{t−1}] = R_{t−1}·(1 − d_t(ν)) ≤ R_{t−1}·e^(−d_t(ν)). Thus G_n := R_n·exp(Σ_{t ≤ n} d_t(ν)) is a nonnegative supermartingale with G_0 = 1, and Pr[sup_n G_n ≥ 1/δ] ≤ δ by Ville's inequality. Square.

(c) E[w(ν_{c*})] = Σ_ν w(ν)·Pr[ν_{c*} = ν] ≤ max_ν Pr[ν_{c*} = ν]·Σ_ν w(ν), because w is not random. ∎

*Reading of (a).* It is the guard test in the form that uses the coins of the protocol only: each property that a hypothesis could key on, simple or not, known to us or not, *provided it is fixed before the coins are drawn*, has either been met by a question or is met by the deployed run with probability at most L/n. It is the random-stopping argument of the researcher's notes (`research/understand/notes.md`, Q14) applied to the round T, together with the passage from a bound summed over the rounds to a bound for a uniformly random round (Section 11). Section 9, part 3, shows that L/n is attained up to a factor close to 1.

*What (a) does not say* (finding M2 of the attack report; the check report on test 1; the prover's table of Section 5 said "for every property at once"). It is false for all sets at once, and for a set chosen after the data. Let X = [N], L = 1 and the input uniform. The run of round T meets a singleton that no earlier question met exactly if its input was never asked before, which has probability (1/n)·Σ_{t ≤ n} (1 − 1/N)^(t−1): 0.915 for N = 50 and n = 10, where L/n = 0.1; 0.954 for N = 200 and n = 20; 0.976 for N = 1000 and n = 50, where L/n = 0.02 (Section 9, part 11, block 2). The researcher's guard F3 ("a choice of variable such that it never triggers in train") is a property chosen with the data in view, and (a) does not cover it. What covers all the sets on which members of the class deviate, taken together, is R1, at the price K (and at the rate √(K/n) per call for a human who is not deterministic).

**Corollary 3.2′ (finitely many properties at once). ADDED (check report, Section 5.2, Proposition B).** Under the assumptions of Theorem 3.2(a), for every finite family 𝒬 of sets of inputs fixed before the protocol starts,

  Pr[for some Q ∈ 𝒬 the deployed run of M^(T) meets Q and no question of rounds 1, …, T − 1 lay in Q] ≤ |𝒬|·L/n.

In particular, for a prefix-free language of descriptions of sets, with probability at least 1 − 2^k·L/n every property with a description of at most k bits that some input of the deployed run has was already the property of an asked input.

*Proof.* Theorem 3.2(a) for each Q and the union bound; by Kraft's inequality at most 2^k sets have a description of at most k bits. ∎

So the guard test is passed simultaneously, with probability 1 − η, for properties of up to log₂(n/L) − log₂(1/η) bits. Beyond that complexity each single property is still met unprepared with probability at most L/n, but some property of that size will be.

*Reading of (b).* Modelling the experiment buys a hypothesis nothing on the asked calls: by (0.5) its deviation at the asked call is its average deviation over the calls of the run. With R1 this is "covered by R1": the total effect of all such hypotheses on the mixture is bounded by K ln 2 whatever their weights.

*Reading of (c)* (corrected; correction 3 of the check report and finding m5 of the attack report; the prover's reading said that the hypothesis that knows the realised coins "has negligible prior weight", and test 3 and objection O11 rested on it). (c) is about the other kind of object. The hypothesis that knows the realised coins exists in the class of all programs, and a prior chosen before the coins were drawn gives it, on average, at most the probability of guessing them: L^(−n) for the sequence J_1, …, J_n of full-length runs, and only 2^(−k), however large n is, if all the coins come from a uniformly drawn seed of k bits. This is not yet a verdict on that hypothesis. What matters is its weight against the human's, 2^(−K). For a deterministic human the posterior odds of a member that fits the data against the human are the ratio of the prior weights, and (c) bounds the expectation of that ratio for ν_{c*} by 2^K·max_ν Pr[ν_{c*} = ν]. So the hypothesis that knows the coins is outweighed by the human, in the expectation of the odds, once what it must know has a probability of being guessed below 2^(−K); before that the bound allows it to outweigh the human, and under some priors it does (Remark 3.2a). (The sentence ended "and not before", which an upper bound cannot show; reworded in the second round with finding N14 of the second check.)

**Remark 3.2a (the table of the data against the human: the referent test is passed as far as R1 is not empty). ADDED (attack report, finding m5; check report, entries for Theorem 3.2(c) and for test 3; the numbers were recomputed by the editor, Section 9, part 11, block 5, and again by the second check and by the editor in the second round, part 13).** *Scope* (stated after finding N14 of the second check). The remark is about priors that charge a table of the data about as many bits as its questions carry: (i) is an exact instance, (ii) an instance with a prior fixed in advance, (iii) a bound for every prior. For a class without tables, or a prior that charges them more, it shows nothing; so it shows that the referent test *can* fail where R1 is empty, not that it must.

(i) A table of the data ("answer as recorded on the asked inputs, something else elsewhere") is a member of the class of all programs for every data set. If the prior charges a table about b bits per recorded question, the tables that fit outweigh the human until n·b exceeds K. An exact illustration: X = [N] with N = 1024, one call per run at a uniform input, the human f ≡ 0 with weight 2^(−K), and for every set A of inputs the member "0 on A, 1 elsewhere" with weight c·(2N)^(−|A|), where c := (1 − 2^(−K))/(1 + 1/(2N))^N, so that the weights of the class sum to 1 and b = 11 bits. (The constant was not stated before the second check; the numbers depend on it. The member with A = X coincides with f; its weight c·(2N)^(−N) is negligible.) After s distinct questions the mixture answers 1 at an input that was not yet asked with probability 0.9993 at s = 8 and 0.6654 at s = 9 when K = 100, with probability 0.9995 at s = 81, 0.9985 at s = 90 and 0.32 at s = 91 when K = 1000, and with probability below 10⁻⁴ from s = 11 on when K = 100 and from s = 93 on when K = 1000 (the earlier text said "from s = 101 on", which is true and not the threshold). R1 is not contradicted: its bound K ln 2/n is above 1 for n < K ln 2, and the mixture spends its K ln 2 exactly there.

(ii) The check report's case. One call per run at an input uniform on Z = 2²⁰ values, n = 1000 questions, and a prior, fixed in advance, that gives weight about 1/C(Z, n) to each of the hypotheses "the human's answers on these n inputs, the opposite elsewhere", one for every set of n inputs. At least one of them fits the data (exactly one if the n inputs are distinct, which has probability 0.62), and the posterior odds of those that fit against the human are at least 2^K/C(Z, n) = 2^(K − 11470). For K above about 11 500 bits the imitator answers a fresh input wrongly more often than not; R1 allows this, since K ln 2/n is then about 8.

(iii) For a human who is not deterministic there is a second point. A hypothesis that knows the realised *answers* is repaid by its likelihood, and what protects against it is the probability of the *questions* only. For an injective family D ↦ ν_D of members indexed by the data set,

  E[w(ν_D)·ν_D(D)/P(D)] = Σ_D c(D)·w(ν_D)·ν_D(D) ≤ max_D c(D),

where c(D) is the product over the rounds of the conditional probabilities with which the protocol asks the questions of D, given the earlier pairs of D. (Pr[the data are D] = c(D)·P(D); ν_D(D) ≤ 1; and Σ_D w(ν_D) ≤ 1 because the family is injective and w is fixed first.)

So where K is larger than n times the number of bits of a question, which is the regime of objection O1, the imitator may be the table's and not the human's. This is the researcher's F9 ("if the shortest program looks at you having made the data set, then you are probably screwed") in exact form. It is the same obstacle as O1, not a separate success.

**Example 3.2b (the guard statement fails for the last mixture). ADDED (check report, Section 5.2, Example C; re-derived by the editor in closed form, Section 9, part 11, block 8).** The scaffold is a chain of L calls with inputs x_1, …, x_L; call j + 1 is made only if call j was answered 1. The human is f ≡ 1. Take the limiting case in which the mixture answers the first link that has not yet been asked wrongly with certainty. (A class with the members g_j, "1 everywhere except 0 at x_j", j < L, and weights w(g_j) proportional to ε^j, w(f) proportional to ε^L, comes within 2ε of it at every call; its K is about L·log₂(1/ε).) Then the run of a round stops at the first link that is not yet learned, and that call is the asked one with probability 1/L; so each round learns the next link with probability 1/L. After link L − 1 is learned every run reaches x_L, which is asked with probability 1/L per round. For Q := {x_L}, the probability that the run of the last mixture M^(n+1) meets Q although no question of rounds 1, …, n lay in Q is therefore the probability that exactly L − 1 of n independent trials of probability 1/L succeed. Maximised over n and divided by L/(n + 1) it is 1.32 for L = 10, 2.55 for L = 40 and 5.06 for L = 160, at n ≈ L²: about 0.4·√L (the binomial probability at its mode is about 1/√(2πL), and (n + 1)/L ≈ L). The reason is that the time at which runs first reach Q has a standard deviation of order L^(3/2), and the last mixture is deployed at a fixed time. For the divergence and the number of unfaithful calls of the last mixture see `proofs/rehearsal-core.md`, Propositions 6.1 to 6.4.

**Example 3.3 (the asked call is computed from the run by a short seed: R1 fails). NEW; R1 is REFUTED without A3(i).** Let k ≥ 1 and L ≥ 2. The top-level input is z = (z_σ)_{σ ∈ {0,1}^k}, a list of 2^k independent uniform elements of {1, …, L}; a run has exactly L calls, with inputs (z, 1), …, (z, L). The human is f ≡ 0. H consists of f with weight 1/2 and, for every σ ∈ {0,1}^k, of the function ν_σ with ν_σ(z, j) := 0 if j = z_σ and 1 otherwise, with weight 2^(−k−1). So A1 and A2 hold and K = 1. Fix a seed s ∈ {0,1}^k and let the protocol ask the call J_t := z_{t,s}: a function of the run and of the seed. Marginally J_t is uniform; given the run it is determined, so A3(i) fails.

* ν_s agrees with the human at every asked call and is never refuted. A seed σ ≠ s survives a round exactly if z_{t,σ} = z_{t,s}, which has probability 1/L, independently over rounds and seeds.
* If A_t is the set of seeds alive before round t, the posterior weight of each is 1/(2^k + |A_t|), and the expected number of unfaithful calls of the run of round t is (L − 1)·E[|A_t|/(2^k + |A_t|)] ≥ (L − 1)/(2^k + 1), because s ∈ A_t.
* So the deployed run has at least (L − 1)/(2^k + 1) unfaithful calls in expectation for every n, while the conclusion of R1 would be at most L·ln 2/n.
* With fresh coins (J_t uniform and independent of z_t) every seed, s included, survives a round with probability 1/L, and the expected number of unfaithful calls of round t is (L − 1)·E[|A_t|/(2^k + |A_t|)] with |A_t| binomial (2^k, L^(−(t−1))), which tends to 0.

Numbers for k = 3, L = 4 (Section 9, part 2): with the seed the expected number of unfaithful calls per run is 0.3506 at n = 100 and 0.3351 at n = 1000 (limit 1/3); with fresh coins it is 0.0228 and 0.0023, below the bounds 0.0277 and 0.0028 of R1.

The floor 2^(−k) is negligible for a long seed *under this prior*. It is not negligible under a prior that favours ν_s, and Theorem 3.2(c) says that no prior fixed in advance can favour the hypothesis that knows fresh coins. That is the difference between a seed and physical randomness. The example needs the inputs of a run to identify the run (here through z); inputs that quote their task do. It is the abstract form of a pseudo-random generator: if all coins of the protocol (the top-level inputs, the scaffold's bits, the asked indices) are outputs of one generator with a seed of k bits, a hypothesis that holds a seed σ recomputes, from the top-level input that its input quotes, the round and the index that seed σ would have asked, and z_σ stands for that index.

## 4. Closed systems

### 4.1 The definition

**Definition 4.1 (closed deployment).** Fix a scaffold Π with its laws D₀ and λ, and a protocol that ends by freezing an answerer A (which may depend on everything the protocol saw) and then *deploys* it: the deployed run is the run of Π answered by A on a pair (z*, ρ*). The deployment is **closed** if:

* **(C1) one more sample:** conditionally on everything the protocol saw and did before deployment, (z*, ρ*) has the law D₀ ⊗ λ, and the coins of A in the deployed run are fresh;
* **(C2) sampled in advance:** the runs of rehearsal and validation *were* generated by the protocol, answered by the imitator of the round (or by the frozen answerer), on fresh pairs (z, ρ) with the law D₀ ⊗ λ, independent of (z*, ρ*), and the protocol could generate as many such runs as it needed;
* **(C3) nothing else gets in:** the inputs of the deployed run are the strings next_j(z*, ρ*, y_1, …, y_{j−1}), and the answers depend on the history of the run and on A's coins alone: no clock, no mark that this run is the real one, no state of the world.

In one sentence: *the deployment is a rehearsal run that happens to count.*

Two corrections were made to the prover's wording. (C2) was an ability ("the protocol can generate … runs … on fresh pairs"); it has to be a fact about the runs that were used, or Example 3.3a is a closed deployment (attack report, finding M3). (C3) said that the answers depend on the inputs and on A's coins alone, which is the condition for a sampler; the definition is for an answerer, which may see the history (one posterior draw per run, the episodic mixture, the answerers of V1) (check report). The note's draft definition ("we can sample z and ρ ourselves, so that we can run it with any answerer we have, as often as we like, before the run that counts") is the ability half of (C2), for sampling by computation. (C1) and (C3) are A3(ii) for the run that counts, and (C2) is A3(v); Examples 3.4, 3.5 and 3.3a are what happens without them. The proofs use the three parts as follows. R1, R2 and R3 use (C2) for the runs of rehearsal (answered by M^(t)), and (C1), (C3) to conclude that the deployed run of M^(T) has the law Q_{M^(T)} given the data. V1 uses (C2) for the runs of validation (answered by A) and (C1), (C3). The lower bounds N1 and N2 are stated for closed deployments and hold a fortiori without closure.

(C2) asks neither for a computation nor for knowledge of D₀:

**Proposition 4.2 (a stock of exchangeable top-level inputs suffices). NEW.** Let z_1, …, z_{n+1} be exchangeable random elements that come from outside (their law may be unknown), independent of the coins of the protocol and of the human; let the protocol compute next_j and out and draw ρ itself. Run Protocol R with z_t as the top-level input of round t, with fresh ρ and fresh draws of the imitator in every run, and deploy M^(T), T uniform on {1, …, n} and independent, on z_{n+1}. Under A1, A2, A3(i), A3(iii), A3(iv) the deployed run satisfies E[Σ_j KL(P ‖ M^(T))(x_j)] ≤ L·K ln 2/n, as in Theorem R1.

*Proof.* D_{t−1} is a function of z_1, …, z_{t−1} and of coins that are independent of all the z's. By exchangeability (D_{t−1}, z_t) and (D_{t−1}, z_{n+1}) have the same law. So the run of M^(t) on z_{n+1} with fresh ρ and fresh coins has the same joint law with D_{t−1} as τ_t, and the expected sum of KL(P ‖ M^(t)) along it equals E[Σ_j KL(P ‖ M^(t))(x_{t,j})]. Lemma 0.2 does not use the law of the top-level inputs; sum its conclusion over t and divide by n. ∎

So in Definition 4.1 the known law D₀ may be replaced by exchangeability of the top-level inputs of rehearsal, validation and deployment. Each input of the stock is used once. If the stock is finite and is sampled with replacement, the guarantee is for a top-level input drawn from the stock, not for a new one from its source.

| deployment | closed? | why |

| one fixed research question z*, worked on by a tree of calls with its own randomness; rehearsal runs are on z* itself | yes | D₀ is the point mass at z*; the run that counts differs from the others in ρ and in the imitator's coins only. (Closed as a deployment; A1 is another matter: one person who answers all its questions works on the real task n times, Remark 1.1a) |
| a problem drawn from a generator that we run (theorems to prove, puzzles), the real problem being a further draw | yes | (C1), (C2) by computation |
| a scaffold acting in a simulated environment whose dynamics we compute and can reset | yes | the environment is part of ρ and next |
| tasks from a recorded stock of exchangeable tasks, each used once | yes | Proposition 4.2 |
| a scaffold that calls an outside resource which is stationary, can be reset, and is also called during rehearsal (a laboratory that repeats an experiment afresh) | yes, as far as stationarity and reset hold | the resource then supplies part of ρ; this is an assumption about the world |
| the same research question, rehearsed on other, easier questions | no | (C1) fails: Example 3.4 with p = 0 |

The last row is the case the motivation has in mind ("run the upload at a thousand times speed"). A closed system is a computation fixed in advance, not a person to be consulted at will.

### 4.3 What the theory offers when the deployment is not closed

**Proposition 4.3 (an open system with the human on call). PROVED (Observation A of `research/understand/notes.md`, Section 6.2; the same statement is Theorem 10.1 of `proofs/rehearsal-core.md` and Theorem 3.1 of `proofs/shift-abstain.md`, which is S5 in the note; it is label-efficient prediction, Cesa-Bianchi, Lugosi and Stoltz 2005, applied to the regret bound, see Section 11).** Let calls x_1, x_2, … arrive in any way; they may depend on the past, on the world and on the system's own answers. At each call, after x_t is fixed, a coin c_t with Pr[c_t = 1] = q is flipped independently of the past and of x_t. If c_t = 1 the human answers (A1) and the pair is added to the data; if c_t = 0 the mixture of the data so far answers. Under A2, for every number 𝒯 of calls,

  E Σ_{t ≤ 𝒯} KL(P ‖ M_{D_{t−1}})(x_t) ≤ K ln 2/q,

the sum running over all calls, asked or not; for a deterministic human the expected number of calls at which the mixture answers wrongly is at most (1 − q)·K ln 2/q.

*Proof.* Lemma 0.1 with a_t = c_t and ν = P gives Σ_t E[c_t·κ_t] ≤ K ln 2 for κ_t := KL(P ‖ M_{D_{t−1}})(x_t). κ_t is fixed before the coin, so E[c_t κ_t] = q·E[κ_t]. For a function f the mixture's draw at x_t is wrong with probability 1 − M_{D_{t−1}}(f(x_t)|x_t) ≤ κ_t, and E[(1 − c_t)·κ_t] = (1 − q)·E[κ_t]. ∎

**Proposition 4.4 (what the theory offers when the deployment is not closed). Each item is proved here or quoted with its place. The list is this project's inventory; that it is complete is not a theorem.** (The prover's title was "the complete list", with the words "the following and nothing else". Both reports found that the list left out the abstaining rules, which are now item 6, and the mixture of task laws, now item 7.) For a deployment that is not closed the theory of this project offers the following.

1. *Where no question was asked, nothing.* If two members P, P′ of the class agree on a set A of inputs and the protocol asks outside A with probability γ, then for disjoint events G, G′ ("good for P", "good for P′") of the deployed run, the two failure probabilities sum to at least 1 − γ (`proofs/lower-bounds.md`, Theorem 1.6; N1). No amount of computation changes this.
2. *A bounded likelihood ratio.* If (C2) and (C3) hold and the top-level input of the deployed run is independent of the protocol's data and has a law D′ with D′ ≤ B·D₀, then every guarantee of the form E_{z ~ D₀}[Φ(z)] ≤ ε for a function Φ ≥ 0 of the top-level input (the expected number of unfaithful calls given z, the probability of failure given z) becomes E_{z ~ D′}[Φ(z)] ≤ B·ε, since Σ_z D′(z)Φ(z) ≤ B·Σ_z D₀(z)Φ(z). Being ready for N task laws with disjoint supports costs a factor N in the number of questions, and this is necessary: an advantage η over a fair guess under each of N such laws needs n ≥ 2ηN questions (same file, Theorem 4.1; N3).
3. *The human on call* (Proposition 4.3). For 𝒯 ≥ K ln 2/ε calls, an average divergence ε per call costs K ln 2/ε questions in expectation, with q = K ln 2/(ε𝒯). The questions are spread over the whole period of use. The human never leaves: after the last possible question, item 1 and Proposition 1.14 apply to every later input of a new kind. This breaks the requirement that the human does not stay in the loop for ever; what the theory adds is that the human's load per call may fall as fast as the tolerated average error allows.
4. *Refusing at the top level.* If the support of D₀ is known and decidable, a top-level input outside it can be recognised by computation and refused; this makes one failure obvious without the human and without an assumption on the human. (For a D₀ given only as a sampler, membership in the support is semi-decidable; for the stock of Proposition 4.2 the law is unknown.) Inside the support nothing separates the top-level inputs on which the imitator is good from the others: by Markov's inequality the set {z : Φ(z) > ε/η} has D₀-probability at most η, and an outside law may sit on it.
5. *The certificate certifies the rehearsed law only.* V1 is a statement about runs drawn as in validation.
6. *A rule that fails only visibly, on any inputs* (ADDED; correction 2 of the check report, finding m3 of the attack report). Let 𝒞 be a set of hypotheses that contains the human. The rule that answers y with probability min_{ν ∈ 𝒞} ν(y|x) and abstains with the remaining probability is, on every scaffold and for every law of top-level inputs, rehearsed or not, the human system's run or an initial part of it followed by a visible abort; every set of complete runs has at most its probability under the human system (`proofs/shift-abstain.md`, Theorem 5.1; Ab2 in the note; the rule and its plausible set are those of Cohen, Hutter and Nanda 2022). Its prices are computed in that file, for plausible sets with a parameter α (the hypotheses whose posterior weight is at least α times the largest, or the top set of Cohen, Hutter and Nanda). That the human is in the set is a theorem only for α ≤ δ·w(P), or α ≤ w(f) for a deterministic human (Proposition 5.2 there). The rule then abstains at every input that a test of about log₂(1/α) bits, which is at least K, separates from the asked ones (Theorem 5.3 there), and on the rehearsed system the bound on its abort probability is small only for n of the order of 2^K for a deterministic human and the top set, and of 4^K for a human who is not deterministic, times a polynomial in L, K and 1/δ (Theorem 5.6 there). With a moderate α, that the human is in the set is an assumption that the data cannot check (Corollary 5.5 there). For a human who stays on call, the threshold rule and the novelty rule (Ab1, Ab3; Theorems 4.1 and 4.4 and Proposition 6.1 there) trade silent errors against abstentions and questions.
7. *Rehearsing a mixture of task laws* (ADDED; finding m3 of the attack report). If rehearsal draws the top-level input from a mixture Σ_i π_i·D_i, item 2 applies to every D_i with B = 1/π_i; for all task laws with a description of ℓ bits the factor is about 2^ℓ, and for laws with disjoint supports it is necessary (`proofs/lower-bounds.md`, Theorem 4.1 and Proposition 4.4; S2 and N3 in the note).

**1. Guard.** *Name an input property that no training input has, in particular one that task decomposition creates. What does the scheme do there, and with what probability?* For a closed deployment of the mixture of a uniformly random round the answer is Theorem 3.2(a) (R7 in the note): for each set Q of inputs fixed in advance, the probability that the deployed run meets Q before any question met Q is at most L/n; for N such sets together it is at most N·L/n, so that all properties of up to about log₂(n/L) bits are covered at once (Corollary 3.2′). Task decomposition creates no property that escapes this bound, because the rehearsal run of round T is a decomposition carried out by the same imitator to the same depth. (The sentence read "creates no property that is new at deployment", and the note "creates none"; read literally that denies that the deployed run has a property that no asked input had, which is false, as the next sentence says. Finding N12 of the second check.) The statement is not "for every property at once", which is what the prover's table said: the set of inputs that were not asked is met with probability close to one when inputs seldom repeat (Section 3.1), and a guard chosen with the data in view, the researcher's F3, is covered only by R1, at the price K. What the scheme *does* at an input with a property that no question had is whatever the prior says: with a universal prior a deviant behaviour b on Q keeps posterior weight at least 2^(−K(Q,b)−c) (`research/understand/repo.md`, D1), and R1 bounds only the expected number of such calls in a run. *Where the candidate overclaims.* (i) "A guard on a property of per-call frequency ρ survives n questions with probability (1 − ρ)^n" assumes one frequency for all rounds. **3. Referent.** *Does a hypothesis that models how the data set was made do as well as the intended one?* On the asked calls it does at best as well, and a hypothesis that deviates from the human on the mixtures' runs loses weight at the rate of its deviation there: Theorem 3.2(b). **6. Rock.** *Does the scheme still act on the inputs that matter, or does it abstain everywhere new?* It acts everywhere: Protocol R has no abstention. The price is the other desideratum. Inside the closed system the unfaithful calls are not marked, and since the class may contain hypotheses that place their deviations where they hurt, the unfaithful calls have to be taken as placed and written by an adversary; the fault model of R3 does this. What is obvious is a failed validation, while the human is still there (for a deterministic human; for a human who varies, validation records mismatches for every imitator), and a top-level input outside a rehearsed support that is known and decidable (Proposition 4.4, item 4): at the top level the scheme is a whitelist of one task law. The theory also contains the opposite corner, which the prover's paragraph did not name (correction 2 of the check report). (The condition "if the human is in the set" and the bound on the test were made explicit in the second round, finding N16 of the second check; the note had "every input of a new simple kind", and for a person K exceeds the complexity of any input one would call simple.) **7. Horizon.** *Granting perfect per-call imitation, does the scaffold do anything?* No theorem about the learner can say. Three points bear on it, all against. ## 6. The budget

### 6.1 The factor K under low stakes: a lower bound for every protocol

`proofs/lower-bounds.md` proves the factor K for critical calls (its Theorem 3.1; N2b in the note) and the factor 1/((2m + 1)δ) under fault tolerance for a class with K ≈ log₂(L/δ) (its Theorem 5.3; the note's N5 is the variant with m + 1, its Proposition 5.6); it leaves the factor K under fault tolerance to this part (its Section 9, item 3). The two constructions combine. Theorem 6.1 is **N8** in the note. In this section m is the number of tolerated faults (k in the note) and m_b the number of blocks.

**Definition 6.0 (replicated block class).** Let m_b, N, B ≥ 1 and m ≥ 0 be integers. X := [m_b] × [N] × [2m + 1], Y := {0, 1}. For a ∈ [N]^(m_b) let f_a(j, i, u) := 1[a_j = i]. The class is {f_a}, with 2^K members, K := m_b·log₂N, and uniform weights. The scaffold draws z = (J, R_1, …, R_B), J uniform on [m_b] and R_1, …, R_B independent and uniform on [N], and makes the L := B·(2m + 1) calls (J, R_b, u), b = 1, …, B, u = 1, …, 2m + 1, whatever the answers. Unit b has value 1 if at least m + 1 of its 2m + 1 answers equal 1; the outcome is the largest unit value. So the human system of f_a has outcome 1[a_J ∈ {R_1, …, R_B}]. A deployed system fails if its outcome (which may be "silent") differs from this.

**Theorem 6.1 (the factor K under fault tolerance). NEW.**

1. For every a, the human system of f_a tolerates m faults with failure 0: a rule that replaces at most m answers of a run, by anything and seeing everything, does not change the outcome.
2. For every protocol (questions anywhere in X, adaptive, randomised; any deployed system, which may see z and may be silent), with n the expected number of questions when a is uniform on [N]^(m_b), and p := 1 − (1 − 1/N)^B:

  N^(−m_b)·Σ_a Pr[failure when the human is f_a] ≥ p·(1 − 2B/N − 2n/(m_b·N)).

3. If the failure probability is at most δ for every member and N = B/(2δ) is an integer, then

  n ≥ (1 − 10δ)·m_b·B/(8δ) = (1 − 10δ)·K·B/(8δ·log₂(B/(2δ))),  where B = L/(2m + 1).

4. Protocol R with the uniform prior has failure probability at most L·K ln 2/((m + 1)·n) for every member, so n = ⌈L·K ln 2/((m + 1)δ)⌉ ≤ 2B·K ln 2/δ + 1 questions suffice. The sufficient number exceeds the necessary one by at most the factor 16 ln 2·log₂(B/(2δ))/(1 − 10δ) (up to the rounding).

*Proof.* (1) The 2m + 1 calls of a unit have inputs in one cell (J, R_b), on which f_a is constant, so the human's 2m + 1 answers in a unit are equal. After at most m replacements a unit whose human answers are 1 still has at least m + 1 ones, and a unit whose human answers are 0 has at most m ones. The inputs do not depend on the answers.

(2) This is the argument of Theorem 3.1 of `proofs/lower-bounds.md` with cells in place of inputs; it is repeated in full. Let a be uniform and independent of the protocol's seed r, of z and of the deployed system's coins. Fix a block j, r and the coordinates a_{−j}. The *j-silent interaction* is the interaction of the protocol on seed r in which inputs of the other blocks are answered according to a_{−j} and every input of block j is answered 0. Let c_1, …, c_k be the distinct cells of block j at which it asks (at any replica u), in the order of first appearance, S := {c_1, …, c_k} and κ_j := k/N.

*Questions.* Let N_j be the number of questions the real interaction puts to block j. If a_j = c_i, the real interaction coincides with the j-silent one until the cell c_i is first asked, and by then c_1, …, c_i have been asked, so N_j ≥ i. If a_j ∉ S the real interaction is the j-silent one and N_j ≥ k. As a_j is uniform and independent of (r, a_{−j}), E[N_j | r, a_{−j}] ≥ (1/N)·(Σ_{i ≤ k} i + (N − k)k) = k − k(k − 1)/(2N) ≥ N·κ_j/2. Summing over j, Σ_j E[κ_j] ≤ 2n/N.

*Failure.* Condition on J = j, r and a_{−j}. For every value a_j = i ∉ S the data are those of the j-silent interaction, so the deployed outcome ô is the same function g₀ of (z, coins) for all these i, while the right outcome is 1[i ∈ set(z)], set(z) := {R_1, …, R_B}. For fixed (z, coins) the number of i ∉ S with g₀ ≠ 1[i ∈ set(z)] is |set(z) ∖ S| if g₀ = 0, N − k − |set(z) ∖ S| if g₀ = 1, and N − k otherwise; if N − k ≥ 2B each of these is at least |set(z) ∖ S|, because |set(z) ∖ S| ≤ B. Each cell lies in set(z) with probability p, and z is independent of (r, a). So Pr[failure | J = j, r, a_{−j}] ≥ (1/N)·(N − k)·p·1[N − k ≥ 2B] ≥ p·(1 − κ_j − 2B/N).

*Average.* J is uniform on [m_b]; so the average failure is at least p·(1 − 2B/N − (1/m_b)·Σ_j E[κ_j]) ≥ p·(1 − 2B/N − 2n/(m_b·N)).

(3) If all failure probabilities are at most δ, then n ≥ (m_b·N/2)·(1 − 2B/N − δ/p). With N = B/(2δ): 2B/N = 4δ, and p ≥ (B/N)·(1 − B/(2N)) = 2δ(1 − δ) gives δ/p ≤ 1/(2(1 − δ)) ≤ 1/2 + δ. So n ≥ (m_b·N/2)·(1/2 − 5δ) = (1 − 10δ)·m_b·B/(8δ), and m_b = K/log₂N.

(4) By (1) and Theorem R3 with δ₀ = 0, the failure probability of the run of M^(T) is at most β/(m + 1), and β ≤ L·K ln 2/n by R1 for a function. L/(m + 1) = B(2m + 1)/(m + 1) < 2B. The ratio is (2B·K ln 2/δ)/((1 − 10δ)·K·B/(8δ·log₂(B/(2δ)))). ∎

**Corollary 6.2 (what "whatever the horizon L" means). NEW.** Let δ < 1/10 and let N = B/(2δ) be an integer, as in Theorem 6.1(3). (For other N the necessary number is another: N = 1 gives a class with one member.) In the family of Definition 6.0 with this N the number of questions that is necessary and sufficient for failure δ is K·B/δ, up to the factor 16 ln 2·log₂(B/(2δ))/(1 − 10δ): it depends on L and m only through B = L/(2m + 1), the number of independent critical units of a run. Hence:

(a) with m = θL faults tolerated, B = L/(2θL + 1) < 1/(2θ), and the bound L·K ln 2/((m + 1)δ) of R3, which is at most K ln 2/(θδ), lies between B·K ln 2/δ and 2B·K ln 2/δ: it does not grow with L because B does not;

(b) the same B units without replication (m = 0, a run of B calls) cost B·K ln 2/δ questions by R1, and at least (1 − 10δ)·K·B/(8δ·log₂(B/(2δ))) by Theorem 6.1(3) with m = 0. Replication changes the sufficient number by the factor (2m + 1)/(m + 1) < 2 and the necessary number not at all;

(c) the human system without replication needs B answers for one run. So for work of this kind fault tolerance does not reduce the number of human answers per critical unit below about K/(8δ·log₂(B/(2δ))); it makes the comparison with the L answers of the *replicated* human system favourable.

*Proof.* Theorem 6.1(3), (4), and R1 for the scaffold with m = 0. ∎

**Remark 6.2a (the logarithm).** `proofs/lower-bounds.md`, Proposition 3.8 (it was Proposition A2 of the check report on that part; the editor of that part re-derived it) is a fault-tolerant version of the targeted-question protocol AB: for a deterministic human in a class of functions, if the human system tolerates m faults with failure δ₀, the set of inputs that may be asked contains every input that the scaffold can produce under arbitrary answers, and L/((m + 1)δ) ≥ 16, then 1 + 2.7·K·L/((m + 1)δ·log₂(L/((m + 1)δ))) questions give failure at most δ₀ + δ. In the family of Definition 6.0 the inputs do not depend on the answers, so the hypothesis on the inputs holds, and L/(m + 1) lies between B and 2B; so for B/δ ≥ 16 at most 1 + 5.4·K·B/(δ·log₂(B/δ)) questions suffice. With Theorem 6.1(3) the number of questions that this family needs is therefore K·B/(δ·log₂(B/δ)) up to a constant factor (at most 44/(1 − 10δ)), and the bound of R3 is larger than necessary by a factor of order log₂(B/δ).

**Proposition 6.4 (additive work: a lower bound that contains K). ADDED (attack report, finding m1; the proof was re-derived by the editor, and its numbers recomputed, Section 9, part 11, block 4). It has this number because it was added after Proposition 6.3.** Let X = [K], let the class be all 2^K functions from X to {0, 1} with equal weights, let the scaffold make L calls at independent uniform inputs, and call a run *good* if at most m of its answers differ from the human's. Then the human system of every member tolerates m faults with failure 0. For every protocol (questions anywhere, adaptive, randomised, n questions in expectation when the human is uniform on the class) and every deployed answerer (which may decline; a declined answer counts as different): if the run is good with probability at least 1 − δ for every member, then

  n ≥ K·(1 − 2m/L − 2δ).

K questions suffice (ask every input).

*Proof.* A rule that replaces at most m answers leaves at most m answers that differ from the human's; this is the tolerance. Let the human f be uniform on the class and independent of all coins. The transcript of the protocol is a function of its seed and of the values of f at the asked inputs, so given the transcript the values of f at the inputs that were not asked are independent fair bits. The inputs of the deployed run are independent of f and of the transcript, and the deployed answer at a call is a function of the transcript, of coins and of the run so far, none of which depends on the values of f at inputs that were not asked. So at every call whose input was not asked the answer differs from f with conditional probability at least 1/2 (exactly 1/2 for an answer in {0, 1}, and 1 for a declined one). Given the number a of distinct asked inputs, the input of a call was not asked with probability (K − a)/K, and E[a] ≤ n. Hence the expected number of answers of the run that differ from the human's is at least L·(K − n)/(2K). A good run has at most m such answers and every run at most L, so the same expectation is at most m + δL, the failure probability averaged over the class being at most δ. Therefore L·(K − n)/(2K) ≤ m + δL. ∎

*What it says and does not say.* For additive work in the most favourable form (every call adds a little, any m wrong calls are harmless) the number of answers still grows linearly in K in the worst case over classes: with θ := m/L = 0.1 and δ = 0.1 the necessary number is 0.6·K, so fifty answers cannot serve a class of more than 2^83 candidates of this kind. The bound of R3 for this class is L·K ln 2/((m + 1)δ), which for θ = δ = 0.1 is about 69·K: the factor 1/(θδ) of R3 is not needed here, and whether it is ever needed for additive work is open (Section 10.1). The prover's file said of additive work "I know of no lower bound that grows with the amount of work"; that remains true of the amount of work L, and false of K.

*The readings of this subsection, with their quantifiers* (rewritten; correction 4(c) of the check report and finding M4 of the attack report). ### 6.3 Where the bound is hopeless, and what is known about K for a person

* **A year per answer.** With the researcher's estimate that a research-capable episode may have to last a year, K ln 2/(θδ) is out of reach for every K above a few bits and every useful θ, δ, and Theorem 6.1 and Proposition 6.4 show that there are classes of 2^K humans for whose worst members the order K cannot be improved by any protocol. The certificate alone, where it exists, costs hundreds of human-years.
* **An hour per answer.** The bound is within reach of one person's working life for K up to about 10³ to 10⁴ bits, and of a large organisation for K up to about 10⁷ bits if the pool objection above is accepted.
* **K for a person.** The notes do not estimate it. Two published figures indicate an order of magnitude when the prior knows nothing of the individual; neither is a bound on K, which is the description length of the whole kernel on the inputs of the closed system under the prior, and which could be smaller (Proposition 2.2) or larger (what is innate is not in the first figure). Landauer estimated the learned content of a person's long-term memory at about 10⁹ bits (T. K. Landauer, "How Much Do People Remember? Some Estimates of the Quantity of Learned Information in Long-term Memory", *Cognitive Science* 10(4):477–493, 1986); Bartol and co-authors estimated at least 4.7 bits per synapse (T. M. Bartol, C. Bromer, J. Kinney, M. A. Chirillo, J. N. Bourne, K. M. Harris, T. J. Sejnowski, "Nanoconnectomic upper bound on the variability of synaptic plasticity", *eLife* 4:e10778, 2015), which with the usual count of 10¹⁴ to 10¹⁵ synapses is 10¹⁵ bits or so. (Both: bibliographic data and the headline figure were seen in web search results by the prover, by both verifiers and again by the editor in this session (the dblp record of Landauer's paper and summaries that give "around 10⁹ bits"; the eLife pages of the second paper, with "a minimum of 26 distinguishable synaptic strengths, corresponding to storing 4.7 bits of information at each synapse"); nobody in this project read either paper. At K = 10⁹ and one hour per answer, K ln 2/(θδ) with θ = δ = 0.1 is 7.9·10⁶ calendar years of unbroken work, or 3.5·10⁷ working years.
* **What could make K small.** Only the prior. By Proposition 2.2 the relevant quantity is the complexity of the human's behaviour on the inputs of the closed system given everything the prior knows, and by Proposition 2.4 a prior can make it small only by excluding candidates. The theorems hold for every prior fixed in advance, so a prior built from all existing knowledge of people may be used; then the claim "K is small" is an empirical bet about that prior, and R1 turns into a promise only if the bet is right. The certificate tests the outcome of the bet at a cost that does not depend on K.

### 6.4 Sequential rounds, batches and parallel questions

**Proposition 6.3 (rehearsal in batches: the bound counts sequential rounds, not answers). NEW.** Let Protocol R be run in S batches of b rounds each (n = S·b): all rounds of batch s use the mixture M^[s] of the data of the batches before s, and the answers of a batch are added at its end. Under A1, A2, A3,

  Σ_{s ≤ S} E Λ(M^[s]) ≤ L·K ln 2,  so for a uniformly random batch s, E Λ(M^[s]) ≤ L·K ln 2/S = b·L·K ln 2/n.

*Proof.* Order the n questions, batch by batch and in any order inside a batch. Each question is a function of earlier data and of coins, and by A1 its answer is a draw from P given everything before, the earlier answers of the same batch included. Lemma 0.1 with ν = P bounds by K ln 2 the sum of E[a·KL(P ‖ M)(x)] over all questions, where M is the mixture of *all* earlier answers. All terms are nonnegative; keep the term of the first round of every batch, for which M = M^[s]. By (0.5) this term is E Λ(M^[s])/L. ∎

*Reading* (rewritten; correction 6 of the check report, findings m12 and m13 of the attack report). The bound credits sequential batches, not answers: it is the standard fact that feedback delayed by b steps costs a factor b (Section 11). Whether parallel answers are in truth worth less depends on the scaffold. The two extremes follow.

**Proposition 6.5 (if the inputs do not depend on the answers, one batch does the work of n rounds). ADDED (check report, Section 5.3, Proposition D; attack report, finding m13; the proof was re-derived by the editor).** Let the inputs of Π not depend on the answers (next_j, the decision to stop included, is a function of (z, ρ) and j). Draw n pairs (a_t, x_t) independently, each as (1[J ≤ len], x_J) for a fresh (z, ρ) and a uniform J; put all the questions to the human at once (A1); let M_t be the mixture of the answers to the questions 1, …, t − 1 in this fixed order, and deploy M_T for T uniform on {1, …, n} and independent. Under A2 and A3(iii), (iv),

  Σ_{t ≤ n} E Λ(M_t) ≤ L·ln(W/w(P)),

as in R1.

*Proof.* By A1 the answer to x_t is a draw from P(·|x_t) given all the questions and the other answers. Lemma 0.1 for the questions in the order t gives Σ_t E[a_t·KL(P ‖ M_t)(x_t)] ≤ ln(W/w(P)). The pair (a_t, x_t) is independent of the earlier questions and answers, and for a scaffold of this kind E[a·g(x)] = (1/L)·E_{Q_B} Σ_j g(x_j) for every answerer B and every g ≥ 0, because the inputs of a run do not depend on who answers. So E[a_t·KL(P ‖ M_t)(x_t) | the questions and answers before t] = Λ(M_t)/L. ∎

**Remark 6.6 (a chain needs as many batches as it has links). The statement and its proof are the editor's; the second check read them line by line and found them correct, which is their first independent reading (Section 12.4). The prover's file asserted the remark without proof, and both reports of the first round marked it as not verified.** Let the scaffold be a chain in which the input of call j + 1 is the list of the answers to calls 1, …, j. Let the human answer the list (c_1, …, c_j), j ≥ 0, with a string c_{j+1} of ℓ bits and every other input with a fixed symbol, and let c_1, …, c_L be independent and uniform (a human drawn uniformly from this class of chains). Consider any protocol that puts its questions in batches of at most b questions, the questions of a batch being fixed before any answer of the batch is seen. Then for every s with s·b < 2^ℓ, the probability that after s batches some answer has revealed a string c_j with j > s is at most s·b/(2^ℓ − s·b).

*Proof.* Fix the protocol's coins. A string c_{j+1} is revealed only by the question (c_1, …, c_j), so the revealed strings always form an initial segment c_1, …, c_i. Call a question a *lucky guess* if it is a list (c_1, …, c_j), j ≥ 1, asked in a batch before which c_j was not revealed. As long as no lucky guess has occurred, a batch reveals at most one new string, namely c_{i+1} through the question (c_1, …, c_i); so after s batches without a lucky guess at most s strings are revealed. Before a batch with i strings revealed and no earlier lucky guess, what has been seen says about (c_{i+1}, c_{i+2}, …) only that fewer than s·b lists were wrong, and each wrong list excludes a set of values of prior probability at most 2^(−ℓ). So, given everything seen, c_{i+1} takes any given value with probability at most 2^(−ℓ)/(1 − s·b·2^(−ℓ)) = 1/(2^ℓ − s·b). A lucky guess of this batch must contain c_{i+1} at position i + 1, and the questions of the batch are fixed before its answers; so each of the at most b questions of the batch is a lucky guess with conditional probability at most 1/(2^ℓ − s·b). Sum over the s batches. ∎

So for long strings a chain of L calls needs L sequential batches, of whatever size, under every protocol, as it needs L sequential answers from the human.

The number of sequential batches that a scaffold needs is thus governed by the depth to which its inputs depend on earlier answers: one for a scaffold of the kind of Proposition 6.5, L for a chain. Task decomposition is of the second kind, since its inputs are written by earlier calls. With a year per answer, a programme of 20 years has at most 20 sequential batches whatever the number of people; for a scaffold as deep as a chain nothing more is credited, and the bound of R3 with S in place of n pays for K ≤ 20·θδ/ln 2 = 0.29 bits at θ = δ = 0.1. (The prover's sentence, "twenty years give twenty rounds whatever the number of people", was unqualified; it is false for the scaffolds of Proposition 6.5.)

### 6.5 The budget, in sum

The prover's summary split the budget test in two; after the two reports it splits in three (finding M1 of the attack report, correction 1 of the check report).

*Learning* by rehearsal, with a guarantee that covers a class of 2^K candidate humans, costs of the order of K/(θδ) answers for a deterministic human and K/(θδ)² otherwise. The order K is forced in the worst case over classes, for work protected by replication and for additive work (Theorem 6.1, Proposition 6.4). For a person under a generic prior this is hopeless at any length of answer.

*Checking a given imitator of a deterministic human* costs ln(1/δ_c)/(θδ) answers whatever the imitator and whatever K. It certifies an imitator and does not produce one, and the fault tolerance that it relies on is assumed (Section 8, O20).

*Checking an imitator of a human who is not deterministic,* which is the human of the problem statement, has no such certificate. Without A2 there are the frequencies of tests fixed in advance, at about 2·ln(2/(π·δ_c))/ε² answers for accuracy ε on a test of weight π (7.4·10⁴ answers for ε = 0.01 and π = 1), which do not bound the number of unfaithful calls; and, for a small answer set and questions put twice, a squared distance of answer laws, from which a bound on the number of unfaithful calls follows at the rate m^(−1/4) (Remark 2.6; the note states it in its Section 8.7). With A2 there is a bound on the number of unfaithful calls from single answers at the rate √(K/m), which contains K.

The prover concluded: "The affordable protocol is therefore: obtain an imitator by any means, rehearsal with an informed prior among them, and certify it; the theorem that carries the weight is V1 with R3, not R1." This stands for a deterministic human, on the untested premise of fault tolerance. For the human of the problem statement it does not stand: there, apart from the slow certificate for a small answer set just named, the only guarantee is the learning bound L·√(K ln 2/(2n)) under A2, which is a bet on the prior. (The exception was added in the second round, with finding N9 of the second check.)

## 7. What remains a bet

Section 13 of the candidate theory (V2, V3) says: rare situations are never sampled; what the imitator does there is fixed by the prior; a prior restricted to isolated hypotheses bets on the simplest continuation and is exact if the human is isolated; no data decide. This section makes each clause exact, and then treats "copy or predictor".

### 7.1 Rare situations

**Proposition 7.1 (on a set that the runs seldom meet, the imitator does what the prior says, and R1 is silent). PROVED.** Let Q ⊆ X, and let q be the supremum, over all answerers B, of the probability that a run answered by B has a call with input in Q.

(a) Under Protocol R the probability that some question of rounds 1, …, n lies in Q is at most n·q.

(b) Let the human be a function f ∈ H and let f_Q ∈ H be a function with f_Q = f outside Q and f_Q(x) ≠ f(x) for x ∈ Q. On the event that no question lies in Q, w_{D_n}(f_Q)/w_{D_n}(f) = w(f_Q)/w(f); the data would have been the same, with the same probability, had the human been f_Q; and at every x ∈ Q the draw of the mixture M_{D_n} differs from f(x) with probability at least w_{D_n}(f_Q) and from f_Q(x) with probability at least w_{D_n}(f).

(c) Let w′ be w with the weights of f and f_Q exchanged. R1 holds under w and under w′ for both humans. On the event of (b), the mixture under w sides with f on Q with odds w(f) : w(f_Q), and the mixture under w′ with the inverse odds.

*Proof.* (a) The question of round t lies in Q only if the run τ_t meets Q. (b) Both functions give every recorded answer probability 1. (c) R1 holds for every prior. ∎

So for q ≪ 1/n what happens on Q is the prior's opinion, and the theorems, which hold for every prior, cannot have one. In the class of all programs with a universal prior, the members that agree with the data and behave like a computable b on Q have posterior weight at least 2^(−K(Q, b) − c), the floor of `research/understand/repo.md`, D1; the candidate's "wrong there with probability about 2^(−sep)" should read "at least", and on another machine it is one half (D2). The stake of the bet on one set Q is small: the human system meets Q with probability at most q. But bets on disjoint sets add up, and nothing limits their number: in the needle class of N2 every input is such a Q, and the sum of the stakes is the whole failure probability (`proofs/lower-bounds.md`, Theorem 2.1). A protocol that chooses its questions freely can put one into a rare Q; then the limit is the number of such sets (same file, Theorem 4.1), not their rarity.

### 7.2 Isolation and patch-free induction in a closed system

`ultranatural/note.md` (Section 5, Theorem 9; read in the file by the prover, by both verifiers and by the editor) is for a deterministic function, inputs drawn independently from one law μ, and the distance d(f, g) = μ{f ≠ g}: f is δ-isolated if every other function within distance δ is more complex, patch-free induction puts the prior on isolated functions only, and after n samples the expected posterior mass off an isolated f is at most 2^(K(f))·(1 − δ)^n. In the present setting the questions are single calls of runs of the imitator, not independent samples, and what matters is the run, not the single input.

**Definition 7.2.** Hypotheses are functions; H′ is a countable class of functions with weights w. The *run distance* of two functions is D(g, g′) := Pr_{(z,ρ)}[run_g(z, ρ) ≠ run_{g′}(z, ρ)]. (The two runs differ exactly if g′ differs from g at an input of run_g(z, ρ).) A function f ∈ H′ is *δ-separated in H′* if every h ∈ H′ has D(f, h) = 0 or D(f, h) ≥ δ; [f] := {h ∈ H′ : D(f, h) = 0} is its *run class*. A member h of a class H is *δ-isolated in H* if w(h′) < w(h) for every h′ ∈ H with 0 < D(h, h′) < δ; H_δ is the set of δ-isolated members, and the *patch-free prior at scale δ* is w restricted to H_δ.

**Lemma 7.3. NEW.** Every member of H_δ is δ-separated in H_δ.

*Proof.* If h, h′ ∈ H_δ and 0 < D(h, h′) < δ, then w(h′) < w(h) and w(h) < w(h′). ∎

**Protocol R′** (the candidate's variant of R4, one posterior draw per run). In round t a function g is drawn from the posterior (the weights w on the members consistent with the answers so far, normalised); a run of Π is generated with g answering every call; J_t is uniform on {1, …, L}; if the run has a J_t-th call, its input is put to the human and the members that disagree with the answer are removed.

**Theorem 7.4 (patch-free rehearsal: exponential rate, the last posterior, all of the closed system; I1 in the note). NEW (it is Theorem 9 of `ultranatural/note.md` for the present setting).** Let the human be a function f ∈ H′ that is δ-separated in H′, and K := log₂(1/w(f)). Assume A1 and A3(i), (iii), (iv), (v); the sentence on the deployed run in (a) also uses A3(ii). (The prover's file listed A1, A3(i), A3(iv); the check report added (ii) and (iii): the weights must be fixed first for R_1 ≤ 1 in the proof, and the deployed pair (z, ρ) must be fresh. Part (v), fresh coins in every round, is used where the proof takes probabilities over the pair (z, ρ) of the round.)

(a) Under Protocol R′ with the class H′, for every n,

  E[posterior weight of H′ ∖ [f] after n rounds] ≤ 2^K·(1 − δ/(2L))^n ≤ 2^K·e^(−δn/(2L)).

Hence a run answered by one draw from the posterior after n rounds is the human system's run on the same (z, ρ) with probability at least 1 − 2^K·e^(−δn/(2L)); n ≥ (2L/δ)·(K ln 2 + ln(1/η)) questions make this at least 1 − η.

(b) If instead N independent runs of the human system are recorded, E[posterior weight of H′ ∖ [f]] ≤ 2^K·(1 − δ)^N.

By Lemma 7.3 both apply to the patch-free prior at scale δ whenever the human is δ-isolated.

*Proof.* (a) For a function h and a pair (z, ρ) let j*(h) be the index of the first call of run_f(z, ρ) at whose input h differs from f, and j*(h) := ∞ if there is none; D(f, h) = Pr[j*(h) < ∞], and the runs of h and of f have the same inputs at the calls 1, …, j*(h). Let S_t be the set of members consistent with the answers before round t, W_t := w(S_t), R_t := w(S_t ∖ [f]), and w_t := w/W_t on S_t the posterior. Let g′ be a second draw from w_t, independent of g, (z, ρ) and J_t. On the event E := {j*(g′) < ∞, j*(g′) ≤ j*(g), J_t = j*(g′)}, the run of g has a call number J_t, its input is the input of call j*(g′) of run_f, and g′ differs from f there; moreover g′ ∉ [f] up to an event of probability 0. So the expected weight removed from S_t ∖ [f] in round t is at least W_t·Pr[E | 𝓕_{t−1}]. Since J_t is uniform and independent of the rest, and (g, g′) is exchangeable,

  Pr[E | 𝓕_{t−1}] = (1/L)·Pr[j*(g′) < ∞, j*(g′) ≤ j*(g)] ≥ (1/(2L))·Pr[j*(g′) < ∞] = (1/(2L))·Σ_{h ∈ S_t} w_t(h)·D(f, h) ≥ δ·R_t/(2L·W_t),

the last step because D(f, h) ≥ δ for h ∉ [f]. Hence E[R_{t+1} | 𝓕_{t−1}] ≤ R_t·(1 − δ/(2L)), and E[R_{n+1}] ≤ (1 − δ/(2L))^n. The human f is never removed, so the posterior weight of H′ ∖ [f] after n rounds is R_{n+1}/W_{n+1} ≤ R_{n+1}/w(f). A draw from [f] has the run of f on almost every (z, ρ).

(b) A member h ∉ [f] is consistent with a recorded run exactly if that run does not meet {h ≠ f}, which has probability 1 − D(f, h) ≤ 1 − δ; so the expected weight of the members of H′ ∖ [f] consistent with N runs is at most (1 − δ)^N. Divide by w(f). ∎

*What it says.* For a separated human the 1/n law of R1 and R4 becomes exponential, the last posterior may be deployed (no random round), and the guarantee covers every run of the closed system, the rare ones included: with probability at least 1 − 2^K·e^(−δn/(2L))/η over the rehearsal, every later run fails with probability at most η. The law "more questions than critical calls over ε" of N2 is a property of classes closed under patches; a class in which the human is separated escapes it, with ln(1/ε) in place of 1/ε. Section 9, part 5, shows the two regimes in a toy class. For a human who is not deterministic there is a statement of the same kind for recorded runs (`proofs/hellinger-runs.md`, Theorem 5.1, which is H2 in the note, with H²(Q_ν, Q_P) in place of D and steeper weights); for single-call rehearsal there is none in this file (Section 10.1).

*What it does not say.* That a human is separated or isolated. Isolation is relative to the weights: by `ultranatural/note.md`, Proposition 8(5) and the remarks after Theorem 9 (read in the file by the prover and by the editor; they rest on its Theorem 3, which both know from the digest `research/understand/repo.md`, G4), a machine about sep(x; A) bits away regards a patch of f as the isolated function and f as its near-miss. And the bet has a price:

**Proposition 7.5 (the price of the bet when the human is not in the separated class). NEW.** Let H′ be a class of functions with weights, let the human be a function f† (in H′ or not), and ε† := inf_{h ∈ H′} D(f†, h).

(a) *Never learned.* An imitator that answers each run with one member of H′, chosen in any way that does not depend on (z, ρ), has a run that differs from the human system's with probability at least ε†, for every number of questions. With a prior that gives f† positive weight, Protocol R′ makes this probability tend to 0 (R4).

(b) *Visible in the end.* Under Protocol R′ with the class H′, the weight W_t of the members consistent with the answers before round t, which is the marginal likelihood ξ(D_{t−1}) of the data, satisfies E[W_{n+1}] ≤ (1 − ε†/(2L))^n.

(c) *Universality can be bought back.* For κ ∈ (0, 1) and any class H ⊇ H′ with weights w, the prior w″ := (1 − κ)·(w restricted to H′) + κ·w has w″(h) ≥ κ·w(h) for every h ∈ H, so every theorem that holds for the prior w with K holds for w″ with K + log₂(1/κ).

*Proof.* (a) Given the chosen member h, the runs differ with probability D(f†, h) ≥ ε†. (b) In the proof of Theorem 7.4(a) replace f by f† throughout and do not restrict to S_t ∖ [f]: the expected fraction of W_t removed in round t is at least (1/(2L))·Σ_h w_t(h)·D(f†, h) ≥ ε†/(2L). (c) Immediate. ∎

*The form in which the note states the price* (second round; finding N5 of the second check). The note speaks of a human f† that is a member h₀ of the class patched on a set which the runs of h₀ meet with probability ε, so that D(f†, h₀) = ε. Part (a) then gives the floor ε only if no member is closer to f† than h₀, that is, if ε† = ε; the note now says so. In a class whose members are pairwise at run distance 0 or at least δ (Lemma 7.3) this holds whenever ε ≤ δ/2: D satisfies the triangle inequality, because two runs that both equal a third are equal; so a member h with D(h₀, h) ≥ δ has D(f†, h) ≥ δ − ε ≥ ε, and a member with D(h₀, h) = 0 has D(f†, h) = ε. For larger ε another member can be closer (in the editor's script of the second round, Section 9, part 13, in 12 001 of 20 000 random cases, and in none with ε ≤ δ/2). The sufficient condition ε ≤ δ/2 is the editor's and no verifier has seen it; the note does not use it. The second clause of the note's sentence, that the data do not show the lost bet until a question lands on the patch, holds in general: until then every answer of f† is an answer of h₀, so the data, and with them the posterior, are those of the human h₀. Example 7.6 below is an instance in which the posterior has by then no doubt left, and part (b) says how fast the loss shows afterwards.

**Example 7.6 (the bet lost: silent, certain, then visible). NEW.** One call per run at an input uniform on [N]. H′ = {f ≡ 0, g ≡ 1} with weights 1/2; both are 1-separated. The human is the needle f† = 1[x = x₀], so ε† = 1/N. The first question outside x₀ refutes g; from then on the posterior gives f the weight 1, and the imitator fails with probability 1/N per run, with no doubt left in the posterior. Each round has probability 1/N of asking x₀; when that happens f is refuted, the class is empty, and the failure of the bet is plain. So for n ≪ N the price of the lost bet is a silent error at the rate of the patch, and for n ≫ N a learner with no hypothesis. The mixture over the class with all needles added (weights 1/4, 1/4 and 1/(2N) each) is also wrong at x₀ while x₀ is unasked: with probability 3/4 − 1/(2N) before any question, and with probability 1 − 2/(3N − 2) > 1 − 2/N once a question elsewhere has refuted the constant 1 (the prover's text gave the second bound without the condition; the check report added it). So at an unasked patch the two do equally badly, as N1 requires. They differ in three ways: the mixture keeps a weight of about 2^(−sep) on the patch, which is what the abstention rules Ab1 and Ab2 of the candidate theory work with; the mixture is exact once x₀ has been asked; and if the human is f ≡ 0 after all, the restricted class is exact after one question, while a run of the mixture still fails with probability about 2/(3N) until a number of questions of the order of N has been asked.

**Proposition 7.7 (the guarantee on average over the prior is free). PROVED (it is the defining property of posterior sampling; Section 11).** Let the members of H be samplers, let the human be drawn from the normalised prior w/W, and then let any protocol, whose questions depend on the human through the answers only, produce data D. Conditionally on D, a run of Π answered throughout by one draw from the posterior w_D has the same law as a run of the human system. So for every protocol, every event G of runs and every n ≥ 0, Pr[the imitator's run is in G] = Pr[the human system's run is in G], the probabilities being over the prior as well.

*Proof.* Conditionally on D the human has the law w_D, as the draw has; the run is the same function of the answerer and of fresh (z, ρ) and coins. ∎

This is the exact sense in which rare situations are a bet. A reader who believes that the human is a draw from the prior needs no theorem and no questions. Every statement of the note that is not this one (R1, R3, the limits N1 and N2) is about a fixed human, and its content is what happens when the human is not typical of the prior. N1 says that on unasked inputs nothing but the prior speaks. Whether to let a universal prior speak there (doubt 2^(−sep) at every new kind of input, slow learning, never certain) or a patch-free one (no doubt, fast, wrong for ever on a patch) is not decided by any data that either protocol collects: for every set Q that the questions miss, the two humans of Proposition 7.1(b) produce the same data.

**The candidate's V2** ("with a worst-case guarantee over a class closed under patches, the number of human answers must exceed the number of critical call types in a run over ε … low stakes is the only exception") needs three qualifications. The law is conditional on closure under patches (Theorem 7.4 is the case without it). "Low stakes" by replication is not an exception to the count per critical unit (Corollary 6.2). And the guarantee on average over the prior is another exception, which is why it should never be confused with the theorems (Proposition 7.7).

### 7.3 Copy or predictor: what the data can and cannot decide

**Proposition 7.8 (the data fix the kernel on the inputs of the closed system, and nothing else). PROVED.** Let P and P′ be samplers that agree on X_Π, the set of inputs that Π can produce. Then Q_P = Q_{P′}, and for every protocol all of whose questions lie in X_Π (rehearsal, validation, recorded runs) the joint law of the data and of the deployed imitator is the same whether the human is P or P′.

*Proof.* Every question is answered by the same kernel in both cases; induct over the questions. ∎

So no theorem about such data separates two humans who differ outside X_Π, or two mechanisms with one kernel. This is "one data law, at most one copy" and "behaviour, not mechanism" of this repository's second paper (`research/understand/repo.md`, F5(a) and F8, quoted from the digest).

**Proposition 7.9 (under A1 no predictor beats the sampler). PROVED (standard: likelihood ratios are supermartingales under the truth; in this repository it is Remark H.1 of Paper 2, item F1 of the digest `research/understand/repo.md`). Stated in the general form that the check report asked for.** Assume A1 and questions chosen as in Lemma 0.1. A *predictor* is a sequence of 𝓖_t-measurable sub-probability distributions q_t on Y: it may use the input x_t, the earlier data and anything else that is realised before the answer. A hypothesis ν is the predictor q_t = ν(·|x_t). Let Λ_n := Π_{t ≤ n: a_t = 1} q_t(y_t)/P(y_t|x_t). Then

  E[ln Λ_n] = −Σ_{t ≤ n} E[a_t·KL(P(·|x_t) ‖ q_t)] ≤ 0  and  Pr[sup_n Λ_n ≥ c] ≤ 1/c for every c > 1.

*Proof.* On {a_t = 1}, E[q_t(y_t)/P(y_t|x_t) | 𝓖_t] = Σ_{y: P(y|x_t) > 0} q_t(y) ≤ 1, so Λ_n is a nonnegative supermartingale started at 1; Ville's inequality gives the second claim. The first is the conditional expectation of the logarithm, with integrability as in Lemma 0.1 (if a term is −∞ with positive probability both sides are −∞). ∎

So a predictor that thinks about the human from outside, however it models the world (the researcher's F13), gains nothing over P in expectation and more than a factor c only with probability 1/c. A hypothesis that predicts as well as P has P's kernel at the asked inputs, and then Proposition 7.8 applies to it. A hypothesis that predicts *better* than P exists exactly when A1 fails: when the conditional law of the answer, given x and something else that the hypothesis can compute, is not P(·|x). Then the data identify the kernel of the data, not the person (`research/understand/repo.md`, F2 to F6: environment-driven coins). So the first half of the copy test is not passed by the theorems; it is assumed away by A1, and inside a closed deployment only the kernel matters in any case.

**Proposition 7.10 (the sampler and the sampler with its coins filled in). PROVED.** Let P be a sampler, and let the *frozen human* G be a random function with G(x) drawn from P(·|x) independently for every x, once and for all.

(a) For every protocol that never asks the same input twice, the law of the data is the same for the human P with fresh coins and for the frozen human. For a scaffold whose runs never contain an input twice, Q_P is the law of a run of the frozen human.

(b) If an input x with P(·|x) not a point mass is asked twice, the two answers differ with probability 1 − Σ_y P(y|x)² > 0 for the fresh human and with probability 0 for the frozen one.

*Proof.* (a) Induct over the questions: given the earlier questions, which were at other inputs, and their answers, G(x) at a new input x is independent of them with the law P(·|x). (b) Immediate. ∎

So whether the human is a sampler or a fixed table of answers is a fact about repeated questions, and it can be put to the test only by asking twice, which needs A1 in full. A scaffold whose inputs never repeat does not care. As for the *imitator*: the mixture of R1 has fresh coins by construction, so the question does not arise for it; it arises for a single selected program, and at weights 2^(−length) nothing in the data favours the sampler over its fill-ins (`research/understand/repo.md`, E11); a steeper prior does, for answers drawn afresh at inputs sampled independently from one law (E5, E8), which is not the regime of rehearsal.

**Summary (copy or predictor).** The data decide: the human's kernel on the inputs of the closed system, to the accuracy of R1; with repeated questions, whether answers are drawn afresh. The data do not decide: the mechanism; the behaviour off the rehearsed law; which part of a dependence on context belongs to the person, if A1 fails. The deployed object of the theorems is a committee that is redrawn at every call (R1), every episode (Theorem 1.7) or every run (R4, Theorem 7.4); in no theorem is it a program that resembles the human inside. For a closed deployment this costs nothing, since only the law of the run enters. Everything that the word "upload" promises beyond a closed deployment (consulting the imitator on new matters, running it on, trusting it where it was never rehearsed) lies in the part that no data decide, and there the only instruments are priors: the universal one, with its floor, or a restricted one, with its bet.

## 8. The objections

The claim under attack: *rehearsal is a human-imitation protocol that works exactly as far as any protocol can.* It has three parts: that what is imitated is a human; that the protocol works, that is, gives a usable guarantee from a reasonable number of answers; and that no protocol does better. Each objection is classed as **answered** (the theory as it stands contains the reply), **answerable** (the objection is right against the claim as worded, and a stated change of the protocol or of the wording meets it) or **fatal** (no change inside the framing meets it; the part of the claim that has to go is named).

The classes below are the prover's as corrected by the two reports. The check report (its Section 3) changed a sentence in O1, O2, O3, O7, O8 and the class or the answer of O5, O9, O11, and asked for two more objections. The attack report (its Section 2), whose task was to make the objections stronger, changed the class of O3, O5, O6, O8, O9, O11, O12, O17 and added five objections, S1 to S5, which are O18 to O22 here (its S1 and S3 are the two that the check report asked for). The editor examined each change and accepted all of them (Section 12); where the two reports differ in wording the stricter is used.

**O1. K.** R1 is a regret bound whose constant is K = log₂(1/w(P)). For a person under any prior that does not already contain the person, K is of the order of a description of the person, perhaps 10⁹ bits (Section 6.3), and the number of answers is K ln 2/(θδ) at best. This is not a weakness of the Bayes mixture: there are classes of 2^K candidates for which some member needs of the order of K·L/(δ log(L/δ)) answers under every protocol, and of the order of K·B/(δ log(B/δ)) under low stakes by replication (`proofs/lower-bounds.md`, Theorem 3.1; Theorem 6.1 here); and no prior makes K small for more than 2^k candidates (Proposition 2.4). Additive work is not an escape: on the class of all functions on K inputs every protocol needs at least (1 − 2θ − 2δ)·K answers (Proposition 6.4), so fifty answers cannot serve more than 2^83 candidates at θ = δ = 0.1. And while n is below K divided by the number of bits that a table needs per question, the imitator may be the table of the data and not the human (Remark 3.2a). The researcher's own remark applies: a constant "could of course be very large" (`research/understand/notes.md`, end of Section 5). *Fatal* to the claim that the protocol works from a reasonable number of answers, read as a theorem about a human. What survives: (i) the bound with K replaced by K_Π under a prior built from prior knowledge, which is a bet about that prior (Propositions 2.2, 2.4); (ii) for a deterministic human, the certificate, whose cost does not contain K (Proposition 2.5); for the human of the problem statement there is none from single answers, and from repeated questions only a slow one when the answers come from a small set (O18).

**O2. Critical steps.** There are scaffolds whose L critical calls fall on independent inputs, and simple classes closed under one-point patches, for which every protocol needs at least L/(4δ) − L answers (N2), which for δ < 1/8 is more than the L answers with which the human does the run. This is not true of every scaffold with L critical calls (the prover's sentence said "for a scaffold in which each of L calls can decide the outcome"; the check report corrected it): if one input is asked L times and the maximum of the answers is taken, every call can decide the outcome and one question suffices (`proofs/lower-bounds.md`, Example 5.5), and in a class in which the human is separated the count is logarithmic in 1/ε (Theorem 7.4). R3 removes L only for scaffolds that tolerate θL arbitrary faults. Where this tolerance comes from replication, the scaffold has fewer than 1/(2θ) independent critical units, and in the family for which this is proved the answers needed per unit are unchanged (Corollary 6.2). So on such scaffolds and classes rehearsal with a worst-case guarantee amplifies human work inside one run only for work in which no single step matters, or in which wrong steps are caught and redone at bounded cost. A proof whose every lemma is needed is not such work unless each lemma is checked mechanically. *Fatal* to the hope that imitation plus decomposition yields long chains of critical reasoning at a cost below the human's own; *answered* as mathematics, since N2, N5 and Theorem 6.1 are part of the theory. What survives: reuse of the same answers by later runs of the same closed system, within the limits of O21 (the guarantee is per run; and for one fixed question and a scaffold without randomness the human system of a deterministic human is a single run of L answers, so there is nothing to reuse); and verifiable or additive work, where the cost does not grow with L but still contains K (Proposition 6.4).

**O3. Short calls, few rounds, long episodes.** With an hour per answer one working life pays for about 1.4·10³ bits of K at θ = δ = 0.1; with a year per answer, for 0.7 bits (Section 6.2). The bound counts sequential batches, not answers (Proposition 6.3): with a year per answer, twenty years give twenty sequential batches whatever the number of people. For scaffolds whose inputs do not depend on the answers this is no restriction, since all questions can be asked at once (Proposition 6.5); for a scaffold as deep as a chain every protocol learns at most one link per batch (Remark 6.6); and task decomposition, whose inputs are written by earlier calls, is of the second kind, graded by the depth of that dependence. (The prover's sentence was unqualified; both reports corrected it.) The researcher's estimate is that an episode able to do research may have to last a year, and that trees of short episodes may be a "bureaucracy" that does nothing (`research/understand/notes.md`, Section 2.13). On these numbers the protocol is affordable exactly for the scaffolds whose value is in doubt. *Fatal on those numbers* for scaffolds whose questions depend on earlier answers; it stops being fatal only if scaffolds of short episodes do research, which no theorem about the learner can show.

**O4. Not a person.** The guarantee is for a closed deployment (Definition 4.1): a computation fixed in advance, whose real run is one more sample. An imitator certified for one such computation is not certified for the next use; on inputs that rehearsal did not meet, no protocol has a guarantee (N1), and what the imitator does there is the prior's choice (Proposition 7.1). The picture that motivates the problem, a copy that can be run on and consulted, is an open system. For it the theory offers the list of Proposition 4.4, whose items keep the human on call for ever, or pay a factor for every further law of tasks, or refuse every input of a new simple kind, or rest on an assumption that the data cannot check. *Fatal* to the reading "upload". What survives: imitation of the human *inside one computation that we can run in advance*.

**O5. Silent failures.** The researcher asked for an imitator that "basically only fails obviously". Inside the closed system the unfaithful calls of the deployed run of Protocol R are not marked, and they must be taken as adversarial. The theory of this project does contain a rule that fails only visibly, inside and outside the closed system: the minimum over a plausible set (Proposition 4.4, item 6; the prover's paragraph mentioned it in half a sentence, and both reports asked for it). What is impossible is the conjunction: to fail only visibly, with no assumption on the human beyond A2, at an affordable number of questions, and without refusing every input of a new simple kind. A rule that is never silently wrong for every member of a class closed under patches abstains at every new input (`proofs/lower-bounds.md`, Corollary 1.7; N4). The minimum rule that is sound by theorem refuses every input that a test of about K bits separates from the asked ones, and on the rehearsed system the bound on its abort probability needs n of the order of 2^K, or 4^K for a human who is not deterministic (`proofs/shift-abstain.md`, Theorems 5.3 and 5.6). *Fatal* to the desideratum at any affordable price without a further assumption. *Answerable as a bet:* with a moderate plausible set the rule is a whitelist, sound if the human is within a stated number of bits of the best explanation of the data, which the data cannot confirm (Corollary 5.5 there). Two weak forms hold: for a deterministic human the protocol as a whole fails obviously, while the human is still there, when validation shows mismatches (Proposition 2.5); and top-level inputs outside a rehearsed support that is known and decidable can be refused without the human (Proposition 4.4, item 4). A restricted prior that is losing its bet also shows it, in the marginal likelihood, but only after a question has landed on the patch (Proposition 7.5(b)).

**O6. A1 is false of people.** People remember, learn, tire and know what they are taking part in, and Example 1.1 shows that the protocol's data need not reveal a failure of A1. Memory inside a unit of work is repaired by rehearsing whole episodes (Theorems 1.7 to 1.9): the theorems then hold with episodes as calls, exactly for a deterministic human and in a weaker form otherwise. A human who changes over the weeks of rehearsal is covered only by a bound against the best fixed sampler, with a term that grows with the drift, and nothing is promised about the human after the last question (Theorem 1.11, Corollary 1.12, Proposition 1.14). Memory across the questions of rehearsal is not repaired for one person at all: the person who answers question t has answered t − 1 questions, and A1 for rehearsal then needs a fresh person for every question (Remark 1.1a, O19). *Answerable only by changing what is imitated:* whole episodes in place of calls, each of which is a datum, which feeds O1 and O3; and a pool of people in place of a person.

**O7. "Exactly as far as any protocol can."** False as worded, in five ways. (i) Rehearsal with the mixture is not optimal. In the comparison of worst cases over classes of K bits, a protocol with targeted questions saves a factor of order log(L/δ) for functions (`proofs/lower-bounds.md`, Theorem 3.3). On a particular class the loss is not a logarithm: on the rare-points class Protocol R needs at least 0.1·K·L/ε rounds, whatever it deploys, and a protocol that chooses its questions needs K (Proposition 3.7 there). (ii) The matching lower bounds are worst cases over particular classes of 2^K candidates with uniform weights; for a particular human and prior far fewer answers can suffice (Theorem 7.4; the researcher's own remark that K well-chosen examples teach a function, `research/understand/notes.md`, Q7). (iii) Under low stakes by replication the bound of R3 and the lower bound differ by the factor 16 ln 2·log₂(B/(2δ))/(1 − 10δ), which is 125 and 139 in the examples of Section 9, part 8 (the realised ratios there are 62.6 without replication and 124 to 127.5 with it) (Theorem 6.1); the logarithm can be saved by targeted questions (Remark 6.2a). (iv) For additive work the lower bound is (1 − 2θ − 2δ)·K, against L·K ln 2/((m + 1)δ) for R3 (Proposition 6.4). On that class the best protocol needs at most K answers, and Protocol R with a uniformly random round needs between 16 and 173 times as many in the cases computed, most of it for the random round: 31 356 answers against 790 for a round drawn from the second half of the rehearsal (O12; Section 9, part 11, block 4). (v) For a human who is not deterministic the orders agree (K/ε² per call, L·K/ε² for whole runs) and the constants of the upper and the lower bounds differ by two to four orders of magnitude (`proofs/lower-bounds.md`, Theorems 6.1 and 6.3, against R1 and `proofs/hellinger-runs.md`, Theorem 3.1). *Answerable* by saying what is true: there are scaffolds and classes of 2^K candidate humans for which every protocol needs at least a constant times K·L/(δ·log(L/δ)) answers, or K·B/(δ·log(B/δ)) under tolerance by replication, and on these rehearsal is within a factor of order log(L/δ) of the least possible number; on other classes it can lose a factor of order L/ε to chosen questions and of order 1/δ to a late round.

**O8. The human reads what the prior wrote.** In the early rounds the runs are driven by the prior, and the questions put to the human are built from its answers. For a deterministic human the expected number of such tainted questions is at most L·K ln 2 (Proposition 1.16). For K of the size of O1 this is no restriction at all: the theory does not exclude that every question of an affordable rehearsal is tainted. (The prover wrote that the bound "means nearly all questions of any affordable rehearsal"; that reads an upper bound as a lower bound, and in the block classes of the lower bounds nearly all questions are untainted. Both reports corrected it.) If reading such text can change the human, A1 fails; if it can harm the reader, the protocol is not safe (the researcher's D11 and F19), and with a class that contains every program its worst members write the text. Three variants in which the human sees no such text were listed by the prover, and the attack report examined them. Recorded runs of the human system (H1 in the note): the human carries out whole runs, so small scaffolds only. Runs driven by a rule that answers only where the weighty hypotheses agree: its price is exponential in K, and the theorem that the prover's source had in mind bounds something else (M. K. Cohen, M. Hutter and N. Nanda, "Fully General Online Imitation Learning", *Journal of Machine Learning Research* 23, 2022, Theorem 1: the expected sum over time of the cubes of the query probabilities is at most |A|·α⁻³·(24/w(π^d) + 12), where A is the set of actions, α the parameter of the top set and w(π^d) the prior weight of the demonstrator; read by the author of the attack report in arXiv:2102.08686v2, not opened by the editor; it bounds a number of questions only when the query probabilities are 0 or 1). A class restricted to hypotheses that cannot write such text: not a construction. So no listed variant keeps the three together. *Fatal* to the combination of a class of all programs, a large scaffold and one reader who must not be harmed, as far as the researcher's requirement that the output must not harm the reader is part of the claim. *Answerable* by restricting the class, which strengthens A2, and for small scaffolds by recorded runs. Whether an ordering of rehearsal reduces the number of tainted questions to order K is open.

**O9. A2 fails silently.** If the human is not in the class, or has weight zero, R1 says nothing, and Example 2.3 shows the protocol settling, with probability 0.44, on a member whose runs differ from the human system's half of the time, although another member is exact on the human system. *Answerable for a deterministic human:* never rely on R1 alone; the certificate needs no A2 (Proposition 2.5), and it shows a failure of this kind at any rate above about 1/m. For a human who is not deterministic the certificate with no mismatch cannot be issued, and the certificate of the number of unfaithful calls assumes A2 (`proofs/rehearsal-core.md`, Theorem 13.1(b3)); a failure of A2 is then shown only by those tests of V1(b) that happen to be in the chosen family, or, for a small answer set, by repeated questions. *Answerable there only as far as the chosen tests reach.* (The prover's class was "answerable: the certificate"; both reports restricted it.)

**O10. A3 in practice.** A call index computed from the run by a seed leaves a floor that no amount of data lowers (Example 3.3), and so do rehearsal runs taken from a generator with a short seed (Example 3.3a); a real task that is not a draw from the rehearsed law, or a date in the inputs, leaves the guard unrefuted (Examples 3.4, 3.5); a prior that has seen the rehearsal data memorises (Example 3.6). *Answerable* by design, and each remedy can be checked by the designer: physical randomness for every coin of the protocol (the asked index, the top-level input and the scaffold's bits of every rehearsal and validation run, the imitator's draws; the prover's list had "for J_t" only, and the attack report added the rest); inputs that carry nothing a rehearsal input could not carry; the real task rehearsed itself or drawn from D₀; the prior fixed before the first coin.

**O11. Hypotheses that model the experiment or act with intent.** They cannot break R1, which holds for every class and prior; they cannot aim at unasked calls (Theorem 3.2(a), (b)); and the one that knows the realised coins has, under a prior fixed first, at most the weight of a guess of those coins (Theorem 3.2(c)). That weight is small against the human's only when the entropy of the protocol's choices exceeds K bits; where it does not, the tables of the data can dominate the posterior (Remark 3.2a). What these hypotheses change besides is that the remaining unfaithful calls must be assumed to be placed and written by an adversary, which the definition of tolerance in R3 already does. *Answered as mathematics; in the regime of O1, where R1 is empty, the answer has no force.* (The prover's class was "answered"; both reports qualified it.)

**O12. A random round.** The imitator of R1 is the mixture of a uniformly random round, half of the time one from the first half of rehearsal. The prover answered: with probability 1 − η over the rehearsal and T its bound is 1/η times the average (Markov), and any single imitator, the last one included, can be certified by validation. The attack report showed that this is not an answer (its finding m2). The first half restates the cost, and the cost is real: on the class of Proposition 6.4, where every round asks a uniform input, the factor 1/δ in the number of answers is the price of the random round alone, because with probability t₀/n the round lies among the first t₀, in which the mixture is not yet trained (for K = 200, L = 100, m = 10 and δ = 0.01: 31 356 answers for a uniformly random round, 790 for a round drawn from the second half, 482 for the last mixture, 181 for the protocol that asks distinct inputs, and at least 156 for every protocol; Section 9, part 11, block 4). The second half is for a deterministic human only (O18). What does answer the objection is a late round: all terms of R1 are nonnegative, so for T drawn uniformly from the last ⌈n/2⌉ rounds E Λ(M^(T)) ≤ 2L·K ln 2/n, and the L/n of Theorem 3.2(a) becomes 2L/n; every guarantee is kept up to the factor 2 (also `proofs/rehearsal-core.md`, Proposition 5.2, Remark (ii)). The last mixture itself is not covered in general (Example 3.2b). *Answerable, by a late random round.*

**O13. The human of rehearsal.** A human who answers differently because no run depends on the answer is imitated as such, and the human whose answer would be used is not sampled by a protocol all of whose questions are answered in the context of rehearsal (Proposition 1.15(b)). *Answerable* by design: by what the human is told, or by a protocol in which the human cannot tell which answers are used. The variant in which every answer continues its rehearsal run (Proposition 1.15(c)) is not such a design, since that run counts for nothing; the prover offered it as one, and the attack report objected. What the right instruction is, is not mathematics.

**O14. The runs that matter.** If the valuable runs are a set S of probability q under the deployed imitator, the guarantee conditional on that set is weaker by 1/q (for the number N of unfaithful calls, E[N | S] ≤ E[N]/q, and nothing better holds in general): after a rare success, what the imitator does with it is constrained by 1/q times fewer questions. The human system pays the same factor, since it too needs 1/q runs per success, so the comparison between the two is unchanged. *Answered*, and it multiplies every number of O1.

**O15. Not a computation.** For the class of all lower semicomputable samplers the conditionals of the mixture are limit-computable and not computable (`research/understand/repo.md`, A9(iv)), so "run the scaffold with the mixture: this uses computation only" is false of ordinary computation, however long. *Answerable*: the theorems hold for every class; take one whose members are uniformly computable (programs with a time bound). Then A2 says that the human is in that class, a stronger assumption, and K is relative to it. (The researcher's own setting sometimes grants more than computation, "if you had a hypercomputer", `research/understand/notes.md`, Section 1.1; with that grant the objection is answered.)

**O16. Additive slack.** A guarantee in total variation leaves a slack that an adversary can put on the worst event. R1 bounds KL(P ‖ M), the direction that makes M cover P, not the one that keeps M inside P. *Answered*, as far as it goes: R3 assumes the worst content for the unfaithful calls; for an event of runs that is fixed in advance and that the human system never produces, the deployed run produces it with probability at most L·K ln 2/n in expectation, a linear rate (`proofs/hellinger-runs.md`, Theorem 3.1(c); the prover quoted the constant 4, which the edition of that part improved to 1); a multiplicative guarantee for every event needs abstention, at a number of questions that is exponential in K (Ab2; Proposition 4.4, item 6).

**O17. The human system.** Whether a tree of calls answered by the human does anything useful, and whether a faithful imitation of it is safe, are questions about the human and the scaffold. The theorems transfer whatever the human system does, including its failures. *Outside the theorems.* It is nevertheless the premise of the only regime in which the budget is not hopeless: low stakes is a property of the human system (O20), and the affordable scaffolds are those of short calls, whose usefulness the researcher doubts (O3).

**O18. The human of the problem statement is a sampler, and for a sampler the protocol has no certificate** (attack report, S1 and finding M1; the check report asked for the same objection). Every favourable number of this file (K ln 2/(θδ) answers, L·K ln 2/n unfaithful calls, the 300 answers of the certificate) is for a function. For a sampler the count under low stakes is K ln 2/(2θ²δ²), and the order K/θ² is needed in an example (`proofs/rehearsal-core.md`, Proposition 9.3); whole-run closeness costs of the order of L·K/ε²; and no check that is free of K bounds the number of unfaithful calls, except repeated questions when the set of answers is small (Remark 2.6; `proofs/rehearsal-core.md`, Proposition 13.5, at a cost of the order of |Y|·ln(1/δ_c)/ε⁴ answers for accuracy ε in β(A)/L; the attack report's count for the estimator of Remark 2.6 is |Y|²·ln(2/δ_c)/(2ε⁴) inputs with two answers each). Without a certificate, "obtain an imitator by any means and certify it" (the prover's Section 6.5) does not exist: what an imitator obtained by other means does on its own runs can be compared with the human only test by test, and a comparison of single calls test by test says nothing about how the calls of a run hang together. A learning guarantee then rests on R1, that is on A2 and on K. *Fatal* to "works as a certificate" for a human whose two answers to one question differ with a probability above about θδ/ln(1/δ_c); *answerable* for answers from a small set, at that cost; *answered* for a deterministic human.

**O19. Rehearsal on the real task puts one person to work on that task n times** (attack report, S2). The closed system that Section 4.2 lists first is one fixed research question with rehearsal runs on the question itself. Then every rehearsal question is a piece of work on the real task, written in part by the imitator, and the person who answers question t has answered t − 1 such questions. A1 fails by the design of the protocol, and the human learns the task from the questions; without low stakes, where n exceeds L, the person answers more calls than the human system has. The repair is a pool: one fresh person per question (Remark 1.1a). Its price is that the imitated object is a system of strangers who pass notes, and that K is the complexity of the pool's kernel. *Answerable by a pool, at that price.*

**O20. The premise of R3 is never tested, and R3 is needed exactly when the premise cannot be tested** (attack report, S3; the check report asked for the same objection). The low-stakes theorem assumes that the human system tolerates m arbitrarily replaced answers. The protocol never observes a run of the human system. A test of tolerance would need whole runs carried out by the human, with answers replaced by every rule; the budget excludes that, and no finite sample covers every rule. There are two cases. If it can be decided by computation whether a run is good, the probability that the imitator's run is good can be estimated by running the imitator, with no human answer and no theorem about imitation; R3 then predicts and does not certify. If it cannot be decided, the guarantee of R3 inherits δ₀ and m from an assumption about the human at inputs that the human system reaches only after faults. Proposition R3a of the note has that form: "from every history, an answer of the human raises Φ" is a statement about the human at histories that only wrong answers produce. Where tolerance follows from the structure of the scaffold alone (replication with a majority, as in Definition 6.0) it can be proved. *Outside the theorems,* like O17: provable for structural tolerance, otherwise a bet; and it is the premise of the one regime in which the budget is not hopeless.

**O21. Reuse does not amplify what must not fail** (attack report, S4). Reuse is what survives O2: the same answers serve every later run. But a deployed imitator that is used for R runs has R·β unfaithful calls in expectation, each to be taken as written by an adversary (O11). By R1 the guarantee for a deterministic human is one expected unfaithful call per n/(K ln 2) calls of the imitator, which is fewer calls than the human answered as soon as K exceeds 1.44 bits; and on the needle class of N2 matched to n and L no protocol guarantees fewer than one failed run in about 4·(n + L) calls (`proofs/lower-bounds.md`, Theorem 2.1 and Corollary 2.2: n ≥ L/(4δ) − L). Reuse pays when every single run is low stakes and the failures of the runs are separately tolerable. It does not pay when one bad output among the R can do the harm (the researcher's F19); the same file, Proposition 7.2 (N2d in the note), states this for a budget F of failed runs among r: on the needle classes every protocol replaces at most 4F/(1 − 4F/r) human answers per answer spent. Selection has the same form: if the user keeps the best-looking of R runs, the kept run can be unfaithful with probability up to 1 − (1 − β′)^R, where β′ is the probability that a run is unfaithful (O14). *Answered by the theory; it limits what "reuse" may be said to rescue.*

**O22. Break-even** (attack report, S5). For one run and a deterministic human, rehearsal with the bound of R3 costs K ln 2/(θδ) answers plus the ln(1/δ_c)/(θδ) of the certificate, and the human system costs L answers. At θ = δ = 0.1 rehearsal is cheaper only if L exceeds 2.4·10³ for K = 30 bits, 7.0·10⁴ for K = 10³, 6.9·10⁷ for K = 10⁶ and 6.9·10¹⁰ for K = 10⁹ (Section 6.2), and only for a human system that tolerates the arbitrary replacement of a tenth of its calls. The rounds are sequential for deep scaffolds (Remark 6.6), while a human system that is a tree can be run by many people in a time proportional to its depth. *Answered as arithmetic;* it turns O1 to O3 into one inequality that a reader can check.

**What survives of the claim** (rewritten). The prover's closing paragraph read: "for a computation that we can run in advance and a human who answers its calls without memory, n answers … give an imitator whose run has at most L·K ln 2/n unfaithful calls in expectation; in the worst case over 2^K candidate humans no protocol needs fewer answers by more than a logarithmic factor; a given imitator can be certified at a cost that does not depend on K". As worded this is not a theorem (finding M4 of the attack report, correction 8 of the check report): it drops the hypothesis of a deterministic human twice, A2 and the random round once, and the quantifier of its sentence on optimality, which is proved for particular classes and false of others. The version that is true:

*For a computation that we can run in advance, a human who answers its calls without memory and is one of the weighted candidates, and fresh coins throughout: n answers to single calls drawn from the imitator's own runs give, for the mixture of a uniformly random round, a run with at most L·K ln 2/n unfaithful calls in expectation if the human is deterministic, and at most L·√(K ln 2/(2n)) otherwise. There are scaffolds and classes of 2^K candidates for which every protocol whose run fails with probability at most δ ≤ 1/20 for every candidate needs, in expectation over a uniform candidate, at least K·L/(16δ·log₂(L/δ)) answers, and at least K·B/(16δ·log₂(B/δ)) when faults are tolerated by replication. A given imitator of a deterministic human can be certified at a cost that does not contain K, if it is good enough to pass. The claim offers nothing outside that computation, where only the prices of Proposition 4.4 are on offer; nothing at rates below about 1/n; and nothing for a human other than the one that answered.*

(Three sentences of this paragraph were made exact in the second round, finding N11 of the second check. The first had "a constant times K·L/(δ·log(L/δ))" with δ undefined and without the range of δ: the constant 1/16 follows from `proofs/lower-bounds.md`, Theorem 3.1, and for replication from Theorem 6.1(3) here, since (1 − 10δ)/8 ≥ 1/16 for δ ≤ 1/20 and 0 < log₂(L/(2δ)) ≤ log₂(L/δ), and likewise with B for L (checked on a grid, Section 9, part 13, block 2). The second said that an imitator "can be certified" without saying that the certificate is issued only to an imitator that passes. The third read "Nothing is offered outside that computation", which is false of the project as a whole: Proposition 4.4 lists what is offered outside, each item at a price, and Theorem 7.4 gives a faster rate under a further assumption. The sentence is about this claim.)

The three parts of the original claim fare as follows. "Human-imitation": of the kernel of an answerer of single calls, or of whole episodes, who for the rehearsal itself has to be a pool of people, inside one computation fixed in advance; not of a person (O4, O6, O19). "Works": for a deterministic human, as a certificate at a cost without K, on the untested premise of fault tolerance (O20); for the human of the problem statement, a sampler, only as the learning bound L·√(K ln 2/(2n)) under A2, which is a bet on the prior, or, for answers from a small set and questions put twice, as a certificate at the slow rate m^(−1/4) (O1, O3, O18). "As far as any protocol can": on the block classes of the lower bounds, up to a logarithm; on other classes the mixture of a uniformly random round loses a factor of order 1/δ to a late round, and questions drawn from the runs lose a factor of order L/ε to chosen questions (O7, O12). The uncomfortable half stands with its quantifier: there are scaffolds and classes for which no protocol amplifies work whose steps are critical (O2).

## 10. What remains open in this part, and what was noticed in other files

### 10.1 Open

1. **Interleaved episodes and a human who is not deterministic.** Partly closed by the check report: for two episodes H²(Q_C, Q_P) ≤ Σ_i KL(Q_{C[i←P]} ‖ Q_C), and in general the factor is at most min(4m_e, L_e/2) (Theorem 1.9, Proposition 1.9a). Open: whether H²(Q_C, Q_P) ≤ c·Σ_i H²(Q_C, Q_{C[i←P]}) holds with an absolute constant c for any number of interleaved episodes (the searches of the verifiers reach 0.57 for the ratio to Σ_i KL_i with three episodes). A version of R3 for an episodic human who is not deterministic is also missing.
2. **Drift.** Corollary 1.12 is an upper bound for Protocol R with a static class. A lower bound that shows a best achievable error of positive order in K°·γ for a human drifting at rate γ, the right exponent, and what a class of switching hypotheses would give, are open.
3. **Without A2.** Example 2.3 shows that the Bayes mixture can destroy the member that is exact on the human system. Is there a protocol with single-call questions whose guarantee is relative to the best member measured on the *human system's* inputs? (With recorded runs, Pr4 of the note does this.)
4. **Low stakes for additive work.** Settled in part by the attack report: every protocol needs at least (1 − 2θ − 2δ)·K answers on the class of all functions on K inputs (Proposition 6.4). Open: whether the factor 1/(θδ) of R3 is ever needed for additive work (in that class it is not), and a lower bound with K for scaffolds as in Proposition R3a of the note.
5. **The logarithm in Theorem 6.1** is settled by `proofs/lower-bounds.md`, Proposition 3.8 (Remark 6.2a).
6. **Batches.** Proposition 6.3 is an upper bound. The two extremes are Proposition 6.5 (one batch suffices) and Remark 6.6 (a chain needs L batches; the editor's proof, read and found correct in the second check). A statement in terms of the depth to which inputs depend on earlier answers is open.
7. **Patch-free rehearsal.** Theorem 7.4 for a human who is not deterministic under single-call rehearsal; whether the exponential rate survives the fall-back prior of Proposition 7.5(c); how the scale δ should be chosen.
8. **Tainted questions.** Whether an ordering of rehearsal brings the expected number of tainted questions (Proposition 1.16) from L·K ln 2 down to order K.
9. **Closure through an outside resource.** A formal statement of "stationary and resettable" for a resource that is called during rehearsal (Section 4.2).
10. **A design in which the human cannot tell which answers are used** (Proposition 1.15, Reading): what theorem holds for it.
11. **A certificate for a human who is not deterministic** that bounds the number of unfaithful calls without A2 and without a small answer set does not exist for single answers (`proofs/rehearsal-core.md`, Proposition 13.4). Whether some other observable quantity suffices for low stakes is open.
