\begin{abstract}
Solomonoff induction is the standard ideal predictor for sequences. We study its analogue for supervised learning, Solomonoff function induction (SFI): a universal Turing machine, run separately on each input, reads the same infinite sequence of fair coin flips from its first bit and outputs a label or diverges; SFI predicts labels by conditioning the law of this random partial function on the observed input--output pairs. This law dominates that of every such machine, universal or not, and does not depend, up to a constant factor, on the choice of universal machine. The shortest consistent program, the program mixture, the mixture of product laws and SFI agree up to the complexity of the set of inputs, but there is no coding theorem for function programs. On every consistent data sequence, SFI's cumulative log loss exceeds that of every computable random labeller by at most the labeller's complexity plus a constant; with i.i.d.\ inputs and a computable deterministic target $f$, the averaged predictor has risk $O(K(f)/n)$. SFI cannot model fresh label noise on repeated inputs. Measured on its unnormalised predictive probabilities, SFI's cumulative loss does not depend on the order of the data: on a random cyclic permutation of $\{0,1\}^d$ revealed backwards along its cycle it pays about $(d-\log_2e)2^d$ bits, while Solomonoff induction on the interleaved sequence, with self-delimiting blocks, pays $O(1)$ bits of label loss in expectation. Hypotheses that see the list of previous inputs give the universal chronological semimeasure. A dominance theorem for computable reveal laws holds pathwise for deterministic laws and, under finiteness assumptions and up to the complexities of the horizon and of the input sets, in expectation for randomised ones. We also treat a coin example of Sam Eisenstat, out-of-distribution behaviour, computable versions and neural-network Bayes.
\end{abstract}
