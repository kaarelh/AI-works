\section{Proofs and remarks for the learning theorems}\label{app:learn}

Notation is that of \cref{sec:learn}; $\KL$ is in bits. For a probability $p$ and a sub-probability $q$ on $Y$: $\KL(p\|q)\ge-\log\sum_yq(y)\ge0$ (Jensen); and adding to $q$ one atom that carries its defect $1-\sum_yq(y)$ leaves $\KL(p\|q)$ unchanged and does not decrease $\|p-q\|_1$, so Pinsker's inequality gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:learn:pinsker}
\|p-q\|_1^2\le2\ln2\cdot\KL(p\,\|\,q).
\end{equation}
In proofs about $\nu\in\{W,\MF\}$ we treat each hypothesis as a deterministic partial function: for $W$ a program $p$ with weight $2^{-|p|}$ and function $\varphi_{V(p)}$; for $\MF$ a tape $\omega$ with measure $\Unif$ and function $f_\omega$. Then $\nu(D)$ is the mass of the hypotheses that extend $D$.

\subsection{Merging}\label{app:learn:merging}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{thm:learn:merging}]
\emph{Posterior given the past.} Fix $t$ and a history $h$ of length $t-1$ with positive probability (histories take countably many values). The event $\{h_{t-1}=h\}$ is $\{F\supseteq D_h\}$ intersected with an event that depends only on $(\xi_s)_{s<t}$: that the rule, fed the labels of $h$, produces the inputs of $h$. As $(\xi_s)$ is independent of $F$, given $h_{t-1}=h$ and $(\xi_s)_{s\le t}$ the law of $F$ is $\nu(\cdot\mid F\supseteq D_h)$. Since $x_t=s_t(h,\xi_t)$, $\Prob[y_t=y\mid h_{t-1},(\xi_s)_{s\le t}]=\nu(y\mid D_{t-1},x_t)$.

\emph{One step.} Let $p:=\nu(\cdot\mid D_{t-1},x_t)$, a probability since $\nu$ is the law of a random total function, $q:=\MF(\cdot\mid D_{t-1},x_t)$, and $Z_t:=\log(p(y_t)/q(y_t))$. At a repeated input both are point masses at the stored label and $Z_t=d_t=0$. Otherwise the conditional expectation of $Z_t$ given the past is $d_t\ge0$.

\emph{Integrability} (added to the notes after the referee's report). With $\log u\le(u-1)\log e$, the conditional expectation of $Z_t^-$ is $\sum_yp(y)\max(0,\log\frac{q(y)}{p(y)})\le\log e\sum_y(q(y)-p(y))^+\le\log e$. On every path the conditionals telescope ($\nu(\emptyset)=\MF(\emptyset)=1$), so $\sum_{t\le n}Z_t=\log(\nu(D_n)/\MF(D_n))\le K(\nu)$ by \cref{thm:learn:dominance}. Hence $\sum_tZ_t^+\le K(\nu)+\sum_tZ_t^-$, each $\Ex Z_t^+$ is finite, and linearity with the tower property gives $\sum_{t\le n}\Ex d_t=\Ex\log(\nu(D_n)/\MF(D_n))\le K(\nu)$.

\emph{Consequences.} $\sum_td_t$ has finite expectation, so it is finite almost surely and $d_t\to0$ almost surely. Summing \eqref{eq:learn:pinsker} and taking expectations gives the $\ell_1$ bound.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Online-to-batch and noise}\label{app:learn:batch}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{thm:learn:batch}]
(a) Labels are defined almost surely since $\mu(\dom\varphi)=1$. As $x_{t+1}\sim\mu$ is independent of $D_t$, $\Ex R(Q_t)=\Ex[-\log\MF(y_{t+1}\mid D_t,x_{t+1})]$ (a repeated input contributes~$0$). Summing over $t<n$ gives $\Ex\LSFI(D_n)\le K(\varphi)+c$ (\cref{thm:learn:dominance}(b)). By convexity of $-\log$, $R(\bar Q_n)\le\frac1n\sum_{t<n}R(Q_t)$. If $\bar Q_n(\varphi(x)\mid x)>\frac12$, then $\varphi(x)$ is the unique maximiser; elsewhere the loss is at least one bit, so Markov's inequality bounds that set's $\mu$-mass by $R(\bar Q_n)$.

(b) Stamped inputs are distinct, so $\MF(\tilde D_n)=\prod_{t\le n}q_t(y_t\mid x_t)$. Given $\tilde D_{t-1}$ and $x_t$, $y_t\sim P(\cdot\mid x_t)$; so $Z_t:=\log(P(y_t\mid x_t)/q_t(y_t\mid x_t))$ has conditional expectation $\KL(P(\cdot\mid x_t)\|q_t(\cdot\mid x_t))$, and, $x_t\sim\mu$ being independent of the past, $\Ex Z_t=\Ex\,\Ex_\mu\KL(P\|q_t)$. The stamp-ignoring product law $\tilde P'(y\mid\langle x,t\rangle):=P'(y\mid x)$ is a machine law of complexity at most $K(P')+c$ (\cref{prop:setting:includes}(a)), so on every path $\sum_{t\le n}Z_t\le K(P')+c+\sum_{t\le n}W_t$ with $W_t:=\log(P(y_t\mid x_t)/P'(y_t\mid x_t))$.
\emph{Integrability} (not written out in the notes for (b)). If $\Ex_\mu\KL(P\|P')=\infty$ there is nothing to prove. Otherwise, as for $Z_t^-$ above, $\Ex Z_t^-\le\log e$ and $\Ex W_t^-\le\log e$; with $\Ex W_t=\Ex_\mu\KL(P\|P')<\infty$ this gives $\Ex W_t^+<\infty$ and then $\Ex Z_t^+<\infty$. Linearity gives $\sum_{t\le n}\Ex\,\Ex_\mu\KL(P\|q_t)\le K(P')+c+n\,\Ex_\mu\KL(P\|P')$, and convexity of $\KL$ in its second argument (also for sub-probabilities) gives the bound for $\bar q_n$. A lower-semicomputable conditional probability is computable, since $P(y\mid x)=1-\sum_{y'\ne y}P(y'\mid x)$ is also upper semicomputable (as in \cref{lem:setting:total}); take $P'=P$.
For 0--1 loss, let $y^*$ be a Bayes label and $\hat y$ the maximiser of $\bar q_n(\cdot\mid x)$. As $\bar q_n(y^*\mid x)\le\bar q_n(\hat y\mid x)$, $P(y^*\mid x)-P(\hat y\mid x)\le[P(y^*\mid x)-\bar q_n(y^*\mid x)]+[\bar q_n(\hat y\mid x)-P(\hat y\mid x)]\le\|P(\cdot\mid x)-\bar q_n(\cdot\mid x)\|_1$. Apply \eqref{eq:learn:pinsker} and Jensen's inequality for the square root.

(c) The product law of a computable conditional probability is a machine law of complexity at most $K(P)+c$ and the law of a random total function; apply \cref{thm:learn:merging}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{prop:learn:freshnoise}]
(a) Two draws of $x_0$ with different labels have positive probability, and the second gets probability~$0$, because the stored label gets probability~$1$ in both conventions. (b) The labels at $x_0$ agree with probability $\sum_{j\ge0}\binom nj\alpha^j(1-\alpha)^{n-j}\sum_yP(y\mid x_0)^j$. For $j\ge1$, $\sum_yP(y\mid x_0)^j\le p_{\max}^{j-1}$, so the sum is at most $(1-\alpha)^n+p_{\max}^{-1}(1-\alpha+\alpha p_{\max})^n$. (c) If some $x$ with $\mu(x)>0$ has two labels of positive probability, (a) gives infinite expected loss for $n\ge2$, while $P$'s is finite. Otherwise the label $\varphi(x)$ with $P(\varphi(x)\mid x)>\frac12$ can be found by search, so $\varphi$ is partial computable with $K(\varphi)\le K(P)+c$ and $\mu(\dom\varphi)=1$, and \cref{thm:learn:batch}(a) applies.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The per-query mixture}\label{app:learn:perquery}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{prop:learn:perquery}]
(a) Each term $\prod_tP_i(y_t\mid x_t)$ satisfies the two inequalities because $\sum_yP_i(y\mid x)\le1$; mixing with $\sum_i\mm(i)\le1$ preserves them; lower semicomputability is clear. The product is symmetric in the pairs, and on distinct inputs it is the definition of $\Mprod(D_n)$. On consistent sequences the factors at repeats are at most~$1$, which gives $\Mpq\le\Mprod(D_n)$, strictly as soon as a term of positive weight has a factor below~$1$ at a repeat. A positive computable conditional probability (for $Y=\{0,1\}^*$, for instance $P(y\mid x)=2^{-2|y|-1}$) is a term with positive weight, so every sequence gets positive mass.
(b) $\mm(i)\ge2^{-K(i)}$ for an index $i$ of $P$.
(c) The product of the predictions telescopes to $\Mpq(y_{1:n}\Vert x_{1:n})/\Mpq(\epsilon\Vert\epsilon)\ge\Mpq(y_{1:n}\Vert x_{1:n})$. Given the past, $y_t\sim P(\cdot\mid x_t)$ and $x_t\sim\mu$ is independent of it, so the expected log ratio $\log(P(y_t\mid x_t)/q_t(y_t\mid x_t))$ is $\Ex_\mu\KL(P\|q_t)\ge0$. Summing and applying (b) to $P'$ bounds $\sum_t\Ex\,\Ex_\mu\KL(P\|q_t)$ by $K(P')+\sum_t\Ex\log\frac{P(y_t\mid x_t)}{P'(y_t\mid x_t)}=K(P')+n\,\Ex_\mu\KL(P\|P')$, with integrability as in \cref{thm:learn:batch}(b). Convexity of $\KL$ gives the averaged bound.
(d) Stamping makes all inputs distinct, so on every sequence the set mixture of the stamp-ignoring laws $\tilde P_i(y\mid\langle x,t\rangle):=P_i(y\mid x)$ equals $\sum_i\mm(i)\prod_tP_i(y_t\mid x_t)=\Mpq(y_{1:n}\Vert x_{1:n})$. The $\tilde P_i$ have indices computable from $i$, so this mixture is a machine law of complexity $O(1)$ (\cref{prop:setting:includes}(a),(d)); apply \cref{thm:setting:universal}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Learning curves, splits and leave-one-out}\label{app:learn:splits}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{prop:learn:curve}]
Before data, the predictive probability of~$0$ at either input is $w+\frac{1-w}2=\frac{1+w}2$. After $(a,0)$ the consistent hypotheses are $\varphi$ and $(0,1)$, and the predictive probability of~$0$ at $b$ is $\frac{w}{w+(1-w)/2}=\frac{2w}{1+w}$; symmetrically after $(b,0)$. After $t\ge1$ samples both inputs have been seen with probability $1-2^{1-t}$ (risk~$0$) and one with probability $2^{1-t}$ (risk $\frac12\log\frac{1+w}{2w}$, as the unseen input has probability $\frac12$). $r_1>r_0$ is equivalent to $(1+w)^3>8w$; the left side minus the right is decreasing on $[0,\frac15]$ and equals $0.128$ at $\frac15$. Finally $\sum_tr_t=r_0+2r_1=\log\frac1w$.
\end{proof}

\emph{Identity of \cref{rem:learn:lastiterate}.} With $x_{n+1}$ an independent sample, $\Ex R(Q_n)=\Ex[-\log Q_n(\varphi(x_{n+1})\mid x_{n+1})]$. SFI sees its data only as a set and $(x_1,\dots,x_{n+1})$ is exchangeable, so the same expectation results with any sample $i$ held out; average over $i$.

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{prop:learn:splits}]
In every order the step losses sum to $-\log(\nu(f|_B)/\nu(\emptyset))\le L_\nu(f|_B)$ (\cref{prop:setting:order}, $\nu(\emptyset)\le1$). The split point is uniform and independent of the order; divide by $|B|$. A shortest program consistent with $f|_B$ is a term of $W(f|_B)$, so $L_W\le\KF$, and $L_{\MF}\le L_W+c$ (\cref{thm:versions:chain}). For the error use $e\le-\ln(1-e)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{prop:learn:loo}]
$e_{\rm LOO}(x)\,\nu(f|_{B\setminus\{x\}})=\nu(f|_{B\setminus\{x\}})-\nu(f|_B)$ is the mass of hypotheses correct on $B\setminus\{x\}$ and wrong or undefined at $x$. A hypothesis in the sets of two points $x\ne x'$ would be correct and not correct at $x'$, so the sets are disjoint, and none contains a hypothesis correct on all of $B$. So the masses sum to at most $\nu(\emptyset)-\nu(f|_B)$. Divide by $\nu(f|_{B\setminus\{x\}})\ge\nu(f|_B)$; the last inequality is as in \cref{prop:learn:splits}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Typical separating properties and per-step error}\label{app:learn:typical}

\emph{Separation step} (the special case of \cref{prop:ood:hanni} needed here, proved so as not to rely on a later section). Let $G$ be decidable, $b$ total computable, $\dom D\cap G=\emptyset$ and $x^*\in G$. Then for $\nu\in\{W,\MF\}$, with $K(G)$ the least complexity of an index of a total decider of $G$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:learn:separation}
\nu\big(D+(x^*,b(x^*))\big)\ \ge\ 2^{-K(G)-K(b)-c}\,\nu(D).
\end{equation}
For $\MF$: $\{(x,\epsilon,b(x)):x\in G\}\cup\{(x,w,y)\in T_U:x\notin G\}$ is c.e.\ and consistent, hence a function machine with an index computable from those of the decider and of $b$; every tape whose $f_\omega$ extends $D$ makes it extend $D+(x^*,b(x^*))$. Apply \cref{thm:setting:universal}. For $W$: let $V'$ read a $V$-program $z$ for the two indices, then a $V$-program $p$, and output an index of ``$b(x)$ if $x\in G$, else $\varphi_{V(p)}(x)$''. Then $V(wu)=V'(u)$ for a fixed $w$; the map $p\mapsto wzp$ is injective and sends programs consistent with $D$ to programs consistent with $D+(x^*,b(x^*))$.

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{prop:learn:typical}]
(1) The prefix $u$ depends only on $x^*$, which is independent of the training inputs, and $x^*\in G_u$. Each training input lies in $G_u$ with probability $2^{-\ell}\le1/(2n)$, independently, so the probability is $(1-2^{-\ell})^n\ge1-n2^{-\ell}\ge\frac12$. (2) A decider needs $u$: a self-delimiting code of $\ell$, then $\ell$ bits. (3) On the event of (1), \eqref{eq:learn:separation} with $G=G_u$ and $b\equiv1$ gives $e^{\rm wr}(x^*)\ge2^{-K(G_u)-c}$. As $\ell\le\log n+2$, $2^{-\ell}\ge1/(4n)$ and $(\ell+1)^2\le(\log n+3)^2$. For computable binary $f$ take $b=1-f$, with $K(b)\le K(f)+c$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{cor:learn:perstep}]
(a) Let $\varepsilon_h:=\mu\{x:h(x)\ne f(x)\text{ or undefined}\}$. For a fresh $x^*$, $e(x^*)$ is the posterior mass of hypotheses not correct at $x^*$ (zero at a training input, where all survivors are correct). By Fubini,
\[ \Ex[e(x^*)\mid D_n]=\frac{\int_{h\supseteq D_n}\varepsilon_h\,d\nu(h)}{\nu(D_n)}\le\varepsilon+\frac{\nu\{h\supseteq D_n:\varepsilon_h>\varepsilon\}}{\nu(D_n)}. \]
The denominator is at least $2^{-K(f)-c}$ on every sample, as a shortest program for $f$ is consistent with $D_n$. A fixed hypothesis survives the $n$ independent inputs with probability $(1-\varepsilon_h)^n$, so $\Ex\,\nu\{h\supseteq D_n:\varepsilon_h>\varepsilon\}\le e^{-\varepsilon n}\nu(\emptyset)\le e^{-\varepsilon n}$. With $\varepsilon:=((K(f)+c)\ln2+\ln n)/n$ the second term is $1/n$ (for $\varepsilon\ge1$ the bound is trivial). Markov's inequality on the surviving bad mass gives the high-probability form. The posterior-majority predictor errs at $x$ only if $e(x)\ge\frac12$.
(b) Condition on the multiset of the $n+1$ sampled values; by exchangeability $x^*$ is a uniformly random one of the $n+1$ positions. If its value occurs twice it is a training input and $e(x^*)=0$. Otherwise the training set is $S\setminus\{x^*\}$ and $e(x^*)=e_{\rm LOO}(x^*;S)$, since SFI sees its data as a set. So the conditional expectation is at most $\frac1{n+1}\sum_{x\in S}e_{\rm LOO}(x;S)$; apply \cref{prop:learn:loo}.
For $f\equiv0$ the lower bound is \cref{prop:learn:typical}, as $e\ge e^{\rm wr}$, and the upper bound is (b) with $K(f)=O(1)$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Occam bound and chosen inputs}\label{app:learn:active}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{prop:learn:occam}]
Put $\varepsilon_p:=(|p|\ln2+\ln(1/\delta))/n$. A fixed $p$ whose disagreement with $\varphi$ exceeds $\varepsilon_p$ is consistent with $D_n$ with probability at most $(1-\varepsilon_p)^n\le e^{-n\varepsilon_p}=2^{-|p|}\delta$. Sum over $p\in\dom V$ and use $\sum_p2^{-|p|}\le1$. A shortest program for $\varphi$ is consistent, so $\KF(D_n)\le K(\varphi)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{prop:learn:active}(2)]
$f_a$ is computable from $a$ and $K(a)\le d+2\log d+c$; apply \cref{thm:learn:dominance}(b). Fix the strategy's coins. Until $a$ is queried every answer is~$0$, so the queries are those made on $f\equiv0$. Identifying $a$ requires querying $a$ or all other $2^d-1$ points, so at least $\min(\mathrm{pos}(a),2^d-1)$ queries, where $\mathrm{pos}(a)$ is the time of the first query of $a$ on $f\equiv0$. These times are distinct, so their mean over uniform $a$ is at least $2^{-d}\big(\sum_{k=1}^{2^d-1}k+(2^d-1)\big)\ge\frac{2^d-1}2$. Average over the coins.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Further remarks}\label{app:learn:remarks}

\emph{Heuristic for \cref{conj:learn:loo}} (not proved). The natural one-exception hypotheses ``$f$ except at $x$'' have weight about $2^{-K(f)-K(x\mid f)}$, and $\sum_x2^{-K(x\mid f)}\le1$. A counterexample needs many more than $K(f)$ points $x$ whose one-point flips $f^x$ have $K(f^x)\lesssim K(f)$; natural attempts (halting-set indicators, pseudo-random seeds, thresholds) give only $O(1)$ or $O(K(f))$ such points.

\begin{remark}[learning curves can increase]\label{rem:learn:increasing}
\status{computed (context c3 Part 2)}\src{context Rem 2.11}
Take a Bayes mixture of the truth $f$ (weight $w_f$) and $|B|$ hypotheses each wrong at exactly one point of $B$ (weight $w$ each). Sampling without replacement, the loss at step $k$ is $\log\frac{w_f+w(|B|-k+1)}{w_f+w(|B|-k)}$ in every order, and it increases with $k$. With $w=w_f=1/(|B|+1)$ and $|B|=16$, the last step (leave-one-out) costs $1$ bit, while the random-split average is $0.255$ bits. Each leave-one-out error is $\frac12$, within a factor of about~$2$ of \cref{prop:learn:loo}. By Kraft's inequality such weights $w=w_f=2^{-K}$ need $|B|\le2^K-1$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[a refuted comparison]\label{rem:learn:cesaro}
\status{refuted}\src{context Prop 2.12, X13}
An earlier version of the context notes read \cref{prop:learn:typical} as ``matching the $O(1/n)$ Ces\`aro upper bound up to $\log^2n$''. The comparison is invalid: it sets a per-step lower bound against an average over steps, which says nothing about the per-step rate. Indeed $\sum_k\Ex e_k\le(\ln2)(K(f)+c)$, so $\liminf_kk\,\Ex e_k=0$. The valid per-step comparison is \cref{cor:learn:perstep}.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[relations among the in-distribution bounds]\label{rem:learn:relations}
\status{remark}\src{context Rem 2.18}
(i) For computable $f$ and distinct inputs, $\sum_{k\le n}e_k\le(\ln2)(K(f)+c)$ on every sequence (\cref{thm:learn:dominance}(b) with $e\le-\ln(1-e)$; this is \cref{thm:ood:cumulative}). \Cref{cor:learn:perstep}(a) gives a per-step rate in distribution at the price of an additive $\ln n$; \cref{thm:learn:batch}(a) is the Ces\`aro statement for the log loss of the averaged predictor.
(ii) \Cref{cor:learn:perstep}(a) is the Markov half of the relevance-based bound in the Vaintrob--H\"anni slides \citep[Appendix~A]{vaintrobhanni2026nnbayes}, specialised to $W$ and $\MF$. The union-bound half and the slides' geometric length prior are not needed: long programs enter only multiplied by $e^{-\varepsilon n}$.
(iii) Leave-one-out is different. For a fixed finite $B$ and a random held-out point, the training set eliminates every hypothesis with two or more errors on $B$; what remains is the mass of one-exception hypotheses (\cref{conj:learn:loo}). If \cref{conj:learn:loo} holds, the proof of \cref{cor:learn:perstep}(b) gives $\Ex e(x^*)=O(K(f)/(n+1))$ up to logarithmic factors, without the additive $\ln n$.
\end{remark}

\emph{Comparison with other bounds} (foundations §6.6; the cited works were not checked against their sources). \emph{Occam} \citep{blumer1987occam}: \cref{prop:learn:occam} is a description-length form of the Occam bound. \emph{PAC-Bayes} \citep{mcallester1999some}: risk bounds with a $\KL(Q\|\text{prior})/n$ term; with prior $2^{-K}$ and a posterior concentrated on the target the term is about $K(\varphi)\ln2/n$, the same rate. The difference is in kind: PAC-Bayes bounds hold with high probability for a randomised classifier, \cref{thm:learn:batch} in expectation for the averaged Bayes predictive. \emph{MDL and the index of resolvability} \citep{barron1991minimum}: \cref{thm:learn:batch}(b) and \cref{prop:learn:perquery}(c) are Ces\`aro-average resolvability bounds $\min_{P'}[K(P')/n+\KL(P\|P')]$ in the conditional setting, for every input distribution. \emph{Expert advice} \citep{vovk1990aggregating,cesabianchi2006prediction}: under log loss a Bayes mixture has regret $\log(1/w_i)$ against expert $i$ on every sequence; \cref{thm:learn:dominance} is this fact with machine laws as experts, each predicting with its own posterior given past labels, so shared randomness is allowed.

\begin{remark}[positive-example induction]\label{rem:learn:positive}
\status{proof sketch; known (snippet)}\src{context Rem 5.5}
H\"anni lists ``positive example induction'', learning a set or a distribution from samples, among the ideal inductions he wants to define \citep{hanni2026notes}. In SFI terms positive labels $(x,1)$ alone are useless, because the constant-$1$ program explains them. The right formalisation is a sampling likelihood: the samples are the labels of the index inputs $i\mapsto x_i$, under index-ignoring hypotheses with private randomness per sample. The mixture is then $\sum_Pw_P\prod_iP(x_i)$ over computable distributions $P$ on $X$, the private-randomness analogue of the universal semimeasure on the sample sequence. It appears to be the object of the ideal learner from positive evidence of \citet{chater2007ideal}; their exact model was not checked.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[interventions]\label{rem:learn:interventions}
\status{remark}\src{context Rem 5.6}
If the inputs are interventions set by the experimenter independently of the system's randomness, x-only dominance (\cref{thm:learn:dominance}(a)) applies to the interventional law $P(y\mid\mathrm{do}(x))$. With observational inputs SFI targets $P(y\mid x)$ like any conditional learner. Nothing specific to SFI is proved here.
\end{remark}
