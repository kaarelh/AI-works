\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

In supervised learning a learner sees input--output pairs $(x_1,y_1),(x_2,y_2),\dots$ and predicts the output at a new input. For predicting sequences, Solomonoff induction (SI) is the standard ideal reference: a Bayes mixture over all computable sources whose cumulative log loss exceeds that of each computable source by at most a constant that depends on the source \citep{li2008introduction}. Solomonoff also posed the corresponding problem for supervised learning, \emph{operator induction}: given an unordered set of question--answer pairs, find the probability distribution of the answer to a new question \citep{solomonoff1999two,solomonoff2008three}. He gave two solutions with universal priors and proved a convergence theorem for one of them \citep{solomonoff2008three}. We call the family of such solutions \emph{Solomonoff operator induction} (SOI) and study several of its versions. \emph{Solomonoff's per-query solution} mixes computable conditional distributions of the answer given the question, with an independent factor for each question, so a repeated question gets fresh randomness. In the \emph{one-tape version}, our default (\cref{sec:setting:sfi}), a universal Turing machine answers each query $x$ by a separate run on $x$ that reads one infinite sequence of fair coin flips, the same for every query, from its first bit, and outputs a label or diverges; this defines a random partial function, whose law is conditioned on the observed pairs.\footnote{The one-tape version first appeared in the author's working notes \citep{hanni2026notes}.} The \emph{coin-free} and \emph{Kolmogorov versions} use deterministic programs only (their mixture, and the shortest consistent one), and the hypotheses of the \emph{history-aware version} \SFIhist{} also see the list of previous inputs. We ask how the versions compare (\cref{sec:versions}) and what they do (\cref{sec:learn,sec:hist,sec:ex,sec:ood,sec:comp,sec:nn}).

\paragraph{How much the version matters.} On distinct inputs, little: on data sets all versions but the history-aware one agree up to $K(\dom D)+O(1)$ bits, which is $O(\log d)$ on the full cyclic permutation of $\{0,1\}^d$, and the per-query solution equals the mixture of product laws there (\cref{thm:versions:chain,prop:learn:perquery}). In the semimeasure convention the lower bounds of the main examples bind every order-independent predictor, in expectation or up to its complexity, and so the per-query solution (\cref{prop:hist:orderfree,prop:hist:operator,prop:ex:orderfree,prop:ex:law}). On repeated inputs the versions differ: the one-tape version cannot produce fresh label noise, the per-query solution can (\cref{prop:learn:freshnoise,prop:learn:perquery}).

\paragraph{Why SOI.} SOI is a candidate for the ideal form of supervised learning. Its guarantees need no assumption on how the inputs are chosen (Theorem~A below), and with i.i.d.\ inputs and a computable deterministic target $f$ its averaged predictor has the Occam rate $O(K(f)/n)$, without a union bound (\cref{thm:learn:batch}). The analogy has limits, which \cref{rem:learn:ideal} makes precise: the guarantees are for log loss and for averaged predictors, fresh label noise defeats the one-tape version, SOI is not computable, and without the previous inputs it is blind to the order. SOI is also the natural setting for two further questions. The hypotheses of Solomonoff's per-query solution are samplers that draw private coins at each input, in which a model is kept apart from its noise. On such hypotheses, simplicity priors that charge a bit of program more than a bit of fit become meaningful: under these priors, in Solomonoff induction and among hypotheses with shared randomness, a universal mixture is itself one hypothesis and absorbs all structure, while no hypothesis with private coins is universal. The companion paper studies the per-query solution with such priors for two purposes: splitting data into a model and noise, and not learning a teacher's systematic mistakes (\cref{sec:steep}). And Solomonoff axiom induction, learning a mathematical theory from sentences labelled true or false by Bayesian inference over axiom systems, is closely related to the coin-free version of SOI on sentences, restricted to logically consistent labellers; we leave this connection to separate work.\footnote{Axiom induction in this sense first appeared in the author's working notes \citep{hanni2026notes}.}

\paragraph{Relation to Solomonoff induction.} Up to constant factors, SI is SOI on the inputs $0,1,\dots,n-1$ (\cref{prop:setting:si}). In general, though, all versions but the history-aware one see the data as a set: each hypothesis answers each query without seeing the other inputs (Theorem~C below). SI applied to the interleaved sequence $x_1y_1x_2y_2\cdots$ sees the order and can do much better (\cref{sec:hist,sec:ex}). The history-aware version removes the gaps of \cref{sec:hist,sec:ex}: \SFIhist{} is a universal lower-semicomputable chronological semimeasure (\cref{cor:hist:H4}), and its loss exceeds SI's joint loss on the interleaved sequence by at most a constant (\cref{thm:hist:si}); against SI's loss on the labels alone the comparison involves SI's loss on the inputs (\cref{prop:hist:inputloss}). Chronological semimeasures, with actions and percepts in place of inputs and labels, are the setting of Hutter's AIXI \citep{hutter2000theory,hutter2005universal}; we define \SFIhist{} directly and use no identification with Hutter's construction.

\paragraph{Relation to neural-network Bayes.} Bayesian learning with a neural network is function-space Bayes with the prior that the parameter prior induces on functions (\cref{prop:nn:functionspace}); the slides of \citet{vaintrobhanni2026nnbayes} argue that for small prior scales it behaves like a circuit version of SOI. \Cref{sec:comp,sec:nn} prove what holds in general about this link.

\subsection{Main results}\label{sec:intro:results}

\paragraph{Two loss conventions.} The predictive probabilities of SOI are sub-probabilities; the missing mass is the posterior probability that the sampled function is undefined at the query. The \emph{semimeasure convention} scores these raw conditionals, the \emph{normalised convention} rescales them to sum to one at each step. Upper bounds in the first convention hold in both; every lower bound says which convention it is for (\cref{rem:setting:conventions}). Logarithms are to base~$2$, losses are in bits, and $c$ is a constant that depends only on the fixed universal machines and encodings.

\paragraph{Theorem A (informal).} A \emph{function machine} is a Turing machine that answers all queries from one random tape, as in the one-tape version above, but need not be universal. There is a universal function machine whose law $\MF$, the prior of the one-tape version, satisfies $\MF\ge2^{-K(\nu)}\nu$ for the law $\nu$ of every function machine, including samplers that see only the input and partial computable functions. Changing the universal machine changes $\MF$ by at most a constant factor, and computable injective re-encodings $\alpha,\beta$ of inputs and labels by at most a factor $2^{K(\alpha,\beta)+O(1)}$. So on every consistent sequence of pairs, however the inputs are chosen, the cumulative log loss of the one-tape version is at most that of the random labeller $\nu$ plus $K(\nu)$ (\cref{thm:setting:universal,prop:setting:includes,thm:setting:invariance,thm:learn:dominance}). The per-query solution has the analogous bound against samplers that see only the input, even on inconsistent sequences; its expected squared-error form is Solomonoff's convergence theorem (\cref{prop:learn:perquery}; \citealp[eq.~(12)]{solomonoff2008three}).

\paragraph{Theorem B (informal).} For every $d\ge2$ there are two inputs $x_1,x_2\in\{0,1\}^d$ such that every program that outputs $0$ on $x_1$ and $1$ on $x_2$ has length at least $\log d-2$, while these programs together have algorithmic probability at least $2^{-O(\log\log d)}$ and the loss of the one-tape version on the two labels is $O(1)$. So there is no coding theorem for function programs, and no two of the Kolmogorov, coin-free and one-tape versions agree up to a constant, although these, the mixture of product laws and the universal weak function semimeasure agree up to $K(\dom D)+O(1)$ bits on every data set $D$
(\cref{thm:versions:nocoding,prop:versions:Wgap,thm:versions:chain}).

\paragraph{Theorem C (informal).} In the semimeasure convention the cumulative log loss of SOI depends only on the set of revealed pairs, not on their order (\cref{prop:setting:order}). So SOI cannot use information that only the order carries: if $x_1,x_2,\dots$ are the consecutive blocks, of lengths $1,2,3,\dots$, of a Martin-L\"of random sequence, revealed as $(x_1,\epsilon),(x_2,x_1),\dots,(x_n,x_{n-1})$, SOI pays $n(n-1)/2\pm O(\log n)$ bits, and every lower-semicomputable order-independent predictor as much up to its complexity, while a predictor of constant complexity that sees the previous pairs pays nothing (\cref{thm:hist:chain,prop:hist:orderfree}). The history-aware version \SFIhist{} is a universal lower-semicomputable chronological semimeasure: on every sequence its loss exceeds that of every lower-semicomputable history-aware predictor $P$ by at most $K(P)+O(1)$, and that of SI on the interleaved sequence by at most a constant (\cref{cor:hist:H4,thm:hist:si}).

\paragraph{Theorem D (informal).} Let $f$ be a uniformly random cyclic permutation of $\{0,1\}^d$, $N:=2^d$, revealed backwards along its cycle as $(x_1,0^d),(x_2,x_1),\dots$ with $x_k:=f^{-1}(x_{k-1})$ and $x_0:=0^d$, so that every label is the previous input. In the semimeasure convention SOI pays at most $\log((N-1)!)+O(1)\approx(d-\log e)N$ bits over the cycle, and with high probability at least $\log((N-1)!)-O(1)$; no order-independent predictor pays less in expectation. In the normalised convention it pays in expectation at least $\delta d-O(\log d)$ bits per step on average over the first $2^{(1-\delta)d}$ steps. SI on the interleaved sequence, with self-delimiting blocks, pays $O(1)$ bits of label loss in expectation, and \SFIhist{} $O(1)$ bits on every cycle, uniformly in $d$ (\cref{thm:ex:cycle,prop:ex:orderfree,prop:ex:normalised,prop:ex:cycsi,prop:ex:cychist}). In Sam Eisenstat's coin example (each index $i\ge0$ is labelled $1$ independently with probability $\frac13$, and each step reveals the smallest unrevealed index labelled $1$ with probability $\frac23$, else the smallest one labelled $0$), the expected loss of SOI is $\frac{H(1/3)}2n\pm O(\log n)$ bits in the semimeasure convention (and almost surely within $\log n+O(\log\log n)$ bits of the loss of the threshold hypothesis), which is optimal among order-independent predictors up to $O(\log n)$ in expectation, while SI pays at most $2.4$ bits plus a constant in expectation (\cref{thm:ex:expected,thm:ex:sandwich,prop:ex:law,thm:ex:eisensi}).

\subsection{Contributions}\label{sec:intro:contrib}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \emph{Definitions and versions} (\cref{sec:setting,sec:versions}): Theorems~A and~B; the larger class defined by the one-point inequality alone, whose universal element exceeds $\MF$ by linearly many bits on some data sets (\cref{thm:setting:weakvsmachine}); and the comparison of all versions, including Solomonoff's per-query solution and shared against private randomness (\cref{rem:versions:operator,prop:versions:shared,tab:versions:compare}).
\item \emph{Learning in and out of distribution} (\cref{sec:learn,sec:ood}): merging, mistake and online-to-batch bounds (\cref{thm:learn:merging,cor:learn:mistakes,thm:learn:batch}); the failure of the one-tape version under fresh label noise on repeated inputs, and its repairs, among them the per-query solution (\cref{prop:learn:freshnoise,prop:learn:perquery}); a posterior probability bounded below, whatever the amount of data, for every simple behaviour on an unseen simple property (\cref{prop:ood:hanni}); and a total posterior error of at most $(\ln2)(K(f)+c)$ on a computable target $f$ over any distinct inputs (\cref{thm:ood:cumulative}).
\item \emph{History-aware prediction} (\cref{sec:hist}): Theorem~C, and the skipped-string example, also normalised (\cref{thm:hist:skipped,prop:hist:normchain}). For computable reveal laws $\Delta_f$, which may depend on the target $f$ but see only the history, the loss of the one-tape version exceeds that of every computable sampler $P$ that sees the history by at most $K(P)+K(\Delta_f)+c$ on every path for deterministic reveal policies and, for samplers that halt with probability~$1$ and under finiteness hypotheses, in expectation with an additional $K(n)+K(A)$ term for randomised reveal laws; whether that term is needed is open. A direct simulation argument with private randomness fails, and the stronger oracle form, with the complexity of the reveal law measured relative to an oracle for $f$, is false (\cref{prop:hist:sketchfails,cor:hist:thm1,thm:hist:randchain,rem:hist:thm1open}).
\item \emph{Worked examples} (\cref{sec:ex}): Theorem~D, and positive examples in which SOI is optimal up to lower-order terms (\cref{sec:ex:positive}).
\item \emph{Computable versions and networks} (\cref{sec:comp,sec:nn}): constant regret of time-bounded mixtures against all time-bounded predictors that see only the input (\cref{prop:comp:round,cor:comp:poly}); for circuit Bayes, mistakes polynomially related to circuit size on every ordering (\cref{thm:comp:circuitbayes}), while simplest-consistent learning makes $2^{\Omega(\ell/\log\ell)}$ mistakes on some ordering for the conjunction of $d$ variables, of description length $\ell=\Theta(d\log d)$ (\cref{thm:comp:ladder}); a separation in squared error between Gaussian-process limits and SOI on sparse parities, and a failure of bias-free two-layer ReLU kernels on odd parities (\cref{cor:nn:separation,rem:nn:oddparity}).
\item \emph{Steeper simplicity priors} (\cref{sec:steep}): a summary of the companion paper \citep{claudeSFI2026steeper}, which studies Solomonoff's per-query solution with a length penalty steeper than likelihood and gives two reasons for it. First, it splits data into a model and noise: with i.i.d.\ inputs and a sampler that draws fresh coins at every query, every MAP of the steeper objective is eventually a shortest program for the sampler, for fixed factors above $1$ at a rate that depends only on the factor, the program's length, a margin and the confidence. Second, with a factor proportional to the sample size, in an explicit window, hypotheses that are near-optimal for the population objective learn a random parity on uniform inputs rather than a teacher's errors, made at a rate below $0.05596$ on an error set of product form that is incompressible relative to the parity.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{What is not achieved.} SOI is not computable, and the constants in our bounds depend on the universal machine and can be large, so the results describe an ideal, not an algorithm. Among the open questions (\cref{sec:discussion:open}) are whether $\MF$ dominates every lower-semicomputable law of a random partial function (\cref{q:setting:rpf}), several lower bounds in the normalised convention (\cref{q:setting:normorder}), and a two-sided bridge between NN Bayes and circuit SOI (\cref{q:nn:bridges}). We do not prove the main theorems of the slides.

\paragraph{Reader's guide.} \Cref{sec:related} describes related work and \cref{sec:setting} sets up the definitions used throughout; \cref{sec:versions}, which compares the versions, can be skipped on a first reading. Each main section states its results with short proof ideas; full proofs are in \cref{app:setting,app:versions,app:learn,app:hist,app:ex,app:ood,app:comp,app:nn}.
