\section{Steeper simplicity priors (summary of the companion paper)}\label{sec:steep}

The companion paper \citep{claudeSFI2026steeper} studies the following proposal: penalise program length more steeply than likelihood, by a factor $\lambda>1$, for hypotheses that use private randomness at each input.\footnote{The proposal first appeared in the author's note \citep{hanni2026grokking}.} A standard objection is that, under a steeper penalty, a hypothesis that is itself a universal mixture absorbs all structure of the data. The companion paper shows that this happens in every class that contains its own mixture as a member, such as Solomonoff induction and SFI with shared randomness, and in the history-aware class, whose mixture is $\MH$ (\cref{cor:hist:H4}); no hypothesis with private randomness is universal. The restriction to private randomness has a price on latent causes shared by several inputs; at $\lambda=1$ it is the gap between $\Mprod$ and $\MF$ (\cref{prop:versions:shared,rem:versions:sharedsize}). The companion paper gives two reasons for such a penalty. The first, and the main one, is the split of data into a model and noise. At $\lambda=1$, with private randomness and in Solomonoff induction, a computable sampler and the programs that fill in its sampled bits get similar posterior weight on a data set; with i.i.d.\ inputs and a sampler that draws fresh coins at every query, every $\lambda$-MAP is eventually a shortest program for the sampler, for fixed $\lambda>1$ at a rate that depends only on $\lambda$, the program's length, a margin and the confidence; with shared randomness, or in Solomonoff induction, a universal mixture beats every sufficiently long sampler. The second reason is not learning a teacher's systematic errors. For a random parity whose labels a teacher flips, at a rate below $0.05596$, on an error set of product form that is incompressible relative to the parity, with uniform inputs and $\lambda$ proportional to the sample size $n$, the companion paper proves that for $\lambda/n$ in an explicit window every hypothesis with private randomness that is near-optimal for the population objective knows the parity and, when the error rule is large, gives the erroneous labels far less probability than a memoriser does; $\lambda$ must grow linearly in $n$, because each bit of an error rule is reused at many inputs, and the density and description length of the error rule alone do not suffice.
