     Steeper Simplicity Priors in Solomonoff Function Induction:
            Absorption, the Teacher’s Mistakes and the Model–Randomness Split

                                                Kaarel Hänni∗

                                                October 2026



                                                   Abstract
          Solomonoff function induction (SFI) predicts labels from inputs with a universal prior
      over random functions. The author’s notes proposed penalising program length more steeply
      than log-likelihood, by a factor λ > 1, with private randomness per input, so that learning
      selects a simple model plus noise, not a model of the teacher’s errors. A standard objection:
      under a steeper penalty, a universal mixture taken as one hypothesis absorbs everything. We
      prove that it does in every class containing its own mixture, such as Solomonoff induction and
      shared-randomness SFI, while no private-randomness hypothesis is universal; the protection
      has a price on latent causes shared across inputs. For a random parity with a systematic error
      rule incompressible relative to it, we prove that for λ = cn, with c in an explicit window,
      every near-optimal private-randomness hypothesis knows the parity and gives the erroneous
      labels little probability. In the original example the window spans three orders of magnitude;
      it needs λ proportional to n and fails for some compressible error rules of the same density and
      description length. At λ = 1 a computable sampler and programs that fill in its sampled bits
      get similar posterior; for λ > 1 the sampler wins with private but not with shared randomness.

1     Introduction
Solomonoff function induction (SFI) is Bayesian prediction of a label y from an input x with
a universal prior over random partial functions (Hänni, 2026b,a). It is the supervised-learning
counterpart of Solomonoff induction (SI), and like SI it learns every computable regularity of
the labels, including the labeller’s mistakes. Hänni (2026b) states the problem as follows: a
human can understand “what someone means when using a word”, “as opposed to becoming a
predictor of what they would say”, whereas “roughly speaking solomonoff would be making the
same mistakes the person would make”. His proposed remedy is “an even stronger simplicity
prior than solomonoff”: “if there are explainable mistakes on a simple model, you want the simple
model that doesn’t predict the mistakes”, with a noisy version of the simple hypothesis and a
likelihood term that is penalised less. In the form studied here, a hypothesis is a program π of
length ℓ(π) with likelihood Pπ , and one minimises

                            Jλ (π; D) = λ ℓ(π) − log Pπ (D),    λ > 1,

or uses the power prior 2−λℓ(π) (Definition 2.5). The proposal is made for function induction in
which “each input gets its own private randomness” (Hänni and Wyeth, undated): a hypothesis
is a probabilistic program that uses fresh coins at every input.
    A standard objection, which we call the absorption objection, is that under a steeper penalty
a hypothesis that is itself a universal mixture is short, penalises complexity inside itself only
in the usual way, and therefore absorbs all the structure of the data, errors included. Hänni’s
note makes this point for sequence prediction, where “it’ll be better to drop bits and take the
likelihood loss”, and suggests that the pathology is absent in the function case when “each input
has to sample its own random bits” (Hänni, 2026b); with private randomness, “I don’t see how
to write down a universal hypothesis that gets good loss here” (Hänni and Wyeth, undated). He
supports the proposal with an example: a 100-bit simple model, “systematic human error” on
a 2−10 fraction of the inputs whose set takes 10,000 bits to specify, and three hypotheses (the
simple model with noise, a model that learns the errors, and a fair coin), of which the first is
selected for a range of λ/n. He leaves open whether another hypothesis does better (“Maybe
I’m just failing to construct the right ‘universal hypothesis’ for this example?”), expects that
“some other pathology” may be present, and calls the example “somewhat cherry-picked for the
idea to work” (Hänni and Wyeth, undated). A second question concerns the split between model
and randomness: whether a steeper prior gives “a preference for a simple sampler over a model
where the sampled bits are filled in (solomonoff doesn’t have a preference here, both get similar
posterior)” (Hänni, 2026c), and whether the split shows as a change in slope when the entropy
left given the hypothesis is plotted against its length, which in the sequence case is “pretty much
just 1 bit paying for 1 bit” (Hänni, 2026b).

2     Setting: hypothesis classes and steeper objectives
This section fixes the data, the two kinds of hypotheses, hypothesis classes indexed by programs,
and the steeper objectives. Proofs and standard facts are in Appendix A.

outputs a label; its random partial function is fωT and its machine law is νT (D) = U{ω :
fωT ⊇ D}. Solomonoff function induction (SFI) is the random partial function of the univer-

3        The absorption objection, and why private randomness blocks
         it
Under a steeper penalty, a hypothesis that is itself a universal mixture, and so penalises complexity
only in the usual way inside itself, can absorb all the structure of the data. This is a standard
point.1 Hänni’s note makes it for sequence prediction, where one has the “pathology of not
specifying even the hypothesis in the seq prediction case (like it’ll be better to drop bits and take
the likelihood loss)”, and suggests that the pathology is absent in the function case “if we make
the randomness not shared between different inputs — each input has to sample its own random
bits” (Hänni, 2026b). We call it the absorption objection. Theorem 3.2 makes it exact in every
class that contains its own mixture; Proposition 3.4 and Corollary 3.5 show that the product class
contains no such hypothesis; Propositions 3.8 and 3.9 give the price and the boundary of this
protection; Remark 3.11 assembles the answer. Proofs are in Appendix B.

3.4    Answer to the absorption objection
Remark 3.11 (Answer to the absorption objection). [remark; cites proved results] steeper §1.8
  (i) The objection is correct wherever a universal hypothesis is one member of the class: in SI
      (U = M ), in SFI with shared randomness (U = MF ), in the weak class (U = M w ) and in
      history-aware SFI (U = MH ). There every λ-MAP has length at most ℓ∗λ and fits almost as
      well as U , so it reproduces a deterministic teacher’s errors (Theorem 3.2(c)), and samplers
      longer than about ℓU are never selected (Theorem 6.8).
 (ii) The construction is unavailable in the product class. No product hypothesis is universal
      (Corollary 3.5); the universal conditional semimeasure never learns (Remark 3.6); seeing
      earlier inputs does not help while the randomness stays private (Proposition 3.10). This
      is Hänni’s reply made exact: “if each input gets its own private randomness, then I don’t
      see how to write down a universal hypothesis that gets good loss here” (Hänni and Wyeth,
      undated).
(iii) The restriction has a price and a boundary. The product class cannot represent latent ran-
      domness shared across inputs: at λ = 1 this costs at most K(dom D) + O(1) bits against
      MF , unboundedly in the worst case (Remark 3.7); at λ > 1 a factor min(λ, 2) on shared
      coins (Proposition 3.8). The protection ends where hypotheses see labelled history (Propo-
      sition 3.9). So “nothing is lost at λ = 1” holds only relative to the product class, not relative
      to MF .

                                                       12
 (iv) The algorithmic-statistics reading. For set models, which share their randomness, the mini-
      mal sufficient statistic of a noisy random parity treats the parity’s d-bit parameter as noise
      (sophistication O(log d)); for product models the parameter is model (product sophistica-
      tion d ± O(log d); Proposition 7.6). Restricting the model family to “parameter plus private
      noise per input” is what moves the split (Remark 7.7).
The restriction is a modelling choice, and we present it as one. Two reasons support it. Hänni’s
dominance template for SFI is stated against x-only samplers that “use different bits of randomness
from the universal semimeasure’s infinite read tape for sampling on different inputs” (Hänni,
2026b), which are exactly the product hypotheses. And in supervised learning “the model” is
naturally the part of the description that is reused across inputs; noise private to each input is,
by construction, not part of it. The price in (iii) is what the choice gives up.

Open question. Which hypotheses should count as shared? Whether every lsc law of a random
partial function is dominated by MF up to a constant is open (Hänni, 2026a). Theorem 3.2 holds
for Hw and for HF either way.


4     The teacher’s-mistakes theorem
Hänni asks how a learner can grasp what a teacher means rather than become a predictor of what
the teacher would say; Solomonoff induction “would be making the same mistakes the person
would make” (Hänni, 2026b). His remedy is the steeper objective of Definition 2.5 over product
hypotheses: “if there are explainable mistakes on a simple model, you want the simple model
that doesn’t predict the mistakes” (Hänni, 2026b). This section makes his example rigorous.
Theorem 4.7 quantifies over all product hypotheses and shows that, for error rules that are
incompressible relative to the model, every near-optimal hypothesis is, up to explicit slack, the
simple model with noise, on a window of c = λ/n of more than three orders of magnitude at his
sizes. Section 5 shows where this stops. Proofs are in Appendix C.

4.1   Hänni’s calculation
Remark 4.1 (Hänni’s calculation). [computed] steeper §2.1; checks c1, T5 re-run Hänni gives two versions

5.3    Other pathologies
Proposition 5.6 (Separable components). [proved; computed]
steeper Prop 2.12 (T5 Thm 3.2, Thm 4.1); check c5(a) Let the inputs split into regions X1 , . . . , Xk and the
hypotheses be products of per-region choices with additive code lengths. Each region selects a
vertex of its own hull for slope c (Lemma 2.8). If region j offers a default or a component j of
extra length kj and population risk reduction rj , component j is selected if and only if rj /kj > c
(ties arbitrary). So a valid component whose rate is at most an error component’s rate cannot be
kept without the error, except at a common tie value. Two worlds with the same teacher channel
and input law, one where a component is an error and one where it is a rare valid rule, get the
same selection at every c.

   The steeper penalty sorts components by rate, risk reduction per bit, not by validity. Three
consequences follow (proofs in Appendix D.3).

Proposition 5.7 (Guard erosion). [proved; computed] steeper Prop 2.13 (T5 Prop 4.4); checks c5(b), c6(G),
c7(E); referee q1(I) In a region of contexts a schema σ is one of nst possible steps, and a guard G

5.4   Unseen properties
For MF , a simple property absent from the training data gets a constant probability of bad
behaviour, 2−K(Q,b)−O(1) , where K(Q, b) is the length of a shortest self-delimiting code for a
decider of a set Q and a program for a behaviour b (Hänni, 2026a, Hänni’s proposition, with no
log term). The steeper counterpart is as follows (proof in Appendix D.5).

Proposition 5.10 (Steeper simplicity and unseen properties). [proved] steeper Prop 2.16 In H× , let
Q ⊆ X be decidable, b partial computable, D a data set with dom D ∩ Q = ∅, and Z ⊆ Q ∩ dom b
finite. For every λ ≥ 1: (i) the programs that answer b(x) on Q and run another program
elsewhere carry posterior mass exactly 2−λ(K(Q,b)+κ) , so ξλ (D ∪ b|Z ) ≥ 2−λ(K(Q,b)+κ) ξλ (D); (ii) if
νπ1 (D) = νπ2 (D) > 0, then Πλ (π1 | D)/Πλ (π2 | D) = 2−λ(ℓ(π1 )−ℓ(π2 )) .

   At λ = 1, (i) is Hänni’s proposition for Mprod . With λ = cn the bound vanishes, so steeper
simplicity removes his constant lower bound, but only through (ii): the posterior concentrates on
the shortest hypotheses that fit the training data, whatever they do on Q. If the shortest one
extrapolates well, steeper SFI is more reliable out of distribution than SFI; if a shorter one fits
the training data and is wrong on Q, it is more confidently wrong. No guarantee about unseen
properties follows.

Open question. Which structured error rules leave a window? For envelopes with error density
θ < 1 in the cheap region only part (i) of Proposition 5.3 carries over; whether near-optimal

Hänni has suggested that a steeper prior might give “a preference for a simple sampler over a
model where the sampled bits are filled in (solomonoff doesn’t have a preference here, both get
similar posterior)” (Hänni, 2026c). This section makes both halves precise. At λ = 1 the sampler
and the programs that fill in its sampled bits get similar posterior, up to explicit factors that
do not depend on how much randomness was sampled (Theorem 6.4 and Proposition 6.5). At
λ > 1 with private randomness the sampler wins in every countable product class (Theorem 6.6).
With shared randomness, or in SI, the universal mixture wins instead and no split is made
(Theorem 6.8). Proofs are in Appendix E.

7     Relation to algorithmic statistics
Hänni’s note proposes a second way to find the split between model and noise: “look at how many
bits of entropy are left given the hypothesis, like imagine this graphed as a function of hypothesis
length, and like see some point at which the derivative changes or sth. (this doesn’t show up in
the seq case because there it’s pretty much just 1 bit paying for 1 bit (until you specify it in full
if it’s finite complexity))” (Hänni, 2026b). For set models this graph is Kolmogorov’s structure
function. We define a product version and show, for a noisy random parity, that the two place
the split about d bits apart. Proofs are in Appendix F.

Remark 7.7 (Restricted model families). [known; unverified (content per abstracts); remark]
steeper §3.5, relation to restricted model families That restricting the model family moves the split is known.
Vereshchagin and Vitányi (2010) study Hamming balls and other distortion balls containing the

private randomness per input, for general lsc product models on partial data sets; (b) its role as

and (c) structured, non-i.i.d. noise with identification bounds (Theorem 4.7). A comparison with
the full texts is left open.
Remark 7.8 (Reading: one bit paying for one bit). [remark]
steeper §3.5, Reading With shared randomness (a set model, or any shared model) a uniformly random
d-bit parameter costs the same in the model as in the data-to-model code: Hänni’s “1 bit paying
for 1 bit”. So the minimal sufficient statistic treats the parity’s parameter as noise. With private
randomness, not knowing the parameter costs about one bit per input, so the product split keeps
it in the model, and the derivative of ρ× D changes at α ≈ d, as Hänni expected. For supervised
learning the product split is the relevant one, because “the model” is the part of the description
reused across inputs. This is also why steeper simplicity is useless over set models in the parity
example (Proposition 7.6(i)) and decisive over product models (Theorem 4.7 and Corollary 4.10).

9    Discussion
What counts as one hypothesis. Whether a steeper simplicity prior can separate a model
from its noise depends on what counts as one hypothesis. When a universal mixture is a member
of the class, as in SI and in SFI with shared randomness, it cannot: selected hypotheses are
shorter than a bound that does not depend on the data, and a computable teacher’s labels, errors
included, are eventually predicted (Theorem 3.2). With private randomness per input no member
is universal, and steeper selection can work. The same contrast appears in the split between
a sampler and its filled-in bits (Theorem 6.6 against Theorem 6.8) and in algorithmic statistics
(Proposition 7.6): the product view counts as model what is reused across inputs and as noise
what is private to each input. This is a modelling choice with a price on latent causes shared
by several inputs (Remark 3.7 and Proposition 3.8), and the protection ends when inputs carry
labelled history (Proposition 3.9).

The window and its price. For error rules incompressible relative to the model, near-optimal
product hypotheses are the simple model with noise for 2−m < c ≤ chi (Theorem 4.7). The lower
end is sharp because a bit of information about the error rule costs c per sample in length and
saves about 2−m per sample in risk. So λ must grow linearly with n, and the learner is then
deliberately inconsistent for the teacher’s labels: it discards every component whose rate is below
c, valid or not, and its guaranteed code length exceeds a program’s own loss by c ℓ(π) bits per


                                                31
sample (Remark 5.2). Imitation data cannot justify this price, because strictly proper scores
prefer the teacher’s channel, errors included (Proposition 5.8).

The gap to realistic errors. Human labelling errors are often cheap, frequent patterns rather
than incompressible sets, and for such errors the trick provably fails (Propositions 5.3 and 5.4):
steeper simplicity sorts components by rate, not by validity (Proposition 5.6), so by itself it does
not tell errors from rare valid rules. This agrees with Hänni’s assessment that “it’s very unlikely
this steeper penalization is anywhere close to a full solution to the philosophical problem here”,
with “some hope that it works in some specific toy cases” (Hänni and Wyeth, undated): it provably
works in one such case and fails in others.

The model–randomness split. At λ = 1 a Bayes mixture is indifferent, up to bounded factors,
between a sampler and the programs that fill in its sampled bits (Theorem 6.4). A steeper prior
over product hypotheses breaks the tie in favour of samplers, in the limited sense of Remark 6.7;
in SI’s representation by deterministic programs no form of steepness does (Remark 6.10).

Relation to other work. The objective is that of λ-weighted two-part MDL in noisy classi-
fication, and the posteriors are generalised, fractional or safe posteriors; those works aim at the
data distribution, while the target here differs from it by design (Remarks 8.5 and 8.6). Cold
posteriors are a different direction (Remark 8.3). A neural network whose labels are independent
given its parameters has a Bayes posterior that mixes product laws, so Theorem 6.6 applies to
countable discretisations; whether a parameter prior behaves like a description-length prior, as
Theorem 4.7 would need, is the bridge question raised by Vaintrob and Hänni (2026) and is not
settled here (Remark 8.4).

9.1   Open problems
The open questions of the sections, collected:
 (1) Structured error rules. For envelopes whose cheap region has error density below one, only
     part (i) of Proposition 5.3 carries over; whether near-optimal hypotheses memorise the
     random part, and what is selected above cenv , are open (Section 5.4). A theory for realistic,
     low-complexity errors is the main gap between the trick and its motivation.
 (2) Other model classes and flip rates. Which simple-model classes give a window (parities do;
     majority-like functions presumably do not)? Can the window method reach flip rates up to
     1/4, where the kink exists (Section 4.5, Remark 5.11)?
 (3) Non-uniform inputs and finite samples. A version of Theorem 4.7 for non-uniform input
     laws (only the separable case is proved, Proposition 5.9), and guarantees at realistic sample
     sizes, far below the 1011 samples that uniform convergence needs (Section 4.5).
 (4) Choosing c. A principled choice of c that does not use the imitation data, and the extra
     information that would tell errors from rare valid rules (Section 5.4).
 (5) Shared hypotheses. Is every lower-semicomputable law of a random partial function domi-
     nated by MF up to a constant (Section 3.4)?
 (6) Resource bounds and computability. Does a time-bounded shared class contain a short
     member that absorbs everything at λ > 1? Is the λ-MAP computable from the data
     (Section 8.2)?

                                                 32
 (7) Lower-semicomputable product models. Does Proposition 7.5 hold for them (Section 7.2)?
 (8) Literature. The novelty claims of Remark 8.8 need a comparison with the full texts of the
     classification papers and of the work on restricted model families (Section 8.2).

A.1    Standard facts
The following facts are standard (Li and Vitányi, 2008); κ is as in Section 2.2.
(F1) K(π) ≤ ℓ(π) + κ for every program π of a class H. The identity map on the c.e. prefix-free
     set H is a prefix machine, which the universal machine simulates with a fixed prefix; for
     canonical classes, H = dom V and the fact is immediate.
(F2) Conditional coding theorem. If a fixed program lower-semicomputes from z a semimeasure
     Qz on a countable set, then K(w | z) ≤ − log Qz (w) + κ.
(F3) K(x) ≤ K(y) + K(x | y) + κ, and K(x, y | z) ≤ K(x | z) + K(y | x, z) + κ.
(F4) Symmetry of information. K(x, y) = K(x) + K(y | x, K(x)) + O(1).
(F5) K(j) ≤ 2 log(j + 1) + κ for every integer j ≥ 0, by a doubling code.

A.2    Proof of the hull lemma
Examples. In {(0, 1), (10, 0.5), (20, 0)} the middle point lies on the segment between the other two,
so it is never the unique minimiser; the end points are the unique minimisers on (0.05, ∞) and on
(0, 0.05). In {(0, 1), (5, 0.9), (10, 0)} the point (5, 0.9) lies above the boundary of C; it solves the
constrained problem for L ∈ [5, 10) and minimises no penalised objective.
    We prove Lemma 2.8. Write A′ := A ∩ {R < ∞} and ⟨(c, 1), (ℓ, R)⟩ = c ℓ + R.
    (i). Since (c, 1) ≥ 0, the infimum of c ℓ + R over C = conv(A′ ) + R2≥0 equals its infimum over
A′ . A minimiser a over A therefore minimises a nonzero linear functional over C. So the line
{c ℓ + R = c ℓ(a) + R(a)} supports C at a, and a lies on the boundary of C.
    Structure of the boundary. Fix a ∈ A′ and s > 0. A point b ∈ A′ with ℓ(b) > ℓ(a) and
R(b) ≤ R(a) − s(ℓ(b) − ℓ(a)) has ℓ(b) ≤ ℓ(a) + R(a)/s, because R(b) ≥ 0; only finitely many points
satisfy this. So from each point the steepest descending direction to the right is attained, and
the lower-left boundary of C is a polygonal line. It consists of a vertical ray above the least-ℓ
vertex, segments between consecutive vertices with slopes −s, s ∈ (0, ∞), strictly increasing from
left to right, and, if a point of least R exists, a horizontal ray to the right of the least-R vertex.
Its corners are extreme points of C, and every extreme point of C lies in A′ , because the extreme
points of a convex hull belong to the generating set and adding the orthant creates none.
    (iii). Let a be a vertex. Its two incident boundary pieces have slopes −s− < −s+ ≤ 0, with
s− = ∞ at the least-ℓ vertex and s+ = 0 at the least-R vertex. For c ∈ (s+ , s− ) the line with
normal (c, 1) through a meets C only at a, so a is the unique minimising point. Conversely, if
a is the unique minimising point for every c in an open interval, then a is an exposed point of
C, hence an extreme point, hence a vertex. A boundary point that is not a vertex lies in the
relative interior of a segment between two vertices, or on one of the two rays. In the first case,
whenever it minimises c ℓ + R, so do both endpoints of its segment. A point on the vertical ray
is beaten by the vertex below it (smaller R), and a point on the horizontal ray by the least-R
vertex (smaller ℓ, same R). So no such point is ever the unique minimising point. The statement
about hypotheses is immediate: a hypothesis is the unique minimiser exactly when its point is
the unique minimising point and no other hypothesis has that point.
    (ii). Add c ℓ(h)+R(h) ≤ c ℓ(h′ )+R(h′ ) and c′ ℓ(h′ )+R(h′ ) ≤ c′ ℓ(h)+R(h) to get (c′ −c)(ℓ(h′ )−
ℓ(h)) ≤ 0, so ℓ(h′ ) ≤ ℓ(h). Then the first inequality gives R(h) ≤ R(h′ ) + c(ℓ(h′ ) − ℓ(h)) ≤ R(h′ ).

for c ∈ (ρG , ρσ ). Otherwise the hull has only the two outer points, and the selection switches at
