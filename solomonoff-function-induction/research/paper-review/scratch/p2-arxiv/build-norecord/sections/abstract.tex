\begin{abstract}
Solomonoff function induction (SFI) predicts labels from inputs with a universal prior over random functions. The author's notes proposed penalising program length more steeply than log-likelihood, by a factor $\lambda>1$, with private randomness per input, so that learning selects a simple model plus noise, not a model of the teacher's errors. A standard objection: under a steeper penalty, a universal mixture taken as one hypothesis absorbs everything. We prove that it does in every class containing its own mixture, such as Solomonoff induction and shared-randomness SFI, while no private-randomness hypothesis is universal; the protection has a price on latent causes shared across inputs. For a random parity with a systematic error rule incompressible relative to it, we prove that for $\lambda=cn$, with $c$ in an explicit window, every near-optimal private-randomness hypothesis knows the parity and gives the erroneous labels little probability. In the original example the window spans three orders of magnitude; it needs $\lambda$ proportional to $n$ and fails for some compressible error rules of the same density and description length. At $\lambda=1$ a computable sampler and programs that fill in its sampled bits get similar posterior; for $\lambda>1$ the sampler wins with private but not with shared randomness.
\end{abstract}
