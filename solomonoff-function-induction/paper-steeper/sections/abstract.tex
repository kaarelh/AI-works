\begin{abstract}
Solomonoff function induction (SFI) predicts labels from inputs with a universal prior on random functions. We study a complexity penalty steeper than log-likelihood by a factor $\lambda>1$, for private-randomness hypotheses, and give two reasons for it. In each class containing its own universal mixture that mixture, taken as one hypothesis, absorbs all structure; no private-randomness hypothesis is universal. The first and main reason is the split into model and noise. With i.i.d.\ inputs and a computable sampler with fresh coins per query, for every $1\le\lambda_n=o(n)$ every $\lambda_n$-MAP is eventually a shortest program for the sampler; for fixed $\lambda>1$, with probability $1-\varepsilon$ at each $n$ beyond a bound depending only on $\lambda$, the program's length, a margin and $\varepsilon$. At $\lambda=1$ no such bound exists, and in a parity class recovery is exponentially slower in that length. On one data set, a kink of the product structure function at a sufficient program makes every $\lambda$ between $1$ and its steepness select it up to windows; per-query samples have one, and so, for large $d$, does the full table of a noisy random parity at each dyadic flip rate below $1/4$, but set models have no steep one. The second reason, the same geometry at a positive rate, is not learning a teacher's errors: an error rule is reused at many inputs, so $\lambda$ must grow linearly in the sample size. For a random parity on uniform inputs, with labels flipped at a rate below $0.05596$ on an error set of product form incompressible relative to the parity, every near-optimal hypothesis of the population objective with $\lambda/n$ in an explicit window knows the parity and, for large error rules, gives the erroneous labels far less probability than a memoriser. These are toy cases of imitation and of learning from a fallible teacher; we offer tools, not solutions.
\end{abstract}
