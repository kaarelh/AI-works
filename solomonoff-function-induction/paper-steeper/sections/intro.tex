\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Solomonoff function induction (SFI) is Bayesian prediction of a label $y$ from an input $x$ with a universal prior over random partial functions \citep{hanni2026notes,claudeSFI2026fundamentals}. It is the supervised-learning counterpart of Solomonoff induction (SI), and like SI it learns every computable regularity of the labels, including the labeller's mistakes. \Citet{hanni2026notes} states the problem as follows: a human can understand ``what someone means when using a word'', ``as opposed to becoming a predictor of what they would say'', whereas ``roughly speaking solomonoff would be making the same mistakes the person would make''. His proposed remedy is ``an even stronger simplicity prior than solomonoff'': ``if there are explainable mistakes on a simple model, you want the simple model that doesn't predict the mistakes'', with a noisy version of the simple hypothesis and a likelihood term that is penalised less. In the form studied here, a hypothesis is a program $\pi$ of length $\ell(\pi)$ with likelihood $P_\pi$, and one minimises
\[
J_\lambda(\pi;D)=\lambda\,\ell(\pi)-\log P_\pi(D),\qquad\lambda>1,
\]
or uses the power prior $2^{-\lambda\ell(\pi)}$ (\cref{def:setup:objectives}). The proposal is made for function induction in which ``each input gets its own private randomness'' \citep{hanni2026lesswrong}: a hypothesis is a probabilistic program that uses fresh coins at every input.

A standard objection, which we call the \emph{absorption objection}, is that under a steeper penalty a hypothesis that is itself a universal mixture is short, penalises complexity inside itself only in the usual way, and therefore absorbs all the structure of the data, errors included. H\"anni's note makes this point for sequence prediction, where ``it'll be better to drop bits and take the likelihood loss'', and suggests that the pathology is absent in the function case when ``each input has to sample its own random bits'' \citep{hanni2026notes}; with private randomness, ``I don't see how to write down a universal hypothesis that gets good loss here'' \citep{hanni2026lesswrong}. He supports the proposal with an example: a $100$-bit simple model, ``systematic human error'' on a $2^{-10}$ fraction of the inputs whose set takes $10{,}000$ bits to specify, and three hypotheses (the simple model with noise, a model that learns the errors, and a fair coin), of which the first is selected for a range of $\lambda/n$. He leaves open whether another hypothesis does better (``Maybe I'm just failing to construct the right `universal hypothesis' for this example?''), expects that ``some other pathology'' may be present, and calls the example ``somewhat cherry-picked for the idea to work'' \citep{hanni2026lesswrong}. A second question concerns the split between model and randomness: whether a steeper prior gives ``a preference for a simple sampler over a model where the sampled bits are filled in (solomonoff doesn't have a preference here, both get similar posterior)'' \citep{hanni2026request}, and whether the split shows as a change in slope when the entropy left given the hypothesis is plotted against its length, which in the sequence case is ``pretty much just 1 bit paying for 1 bit'' \citep{hanni2026notes}.

\paragraph{Contributions.} We answer these questions with proofs; \cref{tab:intro:claims} lists H\"anni's specific claims with their verdicts.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Absorption, and why private randomness blocks it} (\cref{sec:absorb}). In every class that contains its own Bayes mixture, such as SI, SFI with shared randomness and history-aware SFI, every $\lambda$-MAP is shorter than a bound that does not depend on the data, and a computable deterministic teacher's labels, errors included, are eventually predicted (\cref{thm:absorb:absorption}). No product hypothesis, with randomness private to each input, is universal: each loses $2^d-1$ bits on some parity with a program of $d+2\log d+O(1)$ bits (\cref{cor:absorb:nouniversal}). The protection costs a factor $\min(\lambda,2)$ on latent coins shared by pairs of inputs (\cref{prop:absorb:price}) and ends when inputs carry labelled history (\cref{prop:absorb:history}); \cref{rem:absorb:wyeth} assembles the answer to the objection.
\item \emph{The teacher's-mistakes theorem} (\cref{sec:teacher}). A teacher flips a random parity on $\{0,1\}^d$ on the inputs whose first $m$ bits lie in a random error rule $S$ of density $p$. If $S$ is incompressible relative to the parity and $\lambda=cn$ with $c$ in an explicit window $(2^{-m},c_{\rm hi}]$, every near-optimal product hypothesis knows the parity, is short, holds little information about $S$ and gives the erroneous labels small average probability (\cref{thm:teacher:main}). At H\"anni's sizes ($d=100$, $m=20$, $p=2^{-10}$, $11{,}710$ bits for $S$) the window is $(9.54\cdot10^{-7},8.06\cdot10^{-3}]$ with the machine constant set to $0$ for illustration and $(9.54\cdot10^{-7},3.66\cdot10^{-3}]$ with $30$ bits, against his three-hypothesis window $(1.12\cdot10^{-6},8.99\cdot10^{-3})$ (\cref{tab:teacher:numbers}). Full truth tables, sampled data, his other version of the example and the power posterior are treated too (\cref{sec:teacher:more}).
\item \emph{Limits} (\cref{sec:limits}). Plain Bayes, MDL, SI and every schedule with $\lambda_n/n\to0$ learn the errors, so the trick needs $\lambda=\Theta(n)$ and is deliberately inconsistent for the teacher's labels (\cref{prop:limits:plain,rem:limits:schedule}). Incompressibility cannot be dropped: for an error rule with H\"anni's density and about $10{,}400$ bits, made of a cheap subcube and random points, no hypothesis that fits no better than the good model is near-optimal on almost a third of the window's logarithmic width (\cref{prop:limits:envelope}). A cheap, frequent error rule defeats every $c$ once the simple model is long enough (\cref{prop:limits:rateblind,lem:limits:robust}); selection is by rate, not validity, cannot be tuned from imitation data and depends on the input law (\cref{sec:limits:other}).
\item \emph{The model--randomness split} (\cref{sec:split,sec:algstat}). At $\lambda=1$, for every computable sampler of length $k$, the posterior odds of the programs that fill in its sampled bits against the sampler lie in $[2^{-2\log(t_n+1)-\kappa},2^{k+\delta^*}]$ whatever the sampled randomness, with $2\log(t_n+1)$ doubly logarithmic in the input length and $\delta^*$ a Ville deficiency; SI behaves similarly (\cref{thm:split:lambda1,prop:split:si}). For fixed $\lambda>1$ with private randomness every $\lambda$-MAP is at most $(k+\delta^*)/(\lambda-1)$ bits longer than the sampler, so it is not a fill-in once the sampled randomness is larger, and the fill-ins' posterior is exponentially small in the sampled randomness (\cref{thm:split:private}); with shared randomness, and in SI, the universal mixture wins (\cref{thm:split:shared}). For a noisy random parity, product sophistication exceeds set sophistication by $d-O(\log d)$ bits (\cref{prop:algstat:parity}).
\item \emph{Computable classes} (\cref{sec:related}). The lower bounds and the private-randomness split hold in every c.e.\ prefix-free product class, with the class's own constants (\cref{prop:related:computable}).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Prior work.} Two-part codes with a complexity weight above one appear in MDL theory, and the posteriors studied here are generalised, fractional or safe posteriors with learning rate $1/\lambda$ (\cref{sec:setup:objectives}). Closest to the teacher's-mistakes question is $\lambda$-weighted two-part MDL for noisy binary classification \citep{grunwald2007suboptimal,zhu2025quantifying,zhu2026overfitting}; as their abstracts describe them, these works find that a bounded $\lambda$ fits the noise and that $\lambda$ must grow with the sample size. We do not claim this finding. Their target is the data distribution; ours deliberately differs from it (\cref{rem:limits:schedule,rem:related:mdl}). In algorithmic statistics, restricting the model family is known to move the split between model and noise \citep{vereshchagin2010rate}, and product models are such a restriction (\cref{rem:algstat:restricted}). An earlier report formalised H\"anni's proposal for a general imitation channel, mostly by comparing a few hypotheses by rate \citep[section ``Simplicity and normativity from imitation'']{claude2026whatfollows}; we re-derive what we use, with two corrections (\cref{rem:related:t5}). Our literature search went only as far as abstracts, so the novelty claims of \cref{rem:related:new} need a check against full texts.

\paragraph{Credits and scope.} The notion of SFI, the proposal, the example with its numbers, the model--randomness question and the idea of reading the split from the slope of the remaining entropy come from the author's earlier notes and comments \citep{hanni2026notes,hanni2026lesswrong,hanni2026request}, which are cited in the third person, as H\"anni's; \cref{tab:intro:claims} says where the analysis confirms, qualifies or corrects them. The samplers of Sam Eisenstat's coin example, as reported by \citet{hanni2026request}, are covered by \cref{thm:split:lambda1}. The basic theory of SFI is in the companion paper \citep{claudeSFI2026fundamentals}. The positive result is proved only for parities, error sets of product form, uniform inputs, flip rates below $0.056$ and incompressible error rules (\cref{rem:teacher:hypotheses}); for realistic, simple error patterns we have a counterexample but no positive result. Numbers computed with the machine constant $\kappa=0$ are illustrations. The finite-sample guarantee needs about $10^{11}$ samples at H\"anni's sizes (\cref{cor:teacher:sampled}), and with $\lambda=cn$ the learner is a family of batch objectives, not one Bayesian predictor (\cref{rem:setup:persample}).

\paragraph{Reader's guide.} \Cref{sec:setup} fixes data, hypotheses, classes and objectives; \cref{sec:absorb} treats absorption; \cref{sec:teacher,sec:limits} the teacher's mistakes; \cref{sec:split,sec:algstat} the model--randomness split; \cref{sec:related} computable classes and related work; \cref{sec:discussion} the discussion and open problems. Proofs are in the appendices, one per section. Notation follows the companion paper: logarithms are to base $2$, $n$ is the number of samples, $N=2^d$, $c=\lambda/n$, and $\kappa$ is a constant that depends only on the reference machine and fixed programs (\cref{sec:setup:classes}).
