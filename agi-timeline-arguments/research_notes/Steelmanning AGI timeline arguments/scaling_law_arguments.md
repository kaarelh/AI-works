## Q1. What did the original scaling-law papers (Kaplan et al. 2020; Henighan et al. 2020) say about where scaling leads?

### Takeaway
Neither paper states an AGI date. Kaplan et al. found a "contradiction" between their compute-optimal and data-limited laws. It falls at roughly 10^4 PF-days (≈8.6×10^23 FLOP, only ≈2.75× GPT-3's training compute), ~10^12 parameters, ~10^12 tokens and ~1.7 nats/token. They conjectured that this point marks "maximal performance", at which L* would estimate the entropy of natural language. Henighan et al. read the loss as entropy + KL. That makes the reducible loss "the number of bits of information that 'remain to be understood'", so a small reducible loss can still hide "important additional semantic information". Both papers supply the raw material for the later loss-to-capability argument: Kaplan's remark that a "small decrease" near the floor could mean "a significant boost in qualitative performance", and Henighan's "many capabilities simply lie on a spectrum that can be continuously unlocked with increasing scale". Neither paper makes the full argument.

### Cited Findings
- **Kaplan et al., Jan 2020, §6.3 "Contradictions and a Conjecture" [primary]:** "We observe no signs of deviation from straight power-law trends at large values of compute, data, or model size. Our trends must eventually level off, though, since natural language has non-zero entropy. Indeed, the trends for compute-efficient training described in this section already contain an apparent contradiction. At scales several orders of magnitude above those documented here, the performance predicted by the L(Cmin) scaling law decreases below what should be possible given the slow growth in training data with compute. This implies that our scaling laws must break down before this point, but we conjecture that the intersection point has a deeper meaning: it provides an estimate of the point at which Transformer language models reach maximal performance." — [Kaplan et al. 2020, arXiv:2001.08361](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.08361)
- **The numbers [primary]:** "The intersection point of L(D(Cmin)) and L(Cmin) occurs at C* ∼ 10^4 PF-Days, N* ∼ 10^12 parameters, D* ∼ 10^12 tokens, L* ∼ 1.7 nats/token, though the numerical values are highly uncertain, varying by an order or magnitude in either direction depending on the precise values of the exponents from the power-law fits. The most obvious interpretation is that our scaling laws break down at or before we reach this point, which is still many orders of magnitude away in both compute and model size." — [Kaplan et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.08361)
- **Where the contradiction comes from [primary]:** to control overfitting the dataset should grow as D ∝ N^0.74 ∝ C_min^0.54, but compute-efficient single-epoch training lets data grow only as ≈4×10^10 tokens × (C_min/PF-day)^0.26, "much more slowly", so L(D(C_min)) ∝ C_min^−0.03 eventually crosses L(C_min) ∝ C_min^−0.050. — [Kaplan et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.08361)
- **The entropy conjecture [primary]:** "If we cannot increase the model size beyond N* without qualitatively different data requirements, perhaps this means that once we reach C*min and N*, we have extracted all of the reliable information available in natural language data. In this interpretation, L* would provide a rough estimate for the entropy-per-token of natural language. In this scenario, we would expect the loss trend to level off at or before L*." — [Kaplan et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.08361)
- **The "last fraction of a nat" remark, which is the seed of the later steelman [primary]:** about a dataset with added noise, "the distance from the noise floor L − Lnoise would be a more meaningful performance metric, with even a small decrease in this distance potentially representing a significant boost in qualitative performance." — [Kaplan et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.08361)
- **Kaplan's framing of why language matters [primary]:** "Language provides a natural domain for the study of artificial intelligence, as the vast majority of reasoning tasks can be efficiently expressed and evaluated in language, and the world's text provides a wealth of data for unsupervised learning via generative modeling." The Discussion compares the laws to "the ideal gas law" and notes that "there are diminishing returns with increasing scale". — [Kaplan et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.08361)
- **GPT-3's compute, for scale [primary]:** GPT-3 175B used 3.64×10^3 PF-days = 3.14×10^23 FLOP. — [Brown et al. 2020, arXiv:2005.14165](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.14165)
- **Henighan et al., Oct 2020: reading loss as entropy plus KL [primary]:** "The cross-entropy loss has an information theoretic interpretation as S(True) + DKL(True||Model), and the empirical scaling laws suggest a prediction for both the true data distribution's entropy and the KL divergence between the true and model distributions." — [Henighan et al. 2020, arXiv:2010.14701](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.14701)
- **Henighan: diminishing loss is not diminishing value [primary]:** "One might have guessed that as the L(x) curve bends and the loss approaches L∞, returns to increasing N, C, D are diminishing. But the identification of the reducible loss with DKL suggests this is not necessarily the case, and further increases in scale may still provide important additional semantic information… By evaluating the reducible loss for a full image or video, we are actually estimating the number of bits of information that 'remain to be understood' by a given model." — [Henighan et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.14701)
- **Henighan's caveat [primary]:** "This interpretation can only make sense if in the limit of infinite data and compute, we expect the transformer to perfectly model the data distribution"; also "caution is warranted when interpreting L(N) trends… identifying the irreducible loss as an entropy". — [Henighan et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.14701)
- **Henighan did not fit an irreducible loss for language [primary]:** the Fig. 1 caption says "In the case of language we use results from [BMR+20], and only show the full loss L." Their extrapolations to "a few nats" are for images and video. For example, the final video frame would need "a model size around ∼10^13 parameters and compute of around 10^4 PF-days to achieve a loss of just a few nats/frame". They add: "Of course we do not expect to need a perfect model of the probability distribution of real-world images for practical tasks." — [Henighan et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.14701)
- **Henighan's Discussion, the "scaling hypothesis writ large" sentence [primary]:** "The scaling laws also suggest a shift in perspective away from the particularities of neural architectures, loss functions, and training algorithms and towards the broader commonalities that appear when machine learning is studied across a large hierarchy of model, data, and compute scales… Perhaps many capabilities simply lie on a spectrum that can be continuously unlocked with increasing scale, as might be suggested by the metalearning capabilities of the GPT-3 model." Also: "finetuned performance on downstream tasks also follows similar scaling laws. This suggests that trends in the generative modeling loss translate into advantages in practical capabilities." — [Henighan et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.14701)
- **Henighan's App. D, smooth loss with qualitative phases [primary]:** on GPT-3 arithmetic and Q&A, "we find smooth improvement in the loss on the correct answer as the model size increases. However, we also observe some qualitative 'phases of learning', with small models having difficulty understanding the question being asked of them, larger models showing some rudimentary understanding, and the largest models correctly answering the questions." — [Henighan et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.14701)
- **Chinchilla's correction [primary]:** "for compute-optimal training, the model size and the number of training tokens should be scaled equally". The fitted law is L(N,D) = E + A/N^0.34 + B/D^0.28 with E = 1.69, A = 406.4, B = 410.7. The first term "should correspond to the entropy of natural text". — [Hoffmann et al. 2022, arXiv:2203.15556](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.15556)
- **A replication of Chinchilla's parametric fit gives a different floor [primary]:** L(N,D) = 1.8172 + 482.01/N^0.3478 + 2085.43/D^0.3658, against Hoffmann's E = 1.6934. — [Besiroglu et al. 2024, arXiv:2404.10102](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.10102)

### Inferences
- The Kaplan "contradiction" was an artifact of Kaplan's own compute-optimal allocation, in which data grows as ~C^0.26. Under Chinchilla's allocation (tokens ∝ parameters, i.e. data grows as ~C^0.5) the L(D) bound does not bite at C*, so frontier training passed C* (≈8.6×10^23 FLOP, my arithmetic: 10^4 × 10^15 FLOP/s × 86,400 s) within about two years with no sign that loss stopped at "maximal performance". The conjecture that 1.7 nats/token estimates the entropy of English is therefore unsupported. Kaplan's L* (WebText2, GPT-2 tokenizer) and Chinchilla's E (MassiveText) use different data and tokenizers and cannot be compared directly.
- **The ill-conditioning shows up even within one dataset (my arithmetic from the two published fits).** At Chinchilla's own scale (N = 7×10^10, D = 1.4×10^12) the Hoffmann fit gives total loss ≈1.94 nats, of which ≈0.25 is reducible. The Besiroglu fit gives total ≈1.97, of which ≈0.16 is reducible. The two fits agree to about 0.04 nats on the observable total but disagree by ~60% on the "distance to the entropy floor". That distance is exactly the quantity any loss→AGI argument (Direct Approach, Gwern's "last bits") has to extrapolate.
- Read literally, the 2020 papers support three things: loss is predictable; small loss changes near the floor may carry large qualitative change; and capabilities might be "continuously unlocked". They do not supply a mapping from a given loss to a given capability. That gap is exactly the one the user identifies.

### Gaps
- I found no statement of an AGI date by Kaplan, Henighan or McCandlish in their 2020 papers. Jared Kaplan's later talks and interviews (e.g. 2022 talks; any 2025 public statements) could not be retrieved, because the search budget was exhausted.
- I did not check whether Kaplan or colleagues ever publicly revisited the C*/L* conjecture after Chinchilla.

## Q2. Who inferred AGI dates from scaling: person, year, date predicted, reasoning, quotes

### Takeaway
There were two families of inference. (A) **"Prediction requires understanding"** (Sutskever, Gwern; later formalized by Epoch's Direct Approach): driving next-token loss down forces the model to internalize the processes that generate text, so low enough loss means human-level capability. (B) **"Compute converts predictably into capability"** (Amodei's 2017 "Big Blob of Compute", Anthropic's 2023 "Core Views", Aschenbrenner): scaling laws show that a generic learner turns compute into general capability smoothly. Capability per order of magnitude is then calibrated by *intuition from observed qualitative jumps*, not by a loss threshold. Anthropic's 2023 statement says plainly that the step from compute to "human-level" "requires that we use intuition – albeit informed intuition". Dates ranged from "AGI in 2 years" (the title of Amodei's Aug 2023 Dwarkesh episode, not verified as his own words) and "could come as early as 2026" (Amodei, 2024) to 2027 (Aschenbrenner, 2024), against 2040–2050 from the Open Phil-style models (see Q3).

### Cited Findings
**Anthropic, "Core Views on AI Safety" (8 Mar 2023): the clearest primary statement of the inference [primary]**
- "Rapid and continuing AI progress is a predictable consequence of the exponential increase in computation used to train AI systems, because research on 'scaling laws' demonstrates that more computation leads to general improvements in capabilities. Simple extrapolations suggest AI systems will become far more capable in the next decade, possibly equaling or exceeding human level performance at most intellectual tasks." — [Anthropic, Core Views on AI Safety, 8 Mar 2023](https://www.anthropic.com/news/core-views-on-ai-safety)
- History of the idea: "In the mid-2010s, some of us noticed that larger AI systems were consistently smarter, and so we theorized that the most important ingredient in AI performance might be the total budget for AI training computation. When this was graphed, it became clear that the amount of computation going into the largest models was growing at 10x per year… In 2019, several members of what was to become the founding Anthropic team made this idea precise by developing scaling laws for AI, demonstrating that you could make AIs smarter in a predictable way, just by making them larger and training them on more data… back in 2019, it seemed possible that multimodality, logical reasoning, speed of learning, transfer learning across tasks, and long-term memory might be 'walls' that would slow or halt the progress of AI. In the years since, several of these 'walls', such as multimodality and logical reasoning, have fallen." — [Anthropic 2023](https://www.anthropic.com/news/core-views-on-ai-safety)
- **The explicit calibration step (footnote 3):** "We know that the capability jump from GPT-2 to GPT-3 resulted mostly from about a 250x increase in compute. Over the next 5 years we might expect around a 1000x increase in the computation used to train the largest models… If the scaling laws hold, this would result in a capability jump that is significantly larger than the jump from GPT-2 to GPT-3 (or GPT-3 to Claude). At Anthropic, we're deeply familiar with the capabilities of these systems and a jump that is this much larger feels to many of us like it could result in human-level performance across most tasks. This requires that we use intuition – albeit informed intuition – and is therefore an imperfect method… the underlying facts… jointly support a greater than 10% likelihood that we will develop broadly human-level AI systems within the next decade. In this coarse analysis we ignore algorithmic progress… However, the vast majority of internal disagreement here is in the intuition for extrapolating subsequent capabilities jumps given an equivalent compute jump." — [Anthropic 2023](https://www.anthropic.com/news/core-views-on-ai-safety)

### Inferences
- In its own words, the strongest institutional version (Anthropic 2023) is *not* "loss → AGI". It runs: compute ratio → scaling laws say capability improves predictably → the size of the qualitative jump is calibrated against GPT-2→GPT-3 → intuition says a jump 4× larger in log-compute reaches human level. The loss curve serves only as evidence that the compute→capability conversion is smooth and has no walls. This is the user's rescue (3), and it was the original argument, not a later patch.
- The "walls fell" clause (multimodality and logical reasoning fell by 2023) is a separate inductive argument from track record. It does not depend on any loss→capability map.
- The user remembers "AGI by ~2028". I could not find a scaling-law author who gave exactly 2028. Shane Legg's long-standing 50%-by-2028 forecast (made c. 2009–2011 from compute and neuroscience trends, before scaling laws) is a plausible referent, but I did not verify it this session (see Gaps).

## Q3. Cotra's Biological Anchors (2020) and the 2022 update; Kokotajlo's "+12 OOMs" and "What 2026 looks like"; retrospectives

### Takeaway
Bio Anchors produced a median of ~2050 (the model output 2053, which Cotra rounded). Her 2022 update moved this to ~2040, with the probability of TAI by 2036 rising from ~15% to ~35%. In Bio Anchors, scaling laws are only a *conversion step*, from an anchored parameter count to a training-data requirement. The capability claim itself rests on brain-compute anchors and on "horizon length". The model is agnostic about the training objective (the horizon-length anchors are essentially RL-style long-feedback training), so it never asserted "pretraining alone → AGI". The 2026 retrospectives agree that its main error was algorithmic progress, ~200%/yr realized against ~30%/yr assumed. With realized inputs plugged into the compute-centric (Davidson) model, the central estimate comes out ~2030 rather than 2040–2050.

- **Kokotajlo's later dates [primary for RAND]:** in late 2025 Kokotajlo (lead author of AI 2027) shifted "from ~2027 to ~2030", citing Lifland, Kokotajlo & Halstead (2026) and the "AI Futures Model: Dec 2025 Update" (30 Dec 2025). Karpathy described AGI as "a decade away" (Dwarkesh, 17 Oct 2025). — [RAND 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22766)

### Inferences
- **How scaling laws enter Bio Anchors (from my memory of the report; not re-verified this session).** The Neural Network anchors set a model size from brain-equivalent FLOP/s. They then compute training FLOP as (FLOP per forward pass) × (number of training samples) × (effective horizon length). The number of samples scales with parameters by a power law taken from the ML scaling literature, which is where Kaplan et al. come in. "Effective horizon length" is the subjective time per meaningful feedback signal; the short, medium and long anchors span roughly seconds to years. On this reading, Bio Anchors' scaling-law dependence is narrow: it fixes how data must grow with model size. The *capability* claim comes from "a brain-sized model trained on long-horizon tasks ≈ human-level". Under the Chinchilla correction (data ∝ parameters), any Kaplan-derived sub-linear data exponent would have *understated* compute needs at fixed horizon. Even so, the model still came out too late, because algorithmic progress dominated.
- **Bio Anchors already anticipated the RL turn.** Training with long effective horizons on tasks is essentially RL or agentic training, not pure next-token pretraining. Of the major 2020-era quantitative models, then, the most influential one does *not* commit the "pretraining alone" error. Its error was in the opposite direction: its timelines were too long.
- Kokotajlo's "+12 OOMs" is also objective-agnostic. Its scenarios include RL-heavy systems and simulated evolution, so it is a compute-centric bet (rescue 1), not a pretraining-loss bet.

## Q4. Epoch's "Direct Approach" (Barnett & Besiroglu, 2023): argument, results, limitations, critiques; why it seems uncompelling; its best version

### Takeaway
The Direct Approach is the only well-known *formal* bridge from scaling-law loss to a capability threshold. Scaling laws forecast the per-token KL divergence between model and human text. KL gives the expected number of tokens an *ideal* discriminator needs to tell model text from human text. Adjusting for how much worse human judges are (the "human slowdown factor") converts this to a horizon k of human-indistinguishable text. Requiring k long enough for transformative tasks (e.g. scientific papers) gives a compute requirement, and combining that with compute and spending projections gives a date. The authors frame it as an **upper bound** ("soft ceiling"). Barnett himself warned that the bound "may be far above the actual requirements, which could render the Direct Approach fairly useless". Its median in Epoch's interactive model was ~2033 as of March 2026.

### Cited Findings
- **Core argument [search summary]:** scaling laws can forecast the KL divergence of the true distribution from the model, which tells how distinguishable the model is. "The performance of an ideal discriminator can be calculated given only the KL-divergences". A non-zero KL lets one estimate the expected number of tokens over which it becomes possible to discriminate model from true distribution to a given confidence. Combined with scaling laws, this "permits us to directly calculate the compute requirements to train a model with a KL-divergence corresponding to a target k-performance value". — [Epoch, The Direct Approach (Apr 2023)](https://epoch.ai/blog/the-direct-approach); [paper PDF "Scaling transformative autoregressive models"](https://epoch.ai/files/direct-approach.pdf); [Barnett, "A proposed method for forecasting transformative AI" (Alignment Forum)](https://www.alignmentforum.org/posts/4ufbirCCLsFiscWuY/a-proposed-method-for-forecasting-transformative-ai)
- **Upper-bound framing and author caveats [search summary]:** the method gives a "soft ceiling" because a more efficient route than scaling language models may exist. Barnett warns the bound may be far above actual requirements, "which could render the Direct Approach fairly useless". He calls measuring the "human slowdown factor" experimentally "a tractable way of reducing uncertainty". Epoch advises against over-trusting any bottom-line figure and points to future experiments on human discrimination ability. — [Barnett, LessWrong](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/4ufbirCCLsFiscWuY/a-proposed-method-for-forecasting-transformative-ai); [EA Forum explainer, "Now THIS is forecasting"](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/yHjdr5pi2oCPbMxTR/now-this-is-forecasting-understanding-epoch-s-direct)
- **Date [primary for RAND]:** "Epoch AI's 'Direct Approach'… uses neural network scaling laws to directly bound compute requirements for transformative AI without biological reference points… This model produces a median estimate around 2033 (as of March 2026)." — [RAND 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22766). An EA Forum analysis found Bio Anchors and the Direct Approach yield medians within a few decades of each other. — [EA Forum, "Timelines to Transformative AI: an investigation"](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/hzhGL7tb56hG5pRXY/timelines-to-transformative-ai-an-investigation)
- **Why loss can be uninformative about specific capability [primary]:** the irreducible-loss estimate is unstable. Hoffmann's E = 1.69 against Besiroglu et al.'s 1.8172 on the same data leaves the reducible remainder at Chinchilla scale uncertain by ~60% (Q1 Inferences). — [Hoffmann et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.15556); [Besiroglu et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.10102)
- **Token heterogeneity [primary]:** "Not all tokens in a corpus are equally important for language model training… token-level training dynamics… reveal[] distinct loss patterns for different tokens." — [Lin et al. (Rho-1) 2024, arXiv:2404.07965](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.07965)

### Inferences
- **Why it seems uncompelling** (my analysis, building on the cited facts):
  1. *The decisive quantity is a small residual of an uncertain decomposition.* The forecast requires extrapolating the *reducible* KL down by several more factors of ten. Yet the floor E is not pinned down to within ~0.1 nats, about the size of the entire remaining reducible loss at frontier scale (Q1).
  2. *Exponent uncertainty compounds.* Reducible loss falls as ~C^−0.05 to C^−0.15. Halving the KL then costs ~2^(1/0.05) ≈ 10^6 to ~2^(1/0.15) ≈ 10^2 times more compute, so a ±0.05 error in the exponent shifts the requirement by many orders of magnitude.
  3. *Average KL is the wrong functional.* Indistinguishability is about the *worst* or most diagnostic tokens (a wrong proof step, an incoherent plan), not the average. Rho-1 shows token losses are highly heterogeneous. A model can have tiny average KL while failing on exactly the rare tokens that carry reasoning.
  4. *Human text does not record the reasoning.* The distribution being matched is the *output* of human cognition, not the process. Matching it over k tokens for a research paper implicitly requires the model to do the research. That is fine as an *upper bound*, but it gives no handle on when cheaper routes become available.
  5. *Data limits.* Kaplan's own contradiction (Q1) and Villalobos et al.'s data-exhaustion estimates (Q6) mean the L(D) floor may stop the KL from being driven down at all on human text alone.
- **Best version.** Read strictly as an *upper bound*: "pretraining-style imitation, with no algorithmic insight beyond 2023 scaling laws, suffices for transformative AI at ≤X FLOP." History has been kind to that reading. A cheaper route (RL on verifiable rewards, test-time compute) arrived *before* the bound, which is exactly what an upper-bound method allows. The Direct Approach is therefore not refuted by "pretraining alone would not have got there by 2030", because its median (~2033) was never a claim that pretraining *would* get there first. What it cannot do is date the cheaper route.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve the paper's exact central compute estimate, the scaling law it used, its k values or its original 2023 median date (epoch.ai blocked; search budget exhausted).
- No published third-party critique specifically targeting the "average KL vs. hard tokens" issue was found in the time available.

## Q5. "Counting the OOMs": Aschenbrenner (2024), Anthropic's forecasts, Davidson's compute-centric framework (2023), Epoch's GATE

### Takeaway
By 2024 the explicit argument was no longer "pretraining loss → AGI". It was **effective compute**: physical compute (~0.5 OOM/yr) plus algorithmic efficiency (~0.5 OOM/yr), plus "unhobbling" (RLHF, chain of thought, tools, agency) as a third, explicitly non-pretraining term, calibrated qualitatively ("GPT-2 to GPT-4 took us from ~preschooler to ~smart high-schooler"). Davidson's framework and Epoch's GATE formalize the compute-centric view. In both, AGI requires ~1e36 effective FLOP with 2020 algorithms, taken from Bio Anchors, and feedback from partial automation compresses the endgame.

### Cited Findings
- **Aschenbrenner, "From GPT-4 to AGI: Counting the OOMs" (June 2024) [search summary, quoting page text]:** "AGI by 2027 is strikingly plausible." Method: "tracing trendlines in compute (~0.5 orders of magnitude or OOMs/year), algorithmic efficiencies (~0.5 OOMs/year), and 'unhobbling' gains (from chatbot to agent), we should expect another preschooler-to-high-schooler-sized qualitative jump by 2027." Baseline: "GPT-2 to GPT-4 took us from ~preschooler to ~smart high-schooler abilities in 4 years." Forward estimate: "3–6 OOMs of base effective compute scaleup, plus step-changes from chatbots to agents". Hedge: "maybe unhobbling doesn't go as far, and we are stuck with merely expert chatbots, rather than expert coworkers." Framing: "our uncertainty over what it takes to get AGI should be over OOMs (of effective compute), rather than over years." Commentary describes unhobbling as RLHF, chain-of-thought prompting, tool use and agentic scaffolding. — [situational-awareness.ai, ch. I](https://situational-awareness.ai/from-gpt-4-to-agi/); [Zvi's quotes compilation](https://thezvi.substack.com/p/quotes-from-leopold-aschenbrenners)

Epoch coded the model. — [LessWrong](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/Gc9FGtdXhK9sCSEYu/what-a-compute-centric-framework-says-about-ai-takeoff); [ACX, "Davidson On Takeoff Speeds"](https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/davidson-on-takeoff-speeds)

- **GATE's explicit statement of the "scaling hypothesis writ large" lineage [primary]:** "Historical analyses by Moravec, Good, and Kurzweil anticipated that once raw computational power approached certain thresholds, AI capabilities would see large breakthroughs, a view that has found more rigorous support in modern scaling-law research (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022)"; "Compute-based models of AI forecasting were validated further by, among others, Kaplan et al. - **RAND's characterization of Davidson's result [primary]:** "the period from 'AI that marginally accelerates R&D' to 'AI that can fully substitute for human researchers' may be compressed—potentially within a few years rather than decades—because even partial automation creates feedback effects". — [RAND 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22766)

### Inferences
- Aschenbrenner's "unhobbling" term is a pre-emptive answer to the user's puzzle. It concedes that pretraining effective compute alone does not deliver agents, and adds a separate, *unquantified* term. That makes the argument robust to the RL turn, at a cost: the term has no scaling law. In the 2024 text it was calibrated only by examples (RLHF, CoT), so it can absorb almost any outcome. This is an unfalsifiability risk.
- Davidson and GATE inherit their capability threshold (1e36 FLOP) from Bio Anchors, so their dates depend on the brain-anchor argument, not on any loss→capability map. The scaling-law content is the claim that "effective compute" is the right state variable. That is rescue (1).
- RAND reports Amodei's mechanism (AI good at coding and research builds the next generation), and Cotra's 2022 update has the same idea (automating AI R&D suffices). Both lower the target scaling has to hit, from "AGI" to "automated AI researcher". This is a further rescue, listed as (7) in Q8.

## Q6. Would pretraining alone have got there? Evidence on diminishing pretraining returns, data limits, and the "pretraining was rate-limiting, RL elicits" view

### Takeaway
The evidence that *pure* pretraining scale-up was failing to deliver by 2024–2025 is real but specific. The loss scaling laws themselves did not visibly break. GPT-4.5 achieved "technical improvements consistent with scaling laws" but was discontinued for poor value, and GPT-5 beat it with *less* training compute. That is a failure of the loss→value map, not of the loss law. Since late 2024 the biggest capability gains have come from post-training RL and inference-time compute (IASR Oct 2025). Strong evidence also shows that RL's success depends on base-model quality: small bases fail at RL, distillation beats RL for small models, RL gains are largely bounded by base-model coverage, and a base model's pretraining-acquired behaviors predict how well RL works. Counter-evidence: prolonged RL can expand capability boundaries, and pretraining continued to grow (the four largest training runs all date from 2025).

### Cited Findings
**Evidence that pretraining alone was hitting limits**
- **Reports in late 2024 [primary for Ord's summary]:** "recent reports from unnamed employees at the leading labs suggest that their attempts to scale up pre-training substantially beyond the size of GPT-4 have led to only modest gains which are insufficient to justify continuing such scaling and perhaps even insufficient to warrant public deployment of those models (Hu & Tong, 2024). A possible reason is that they are running out of high-quality training data. While the scaling laws might still be operating… the ability to harness them through rapid scaling of pre-training may not." Ord adds: "It seems like that rate [~5×/yr pretraining compute growth 2020–2024] has now fallen, but it is not yet clear if it has gone to zero… or to some fraction of its previous rate." — [Ord, "Inference Scaling Reshapes AI Governance" (2025), arXiv:2503.05705](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.05705)
- **GPT-4.5 [primary]:** scaling laws "predict technical metrics – not end-user value… For example, OpenAI discontinued GPT-4.5 although it achieved technical improvements consistent with scaling laws, suggesting that additional scaling may not always translate into proportionate economic value". Also: "GPT-5 is more efficient than GPT-4.5, because it was likely trained with less compute, but it outperforms 4.5 on a range of benchmarks, such as GPQA Diamond". The source cited is Edelman, Denain, Sevilla & Ho, "Why GPT-5 Used Less Training Compute than GPT-4.5 (but GPT-6 Probably Won't)" (Epoch AI, 2025). — [International AI Safety Report 2026, arXiv:2602.21012](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21012)
- **The shift to post-training (Oct 2025) [primary]:** "Recent capability improvements come (in significant part) from new post-training techniques and from using more computing power during deployment. Previously, developers improved general-purpose AI models largely by using more training data and computing power during the 'pre-training' stage… Pre-training still remains important. For example, the four largest AI models yet, measured by training run size, were all published in 2025… However, many of the biggest gains in AI capabilities over the past year have come from innovations during the post-training phase." Also: "Training techniques that teach AI systems to reason step-by-step via reinforcement learning have driven these improvements, as opposed to developers building larger models, which drove previous advances." — [IASR First Key Update, Oct 2025, arXiv:2510.13653](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.13653)
- **Inference scaling as the fallback (Jan 2025) [primary]:** inference-time scaling "may provide an additional path if traditional training scaling yields diminishing returns", and "a breakdown of the main scaling laws has not been scientifically established either, despite ongoing news reports." — [International AI Safety Report 2025, arXiv:2501.17805](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.17805)
- **Data stock [primary]:** "models will utilize the full supply of public human text data at some point between 2026 and 2032, or one or two years earlier if frontier models are overtrained". The median year is 2028, with a stock of "around 4e14 tokens, corresponding to training compute of ∼5e28 FLOP for non-overtrained models". — [Villalobos et al. 2022/2024, arXiv:2211.04325](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.04325). The IASR 2026 says "approximately 10^13 tokens of high-quality text exist on the public internet, with models already training on datasets approaching this limit", with multimodal and synthetic data as mitigations. — [IASR 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21012)
- **Repetition helps only so far [primary]:** "training with up to 4 epochs of repeated data yields negligible changes to loss compared to having unique data. However, with more repetition, the value of adding compute eventually decays to zero." — [Muennighoff et al. 2023, arXiv:2305.16264](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.16264)
- **Compute kept growing regardless [primary]:** "For the most compute-intensive models, training compute has grown about 5x per year. If this trend were to continue until 2030, these models could be trained with roughly 3,000 times more compute than they are today." — [IASR 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21012)

**Evidence that pretraining was the rate-limiting input that RL then elicits**
- **DeepSeek-R1, importance of the base checkpoint [primary]:** "we experimented with smaller-scale models, specifically a 7B dense model and a 16B Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) model… these configurations consistently failed to yield meaningful improvements when evaluated on the AIME benchmark… smaller models exhibited a tendency toward repetition and were unable to effectively leverage long chains of thought… we transitioned to larger-scale models, including a 32B dense model…, a 230B MoE model…, and a 671B MoE model… With these more capable architectures, we finally observed substantial performance gains attributable to pure RL training. These findings suggest that the effectiveness of reinforcement learning from base models is highly dependent on the underlying model capacity." — [DeepSeek-AI, arXiv:2501.12948 (latest version)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12948)
- **Distillation versus RL in small models [primary]:** "First, distilling more powerful models into smaller ones yields excellent results, whereas smaller models relying on the large-scale RL mentioned in this paper require enormous computational power and may not even achieve the performance of distillation. Second, while distillation strategies are both economical and effective, advancing beyond the boundaries of human intelligence may still require more powerful base models and larger-scale reinforcement learning." — [DeepSeek-R1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12948)
- **The base model's pretraining data already contains reasoning [primary]:** "DeepSeek-V3-Base has been exposed to a significant volume of reasoning trace data. This extensive exposure equips the model with the capability to generate plausible solution candidates, from which reinforcement [learning can select]…" (text truncated in my extraction). Counterpoint in the same paper: Qwen2-Math-7B-Zero, trained before o1 to avoid contamination, "significantly outperformed the non-reasoning models like Qwen2-Math-7B-Instruct and GPT-4o", so a math-specialized 7B base *can* benefit. — [DeepSeek-R1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12948)
- **RLVR is bounded by the base [primary]:** "current training rarely elicit[s] fundamentally new reasoning patterns… base models achieve higher pass@k score when k is large… the reasoning paths generated by RLVR models are already included in the base models' sampling distribution, suggesting that their reasoning abilities originate from and are bounded by the base model… In contrast, we find that distillation can introduce new reasoning patterns from the teacher". — [Yue et al. 2025, arXiv:2504.13837](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.13837)
- **Pretraining-acquired behaviors determine whether RL works [primary]:** "Qwen-2.5-3B far exceeds Llama-3.2-3B under identical RL training… Qwen naturally exhibits these reasoning behaviors [verification, backtracking, subgoal setting, backward chaining], whereas Llama initially lacks them… leveraging continued pretraining with OpenWebMath data, filtered to amplify reasoning behaviors, enables the Llama model to match Qwen's self-improvement trajectory." — [Gandhi et al. 2025, arXiv:2503.01307](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.01307)
- **Random rewards work only on some bases [primary]:** on Qwen2.5-Math, "randomly assigned rewards yield a 21.4% absolute accuracy gain on MATH-500, compared to a 29.1% gain from ground-truth rewards… for other model families, including Qwen2.5, OLMo2, and Llama3, training with spurious rewards yields minimal improvement or even performance degradation… pretraining priors… strongly shape RL training dynamics." — [Shao et al. 2025, arXiv:2506.10947](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.10947)
- **Elicitation is cheap once the base is good [primary]:** LIMA: "Superficial Alignment Hypothesis: A model's knowledge and capabilities are learnt almost entirely during pretraining, while alignment teaches it which subdistribution of formats should be used." — [Zhou et al. 2023, arXiv:2305.11206](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11206). LIMO: "In foundation models where domain knowledge has been comprehensively encoded during pre-training, sophisticated reasoning can emerge through minimal but strategically designed demonstrations of cognitive processes." — [Ye et al. 2025, arXiv:2502.03387](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.03387). s1: supervised fine-tuning of Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct on 1,000 reasoning traces plus "budget forcing" "exceeds o1-preview on competition math questions by up to 27%". — [Muennighoff et al. 2025, arXiv:2501.19393](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.19393)
- **Test-time compute substitutes for base size only where the base is competent [primary]:** "on problems where a smaller base model attains somewhat non-trivial success rates, test-time compute can be used to outperform a 14× larger model." — [Snell et al. 2024, arXiv:2408.03314](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.03314)
- **Post-training gains are predictable from base capability [primary]:** "we show how to predict the impact of post-training interventions like Chain-of-Thought and Self-Consistency as language model capabilities continue to improve." — [Ruan et al. 2024, arXiv:2405.10938](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.10938)

**Counter-evidence: RL does more than elicit**
- **ProRL [primary]:** "prolonged RL (ProRL) training can uncover novel reasoning strategies that are inaccessible to base models, even under extensive sampling… RL-trained models consistently outperform base models across a wide range of pass@k evaluations, including scenarios where base models fail entirely regardless of the number of attempts… reasoning boundary improvements correlates strongly with task competence of base model and training duration". — [Liu et al. (NVIDIA) 2025, arXiv:2505.24864](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.24864)
- **RL follows its own predictable compute laws, but saturating ones [primary]:** "We fit sigmoidal compute-performance curves for RL training… Not all recipes yield similar asymptotic performance… Details such as loss aggregation, normalization, curriculum, and off-policy algorithm primarily modulate compute efficiency without materially shifting the asymptote… Stable, scalable recipes follow predictable scaling trajectories". This was demonstrated up to 100,000 GPU-hours. — [Khatri et al. (Meta) 2025, arXiv:2510.13786](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.13786)

### Inferences
- **What failed is narrower than "scaling laws".** The GPT-4.5 episode shows the *loss* law held ("consistent with scaling laws") while the *value* per unit of pretraining compute disappointed. That is precisely the user's puzzle: the loss extrapolation was fine, and the loss→usefulness step was the weak link.
- **The base-model evidence supports a threshold model.** RL elicitation works once the base passes a capability threshold. The threshold depends on pretraining data mix (math and reasoning traces, Qwen vs Llama) as well as on scale, and it was crossed around the 32B–671B, GPT-4-class level in 2024. On this reading pretraining scale was *necessary* (binding until ~2023–24) but not *sufficient*. After the threshold, marginal gains moved to RL and inference.
- The evidence does not settle whether pretraining *alone* (plus ever more data) would have got there by 2030 at much higher compute. The Direct Approach's upper-bound framing (~2033 median as of March 2026) remains logically consistent with "eventually, at far more compute". Data limits (2026–2032 exhaustion) make "alone" practically doubtful.
- Since the training mix of frontier models is mostly undisclosed, "pretraining alone" vs "pretraining + RL" cannot be cleanly separated in public data.

### Gaps
- **RL compute share 2025–2026:** I found no primary-source quantification in the time available. Recalled but unverified: OpenAI said o3 used ~10× o1's RL compute; xAI said Grok 4's RL compute approached its pretraining compute (Jul 2025); Epoch (2025) analyzed how far RL compute could scale before catching up with pretraining. One secondary summary of the Nov 2025 Sutskever interview says RL costs now exceed pretraining costs ([MindStudio summary](https://www.mindstudio.ai/blog/ilya-sutskever-age-of-research-scaling)), and this is unverified.

## Q7. Average loss hides the hard tokens; emergence vs "mirage"; how smooth are loss→benchmark maps?

### Takeaway
Two facts coexist. *Aggregate* downstream performance is fairly predictable from compute or loss: Owen 2024 gives ~6pp error for BBH aggregate one OOM ahead; Llama 3's two-step loss→accuracy prediction held over four OOMs; GPT-4's HumanEval pass rate was predicted from ~1,000× less compute. *Specific* capabilities are much less predictable: ~18pp error on individual tasks, emergent jumps, and multiple-choice metrics that degrade the loss signal. Du et al. offer a partial reconciliation: abilities emerge when *pretraining loss* falls below a task-specific threshold, regardless of model or data size. That makes loss a good state variable but leaves the threshold location unpredictable in advance. Average loss is dominated by easy tokens (Rho-1), so the capability-relevant signal is a small part of the average. That makes a loss→AGI map ill-conditioned, even though loss→aggregate-benchmark maps are empirically usable.

### Cited Findings
- **Ganguli et al. (Anthropic), 2022 [primary]:** "these generative models have a paradoxical combination of predictable loss on a broad training distribution (as embodied in their 'scaling laws'), and unpredictable specific capabilities, inputs, and outputs." — [Ganguli et al. 2022, arXiv:2202.07785](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.07785)
- **Wei et al., 2022 [primary]:** "We consider an ability to be emergent if it is not present in smaller models but is present in larger models. Thus, emergent abilities cannot be predicted simply by extrapolating the performance of smaller models." On loss: "cross-entropy loss improves even for small model scales where the downstream metrics… are close to random and do not improve, which shows that improvements in the log-likelihood of the target sequence can be masked by such downstream metrics. However, this analysis does not explain why downstream metrics are emergent or enable us to predict the scale at which emergence occurs." They also plot emergence against WikiText103 perplexity. — [Wei et al. 2022, arXiv:2206.07682](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.07682)
- **Schaeffer et al., 2023 ("mirage") [primary]:** "emergent abilities appear due [to] the researcher's choice of metric rather than due to fundamental changes in model behavior with scale. Specifically, nonlinear or discontinuous metrics produce apparent emergent abilities, whereas linear or continuous metrics produce smooth, continuous, predictable changes in model performance." — [Schaeffer, Miranda & Koyejo 2023, arXiv:2304.15004](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.15004)
- **Schaeffer et al., 2024 [primary]:** "downstream performance is computed from negative log likelihoods via a sequence of transformations that progressively degrades the statistical relationship between performance and scale… accurately predicting downstream capabilities requires predicting not just how probability mass concentrates on the correct choice with scale, but also how probability mass fluctuates on the alternative incorrect choices". — [Schaeffer et al. 2024, arXiv:2406.04391](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.04391)
- **Du et al., 2024 (loss as the state variable) [primary]:** "Transformer models with the same pre-training loss, but different model and data sizes, generate the same performance on various downstream tasks… a model exhibits emergent abilities on certain tasks—regardless of the continuity of metrics—when its pre-training loss falls below a specific threshold. Before reaching this threshold, its performance remains at the level of random guessing… these abilities cannot be predicted by merely extrapolating the performance trends of models with higher pre-training losses." — [Du et al. 2024, arXiv:2403.15796](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.15796)
- **Owen 2024 [primary]:** "average benchmark performance… is decently predictable as a function of training compute scale. Specifically, when extrapolating BIG-Bench Hard performance across one order of magnitude in compute, we observe average absolute errors of 6 percentage points (pp). By contrast, extrapolation for individual BIG-Bench tasks across an order of magnitude in compute yields higher average errors of 18pp." — [Owen 2024, arXiv:2401.04757](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.04757)
- **Llama 3 [primary]:** a two-stage method in which (1) the negative log-likelihood on downstream tasks is fitted against FLOPs and (2) NLL is mapped to accuracy with a sigmoid using older models. "We find this two-step scaling law prediction, which extrapolates over four orders of magnitude, to be quite accurate: it only slightly underestimates the final performance of the flagship Llama 3 model" (ARC Challenge). — [Llama Team 2024, arXiv:2407.21783](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21783)
- **GPT-4 [primary]:** final loss predicted "from models trained using the same methodology but using at most 10,000x less compute than GPT-4… The fitted scaling law predicted GPT-4's final loss with high accuracy"; HumanEval pass rate was predicted on a subset. "Certain capabilities remain hard to predict", e.g. the inverse-scaling Hindsight Neglect task, where GPT-4 reversed the trend. — [OpenAI 2023, arXiv:2303.08774](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.08774)
- **Ruan et al. 2024 [primary]:** performance is "a function of a low-dimensional capability space, and model families only vary in their efficiency in converting training compute to capabilities… several emergent phenomena follow a smooth, sigmoidal behavior and are predictable from small models; we show that the agent performance of models such as GPT-4 can be precisely predicted from simpler non-agentic benchmarks". — [Ruan et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.10938)
- **Gadre et al. 2024 [primary]:** "we relate the perplexity of a language model to its downstream task performance by proposing a power law… to predict top-1 error averaged over downstream tasks… using experiments that take 20× less compute." — [Gadre et al. 2024, arXiv:2403.08540](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.08540)
- **Snell et al. 2024 (emergence prediction) [primary]:** "pretraining loss is known to be highly predictable as a function of compute. However, downstream capabilities are far less predictable—sometimes even exhibiting emergent jumps… Using only small-scale LLMs, we find that, in some cases, we can accurately predict whether models trained with up to 4x more compute have emerged." — [Snell et al. 2024, arXiv:2411.16035](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.16035)
- **Token heterogeneity [primary]:** see Rho-1 in Q4. — [Lin et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.07965)
- **Consensus as of 2025–2026 [primary]:** "While aggregate AI capabilities improve predictably with scale, it is difficult to predict when specific capabilities will appear." — [IASR 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.17805). "Researchers cannot reliably predict when AI systems will have certain capabilities… The relationship between benchmark performance and real-world performance also remains poorly understood." Capabilities remain "jagged". — [IASR 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21012)

### Inferences
- **The ill-conditioning argument, made explicit.** Suppose a fraction f of tokens are "reasoning-critical" and carry excess loss ℓ_r, and the rest carry ℓ_e. Average reducible loss is f·ℓ_r + (1−f)·ℓ_e. If f is ~10⁻³–10⁻², even large changes in ℓ_r (the capability we care about) move the average by thousandths of a nat, less than the uncertainty in the floor E (~0.1 nat, Q1). Inverting from average loss to reasoning ability is then numerically ill-posed. Kaplan's "small decrease… significant boost in qualitative performance" is the optimistic reading of the same arithmetic. (The f values are illustrative assumptions, not measurements.)
- **Du et al. turn this into a partial rescue.** If same-loss models have the same downstream performance, loss is a *sufficient statistic* (for fixed data and tokenizer), and capability thresholds are thresholds in loss. Scaling laws then predict *when* a given loss is reached. What remains unpredictable is *which* loss is needed for a capability you have not yet seen emerge, and that is the AGI question.
- The mirage debate cuts both ways for timelines. If emergence is a metric artifact, capability is smooth and extrapolable (good for rescue 4). But the metrics that matter for AGI (did the proof work, did the agent finish the task) are inherently thresholded, so apparent discontinuities will show up in practice anyway.

### Gaps
- I found no paper that directly measures how much of average pretraining loss is attributable to "reasoning-critical" tokens.
- Not retrieved: any 2025–2026 work mapping *base-model* loss to *post-RL* capability quantitatively (beyond Ruan's CoT predictions).

## Q8. Steelman of "log loss keeps falling → AGI by year n", and evaluation of the candidate rescues

### Takeaway
The best steelman is not "loss L* ⇒ AGI". It is: **(i)** scaling laws are strong evidence that a generic learner turns compute into general capability smoothly, with no walls, across modalities and (later) across objectives (RL, test-time compute). **(ii)** Effective compute grows ~10×/yr (compute ~4–5×/yr times algorithmic efficiency ~3×/yr). **(iii)** The capability per OOM can be calibrated from observed jumps and anchored loosely to brain-scale compute. **(iv)** The required target is not AGI but "automated AI research", after which feedback loops take over. Read that way, the argument survives the end of pure pretraining scale-up. Much of what drove the 2020–2026 acceleration (algorithmic progress, RL, inference compute) is exactly what "effective compute" was meant to absorb. What does *not* survive is the literal Direct-Approach or Gwern-style claim that a specific pretraining loss marks the date. The surviving argument's weakest link is that the compute→capability *exchange rate* is calibrated by intuition and post hoc benchmarks, not derived.

### Cited Findings (evidence for and against each rescue)

**Rescue 1: scaling laws as evidence for the scaling hypothesis writ large; the objective was incidental; the inference runs through compute**
- *For:* exponents were "nearly universal across all data domains", and the laws "suggest a shift in perspective away from the particularities of neural architectures, loss functions, and training algorithms". — [Henighan et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.14701). GATE: compute-based forecasting was "validated further by… Kaplan et al. (2020)", effective compute is its core variable, and it quotes Sutton's Bitter Lesson. — [GATE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.04941). RL compute also follows predictable curves. — [Khatri et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.13786). Test-time compute trades off against train-time compute in AlphaZero/Hex: "the test-time and train-time compute available to an agent can be traded off while maintaining performance". — [Jones 2021, arXiv:2104.03113](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.03113). Model families "only vary in their efficiency in converting training compute to capabilities". — [Ruan et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.10938)
- *Against:* compute alone does not give the *amount*. That needs an anchor, and the anchor-based models (Bio Anchors) were off mainly because algorithmic progress, an input the scaling laws treat as exogenous, ran ~200%/yr against 30%. — [ACX 2026](https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/what-happened-with-bio-anchors). RL curves are *sigmoidal* with recipe-dependent asymptotes, not open-ended power laws. — [Khatri et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.13786). Retrospective analyses of hardware-parity arguments (Moravec, Kurzweil) suggest "algorithms, data, and training methodology appear to matter independently, beyond matching hardware capacity". — [RAND 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22766)

**Rescue 2: pretraining scale was the binding constraint; cheap elicitation follows once the base is good enough (evolution ≈ pretraining, lifetime learning ≈ RL)**
- *For:* DeepSeek's 7B/16B bases failed at pure RL while 32B+ succeeded; distillation beats RL for small models; RLVR is bounded by base coverage; Qwen/Llama behavior differences are fixed by continued pretraining; spurious rewards work only on suitable bases; LIMA's and LIMO's hypotheses; s1's 1,000-example result (all in Q6). — [DeepSeek-R1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12948); [Yue et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.13837); [Gandhi et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.01307); [Shao et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.10947); [LIMA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11206); [LIMO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.03387); [s1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.19393). Du et al. show abilities switch on below a *pretraining-loss* threshold. — [Du et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.15796)
- *Against:* ProRL shows RL can reach regions the base cannot. — [Liu et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.24864). By 2025 the marginal gains came from post-training, not bigger bases. — [IASR Oct 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.13653). A model trained with less compute (GPT-5) beat the bigger-pretraining model (GPT-4.5). — [IASR 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21012). DeepSeek itself says going beyond human level "may still require more powerful base models *and* larger-scale reinforcement learning". — [DeepSeek-R1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12948)

**Rescue 3: "effective compute" OOM-counting calibrated qualitatively (GPT-2→GPT-4 repeated)**
- *For:* this was the stated method of Anthropic (2023) and Aschenbrenner (2024) (Q2, Q5). Qualitative jumps did continue: IMO gold-level performance, >60% on SWE-bench Verified, and AI agents doing multi-hour tasks by 2025. — [IASR Oct 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.13653). METR measured Claude Opus 4.5 (Dec 2025) at a ~5-hour 50% horizon, "above the exponential trend". — [RAND 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22766)
- *Against:* Anthropic concedes that "the vast majority of internal disagreement here is in the intuition for extrapolating subsequent capabilities jumps". — [Core Views](https://www.anthropic.com/news/core-views-on-ai-safety). "Unhobbling" has no scaling law and can absorb any shortfall. — [Situational Awareness](https://situational-awareness.ai/from-gpt-4-to-agi/). Algorithmic progress in effective-compute accounting is measured in *perplexity-equivalent* compute (WikiText/PTB), so it inherits the loss→capability problem. — [Ho et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.05812). Capabilities are "jagged". — [IASR 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21012)

**Rescue 4: loss→benchmark maps are smooth even if loss→AGI is not, so extrapolate benchmarks**
- *For:* Owen (6pp aggregate error), Llama 3 two-step (accurate over 4 OOMs), GPT-4's HumanEval prediction, Ruan (agentic performance from simple benchmarks), Gadre (power law from loss to average error) (Q7). METR's time-horizon metric doubled "approximately every 7 months for the last 6 years", and "within 5 years, AI systems will be capable of automating many software tasks that currently take humans a month" if it generalizes. — [Kwa et al. 2025, arXiv:2503.14499](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14499). The IASR 2026 adopts this extrapolation: "tasks lasting several hours by 2027 and tasks lasting several days by 2030". — [IASR 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21012)
- *Against:* individual-task error is 18pp. — [Owen](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.04757). Downstream predictability is degraded by incorrect-choice mass. — [Schaeffer et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.04391). Emergence is predictable only up to ~4× compute ahead. — [Snell et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.16035). Benchmarks saturate and are adversarially selected. — [Kwa et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14499). The benchmark→real-world link is "poorly understood". — [IASR 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21012)

**Rescue 5: the hyperbolic-population analogy (the underlying variable persisted while the mechanism changed)**
- *For:* Amodei: "Every once in a while, the underlying thing that is being scaled changes a bit, or a new type of scaling is added". — [quoted in Ord 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.05705). There is an exchange rate between mechanisms: "increasing inference compute on the task at hand by 1 order of magnitude often improves performance as much as increasing pre-training compute by 0.5 to 1 orders of magnitude (Jones 2021, Villalobos & Atkinson 2023). So we may be tempted to see it simply as an implementation detail in the bigger story of scaling up compute in general." — [Ord 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.05705). Total frontier compute kept growing ~5×/yr. — [IASR 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21012). The idea has a lineage in Kurzweil's Law of Accelerating Returns (successive paradigms sustaining one trend). — [RAND 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22766); [GATE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.04941)
- *Against:* Ord argues "a closer look suggests that may be a mistake". Inference-at-deployment scaling changes the economics: fewer simultaneous copies, a higher price for first human-level systems, and "each additional order of magnitude that goes to inference-at-deployment may increase the cost of using these systems by up to an order of magnitude". — [Ord 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.05705). Trend-continuation arguments have counter-examples. The IASR's "progress stalls" scenario cites passenger-aircraft speed, which "climbed quickly from 1930 to 1960 before levelling off at 500 knots". — [IASR 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21012)

**Other rescues found in the literature**

Amodei on barriers "dissolving within the big blob of compute" [search summary]. — [langenkamp.io](https://langenkamp.io/entries/big-blob-of-compute/)

Amodei's stated mechanism is AI good at coding and research building the next models ([RAND 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22766)). In Davidson's model, partial automation compresses the 20%→100% gap to "potentially within a few years" ([RAND 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22766)).
- **(8) Upper-bound reading:** the Direct Approach is explicitly a "soft ceiling", so a cheaper route arriving first is consistent with it. — [Barnett, LessWrong](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/4ufbirCCLsFiscWuY/a-proposed-method-for-forecasting-transformative-ai)
- **(9) Loss as a sufficient statistic, with abilities at loss thresholds** (Du et al.). — [Du et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.15796)
- **(10) Retrospective scoring:** the compute-centric models were wrong in the *conservative* direction. With realized inputs plugged in, they give ~2030. — [RAND 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22766); [ACX 2026](https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/what-happened-with-bio-anchors). Forecasters later lengthened from ~2027 to ~2030 (Kokotajlo), while Karpathy said "a decade away" and Sutskever "5–20 years" for human-like learning. — [RAND 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22766); [Zvi on LessWrong](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/bMvCNtSH8DiGDTvXd/on-dwarkesh-patel-s-second-interview-with-ilya-sutskever)

### Inferences (my synthesis: what can be saved)
1. **There were three distinct 2020-era inferences; only one is falsified by "pretraining alone would not have sufficed".**
   - (A) *Loss-threshold* (Sutskever, Gwern, Direct Approach): "when reducible KL on human text is small enough, the model is human-level". This is falsified as a *dating* method (ill-conditioned: uncertain floor, compounding exponents, average-vs-hard tokens, data limits), though not as an upper bound.
   - (B) *Compute-centric with an anchor* (Cotra, Kokotajlo, Davidson): "a generic learner given ~1e36 effective FLOP of training on long-horizon tasks suffices". This was objective-agnostic from the start and turned out too *conservative*.
   - (C) *Compute-centric with qualitative calibration* (Anthropic 2023, Amodei, Aschenbrenner): "a jump of the GPT-2→GPT-3 kind, but larger, gets there". This is objective-agnostic, includes non-pretraining "unhobbling", and has been roughly on track in capability terms through 2026, though AGI has not arrived and dates have slipped at the margin (2026–27 → ~2030 for some).
2. **Best single steelman.** "The scaling laws showed that capability is a smooth, wall-free function of effective compute for a generic learner. Effective compute was growing ~10×/yr. Each ~2 OOMs of effective compute had produced one large qualitative jump. ~4–6 more such jumps beyond GPT-4 plausibly reach automated AI research, after which feedback loops dominate." The pretraining loss curve is *evidence for the smoothness premise*, not a ruler for the threshold. Under this reading, replacing pretraining with RL and test-time compute as the marginal mechanism is not a refutation. It is the predicted behavior of a regime where "effective compute" can be spent in several exchangeable ways (Ord: 1 OOM of inference ≈ 0.5–1 OOM of pretraining). This is the user's rescue 5, and it is the strongest.
3. **Rescue 2 is true but weaker than it looks.** The evidence strongly supports "a good-enough base is necessary for cheap elicitation". But (a) the threshold base (GPT-4-class, 2023) arrived ~when naive extrapolations said it would, while the RL elicitation was a *discovery* (o1, Sept 2024) that no scaling law predicted; and (b) post-threshold, RL compute and algorithmic progress dominated, and neither is modeled by the pretraining law. So the scaling law predicts a *necessary-condition date*, not the AGI date. The evolution/lifetime analogy fits the necessity claim but adds no timing information.
4. **Rescue 4 is the most empirically grounded but changes the argument.** METR-style horizons and aggregate benchmarks extrapolate well over 1–2 OOMs, yet they require choosing an "AGI horizon" (e.g. month-long tasks), which is another intuition-laden threshold. They also only became available after 2023, so they cannot vindicate what people inferred in 2020.
5. **Verdict.** "Pretraining scaling laws → AGI ≈ year n" cannot be saved *as stated* if "→" means a computable loss→capability map. It can be saved as "scaling laws → (smooth compute→capability conversion) + (effective-compute growth) + (qualitative calibration or anchor) → AGI ≈ year n". In that form it made a *roughly correct directional prediction*: rapid, compute-driven capability gains through the 2020s. Its anchored versions were *too slow* (2040–2050), and its calibrated versions were *somewhat too fast* (2026–2027 for "powerful AI"), with the truth (TBD as of Nov 2026) apparently in between. The literal pretraining reading gave a correct conclusion (rapid progress) via a wrong mechanism, and the user's suspicion of that reading is justified.
