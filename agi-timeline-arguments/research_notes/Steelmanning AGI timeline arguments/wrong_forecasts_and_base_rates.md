## 1. Too early: forecasts whose dates have already passed

### Takeaway
The classic early forecasts came from the field's founders and leading experts. All of them were wrong, and by decades: Dartmouth 1955, Simon & Newell 1958, Simon 1965, Good 1965, Minsky 1967 and 1970, Turing 1950, Japan's Fifth Generation project, the 1980s expert-system boom, Musk's 2015–2020 self-driving dates, Hinton's 2016 radiology remark, and Yudkowsky's 1996 dates of 2021 and 2025. In Armstrong & Sotala's data, failed predictions look statistically just like the predictions still pending. So being "too early" is not unusual. It is what this kind of forecasting normally produces.

### Cited findings

**Dartmouth proposal (written 1955, about summer 1956).** The proposal reads: "We propose that a 2 month, 10 man study of artificial intelligence be carried out during the summer of 1956 at Dartmouth College… An attempt will be made to find how to make machines use language, form abstractions and concepts, solve kinds of problems now reserved for humans, and improve themselves. We think that a significant advance can be made in one or more of these problems if a carefully selected group of scientists work on it together for a summer." — [Armstrong, Sotala & Ó hÉigeartaigh 2014, §5.1](https://web.archive.org/web/20210217010353id_/http://www.fhi.ox.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/FAIC.pdf), quoting the proposal ([original copy](http://www-formal.stanford.edu/jmc/history/dartmouth/dartmouth.html)). Full text read via the [Lens-Academy mirror](https://github.com/Lens-Academy/lens-edu-staging/blob/11496403d01e07b0acb95fc821d8e5fcfe54eaea/articles/archive-the-errors-insights-and-lessons-of-famous-ai-predictions-and-what-they-mean-for-the-future.md).
- The same paper's verdict: "The Dartmouth conference proposal was surprisingly good – despite being wildly inaccurate, it would have seemed to be the most reliable estimate at the time." And: "by all reasonable measures, the Dartmouth conference predictions should have been much more reliable than Dreyfus's outside predictions, and yet reality was completely opposite." — [ASO 2014](https://web.archive.org/web/20210217010353id_/http://www.fhi.ox.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/FAIC.pdf)
- A RAND review adds a caution: the scale of early hype is "commonly exaggerated". It was "concentrated among a few prominent voices, while others, including Claude Shannon, expressed considerably more caution". RAND cites Muehlhauser 2016 (Open Philanthropy, "What should we learn from past AI forecasts?") for this. — [RAND RR-A4692-1, arXiv:2604.22766](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22766). The Muehlhauser report itself could not be fetched.

**Simon & Newell, 1958.** They predicted that "within ten years a digital computer will be the world's chess champion" and that "within ten years a digital computer will discover and prove an important new mathematical theorem." — [Wikipedia, History of AI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_artificial_intelligence) (read via [a GitHub copy of an older revision](https://github.com/berelevant-ai/slopless/blob/HEAD/datasets/human/reference/wikipedia/wiki-00426-history-of-artificial-intelligence.md)); also [Kuipers, "Progress in AI"](https://web.eecs.umich.edu/~kuipers/opinions/AI-progress.html) (search summary).
- **Outcome:** the predicted date was 1968. Deep Blue beat the reigning world champion in 1997, 29 years late. The search summary noted that this outcome came from background knowledge and not from the retrieved sources. **[Outcome date UNVERIFIED in session.]**
- The original appeared in *Operations Research* in 1958 ("Heuristic Problem Solving: The Next Advance in Operations Research"). **[Citation UNVERIFIED.]**

**Simon, 1965.** "Machines will be capable, within twenty years, of doing any work a man can do." The source is Simon (1965, p. 96), *The Shape of Automation for Men and Management* (Harper & Row). — [Muehlhauser & Salamon 2012, "Intelligence Explosion: Evidence and Import", fn. 4](https://intelligence.org/files/IE-EI.pdf), read via [mirror](https://github.com/Lens-Academy/lens-edu-production); see also [Quote Investigator 2020](https://quoteinvestigator.com/2020/11/11/ai-can-do/) (search summary).
- **Outcome:** the predicted date was 1985. The prediction missed by at least 40 years, and by more than that if AGI arrives in 2027–2030.

**I. J. Good, 1965 ("Speculations Concerning the First Ultraintelligent Machine").** "It is more probable than not that, within the twentieth century, an ultraintelligent machine will be built and that it will be the last invention that man need make, since it will lead to an 'intelligence explosion.'" — [Good 1965 (PDF copy)](https://flyingpenguin.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/good-1964-.pdf), read via the [Lens-Academy mirror](https://github.com/Lens-Academy/lens-edu-production).
- Elsewhere the paper says the first ultraintelligent machine "is likely to be achieved with the help of a very large artificial neural net" and "will have high linguistic ability". The *mechanism* he described looks prescient; the *date* was wrong.
- **Outcome:** the stated probability was above 50% by 2000. It missed by about 30 years.

**Minsky, 1967.** "Within a generation… the problem of creating 'artificial intelligence' will substantially be solved." — [Wikipedia, History of AI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_artificial_intelligence) (GitHub copy); [Kuipers](https://web.eecs.umich.edu/~kuipers/opinions/AI-progress.html) (search summary).
- **Outcome:** "a generation" means roughly 1990–2000. Missed.

**Minsky, 1970.** "In from three to eight years we will have a machine with the general intelligence of an average human being." It appeared in *Life*, 20 Nov 1970, in Brad Darrach's article "Meet Shaky, the First Electronic Person". — [Wikipedia, History of AI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_artificial_intelligence); [AIWS](https://aiws.net/the-history-of-ai/this-week-in-the-history-of-ai-at-aiws-net-in-three-to-eight-years-we-will-have-a-machine-with-the-general-intelligence-of-an-average-human-being/) (search summary).
- **Outcome:** the predicted window was 1973–1978. A Conversation piece judged it "off by at least a factor of 10" — [The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/ai-is-here-and-everywhere-3-ai-researchers-look-to-the-challenges-ahead-in-2024-218218) (search summary).
- **Caveat:** no primary scan of *Life* was checked. Minsky reportedly said later that he was misquoted, but **I found no source for that** (the search turned up no dispute).

**Turing, 1950.** Turing estimated a 30% pass rate on the Turing test by the year 2000. Armstrong & Sotala open their survey with this. — [Armstrong & Sotala 2012](https://files.givewell.org/files/labs/AI/ArmstrongandSotala2012.pdf), read via the [Lens-Academy mirror](https://github.com/Lens-Academy/lens-edu-production/blob/239eca6e7d99b3588c36c3da1c6a85f4dd6a44fa/articles/armstrong-sotala-how-were-predicting-ai-or-failing-to.md).

**Japan's Fifth Generation Computer Systems project (MITI/ICOT, 1982–1992).**
- In 1981 MITI "set aside $850 million". The goal was to "write programs and build machines that could carry on conversations, translate languages, interpret pictures, and reason like human beings."
- Others responded with their own programmes: the UK's £350M Alvey project, the US MCC consortium, and DARPA's Strategic Computing Initiative.
- "By 1991, the impressive list of goals penned in 1981… had not been met. Indeed, some of them, like 'carry on a casual conversation' had not been met by 2010."
- Source for the above: [Wikipedia, History of AI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_artificial_intelligence) (GitHub copy).
- **Budget:** the most cited total is about ¥54 billion (IPSJ figure). Some sources give just under ¥57B including a 1993–94 successor phase, and one gives about $400M. — [Wikipedia, FGCS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fifth_generation_computer_systems_project) (search summary).
- Sources disagree on the verdict. All call it a commercial failure. Some say it met many of its own technical milestones (parallel inference machines, logic-programming research). — (search summary, same source)

**The 1980s expert-system boom.**
- DEC's XCON was saving "40 million dollars annually by 1986".
- "By 1985 they were spending over a billion dollars on AI."
- In 1987 "the sudden collapse of the market for specialized AI hardware… An entire industry worth half a billion dollars was demolished overnight."
- Expert systems proved "brittle"; DARPA's Strategic Computing Initiative cut AI funding "deeply and brutally".
- Source for the above: [Wikipedia, History of AI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_artificial_intelligence) (GitHub copy).
- **Gap:** no specific dated *forecasts* from expert-system promoters were collected (see Gaps).

- Electrek counts about 10 self-driving or robotaxi deadlines since 2013, "every single one that has come due has been missed".

**Hinton on radiology (2016).**
- He said it was "just completely obvious" that within five years deep learning would outperform radiologists, and that "people should stop training radiologists now". He added: "It might be 10 years, but we've got plenty of radiologists already."
- **Outcome:** Mayo Clinic's radiology staff grew 55% to more than 400. US diagnostic radiology programmes offered a record 1,208 residency positions in 2025. The US faces a radiologist *shortage*.
- In May 2025 Hinton said interpretation will be done by "a combination of AI and a radiologist". He acknowledged that he had been wrong on timing.
- Sources for the above: [The Star, 16 May 2025](https://www.thestar.com.my/tech/tech-news/2025/05/16/your-ai-radiologist-will-not-be-with-you-soon); [AuntMinnie, "Hinton acknowledges mistake…"](https://www.auntminnie.com/imaging-informatics/artificial-intelligence/article/15746014/hinton-acknowledges-mistake-in-predicting-ai-replacement-of-radiologists); [swiftbotlab write-up](https://github.com/swiftbotlab/worst-2016-tech-prediction) (search summaries; the residency and pay figures are secondary).
- Some argue he "correctly identified the destination while drastically underestimating the length… of the journey". — [Robert Wachter](https://robertwachter.substack.com/p/why-do-you-still-have-a-job) (search summary)
- **Context:** the event was a Toronto conference in Nov 2016 (Creative Destruction Lab, "Machine Learning and the Market for Intelligence"). **[Venue UNVERIFIED.]**

### Inferences
- Getting AI dates wrong has been the norm for the field's most credentialed people: Simon (later a Nobel laureate), Minsky, Good and the Dartmouth organisers. Expertise did not protect them.
- The misses were not just "early". Many put the date only 3–20 years away, a pattern Armstrong & Sotala also find in later decades. Musk and Hinton show the same failure in the deep-learning era, but for *specific applications* (driving, radiology) rather than AGI.
- **Lesson for the "hits":** Good 1965 and Yudkowsky 1996 had the right *mechanism* (neural nets, intelligence explosion; hardware extrapolation) and still missed the date by decades. So a correct mechanism does not imply a correct date. The reverse also holds: a correct date (Kurzweil 2029) does not certify the mechanism.

### Gaps
- No primary scan of *Life* (20 Nov 1970) was checked, and no source was found for Minsky's alleged later denial.
- The Simon & Newell 1958 citation (*Operations Research* 6:1) was not verified.
- No specific dated forecasts from 1980s expert-system vendors or analysts were found. Examples would be market-size projections, or Feigenbaum & McCorduck's *The Fifth Generation* (1983). The search budget was exhausted.
- Other self-driving forecasts could not be sourced: Ford (2016: Level-4 car without steering wheel by 2021), Lyft's John Zimmer (2016: majority of rides autonomous within five years), GM/Cruise (2019 launch) and Nissan (2020). The search budget was exhausted. **[All UNVERIFIED]**
- Luke Muehlhauser's 2016 Open Philanthropy report "What should we learn from past AI forecasts?" could not be reached. It is the key source for the claim that the early hype is overstated.

## 2. Too late: if AGI arrives about 2027–2030

### Takeaway
Almost every *systematic* method used between 1972 and 2023 would be badly late if AGI came in 2027–2030. That covers expert surveys, Metaculus before 2022, Cotra's biological anchors, Davidson's semi-informative priors, Hanson's fraction-of-the-way method, market interest rates, Nordhaus's growth tests, and the XPT superforecasters. Typical misses would be 15–40 years for HLMI-type questions and about 90–140 years for "full automation of labour". The surveys moved sharply earlier only *after* GPT-3 and ChatGPT. On resolved benchmark questions, XPT experts and especially superforecasters put low probability on what actually happened.

### Cited findings — expert surveys (chronological)

**Michie (poll 1972, published 1973).**
- An "opinion poll taken last year among sixty-seven British and American computer scientists working in, or close to, the machine intelligence field". It asked for years until "computing exhibiting intelligence at adult human level".
- **Responses:** 5 years 0%; 10 years 1%; 20 years 17%; 50 years 19%; more than 50 years 25%. The figures as reported sum to 62%; the rest is not given.
- **Michie's note on risk:** about half thought the risk of an ultimate "takeover" negligible, and most of the rest thought it "substantial".
- **Against AGI in 2027–2030 (about 55–58 years after 1972):** only the ">50 years" group (25%) would be right on timing. The 37% who chose ≤50 years were too early, and those who chose ≤20 years were far too early.
- Source: [Müller & Bostrom 2016, §2.4, arXiv:2508.11681](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.11681) (full text read)

**AI@50, Dartmouth (2006).** Live votes by attendees; this is a striking "too late" data point. Question: "The earliest that machines will be able to simulate learning and every other aspect of human intelligence". Out of 123 votes:

| Answer | Votes | Share | Implied date |
|---|---|---|---|
| Within 10 years | 6 | 5% | by 2016 |
| 11–25 years | 3 | 2% | 2017–2031 |
| 26–50 years | 14 | 11% | |
| More than 50 years | 50 | 41% | |
| Never | 50 | 41% | |

- With AGI in 2027–2030, **only 2% of AI@50 votes would be right**, and 82% would be wrong by decades or forever.
- These results had never been published before; Müller & Bostrom obtained them from the organisers, Moor and Heckman. — [Müller & Bostrom, arXiv:2508.11681](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.11681)

**Baum, Goertzel & Goertzel (2011; AGI-09 conference).** 21 respondents, not anonymous, response rate unknown. Median years for a 50% chance: Turing test 2040; third-grade exam 2030; Nobel-level research 2045. — [Müller & Bostrom, §2.4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.11681)

**Sandberg & Bostrom (2011; Winter Intelligence Conference).** 35 respondents, 41% response rate. Median 50% year for HLMI: **2050**. — [Müller & Bostrom](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.11681)

**Müller & Bostrom (fielded 2012–13; published 2014 and 2016).**
- **Groups and response rates:** PT-AI 2011 (43 of 88); AGI-12 / AGI-Impacts 2012 (72 of 111); EETN, the Greek AI association (26 of 250); TOP100 most-cited AI authors (29 of 100). In total 170 of 549 responded (31%).
- **All respondents, medians:** 10% by **2022**; 50% by **2040**; 90% by **2075**.
- **By group, 50% year:** AGI 2040, PT-AI 2048, EETN 2050, TOP100 2050. Means were much later (overall 50% mean 2081, SD 153).
- Respondents expected superintelligence within 30 years of HLMI.
- Source: [arXiv:2508.11681](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.11681) (full tables read)

**Grace et al. 2016 survey (published 2017, "When Will AI Exceed Human Performance?").**
- 50% chance of HLMI in **45 years** (about 2061). Full automation of jobs in **120 years** (about 2136).
- Milestone medians: translation 2024; high-school essays 2026; truck driving 2027; retail 2031; best-seller 2049; surgeon 2053.
- Asian respondents expected these dates much sooner than North Americans.
- Source: [arXiv:1705.08807](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08807)
- The 2024 paper confirms the HLMI 50% year for 2016 as **2061**. — [arXiv:2401.02843](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.02843)

**Grace et al. 2022 ESPAI.** HLMI 50% year **2060**. It was originally reported as 2059 and revised after code changes; the 2016→2022 shift was "only one year earlier". 10% year: 2029. FAOL 50% year: **2164**. — [arXiv:2401.02843](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.02843) (HTML read)

**Grace et al. 2023 ESPAI ("Thousands of AI Authors on the Future of AI").**
- Fielded 11–24 Oct 2023. 2,778 responses from 18,459 working addresses (15%).
- HLMI: 10% by **2027**, 50% by **2047**, which is 13 years earlier than 2022.
- FAOL: 10% by 2037, 50% by **2116**, which is 48 years earlier (p = .0052 under fixed-probabilities framing).
- **Framing matters:** the HLMI 50% point was 34 years out under fixed-years framing but 17 years out under fixed-probabilities framing.
- The HLMI–FAOL gap of about 70 years appeared in 2016, 2022 and 2023 alike.
- Source: [arXiv:2401.02843](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.02843)
- RAND observes that the 2023 median of 2047 "still falls within the characteristic two-decade window", which leaves open "whether empirical progress has genuinely broken the pattern or merely shifted it". — [RAND 2026, arXiv:2604.22766](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22766)

### Cited findings — models and reports
- **Cotra 2020 (biological anchors).** Median about 2050; other summaries say 2052, with about 15% by 2036. — [Epoch, "Grokking semi-informative priors"](https://epoch.ai/publications/grokking-semi-informative-priors) (search summary); "The report predicted a median of 2050" — [AI Futures Dec 2025 update](https://blog.ai-futures.org/p/ai-futures-model-dec-2025-update) (read)
  - RAND says that later updates (Cotra 2022, "Two-Year Update on My Personal AI Timelines", and Karnofsky 2021) "shifted probability mass substantially toward earlier dates". ### Inferences
- If AGI arrives in 2027–2030, the "too late" errors are **as large as, or larger than, the famous "too early" errors**, and they are spread across more *systematic* methods.
  - Surveys: 12–33 years for HLMI; 2–4 centuries for Hanson.
  - Most "outside-view" or prior-based methods (Davidson, Hanson, interest rates, Nordhaus) erred late.
  - Bio-anchors erred late at first, but used the right *variable* (compute).
- The pattern on resolved questions points one way. XPT, Steinhardt and FRI (2025–26) all show forecasters *underestimating* AI progress from 2021 to 2025, and superforecasters doing worse than domain experts. This is evidence against simply trusting generalist base rates in this domain. It is also evidence that the recent near-term "hits" are not mere luck: errors on short horizons have been *systematically* in the slow direction.
- Surveys shrank by 13 years (HLMI) and 48 years (FAOL) between 2022 and 2023. That is a *reactive* update, not a forecast. Surveys tend to follow capability shocks rather than anticipate them.

## 3. Base rates: Armstrong & Sotala, the MIRI dataset, and AI Impacts

### Takeaway
The MIRI/SIAI dataset was assembled in 2012 by Jonathan Wang and Brian Potter. It holds 257 AI predictions from 1950–2012, of which 95 give timelines; AI Impacts' edited version has about 65.
- **Armstrong & Sotala's robust finding:** timeline predictions cluster at 15–25 years out, whoever makes them and whenever.
- **Their headline finding that "experts are indistinguishable from non-experts"** came from a spreadsheet error, and both authors accept this.
- **The Maes–Garreau "rapture of the nerds" law** is *not* supported.
- **The predictions themselves:** in AI Impacts' edited data the median prediction is 2030. Predictions are widely dispersed (SD about 26 years) and have not converged.

### Cited findings — Armstrong & Sotala 2012 (published 2015 in *Beyond AI: Artificial Dreams*, pp. 52–75)
- **Sample:** 95 timeline predictions in Figure 1, cut off at 2100; "there were seven predictions setting dates beyond the year 2100, three of them expert."
- **Spread:** "expert predictions span the whole range of possibilities… fifty year gaps between predictions are common." ASO 2014 adds that, ignoring predictions after 2100, the standard deviation is 26 years for experts and 27 for non-experts, with "no signs of convergence". — [AS 2012](https://files.givewell.org/files/labs/AI/ArmstrongandSotala2012.pdf); [ASO 2014](https://web.archive.org/web/20210217010353id_/http://www.fhi.ox.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/FAIC.pdf)
- **The 15–25 year cluster.** "21 of the 62 expert predictions were in that range (34%)" (16–25 years). For non-experts it was "13 of the 33". The 15 failed predictions show "an uncanny similarity". ASO 2014 summarises: "over a third of all predictions are in this timeframe… Experts, non-experts, and failed predictions all exhibit this same pattern."

    |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
    | 0 | 5 | 4 | **14** | **7** | 5 | 5 | 3 | 2 | 6 | 1 | 2 | 0 | 1 | 1 | 6 |

    So the median expert horizon is about 26–30 years, with a strong mode at 16–20. — [Fig. 3 image](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Lens-Academy/lens-edu-production/11450062e38f25fd37ec231d873bb6a80bca10b8/attachments/armstrong-sotala-how-were-predicting-ai-or-failing-to-fig3-4c6aa2ea.png) (read off the chart; ±1 per bar)
- **Maes–Garreau law.** "Fifty-five predictions were retained, in which it was possible to estimate the predictor's expected lifespan… only twelve predictions (22% of the total) were within five years in either direction of the zero point." Conclusion: "Nerds Don't Get Raptured." The law was coined by Kevin Kelly (*Wired*) in 2007, "after being influenced by Pattie Maes at MIT and Joel Garreau". — [AS 2012 §4.2.1 and note 5](https://files.givewell.org/files/labs/AI/ArmstrongandSotala2012.pdf)
- **Kinds of method in the database.**
  - Some predictions are "unabashedly based simply on the feelings of the predictor (Good 1962; Armstrong 2007)".
  - Others are "Moore's law hence AI" arguments, which assume grind rather than insight "and that step is often absent from the argument".
  - Kurzweil 1999, Hanson 1994 and Waltz 1988 are cited as examples of better, decomposed or model-based methods.
  - Source: [AS 2012 §4.1](https://files.givewell.org/files/labs/AI/ArmstrongandSotala2012.pdf)

## 4. What fraction of pre-2012 predictions fall in 2025–2032, and what distinguishes the hits?

### Takeaway
- **Share in the window:** from the MIRI dataset's Figure 1, roughly 20–25 of the 95 timeline predictions (about a quarter) put human-level AI in 2025–2032. Almost all were made between about 1995 and 2012. This is a visual estimate.
- **What the hits share:** they were made in the 1990s–2000s with ordinary 20–35 year horizons. Most of them (Kurzweil, Legg, Moravec's PC date, Vinge, Bostrom 1998, Yudkowsky 1996) rested on hardware or compute extrapolation.
- **Hardware forecasts vs intuition:** no systematic study compares them. Only commentary exists: favourable (Kokotajlo/AI Futures), critical (Yudkowsky 2021) and mixed (RAND, ASO 2014).

### Cited findings
- **My eyeball count from Figure 1** of Armstrong & Sotala ([image](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Lens-Academy/lens-edu-production/11450062e38f25fd37ec231d873bb6a80bca10b8/attachments/armstrong-sotala-how-were-predicting-ai-or-failing-to-fig1-89400b62.png)). This is my reading of the chart, ±5; treat it as approximate.
  - About 15 expert and about 7 non-expert predictions have medians in 2026–2032, rising to about 25 if 2025 is included.
  - Nearly all were made 1995–2012, with dense clusters for predictions made 2006–2012 targeting 2026–2032.
  - About 30–33 have medians at or before 2025 (failed). About 35–40 have medians after 2032, including 7 beyond 2100.
  - Implied split: about one third failed early, about one quarter in the 2025–2032 window, about 40% later.
- **AI Impacts:** the median "median" across the edited dataset (about 65 predictions) is **2030**. — [AI Impacts](https://aiimpacts.org/miri-ai-predictions-dataset/) (search summary). So roughly half of the edited dataset's predictions are at or before 2030. Many of those before 2025 have failed, which means a 2027–2030 AGI lands right at the *centre* of the pre-2012 distribution of public statements.
- **The prominent hits: what they said and how they reasoned.**
  - **Kurzweil.** "In 1999 that AI would achieve human-level intelligence by 2029", maintained "for over 25 years". He staked it in Long Bet #1 with Mitch Kapor (Turing test by 2029). — [AI Futures Dec 2025, fn. 4](https://blog.ai-futures.org/p/ai-futures-model-dec-2025-update); [Long Bets #1](https://longbets.org/1/)
    - **Method:** the Law of Accelerating Returns plus Moore's-law extrapolation. ASO 2014 calls this an outside-view grouping of non-causal trend laws, "based strongly on Kurzweil's expert judgement". — [ASO 2014 §5.4](https://web.archive.org/web/20210217010353id_/http://www.fhi.ox.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/FAIC.pdf)
    - Horizon: 30 years.
  - **Legg.**
    - In his own words: "I've publicly held the same prediction since 2009: there's a 50% chance we'll see #AGI by 2028." — [Shane Legg on X](https://x.com/ShaneLegg/status/1999180585407848776) (search summary; post date about Dec 2025)
    - AI Futures says he has held it "since 2001", citing his Dec 2009 vetta.org post "Tick, tock, tick, tock… BING". — [AI Futures](https://blog.ai-futures.org/p/ai-futures-model-dec-2025-update); [vetta.org 2009](http://www.vetta.org/2009/12/tick-tock-tick-tock-bing/)
    - The distribution was reportedly log-normal with **mode 2025, mean 2028**. — [officechai](https://officechai.com/ai/google-deepmind-co-founder-shane-legg-has-been-predicting-agi-by-2028-since-2009-and-he-still-sticks-with-it/) (search summary) **[Mode/mean detail UNVERIFIED against the original post]**
    - **Method:** brain-compute extrapolation. — [AI Futures](https://blog.ai-futures.org/p/ai-futures-model-dec-2025-update)
    - Horizon: 19 years (from 2009) or 27 years (from 2001).
  - **Moravec.**
    - In 1988 he gave 2010 for supercomputers and 2030 for PCs; in 1999 he revised to 2040. — [AI Futures](https://blog.ai-futures.org/p/ai-futures-model-dec-2025-update)
    - In 1998 he estimated human-brain capacity at about 100 TFLOPS (10¹⁴ ops/s). SIAI noted in 2008 that this was "about ten times slower than the fastest supercomputer in 2008". — [Uncertain Future Q6](https://github.com/kanzure/uncertainfuture/blob/HEAD/web/ufHelp/Q6.html), citing [Moravec 1998, *JET* 1](http://www.transhumanist.com/volume1/moravec.htm)
    - Yudkowsky (2021): on the hardware date "Moravec just about nailed this part; the actual year was 2008", but human-level AI did not follow. — [Yudkowsky, "Biology-Inspired AGI Timelines: The Trick That Never Works"](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/ax695frGJEzGxFBK4/biology-inspired-agi-timelines-the-trick-that-never-works) (search summary)
  - **Vinge (1993).** "Based largely on this trend [computer hardware], I believe that the creation of greater than human intelligence will occur during the next thirty years… I'll be surprised if this event occurs before 2005 or after 2030." Horizon: 12–37 years. — [Vinge 1993](https://github.com/yuxi-liu-wired/yuxi.ml/blob/HEAD/source/docs/posts/1993-vernor-vinge/index.qmd)
    - In the 2003 reprint Vinge "notes the insufficiency of this reasoning: even if we acquire hardware sufficient for AI, we may not have the software problem solved". — [Muehlhauser & Salamon 2012](https://intelligence.org/files/IE-EI.pdf)
  - **Bostrom (1998, "How long before superintelligence?").** "This paper outlines the case for believing that we will have superhuman artificial intelligence within the first third of the next century." **But the same page, as quoted by SIAI in Aug 2008, adds: "I would all-things-considered assign less than a 50% probability to superintelligence being developed by 2033."** This was apparently a later postscript. — [Uncertain Future Q6](https://github.com/kanzure/uncertainfuture/blob/HEAD/web/ufHelp/Q6.html), quoting [nickbostrom.com/superintelligence](https://nickbostrom.com/superintelligence) (retrieved 8 Aug 2008)
    - So the "hit" attributed to Bostrom is a claim he himself put below 50%.
    - In 1997 he had said "at least 50% … within 50 years. Perhaps I should strengthen this to 30 years?" — [Extropians 3Q97](https://github.com/weidai11/extropians/blob/HEAD/extropians.3Q97/2772.html)
  - **Von Foerster (1960).** This is not an AI forecast. It is a hyperbolic population-growth fit ("Doomsday: Friday, 13 November, A.D. 2026", *Science*). Its date "was mostly a joke; the equations were not really that precise", and it was meant as an upper bound. — [Scott Alexander, "1960: The Year The Singularity Was Cancelled" (2019)](https://www.slatestarcodexabridged.com/1960-The-Year-The-Singularity-Was-Cancelled) (read via mirror); [von Foerster et al. 1960 PDF](http://www.bioinfo.rpi.edu/bystrc/courses/biol4961/Doomsday.pdf)
  - **Yudkowsky (1996)** also used hardware extrapolation, and got 2021, then 2025. — [AI Impacts list](https://aiimpacts.org/list-of-analyses-of-time-to-human-level-ai/) (search summary)
    - This is a hardware-based forecast that *missed*. It shares its method with the hits.
- **Hardware vs intuition, the commentary.**
  - **AI Futures (Kokotajlo et al., Dec 2025):** "our best guess is that overall, forecasts by quantitative trend extrapolation have a better historical track record than intuitions alone." And: "this forecasting method seems to us to have a surprisingly good track record: Moravec, Kurzweil, and Legg especially look to have made predictions a long time ago that seem to hold up well relative to what their contemporaries probably would have said." — [AI Futures](https://blog.ai-futures.org/p/ai-futures-model-dec-2025-update) (read)
  - **Yudkowsky (Dec 2021)** argued that biological-anchor estimates are "useless for timing" because they skip the knowledge or software requirement. — [MIRI/LessWrong](https://intelligence.org/2021/12/03/biology-inspired-agi-timelines-the-trick-that-never-works/) (search summary)
    - Critics replied that Moravec's 10¹³ FLOP/s and Cotra's 10¹⁵ differ by only "a few years" of hardware progress. — [LessWrong reply, "Biological Anchors: The Trick that Might or Might Not Work"](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/NGkBfd8LTqcpbQn5Z/biological-anchors-the-trick-that-might-or-might-not-work) (search summary)
  - **ASO 2014:** Moore's-law forecasts are "grind" arguments, valid only "if the predictor has shown that reaching AI does not require new insights". They also argue that model-based forecasts beat judgement-based ones. — [ASO 2014](https://web.archive.org/web/20210217010353id_/http://www.fhi.ox.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/FAIC.pdf)
  - **RAND 2026:** "Retrospective analysis suggests that algorithms, data, and training methodology appear to matter independently, beyond matching hardware capacity." — [arXiv:2604.22766](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22766)

### Inferences
- **The hits have ordinary horizons.** Kurzweil (30 years), Legg (19–27), Vinge (≤37) and Bostrom (≤35) all sit inside the 15–35 year band where Armstrong & Sotala find most predictions cluster.
  - So the hits do not stand out by an unusual *horizon*. They stand out by **when they were made** (1988–2009) and by **using a dated external anchor**: brain-equivalent compute reaching affordability.
  - A forecaster in 1965 using a "20 years" rule would have said 1985. Kurzweil in 1999, using the same rule plus a compute anchor, said 2029.
- **Two features separate the hits from a "stopped clock".**
  1. **Stability of the date.** Kurzweil kept 2029 from 1999 to 2025 and later. Legg kept 2028 from 2001 or 2009 onward. That is the opposite of the "always 20 years away" moving horizon.
  2. **An external variable.** Their dates were tied to compute, not to the forecaster's own position in time.
  - *But* the same method gave misses: Moravec's 2010, Yudkowsky's 2021 and 2025, Vinge's early bound of 2005. And Kurzweil's 2009 sub-predictions were only about 42% true or weakly true.
  - So hardware anchoring improves the base rate somewhat; it does not make the method reliable.
- **A sharper test of luck.** Among all *hardware-anchored* predictions in the dataset, how many fall in 2025–2035, and how many already failed? Compare this with *intuition-based* predictions. It needs the dataset's method column; AS 2012 categorised predictions by method ("Moore's law hence AI", feelings, models), but that coding is not in the timeline spreadsheet as far as I could tell. **[UNVERIFIED]**

### Gaps
- No systematic, published scoring of "hardware-extrapolation vs intuition" AI forecasts was found.
- Exact counts need the .xls. My numbers are eyeballed from a figure.
- Birth years for a Maes–Garreau check on the hits could not be sourced in the session: Kurzweil b. 1948, so 2029 at about 81; Vinge b. 1944, so 2030 at about 86; Moravec b. 1948; Legg about 1973; Bostrom 1973. **[UNVERIFIED]** If correct, Kurzweil's and Vinge's dates fall near their expected lifespans, which fits Maes–Garreau, while Legg's and Bostrom's do not.

## 5. Performative and self-fulfilling effects: did short-timeline forecasters help cause the outcome?

### Takeaway
There is a documented causal chain from short-timeline *belief* to lab *founding*, in DeepMind's case. Legg formed his 2028 view after reading Kurzweil's *Age of Spiritual Machines*. He co-founded DeepMind in 2010, whose early investors included Thiel and Musk. Leading lab heads hold short timelines publicly (Amodei: powerful AI "as early as 2026"). RAND explicitly flags this reflexivity, using Moore's law as the analogy. Documentation on fundraising pitches that relied on timelines was not obtained.

### Cited findings
- **Legg and Kurzweil.** Legg "actually made this prediction more than two decades ago while working as a software engineer, after reading The Age of Spiritual Machines by Ray Kurzweil". — [officechai](https://officechai.com/ai/google-deepmind-co-founder-shane-legg-has-been-predicting-agi-by-2028-since-2009-and-he-still-sticks-with-it/) (search summary, secondary)
- **DeepMind's founding and backers.** Legg met Hassabis at UCL in 2009. With Mustafa Suleyman they founded DeepMind in 2010. Early investors included Scott Banister, **Peter Thiel and Elon Musk**. — [Wikipedia, Shane Legg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shane_Legg); [Wikipedia, Google DeepMind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_DeepMind) (search summaries)
  - A single Substack narrative says Hassabis and Legg pitched at the 2010 Singularity Summit (SIAI's conference) and that Thiel invested $1.4M "within weeks". — [shortdistance substack](https://shortdistance.substack.com/p/the-year-was-2010) **[UNVERIFIED; single secondary source]**. The account is commonly attributed to Cade Metz's *Genius Makers* (2021); **not checked**.
  - The Singularity Summit was "a conference hosted by MIRI (which was then called the Singularity Institute)". — [IABIED ch. 5 online extension](https://github.com/Lens-Academy/lens-edu-production) (mirror note)
- **Lab leaders' public timelines.**
  - Amodei, Oct 2024, *Machines of Loving Grace*: "I think it could come as early as 2026, though there are also ways it could take much longer." — [darioamodei.com](https://darioamodei.com/essay/machines-of-loving-grace) (read via mirror)
  - Legg reaffirmed 2028 in 2025–26. — [X](https://x.com/ShaneLegg/status/1999180585407848776)
- **RAND on reflexivity:** "Forecasts help shape the trajectory they aim to predict. Optimistic forecasts drive funding, attract talent, and signal regulatory urgency, which may accelerate development." It quotes Gordon Moore: "Once something like this gets established, it becomes more or less a self-fulfilling prophecy… Everyone in the industry recognizes that if you don't stay on essentially that curve they will fall behind." — [RAND 2026, arXiv:2604.22766](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22766) (read)
- **RAND on market manipulation:** "participants with stakes in AI companies or the broader AI ecosystem may have incentives to distort market prices to influence narratives related to AI timelines." — [RAND](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22766)
- **The Japanese precedent** shows that forecasts and pledges can mobilise money without producing the outcome. Japan's 1981 pledge triggered the UK Alvey programme (£350M), the US MCC consortium and DARPA's Strategic Computing Initiative, which "tripl[ed] its investment in AI between 1984 and 1988". All of it was followed by the 1987–93 winter. — [Wikipedia, History of AI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_artificial_intelligence)

### Inferences
- **The performative channel is real but asymmetric.** Optimistic forecasts reliably raise capital: the 1980s, Fifth Generation and DeepMind all show this. They produce the forecast outcome only when the technology is ready; the 1980s money did not produce AI.
- So a "self-fulfilling" account of Kurzweil's or Legg's hit must explain why the 2010s money worked when the 1980s money did not. Compute availability, the very variable their forecasts tracked, is the obvious candidate. Performativity would then *amplify* a hardware-anchored forecast rather than replace it.
- **This channel cannot explain von Foerster, Vinge or Bostrom 1998.** No lab was founded on their dates. Good 1965 and Simon 1965 were also influential with funders, and their dates still failed.
- **Selection inside the performative story.** The people who founded labs (Legg, Hassabis, Sutskever, Amodei) were self-selected for short timelines. Their institutions' success makes their *forecasts* more famous, a survivorship mechanism separate from causation.

### Gaps
- No documents were obtained on OpenAI's 2015 founding (the $1B pledge and founders' stated timelines) or Anthropic's 2021 founding and 2023 fundraising. That includes any pitch-deck language on timelines, and Kurzweil's direct influence on other founders (e.g. Page hiring Kurzweil at Google in 2012). The search budget was exhausted. **[All UNVERIFIED]**
- No empirical estimate of the *size* of the performative effect was found.

## 6. Survivorship and selection: the forgotten forecasters and the full denominator

### Takeaway
The denominator is large and is mostly made up of misses or long dates. The 2012 MIRI search found 257 AI predictions from 1950–2012, of which 95 have timelines. About a third of those have already failed, about a quarter land in 2025–2032, and about 40% lie later. Add every respondent in every expert survey (170 for Müller & Bostrom, 2,778 for Grace 2023, 123 votes at AI@50, 67 in Michie's poll) and an AGI arriving in 2027–2030 is *guaranteed* to have been "predicted" by someone. The remembered hits are a small, prominent subset. It is not hard to name forecasters who were early or late and are rarely mentioned.

### Cited findings
- **Total dated predictions:** 257 predictions from 1950–2012, of which 95 have timelines. Of the 95, 7 are beyond 2100 and 15 had already failed by 2012. — [ASO 2014](https://web.archive.org/web/20210217010353id_/http://www.fhi.ox.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/FAIC.pdf); [AS 2012](https://files.givewell.org/files/labs/AI/ArmstrongandSotala2012.pdf)
  - AI Impacts' edited version has about 65 (survey summaries removed). — [AI Impacts](https://aiimpacts.org/miri-ai-predictions-dataset/)
  - A 2026 aggregator claims "10,000 predictions analyzed". — [AIMultiple](https://aimultiple.com/artificial-general-intelligence-singularity-timing) (low-quality source; unverified)
- **Forgotten "too early" forecasters with dates in the 2000s and 2010s** (examples from Figure 1 and elsewhere):
  - Turing: 30% pass by 2000.
  - Good: >50% by 2000.
  - Moravec: 2010 for supercomputers.
  - Yudkowsky: 2021/2025, and "2008 or 2010" for his institute [secondary].
  - Vinge's lower bound: 2005.
  - Bostrom's nanotech "2015?".
  - Unnamed Figure-1 entries: a 2001 expert for about 2013, a 2007 expert for about 2014, a 2006 expert for about 2016, 1995 non-experts for 2010 and 2011, 2003 non-experts for 2015 and 2017. — sources as in §1 and §4
- **Forgotten "too late" forecasters** if AGI arrives in 2027–2030:
  - Hofstadter 2008: "not in the next 60 years". — [Uncertain Future Q6](https://github.com/kanzure/uncertainfuture/blob/HEAD/web/ufHelp/Q6.html)

  - Allyn-Feuer & Sanders: 0.4% by 2043. — [arXiv:2306.02519](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.02519)

  - AGI researchers' late-prediction median (2033) is earlier than AI researchers' (2051). — [AI Impacts](https://aiimpacts.org/miri-ai-predictions-dataset/)
- **Selection in what gets remembered.** ASO 2014 chose "five famous AI predictions" (Dartmouth, Dreyfus, Searle, Kurzweil, Omohundro), and fame was itself the selection criterion. — [ASO 2014](https://web.archive.org/web/20210217010353id_/http://www.fhi.ox.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/FAIC.pdf)
  - The Kurzweil assessment insisted that predictions "had to be useful to a person in 1999 planning their future, not simply impressive to a person in 2009 looking back". — [ASO 2014, n. on assessment](http://lesswrong.com/r/discussion/lw/gbh/assessing_kurzweil_the_gory_details/)

### Inferences
**A quick luck model.** Suppose predictions are drawn from the MIRI distribution: median about 2030, SD about 26 years, and a quarter in 2025–2032.
- Then *at least* about 20 named individuals would be "right" about a 2027–2030 AGI by chance alone, out of 95 predictions.
- The prominent hits (Kurzweil, Legg, Vinge, Moravec-PC, Bostrom) form a subset of these, made famous partly *because* they hit.
- This is strong prima facie support for the selection or luck hypothesis about *which individuals* we remember.

**But it does not show that the *aggregate* was uninformative.** The dataset's centre (2030, or 2033–2037 for the more-likely-than-not crossover) is close to the assumed outcome.
- Under this framing, the community of public forecasters of 1990–2012 was roughly right *on average*. Experts in formal surveys were systematically late: 2040–2061 medians.
- So "the hits were lucky" (true at the individual level) and "public timeline statements contained signal" (plausible at the aggregate level) can both hold.

### Gaps
- No full census of dated AI predictions after 2012 was found. AI Impacts' later compilations, Metaculus and Manifold archives were unreachable.
- No analysis was found that weights forecasters by how often they are cited, which would quantify the selection effect.

## 7. Synthesis: did the prescient forecasts just get lucky?

### Takeaway
The evidence partly supports "luck plus selection" and partly undermines it.
- **For luck and selection:**
  - The field's base rate is poor.
  - Predictions cluster at 15–25 or 30 years regardless of when they are made.
  - A quarter of all pre-2012 dated predictions land in 2025–2032, so some hits were guaranteed.
  - The famous hits were made by forecasters with mediocre measured records (Kurzweil about 42% on 2009) or with hedges (Bostrom <50% by 2033).
  - Their own methods also produced misses (Moravec 2010, Yudkowsky 2021, Good 2000).
- **Against pure luck:**
  - The hits share a method (compute anchoring) and *stable* dates rather than moving horizons.
  - Since 2021, forecasters have systematically underestimated progress on resolved near-term questions, with superforecasters worst.
  - Formal surveys erred in the same "late" direction for 50 years.
- **Maes–Garreau** (predicting just before one's own death) is not supported in the data, and is not needed to explain the hits.

### Cited findings
- The 15–25 year clustering: [AS 2012](https://files.givewell.org/files/labs/AI/ArmstrongandSotala2012.pdf); [ASO 2014](https://web.archive.org/web/20210217010353id_/http://www.fhi.ox.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/FAIC.pdf).
- RAND (2026) still labels the pattern "moving horizon bias… though this finding is based on a limited dataset". — [arXiv:2604.22766](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22766)
- No Maes–Garreau effect: 22% of 55 within ±5 years ([AS 2012](https://files.givewell.org/files/labs/AI/ArmstrongandSotala2012.pdf)); r = −0.017 ([AI Impacts](https://aiimpacts.org/miri-ai-predictions-dataset/)).
- Kurzweil's 2009 record of 27% true and 15% weakly true. — [ASO 2014](https://web.archive.org/web/20210217010353id_/http://www.fhi.ox.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/FAIC.pdf)
- Bostrom's own probability below 50% for 2033. — [Uncertain Future Q6](https://github.com/kanzure/uncertainfuture/blob/HEAD/web/ufHelp/Q6.html)
- Systematic underestimation of 2021–25 progress (XPT, Steinhardt, FRI). — [FRI](https://forecastingresearch.org/research/near-term-xpt-accuracy); [RAND](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22766)
- Trend extrapolation's "surprisingly good track record" (an advocate's view). — [AI Futures](https://blog.ai-futures.org/p/ai-futures-model-dec-2025-update)
- The critic's view: hardware anchors "never work" because software is the bottleneck. — [Yudkowsky 2021](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/ax695frGJEzGxFBK4/biology-inspired-agi-timelines-the-trick-that-never-works)

### Inferences
**Suggested tests for the report.**
1. **Horizon test.** Were the hits' horizons unusual? No. They were 19–37 years, typical of the dataset. This fits luck *in timing of utterance* (being born at the right time).
2. **Stability test.** Did the hits move their date as time passed? No. Kurzweil and Legg held fixed calendar years for 25 or more years, unlike the moving-horizon pattern. This is evidence of an external anchor, not a stopped clock.
3. **Method base rate.** Among hardware-anchored forecasts, the visible hit rate is mixed: Kurzweil, Legg and Vinge hit; Moravec-1988-supercomputers, Yudkowsky-1996 and Good missed. Among intuition-based ones, the early founders all missed. A formal comparison needs the dataset.
4. **Track record outside the headline date.** Kurzweil's 42% on 2009 sub-predictions argues against general clairvoyance. Hinton's radiology miss and Musk's misses show that even deep-learning-era insiders badly mistimed *applications*.
5. **Direction-of-error test.** Pre-1980 errors were early; errors from roughly 2000 to 2023 by surveys, models and markets were mostly late. If errors were random, early and late should mix within each era. The era dependence suggests forecasters were tracking (and lagging) something real, namely compute and visible capability.

**Bottom line for the steelman.** At the individual level, the "lucky" reading is strong: some hits were guaranteed, and the famous ones are selected. At the method level it is weaker: compute anchoring plus stability looks better than chance. Under the assumed AGI date, the systematic late bias of formal surveys and markets is at least as embarrassing as the 1960s optimism.

### Gaps
- **The decisive quantitative test needs the raw MIRI and AI Impacts spreadsheets.** That test is the hit rate by method (hardware vs intuition), by era and by predictor type. Their URLs are recorded in §3 but could not be downloaded here.
- A proper Brier-style scoring of probabilistic AGI forecasts (Davidson, Cotra, Karnofsky, XPT, Metaculus) would require their full distributions, which were not retrieved.
