\section{Envelopes: the common mechanism}\label{sec:envelopes}

All our upper-bound-capable methods, with and without a prover, are instances
of one estimator. It turns a \emph{deductive} fact (``$C(x)=1$ implies
$\varphi(x)\ge1$'') and an \emph{exact} computation ($\E_D\varphi$) into an
importance sampler whose cost depends only on how loose the deduction is.

\subsection{Envelope circuits}

\begin{definition}[envelope]\label{def:envelope}
An \emph{envelope circuit} $\Phi$ is an arithmetic circuit whose leaves are
literal indicators $[x_i=b]$, constants $0,1$, and (\cref{sec:boxes})
\emph{boxes} $[B(x)=1]$ for circuits $B$; whose gates are $\Sum$ and
$\Prod$; and whose $\Prod$ gates are \emph{decomposable}: their children have
pairwise disjoint supports. It computes $\varphi:\bits^n\to\R_{\ge0}$. It
\emph{dominates} $C$ if $\varphi(x)\ge1$ whenever $C(x)=1$. Its \emph{mass} is
$Z=\E_D\varphi$, and its \emph{looseness on $C$} is $K=Z/p$.
\end{definition}

A \emph{derivation} of $\Phi$ is a subcircuit obtained by keeping, top-down,
one child of every $\Sum$ gate reached and all children of every $\Prod$ gate
reached. For literal leaves, $\varphi(x)$ is the number of derivations all of
whose leaves are satisfied by $x$. A DNF $\bigvee_j T_j$ gives the envelope
$\sum_j\prod_{\ell\in T_j}[\ell]$, and a DNNF gives the envelope obtained by
reading $\vee$ as $\Sum$ and $\wedge$ as $\Prod$.

\begin{lemma}[exact mass, exact sampling]\label{lem:envmass}
Let $\Phi$ have literal and constant leaves only, and $D$ be a product
distribution. Then $Z=\E_D\varphi$ is computed by one bottom-up pass
($\Sum\mapsto$ sum of children's masses, $\Prod\mapsto$ product), and
the following top-down procedure samples exactly from
$Q_\varphi(x)=D(x)\varphi(x)/Z$: at a $\Sum$ gate choose a child with
probability proportional to its mass; at a $\Prod$ gate visit all children; at
a literal leaf set the literal; finally draw every unset variable from $D$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By decomposability and independence, $\E_D\prod_c\varphi_c=\prod_c\E_D\varphi_c$;
linearity handles $\Sum$. The procedure selects a derivation $\mathcal T$ with
probability $w(\mathcal T)/Z$, where $w(\mathcal T)=\prod_{\ell\in\mathcal
T}D(\ell)$ (the choice probabilities telescope), and then draws $x\sim D$
conditioned on all literals of $\mathcal T$, whose density is
$D(x)\one[x\models\mathcal T]/w(\mathcal T)$. Summing over derivations,
$\Pr[x]=\sum_{\mathcal T}D(x)\one[x\models\mathcal T]/Z=D(x)\varphi(x)/Z$.
(A literal is set at most once per derivation: two literals on the same
variable in one derivation would lie below different children of a
decomposable $\Prod$.)
\end{proof}

\subsection{The envelope estimator}

\begin{theorem}[envelope estimator]\label{thm:envelope}
Let $\Phi$ dominate $C$, with exact mass $Z$ and an exact sampler for
$Q_\varphi$. For $X\sim Q_\varphi$ let $W=C(X)/\varphi(X)$. Then
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item $W\in[0,1]$ and $\E W=p/Z=1/K$;
\item from $N$ independent copies, the Hoeffding--KL interval $[\ell,u]$ for
$\E W$ at level $\delta$ gives an interval $[Z\ell,Zu]$ containing $p$ with
probability at least $1-\delta$, \emph{for every circuit $C$} as long as $\Phi$
dominates it;
\item if $\Phi$ does not dominate $C$, the same procedure gives a valid
interval for $\Pr_D[C(x)=1\wedge\varphi(x)>0]\le p$; in particular $Z\ell$ is
still a valid lower bound on $p$;
\item run sequentially (doubling $N$, level $\delta_j=6\delta/(\pi^2j^2)$ at
stage $j$), the procedure stops with $u\le(1+\eps)\ell$ after
$N=O\big(K(\log(1/\delta)+\log\log K)/\eps^2\big)$ samples with probability
$1-\delta$, each costing $O(|C|+|\Phi|)$ time.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
(i) If $Q_\varphi(x)>0$ then $\varphi(x)>0$; if moreover $C(x)=1$ then
$\varphi(x)\ge1$, so $W\le1$. $\E W=\sum_xD(x)\varphi(x)C(x)/(Z\varphi(x))=p/Z$.
(ii) The KL interval is valid for i.i.d.\ $[0,1]$ variables \cite{hoeffding1963}.
(iii) Without domination, $W$ may be unbounded only where $C(x)=1$ and
$0<\varphi(x)<1$; envelopes with literal leaves are integer-valued, so $W\in[0,1]$
still, and $\E W=\Pr[C=1,\varphi>0]/Z$. (For real-valued $\varphi$ clip $W$ at
1, which only decreases its mean.)
(iv) Let $\mu=1/K$. By the multiplicative Chernoff bound, once
$N\ge c\log(1/\delta_j)/(\mu\eps^2)$ the empirical mean $\hat\mu$ is within
a factor $1\pm\eps/8$ of $\mu$ with probability $1-\delta_j$. Since
$\KL(a\|b)\ge(a-b)^2/(2\max(a,b))$, every $m$ in the KL interval satisfies
$|m-\hat\mu|\le\sqrt{2\max(m,\hat\mu)\log(2/\delta_j)/N}\le(\eps/4)\hat\mu$ for
a suitable constant $c$, so $u/\ell\le(1+\eps/4)/(1-\eps/4)\le1+\eps$. The
doubling stage at which this first holds has $j=O(\log K)$, whence the
$\log\log K$ term; the union bound over stages makes all intervals
simultaneously valid.
\end{proof}

So the cost of an envelope is governed by one number. Monte Carlo is the
trivial envelope $\varphi\equiv1$, with $K=1/p$; exact computation is
$\varphi=C$ (when $C$ is itself a deterministic DNNF), with $K=1$. In between:

\begin{lemma}[what $K$ measures]\label{lem:K}
$K=\E_D[\varphi\mid C=1]+\E_D[\varphi\,\one_{C=0}]/p$. For an envelope with
literal leaves whose support is exactly $C^{-1}(1)$ (e.g.\ a DNNF for $C$),
$K$ is the expected number of derivations of a random accepted input, its
\emph{expected ambiguity}. For a DNF with $m$ terms, $K\le m$.
\end{lemma}

The first term is overcounting (union bounds); the second is mass that the
deduction failed to exclude. $K$ bounds the relative variance; its exact value is
$\frac{Z}{p}\E_D[1/\varphi\mid C=1]-1$, so what costs samples is the
\emph{dispersion} of the number of derivations, not its mean: a deterministic
DNNF has $\varphi=\one_C$ and zero variance, while
$E=\bigwedge_{i\le m}(x_i\vee y_i)$, used as its own envelope, has relative variance
$(10/9)^m-1$ under every reweighting of its $\vee$-edges. \Cref{thm:sis} removes
this cost for DNNF envelopes. In surprise-accounting terms
\cite{hilton2024surprise}: of the $\log_2(1/p)$ bits of surprise in the event
$C(x)=1$, the envelope explains $\log_2(1/Z)$ bits by deduction and exact
computation, at polynomial cost, and leaves $\log_2K$ bits to sampling, at
cost $2^{\log_2K}=K$. Monte Carlo explains nothing; exact computation explains
everything, possibly at exponential cost in $|\Phi|$.

\subsection{Dense leaves}\label{sec:boxes}

Products of independent events that are individually not rare are the most
common source of rarity, and they need not have small envelopes: an
$\wedge$ of $k$ independent random sub-circuits $B_j$, each true with
probability $1/2$, has $p=2^{-k}$ but its literal envelope may lose a constant
factor per block. Boxes repair this: a box is a sub-function used as its own
envelope, its mass estimated by Monte Carlo and its conditional distribution
sampled by rejection. Estimation errors in the masses bias the proposal, but
the bias on each derivation is a product of per-box ratios that we can bound
exactly.

\begin{theorem}[envelopes with dense leaves]\label{thm:boxes}
Let $\Phi$ dominate $C$, with literal leaves and box leaves $B_1,\dots,B_r$
($\Prod$ gates decomposable with respect to supports), where
$m_j=D(B_j)\ge1/R$ and every derivation contains at most $L$ box leaves.
Estimate each $m_j$ by Monte Carlo to an interval $[\ell_j,u_j]$ at level
$\delta/(2r)$ with point value $\hat m_j$; compute masses $\hat Z$ and sample
$\hat Q$ as in \cref{lem:envmass} with $\hat m_j$ in place of $m_j$, filling
each box by rejection sampling from $D$. For $\rho(\mathcal T)=\prod_{B_j\in\mathcal
T}\hat m_j/m_j$ let $\rho_{\min},\rho_{\max}$ be its extreme values over
derivations when each $m_j$ ranges over $[\ell_j,u_j]$ (computed by a
min-/max-product pass over $\Phi$). Then, with $[\ell,u]$ a level-$\delta/2$ KL
interval for $\E_{\hat Q}W$, $W=C/\varphi$,
\[
p\in\big[\hat Z\ell/\rho_{\max},\;\hat Zu/\rho_{\min}\big]
\]
with probability at least $1-\delta$. If each $[\ell_j,u_j]$ has relative
width at most $\eps/(4L)$ the output interval is tight after
$O(K\log(1/\delta)/\eps^2)$ samples of $\hat Q$, each costing
$O(|C|+|\Phi|+LR\max_j|B_j|)$ in expectation; estimating the boxes costs
$O(rL^2R\log(r/\delta)/\eps^2)$ evaluations.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The sampler picks derivation $\mathcal T$ with probability
$\hat w(\mathcal T)/\hat Z$ where $\hat w$ uses the $\hat m_j$, and then draws
$x$ exactly from $D$ conditioned on $\mathcal T$'s leaves (rejection sampling
is exact; decomposability makes the leaves' conditionings independent), whose
density is $D(x)\one[x\models\mathcal T]/w(\mathcal T)$ with the true masses.
Hence $\hat Q(x)=\frac{D(x)}{\hat Z}\sum_{\mathcal T\ni x}\rho(\mathcal T)$, and
\[
\hat Z\,\E_{\hat Q}W=\sum_xD(x)C(x)\cdot\frac{1}{\varphi(x)}\sum_{\mathcal
T\ni x}\rho(\mathcal T)\;\in\;[\rho_{\min},\rho_{\max}]\cdot p,
\]
because $\varphi(x)$ is the number of derivations satisfied by $x$, so the
middle factor is an average of $\rho(\mathcal T)$. On the event (probability
$\ge1-\delta/2$) that all $m_j\in[\ell_j,u_j]$, $\rho_{\min}$ and $\rho_{\max}$
bound this average; conditionally on the box estimates the KL interval fails
with probability at most $\delta/2$. With relative widths $\eps/(4L)$,
$\rho_{\max}/\rho_{\min}\le(1+\eps/(4L))^{2L}\le e^{\eps/2}$, and
\cref{thm:envelope}(iv) applies to the importance-sampling stage with $\eps/3$.
\end{proof}

With boxes, the product of $k$ independent dense blocks has $K=1$ and costs
$O(k^3R\log(k/\delta)/\eps^2)$ evaluations, against $2^{\Theta(k)}$ for Monte
Carlo; a DNF whose terms are products of dense blocks has $K\le m$
(\cref{cor:dfsb}).

\subsection{Soft envelopes: Chernoff bounds as certificates}\label{sec:tilting}

Envelopes need not be $\{0,1,2,\dots\}$-valued. If
$C(x)=[S(x)\ge t]$ with $S=\sum_{j=1}^k g_j(x_{B_j})$ a sum of
$\{0,1\}$-valued sub-circuits on disjoint blocks, then for $\theta\ge0$
\[
\varphi_\theta(x)=e^{\theta(S(x)-t)}\ \ge\ \one[S(x)\ge t],\qquad
Z_\theta=e^{-\theta t}\prod_{j=1}^k\big(1-p_j+p_je^{\theta}\big),
\]
where $p_j=D(g_j=1)$. $Z_\theta$ is the Chernoff bound, a deductively valid
upper bound, and $Q_{\varphi_\theta}$ is the exponentially tilted product
distribution, sampled block by block. This is the classical efficient
importance sampler for large deviations of sums \cite{siegmund1976,bucklew2004};
here is a clean uniform bound on its looseness.

\begin{proposition}[tilting is polynomially tight]\label{prop:tilting}
Let $\E_D S\le t<k_+$, where $k_+=|\{j:p_j>0\}|$ and $t$ is an integer, and
choose $\theta\ge0$ with $\E_{Q_\theta}S=t$. Then
$K=Z_\theta/p\le\tfrac43\big(2\sqrt{k}+1\big)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Under $Q_\theta$ the $g_j$ are independent Bernoulli$(p_j^\theta)$, so $S$ is
Poisson-binomial with integer mean $t$. A Poisson-binomial distribution is
log-concave, hence unimodal, and its mode differs from its mean by less than
one \cite{darroch1964}, so its mode is $t$. By Chebyshev,
$Q_\theta(|S-t|\le2\sigma)\ge3/4$ with $\sigma^2=\mathrm{Var}_{Q_\theta}S\le
k/4$, and this window contains at most $4\sigma+1$ integers, so
$Q_\theta(S=t)\ge\frac{3}{4(4\sigma+1)}$. Finally
$p=Z_\theta\,\E_{Q_\theta}[e^{-\theta(S-t)}\one_{S\ge t}]\ge Z_\theta\,Q_\theta(S=t)$.
\end{proof}

When the block probabilities $p_j$ are known exactly, the integer-weight case
can also be computed exactly by dynamic programming over $S$, and real weights
have a deterministic FPTAS under product distributions \cite{gkm2010,gopalan2011fptas}; the point of
\cref{prop:tilting} is that the Chernoff bound, a one-line deduction from the
syntax ``$C$ is a threshold of independent blocks'', explains all but
$O(\log k)$ bits of the surprise, and sampling pays for the rest. The same
estimator applies verbatim when the $p_j$ are estimated (boxes).

\subsection{Certified presumption of independence}\label{sec:janson}

The \emph{presumption of independence} \cite{christiano2022presumption}
estimates $\Pr[\bigwedge_iA_i]$ by $\prod_i\Pr[A_i]$. For monotone events it can
be certified: correlation inequalities bound the error in terms of the
probability carried by dependent pairs, which can be computed or estimated
without ever conditioning on the rare event.

\begin{theorem}[Harris--Janson sandwich]\label{thm:janson}
Let $C=\bigwedge_{i=1}^m\mathrm{cl}_i$ be a CNF in which every variable occurs
with only one sign, $B_i=[\mathrm{cl}_i=0]$ (a subcube), $D$ a product
distribution, $M=\prod_i(1-D(B_i))$, $\eps_0=\max_iD(B_i)$ and
$\Delta=\sum_{i\ne j,\ \sv(\mathrm{cl}_i)\cap\sv(\mathrm{cl}_j)\ne\emptyset}D(B_i\wedge B_j)$
(ordered pairs). Then
\[
M\;\le\;p\;\le\;M\exp\Big(\frac{\Delta}{2(1-\eps_0)}\Big),
\]
and $M,\Delta,\eps_0$ are computable exactly in time $O(\sum_i|\mathrm{cl}_i|^2\cdot d)$, $d$ the maximum
number of clauses sharing a variable.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Flip the variables that occur negatively; then every $B_i$ is a principal
up-set $\{x:x_{S_i}=\one\}$ under a product measure. The lower bound is the
Harris--Kleitman inequality (the events $\neg B_i$ are decreasing, hence
positively correlated) \cite{harris1960}. The upper bound is Janson's inequality
\cite{janson1990}, in the form of \cite[Thm.~8.1.1]{alonspencer} (with $\Delta$
over ordered pairs). $D(B_i)$ and $D(B_i\wedge B_j)$ are products of
marginals over $S_i$ and $S_i\cup S_j$.
\end{proof}

So for unate CNFs whose dependent pairs carry total probability $\Delta\le\eps$,
the deterministic estimate $\sqrt{M\cdot Me^{\Delta/(2(1-\eps_0))}}$ has
relative error $O(\eps)$, however small $p$ is. Example: $m=6\cdot10^4$ random
clauses of width $12$ over $2\cdot10^5$ variables give $p\approx4.7\cdot10^{-7}$ and an interval of ratio $1.17$ in one second
(\cref{sec:experiments}). Riordan and Warnke \cite{riordan2015janson} show that
Janson-type bounds hold for arbitrary up-sets, so the same sandwich applies to
$\bigwedge_iA_i$ with each $A_i$ a monotone sub-circuit in a common orientation,
with $D(B_i)$ and $D(B_i\wedge B_j)$ estimated rather than computed; we have not
worked out the resulting error budget.
