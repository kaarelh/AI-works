\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Let $C:\bits^n\to\bits$ be a Boolean circuit and $D$ a distribution on its inputs.
We want $p=\Pr_{x\sim D}[C(x)=1]$ when $p$ is tiny. Monte Carlo sampling finds
it to relative error $\eps$ with $\Theta(\log(1/\delta)/(p\eps^2))$ evaluations
of $C$: polynomial in $1/p$, so exponential in the \emph{surprise}
$\log_2(1/p)$ of the event. This note asks two questions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Without help.} For which circuits can a polynomial-time procedure,
one that may check some property of the circuit and then sample, estimate $p$
to relative error at a cost polynomial in $\log(1/p)$ instead of $1/p$, while
never being wrong (beyond probability $\delta$) on any circuit?
\item \emph{With a prover.} An unbounded prover sends one argument; a
polynomial-time verifier checks it and samples. No argument may make the
verifier accept a false claim with probability above $\delta$, on any circuit.
For which circuits do convincing arguments exist?
\end{enumerate}
These are certified versions of the low-probability estimation problems studied
by the Alignment Research Center \cite{wu2024lowprob,christiano2022presumption},
where the aim is to estimate the probability of a rare behaviour of a model
faster than by sampling, using the model's internals.

\subsection{The picture}

One idea organises almost everything: \textbf{upper bounds need deduction;
lower bounds need proposals; sampling pays for whatever surprise is left}.
\begin{itemize}
\item A lower bound $p\ge L$ can be certified by exhibiting mass: a proposal
distribution concentrated where $C=1$, with computable likelihood ratios.
Clipped importance weights make this sound for any proposal (\cref{prop:proposal}).
\item An upper bound below the sampling scale cannot be certified by
evaluating $C$, however cleverly: a verifier that makes $T$ evaluations cannot
certify $p<1/T$ (up to constants), whatever argument it is given
(\cref{thm:blackbox,thm:surprise}). It must read the circuit and \emph{deduce}
that $C(x)=1$ confines $x$ to a region of small, exactly computable measure.
\item Combining the two, an \emph{envelope}, a tractable function $\varphi$ with
$\varphi\ge\one_C$ whose mass $Z=\E_D\varphi$ is computable and from which one can
sample, yields an importance sampler with bounded weights, hence two-sided
intervals, at cost $O(K\eps^{-2}\log(1/\delta))$ where $K=Z/p$
(\cref{thm:envelope}). The envelope explains $\log_2(1/Z)$ bits of the
surprise; the remaining $\log_2K$ bits are paid for by sampling, and this
residual cost is unavoidable for anything that treats the unexplained part as
a black box (\cref{thm:surprise}(b)).
\end{itemize}
Karp--Luby--Madras estimation of DNF probabilities \cite{klm1989}, exact
counting for decision-DNNFs and bounded treewidth, exponential tilting for
large deviations \cite{siegmund1976} and the Chernoff bound are all instances.

\subsection{Results}

\paragraph{Limits (\cref{sec:limits}).} Beating sampling on all circuits is
impossible: it would put $\NP$ in $\BPP$, and with exponentially hard
pseudorandom generators no procedure beats sampling by more than a $p^{-o(1)}$
factor even on explicit small circuits (\cref{prop:worstcase}). With a prover, no
argument system is complete for upper bounds unless $\coNP\subseteq\MA$
(\cref{thm:ma}), and a verifier that only evaluates $C$ cannot certify upper
bounds below about $1/T$ with $T$ evaluations (\cref{thm:blackbox}). Interaction would trivialise the problem (sum-check
gives exact $p$ for every circuit), so the one-message model is the
interesting one.

\paragraph{Without a prover (\cref{sec:part1}).} The procedure DFSB
(Decompose--Force--Sample--Branch) recursively splits the event by exact rules
(independence of variable-disjoint parts, literals forced by propagation,
Shannon expansion) and samples only at leaves that are dense or have tight
envelopes. It is sound on every circuit, and complete, with
$\tilde O(m\,K\,d^2\eps^{-2}\log(m/\delta))$ evaluations besides its pilots, on every
circuit whose ideal $K$-explanation tree has $m$ nodes (\cref{thm:dfsb}). This covers
read-once formulas (exactly); read-once compositions of sub-circuits each of which, in
the polarity in which it occurs, is \emph{component-dense}: forcing and independence
split it into exact or $K$-dense pieces (\cref{cor:dfsb}); backdoor-style triggers;
circuits with small cutsets (exactly, by cutset conditioning); and versions of all of
these disguised by rewriting cones of logarithmic support, which are computed exactly.
DNFs and DNNFs, whose ideal trees can be exponential, are covered instead by envelope
leaves built from their own envelopes ($K$ at most the number of terms for a DNF), and
by the \#DNNF FPRAS, which the meta-procedure runs (\cref{prop:dnnf-env,thm:meta}).
Beyond DFSB:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Context width} (\cref{thm:cw}): every circuit whose NNF $N$ has no opaque
gates compiles into an equivalent DNNF of size $O(|N|(\mathrm{cw}+1)2^{\mathrm{cw}})$
($|N|=O(|C|)$ for $\{\wedge,\vee,\neg,\oplus\}$-circuits), where $\mathrm{cw}$ measures,
for the given bracketing of the $\wedge$-gates, how many variables a gate must be
conditioned on to make every $\wedge$ decomposable; with the \#DNNF FPRAS
\cite{meel2026dnnf} this gives an FPRAS whenever $\mathrm{cw}=O(\log|N|)$, e.g.\ for ORs
of bounded-pathwidth CNFs, each bracketed along a path decomposition
(\cref{lem:pathcw}), whose exact counting is $\#\mathsf P$-hard.
\item \emph{Modules} (\cref{lem:quotient}): functions of independent sub-circuits
(thresholds, majorities) are estimated through their quotients, walking the module
tree; the log-ratios of the quotient estimates add, up to lower-order terms, when each
module is estimated to small relative error on the polarity its parent uses.
\item \emph{The presumption of independence, certified}
(\cref{thm:janson-general}): for unate $\wedge$'s of arbitrary sub-circuits,
Harris and Janson inequalities pin $p$ to within a factor
$e^{\Delta/(1-\max_ib_i)}$ of the independence estimate $\prod_iD(A_i)$
($b_i=D(\neg A_i)$, $\Delta$ the probability carried by dependent pairs), at a
sampling cost linear in $\sum_ib_i\le\log(1/p)+\Delta/(1-\max_ib_i)$ and independent
of $1/p$; a covariance correction reduces the error to third order, and pairwise
data provably cannot do better than $e^{0.29\Delta}$
(\cref{prop:pairwise-barrier}).
\item \emph{Certified telescoping} (\cref{thm:cftp}): annealing estimators become
sound when the intermediate samplers are exact Las Vegas samplers (coupling from
the past) whose truncation is certified empirically; this handles unate circuits
with strong, extensive correlations whose bounding chains coalesce quickly.
\item \emph{Local thresholds} (\cref{sec:local}): for $C=[\sum_jg_j(x_{A_j})\ge t]$
with arbitrary sub-circuits $g_j$ of bounded arity and degree, whose scopes may
overlap like an expander, exponential tilting with a heat-bath sampler whose mixing is
certified by a locally checked Dobrushin condition, and a normaliser certified by
thermodynamic integration, is sound on every input (\cref{thm:local}); if every $g_j$
has a private pivotal input, it is complete with $\poly(n)/\eps^2$ samples at every
threshold reachable by a certified tilt, a range that contains thresholds with
$p=e^{-\Omega(m)}$. For supermodular statistics coupling from the past removes the
certificate (\cref{prop:local-cftp}).
\item \emph{Affine structure} (\cref{sec:affine}): certified affine gates and an exact
affine and triangular presolve (\cref{prop:presolve}) factor out the parity-check part
of the surprise exactly, in front of any sound estimator, and unions of affine
subspaces give envelopes (\cref{thm:affenv}). Parity checks are also where
decomposition fails: for $m\le n/6$ random checks and events that fill a $1/\poly(n)$
fraction of their solution set, with high probability envelopes with small leaves,
context-width compilation and DFSB's completeness guarantee cost Monte Carlo's price up
to polynomial factors, and any use of a DNNF envelope at least its square root
(\cref{thm:parity-barrier,cor:barrier-methods}). Under biased product distributions,
small $\wedge$'s of parity checks on $N$ inputs admit no sound procedure complete within
a factor $2^{N^{1-\gamma}}$ unless $\NP\subseteq\BPP$, and no argument system complete
within a factor $2$ unless $\coNP\subseteq\MA$ (\cref{thm:biased-hard}).
\item Bounded treewidth (exact), thresholds of independent blocks (exponential
tilting with $K=O(\sqrt k)$, \cref{prop:tilting}), and the known FPRAS classes
\cite{moitra2019,gkm2010}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{With a prover (\cref{sec:part2}).} Proof-carrying importance sampling:
the argument is a DAG of deductive steps checked by any Cook--Reckhow proof
system (implications, exclusivity, independence) and sampling steps with
declared confidence budgets. It is sound on every circuit and every argument
(\cref{thm:pcis-sound}), and complete on every circuit that has a short proof of
being confined to a tractable region in which it is not rare
(\cref{thm:certenv}). Lower bounds need no proof at all. XOR-propagation-tree
arguments add parity reasoning by gate propagation and Gaussian elimination, with
affine pieces under product distributions (\cref{thm:xpt}), and Gibbs envelopes extend
the soft envelopes to statistics with overlapping scopes, with the mixing certificate
recomputed by the verifier (\cref{prop:gibbs}). The verifier's cost is
$\poly(|\pi|)+2^{\sigma_u}$ (times $\eps^{-2}\log(1/\delta)$ and the cost of a
sample), where $\sigma_u$ is the surprise left unexplained by the argument. In
the black-box model both terms have a sharp meaning: each bit of argument
reduces the number of evaluations needed for a lower bound by at most a factor
$2$, and once an envelope is certified the $2^{\sigma_u}$ term cannot be avoided
for two-sided intervals (\cref{thm:surprise}). The prover helps strictly: for lower
bounds unless $\NP\subseteq\BPP$; for upper bounds on circuits with short
refutations in the verifier's proof system $P$ (and on padded versions with exactly
certified $p>0$) whenever the corresponding slice of the canonical pair of $P$ is not
separable in randomized polynomial time (\cref{prop:canonical}), which holds for
$\mathrm{TC}^0$-Frege, Frege and extended Frege if Blum integers cannot be factored by
polynomial-size circuits (\cref{cor:automatability}); and two-sidedly, for a verifier
that checks proofs in a theory such as ZFC, if factoring is not in $\BPP$. Whether
weak automatability of $P$ removes the prover's advantage on general envelope classes
is open. Upper-bound and two-sided separations cannot be derived from $\NP$-hardness by
randomized many-one reductions to gap problems with gap at least $1+1/\poly$ unless
$\coNP\subseteq\AM$.

\paragraph{Experiments (\cref{sec:experiments}).} A Python implementation
(\texttt{rareprob}) estimates, e.g., $p\approx4\cdot10^{-46}$ for an OR of eight
window-CNFs on $800$ variables to $0.4\%$ from $1{,}024$ samples (by context-width
compilation), $p\approx7\cdot10^{-25}$ for an 80-bit trigger with $2\cdot10^4$
samples, and $p\in[6.18,7.35]\cdot10^{-22}$ for a $2\cdot10^5$-clause unate CNF
deterministically; it certifies $p\in10^{[-20.30,-20.08]}$ (exact $10^{-20.19}$) for a
chain of $1600$ overlapping random local detectors with $7\cdot10^4$ Glauber samples,
where Monte Carlo would need about $10^{22}$; with SAT-solver proofs as arguments it
certifies SPN-hidden triggers, which Part~1 handles only by tabulating small cones and
branching, at a cost exponential in the trigger length; and in 2{,}450 adversarial and random trials no
interval missed $p$ more often than allowed.

\subsection{Related work}

\emph{Approximate counting.} Karp--Luby--Madras \cite{klm1989} and the stopping
rule of \cite{dklr2000} are the prototypes; for self-reducible classes, approximate
counting and approximate sampling are equivalent \cite{jvv1986}, and Karger's
unreliability FPRAS \cite{karger1999} is an early instance of a combinatorial
theorem (few near-minimum cuts) serving as the envelope. FPRASs exist for DNNF
\cite{meel2026dnnf} (and before it nFBDD \cite{meel2025nfbdd} and NFA
\cite{arenas2021nfa}), for CNFs in the local-lemma regime
\cite{moitra2019,fgyz2021,fhy2021,jpv2021,hwy2022,wangyin2024}, and for knapsack
\cite{gopalan2011fptas,gkm2010}; d-DNNF, OBDD, SDD and bounded treewidth admit exact
counting \cite{darwiche2002map,samer2010}. Hashing-based counters
\cite{chakraborty2013approxmc} use an NP oracle; partial compilation with
sampling \cite{lai2023partialkc} and AND/OR importance sampling
\cite{gogate2008andor} combine decomposition with sampling for variance reduction,
without relative-error guarantees for rare events. Certified exact model counting
\cite{bryant2023cpog} checks compilation by proofs. Affine decision trees
\cite{koriche2013} and the elimination of defined variables \cite{lagniez2016} use
parity and functional structure in exact counting, and tilting with local central
limit theorems counts independent sets of a given size \cite{daviesperkins2021,jpss2021}.
\emph{Rare-event simulation} \cite{asmussenglynn2007,bucklew2004} studies
importance sampling with bounded relative error; the soundness requirement
here (valid intervals on every input) is the computer-science twist.
\emph{Interactive proofs} certify exact counts \cite{lfkn1992} and lower bounds
\cite{gs1986}; the Merlin--Arthur setting connects to proof complexity
\cite{cookreckhow1979,kp1998,bpr2000,pudlak2003pairs} and to the class $\SBP$
\cite{bgm2006}.
\emph{Heuristic arguments.} ARC's programme
\cite{christiano2022presumption,hilton2024surprise,wu2024lowprob} seeks estimators
that use a model's internals to beat sampling, judged by ``surprise accounting'';
this note isolates the part of that programme that can be made sound, and shows
where soundness forces deduction. ARC's ``matching sampling principle''
\cite{neyman2025competing} asks for estimators that do at least as well as sampling
(on average over a distribution of circuits, without soundness), and its
``computational no-coincidence principle'' \cite{neyman2025nocoincidence}
conjectures that structure in random reversible circuits always has short
explanations checkable by a verifier; our Part~2 is a worst-case, statistically
sound relative of the latter, for probabilities instead of properties.
