\section{Experiments}\label{sec:experiments}

The package \texttt{rareprob} (Python, numpy) implements the procedures above:
bit-parallel circuit evaluation, NNF conversion with small-cone
canonicalisation, the envelope compiler and estimator with boxes, DFSB and the
meta-procedure, context-width compilation, variable elimination, exponential tilting,
the Harris--Janson sandwich, certified telescoping, the tilted Glauber estimator, the
affine presolve and affine envelopes, Gaussian-elimination forcing, a
two-watched-literal DRUP checker, the PCIS verifier for envelope arguments with an
honest prover that calls a CDCL solver with proof logging (Glucose~4), and the XPT
verifier. Threshold gates are evaluated exactly, as defined in \cref{sec:setting}, and
constants are normalised to $0$ and $1$; all threshold gates in the experiments have
integer weights and thresholds. The implementation simplifies some
procedures: it fixes the atom size at $a=12$ and the (R7) constants in DFSB, uses
greedy elimination orders, replaces the pilot of the tilted Glauber estimator by a
heuristic one, runs a subset of the modules in the meta-procedure
(\cref{sec:modules}), and expands a threshold gate only when the absolute weights of its
integer form sum to at most $4096$ (others are opaque); none of this affects
soundness. Intervals are computed as in the proofs (Hoeffding--KL intervals combined
by union bounds, and the Bernstein and Maurer bounds of \cref{sec:local}). Monte Carlo
is not run for tiny $p$: we report the number of samples its KL interval needs, at the
same $(\eps,\delta)$, when the empirical mean equals $p$ (computed for $p\ge10^{-6}$ and
extrapolated as $c/p$ below; this favours Monte Carlo). Times are wall-clock seconds on
a shared 4-core machine on which several experiments ran at once, so they indicate
orders of magnitude only. Scripts are in \texttt{experiments/}.
\Cref{tab:coverage,tab:speedup,tab:ctx,tab:janson,tab:cftp,tab:arguments} and the figures
are generated by \texttt{make\_tables.py} and \texttt{make\_figures.py} from the raw
output of the script named in each caption; \cref{tab:local,tab:affine} are transcribed
from \texttt{results/local.json} and \texttt{results/affine.json}.

\paragraph{Soundness (\cref{tab:coverage}).} We ran each procedure $100$ to $300$ times
at $\delta=0.1$ on random circuits and on families with known $p$, and counted the
intervals that miss $p$. The reference values are exact rationals: computed by
enumeration (random circuits with random marginals, DNFs, unate CNFs, and the circuits
of the arguments), or exact products of dyadic block probabilities, or, for thresholds
of blocks, exact Poisson-binomial tails over the dyadic block probabilities.
Intervals are compared with them without slack, so floating-point errors of the
procedures would count as misses. The arguments, on obfuscated DNFs, were of five
kinds: the honest envelope with a DRUP proof from the solver; a wrong envelope (one
term dropped) with the honest proof of the right one; the wrong envelope with ten random
unit clauses and the empty clause as ``proof''; an envelope disjoint from most of
$C$'s mass, with no proof; and a malformed envelope, the honest one plus a
non-decomposable product $x_i\wedge\neg x_i$ whose second literal declares an empty
support, with the honest proof and with none. No interval missed $p$ in the $2{,}250$
trials, $750$ of them with dishonest arguments, nor in the $600$ affine and $16$
local-threshold coverage runs of \cref{sec:affine,sec:local}; the intervals are
conservative (union bounds, KL intervals, outward rounding), so miss rates far below
$\delta$ are expected.

\paragraph{Cost versus rarity (\cref{fig:speedup}, \cref{tab:speedup}).}
Six families with exactly known $p$ under the uniform distribution, at $\eps=0.1$,
$\delta=0.05$, all run through DFSB except the last:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{obfuscated DNF} (\texttt{dnf-obfuscated}): $16$ random terms of width $w=8,\dots,56$ over $64$
variables, each literal routed through up to two $(x\oplus r)\oplus r$ gadgets, $r$ a
random other input variable, terms built as balanced trees, the $\vee$ written as
$\neg\wedge\neg$. Small-cone canonicalisation recovers the literals, and for $w\ge16$
the root is an envelope leaf whose envelope is the DNF itself ($K\approx1$): $1.24$ to
$1.65\cdot10^5$ evaluations and ratio $1.004$ at every $w$, against $1.33\cdot10^7$ to
$1.46\cdot10^{19}$ for Monte Carlo. At $w=8$ ($p=0.06$) the root is a dense leaf, and
the accuracy split (the leaf is run to ratio $1+\eps/3$) and the pilots make DFSB $22$
times costlier than Monte Carlo;
\item \emph{triggers} (\texttt{trigger}): $R(x_{\rm body})\wedge[x_S=a]$ with
$k=10,\dots,80$ trigger bits, the test written as $\neg(x_i\oplus a_i)$ gates and $R$
a random $40$-gate circuit drawn on $16$ body variables (of probability $0.5$ to $0.78$),
whose output depends on only $4$ to $9$ of them. Forcing finds $x_S=a$, the restricted
body is then an atom (at most $12$ variables), and DFSB computes $p$ exactly, with no
samples, down to $p=4\cdot10^{-25}$ (\cref{cor:dfsb}(b));
\item \emph{triggers with a dense body} (\texttt{trigger-cycle20}): an $\wedge$ of
$k=20,40,80$ literals and the body $[\sum_i(x_{v_i}\oplus x_{v_{i+1}})\ge12]$ around a
cycle of $20$ variables (probability $0.32$; not decomposable, and above the atom
size). After forcing, the body is one dense leaf: $1.8\cdot10^5$ evaluations and ratio
at most $1.033$ for every $k$, down to $p=2.7\cdot10^{-25}$;
\item \emph{products of atoms} (\texttt{blocks}): $\bigwedge_{j\le t}B_j$ with $B_j$
random $12$-gate circuits on disjoint $6$-variable blocks, $t=4,\dots,32$; every block
is an atom, so DFSB computes $p$ exactly by (F), (D) and (T$'$), with no samples
(\cref{cor:dfsb}(a));
\item \emph{products of dense blocks} (\texttt{blocks-cycle16}): $\bigwedge_{j\le
d}B_j$, $d=2,\dots,6$, each $B_j$ the cycle threshold above on its own $16$ variables
(threshold $12$, probability $0.059$). Each block is a dense leaf estimated to
accuracy $\eps/(3d)$ (\cref{thm:dfsb}), so the cost grows like $d^3$
(\cref{cor:dfsb}(a)): from $1.7\cdot10^7$ evaluations at $d=2$ to $4.0\cdot10^8$ at
$d=6$. Monte Carlo is cheaper for $d\le3$; the curves cross between $d=3$ and $d=4$
(speed-up $1.96$ at $d=4$), and at $d=6$ DFSB is $187$ times cheaper;
\item \emph{thresholds of blocks} (\texttt{threshold}): $[\sum_jB_j\ge t]$ over
$k=50,\dots,800$ random $8$-gate blocks on $5$ variables each, with $t$ three eighths
of the way from the mean to $k$, by exponential tilting (\cref{prop:tilting}):
$3.3\cdot10^4$ to $1.3\cdot10^5$ evaluations, with looseness $K$ from $4.7$ to $16.6$,
growing like $\sqrt k$.
\end{itemize}
The cost of Monte Carlo grows like $2^{\sigma}$ in the surprise $\sigma=\log_2(1/p)$.
Ours is zero when every leaf is exact, flat in $\sigma$ for DNFs, for triggers with a
dense body and for thresholds of blocks, and polynomial in the number of sampled
leaves, with a factor $d^3$ that lets Monte Carlo win on small products of dense
blocks. Each run took at most $1.2$ seconds, except the products of dense blocks ($8.9$ to
$528$ seconds).

\paragraph{Context width (\cref{tab:ctx}).} ORs of $t$ CNFs with clauses on
sliding windows of width $w$, each CNF along its own random variable order, built as
left-deep $\wedge$-chains in window order (the bracketing of \cref{lem:pathcw}, kept by
the unflattened NNF). The compiler of \cref{thm:cw} finds context width $w$, the bound
of \cref{ex:or-cnf}, and produces an equivalent DNNF $1.5$ to $2.6$ times the size of
the circuit; the envelope estimator on it stops at the minimum batch of $1{,}024$
samples, and for the rare instances the top $\vee$'s children are almost disjoint
($K\approx1$, ratio $1.004$). At $n=800$ and $t=8$ this gives
$p\in[3.93,3.95]\cdot10^{-46}$ in $11$ seconds, where Monte Carlo would need about
$8\cdot10^{48}$ samples. Instances with $n\le24$ agree with enumeration.

\paragraph{Certified independence (\cref{tab:janson}).} Unate random $k$-CNFs with
up to $2\cdot10^5$ clauses on up to $2\cdot10^6$ variables: the Harris--Janson
interval is deterministic and took from under a second to about $3$ minutes in Python,
and on $200$ small instances it contained the exact $p$ (computed in rational arithmetic)
every time. At $n=2\cdot10^6$ it gives $p\in[6.18,7.35]\cdot10^{-22}$ (ratio $1.19$) in
$71$ seconds; Monte Carlo would need about $1.6\cdot10^{24}$ evaluations for the same
ratio. The second-order sandwich
of \cref{app:second-order} was checked against enumeration on $382$ random instances
with no violations.

\paragraph{Certified telescoping (\cref{tab:cftp}).} Hard-core ladders: $x\sim
\mathrm{Bern}(0.3)^{n}$ and $C$ = ``$x$ is an independent set of the $2\times L$
ladder'', $L=20,40,60$. Janson's $\Delta$ is extensive, so the sandwich is useless (its
window spans a factor $25$ to $2.6\cdot10^4$); bounding-chain CFTP gives exact samples,
the truncation certificates contribute a bias factor below $e^{0.02}$, and the
certified intervals (ratios $1.29$ to $1.61$) contain the exact $p$ (a rational, from a
transfer matrix), as do the Harris--Janson windows. The number of CFTP runs grows linearly
in $L$, with the number of stages, from $2.3\cdot10^5$ to $6.8\cdot10^5$, while $1/p$ grows
exponentially. In pure Python the constants are poor (a run for a later stage makes at
least $4|A|$ single-site updates of both bounding chains, $A$ the variables of the
earlier stages, and the three instances took $22$ to $294$ seconds), so for these
moderate $p$ Monte Carlo is still faster; the point is that the cost grows polynomially
in $L$ and that the method is sound without any mixing-time proof.

\paragraph{Local thresholds (\cref{tab:local}).} \Cref{sec:local} reports the tilted
Glauber estimator on chain and random-cubic-graph local threshold circuits with random
local tables: $p=10^{-20.19}$ and $p\approx10^{-23.7}$ certified to ratios $1.66$ and
$1.84$ with about $7\cdot10^4$ chain samples, where Monte Carlo would need about
$2\cdot10^{22}$ and $3\cdot10^{25}$, and $p=10^{-169.8}$ to ratio $2.88$ with coupling from
the past beyond the Dobrushin regime. The values are checked against exact
transfer-matrix values for chains and $10^7$ Monte Carlo samples for a small cubic
instance (\texttt{exp\_local.py}).

\paragraph{Affine structure (\cref{tab:affine}).} \Cref{sec:affine} reports the
presolve, affine envelopes, XPT arguments and DFSB on parity-check families under
uniform and biased product distributions, with exact values computed without the
module under test (\texttt{exp\_affine.py}). For instance, $100$ dense random parity
checks on $600$ inputs and a 3-DNF give $p=4.30\cdot10^{-31}$, which the presolve
certifies to ratio $1.045$ with $3.3\cdot10^4$ samples, where Monte Carlo would need
$4.1\cdot10^{34}$.

\paragraph{What the prover adds (\cref{tab:arguments}, \cref{fig:proofs}).}
Hidden triggers $R\wedge T(x_S)$, with $R$ a random $40$-gate circuit on $12$ body
variables and a test that accepts only $x_S=a$: \emph{linear-hash} tests $[Mx_S=Ma]$
with a dense $(k+4)\times k$ matrix of rank $k$ over $\F_2$, and \emph{SPN} tests
$[\mathrm{SPN}(x_S)=\mathrm{SPN}(a)]$ with random bijective 4-bit S-boxes, dense
invertible linear layers and random round constants. Without a prover,
Gaussian-elimination forcing recognises linear hashes: Part~1 fixes all $k$ trigger
bits and computes $p$ exactly. SPN tests are reached only through small-cone
canonicalisation: a test on at most $a=12$ variables is an atom whose OBDD exposes
$x_S=a$ to forcing ($k=8,12$: exact, with no samples), and DFSB reduces larger tests to
atoms by branching, with about $2^{k-a}$ leaves. At $k=16$ it computed $p$ exactly
after $1.1\cdot10^8$ evaluations in its pilots ($659$ seconds); at $k=20$ it gave no
answer within $900$ seconds. With a prover, the argument is the subcube $x_S=a$ plus a
DRUP proof from the solver that $C\Rightarrow(x_S=a)$; the verifier checks it with its
own RUP checker and samples only $R$, reaching ratio at most $1.093$. The proofs found by
the solver grow exponentially with $k$ even for linear hashes ($329$ to $32{,}910$ steps
for $k=12$ to $24$; CDCL solvers do not perform Gaussian elimination); this is evidence,
not a resolution lower bound, that the class of \cref{thm:certenv} depends on the proof
system. The solver found no proof for the $16$-bit SPN instance within $1200$ seconds,
but one of $25{,}944$ steps for the $20$-bit instance, on which the verifier (proof check
and sampling) took $39.5$ seconds.

\paragraph{Surprise accounting (\cref{fig:surprise}).} For a $40$-bit trigger on a
body $R$ with $\Pr[R]=1/8$, an argument that reveals $j$ of the bits (a subcube envelope
on those bits, with a DRUP proof of domination) leaves $\sigma_u=(40-j)+3$ bits to
sampling. At $\eps=0.2$, $\delta=0.05$ the verifier's sample count grows from
$1.6\cdot10^4$ with every bit revealed to $1.7\cdot10^7$ with ten unrevealed, a factor
$2^{10}$ over ten bits; with twelve unrevealed it stopped at its cap of $2\cdot10^7$
samples with a valid interval of ratio $1.42$. This is the $O(2^{\sigma_u})$ count of
the envelope estimator, and it is consistent with the lower bound of
\cref{thm:surprise}(b) for verifiers that use the rest of the circuit only through
evaluations; that bound is a worst case over planted circuits and says nothing
specific about this instance.
