RESPONSE:
Below are the drafting problems and gaps I found in the October 6 manager's amendment text. I worked only from the text you provided, without legislative history, the committee report, or access to the markup. References to other statutes (the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act of 1980, OCSLA, UNCLOS, the APA, *INS v. Chadha*) are from my general knowledge and should be checked by staff or counsel before publication.

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A):** The 10,000-ton threshold applies "by a single vessel," so an operator can run several vessels each below the limit, or split work across affiliated companies, and fall outside the definition.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A):** Because the definition is tonnage-based, sustained recovery below 10,000 tons per year is not "commercial deep seabed mining" and is not prohibited, even if the material is sold.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A):** "Per year" has no measurement method. The text doesn't say whether tonnage counts material lifted, retained, processed, or discharged, or how partial years and multiyear averages are counted.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A):** "Other mineral resources" and "from the seabed" are undefined. Read literally, the phrase could reach hydrocarbons or subseafloor extraction (such as sulfides), which creates both scope uncertainty and a basis for challenge.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(B):** "The Secretary" is never identified. Section 6 names the Secretary of State expressly, so readers can't tell whether this refers to Commerce, Interior, or another official.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B):** The Secretary's power to determine that an activity is "comparable" has no criteria, process, notice, or record requirement. This makes the provision vulnerable to challenge as arbitrary under the APA or as an unguided delegation, and it gives operators a ready argument that their activity falls outside the definition.

7. **Sec. 2(2):** "U.S. person" covers only citizens and state-chartered corporations. It excludes LLCs, partnerships, and foreign-incorporated subsidiaries, which are common in deep-sea mining structures. "State" is also undefined, so it's unclear whether D.C. and the territories are included.

8. **Sec. 3(a):** "In the United States" is undefined. It doesn't say whether the territorial sea, the EEZ, the outer continental shelf, or the territories are included, and it's unclear whether a foreign company with a U.S. office is a "person in the United States."

9. **Sec. 3(a):** The prohibition reaches only U.S. persons "anywhere" and persons "in the United States." A foreign-incorporated operator with no U.S. nexus working in the Clarion-Clipperton Zone would fall outside it.

10. **Sec. 3(a):** "Engage in" is undefined. The prohibition doesn't reach financing, chartering, vessel or equipment supply, or processing and offtake, which are common U.S. roles in these projects.

11. **Sec. 3(b):** "Exploration license" is undefined, and the bill doesn't say which regime issues it. It likely refers to licenses under the 1980 Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act, which the bill neither mentions nor amends.

12. **Sec. 3(b):** The grandfather clause covers "mining conducted under" an exploration license, which conflates exploration with commercial recovery. It also sets no limits on volume, area, term, renewal, or transfer.

13. **Sec. 3(c):** "Activities conducted for the purpose of environmental assessment" is undefined. The exemption has no permit requirement, volume cap, duration limit, reporting duty, or approval step, so full-scale recovery could be labeled as assessment, and the exemption applies regardless of the Sec. 2(1)(A) threshold.

14. **Sec. 4(a)–(b) and Sec. 5:** The registry and reporting duties have no enforcement mechanism. Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3, so failing to register or to file a report carries no penalty.

15. **Sec. 4(b):** The reporting duty applies only to "each registered operator," so an operator that doesn't register has no reporting obligation at all. The bill also doesn't require anyone to notify NOAA of unregistered vessels.

16. **Sec. 2(3) and Sec. 4:** "Covered vessel" (more than 5,000 gross tons) doesn't match the production threshold in Sec. 2(1)(A). A vessel under 5,000 gross tons can exceed 10,000 tons per year and still be unregistered and unreported, and "equipped for the recovery of seabed mineral resources" is undefined.

17. **Sec. 4(b):** The reports have no content requirements (location, depth, tonnage, discharge, monitoring), no deadlines, no public availability requirement, and no verification beyond self-certification. The bill gives NOAA no audit authority.

18. **Sec. 4(a)–(b):** "Operator" is undefined. It's unclear whether the owner, charterer, contractor, or flag-state operator bears the registration and reporting duties.

19. **Sec. 5:** The bill doesn't designate an agency to enforce the civil penalty. It also provides no administrative penalty process, investigation or subpoena authority, injunctive relief, or vessel or equipment forfeiture. Enforcing against vessels on the high seas or under foreign flags would be very difficult.

20. **Sec. 5(a):** The penalty applies "for each day on which the violation occurs," but the bill doesn't say how days are counted for continuous vessel operations or whether each vessel, ton, or operation is a separate violation. There's also no limitations period or minimum penalty.

21. **Sec. 5:** Penalties reach only persons who "violate section 3." Financing, aiding, conspiracy, and attempt are not covered. It's also unclear whether civil liability requires any knowledge or intent.

22. **Sec. 5(a) and Sec. 8:** The civil penalty applies to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the prohibition doesn't take effect until 180 days later. This invites the argument that penalties attach to conduct that wasn't yet prohibited.

23. **Sec. 3(b) and Sec. 8:** Commercial mining during the 180-day gap appears lawful under Sec. 3. The bill doesn't say whether operations begun during that window may continue once Sec. 8 takes effect.

24. **Sec. 6:** "Shall seek to negotiate" imposes no duty to conclude an agreement, no reporting requirement, and no consequence for failure. As drafted it is aspirational.

25. **General (no provision):** The bill doesn't address the International Seabed Authority or UNCLOS Part XI, and it doesn't say whether it reaches the international seabed Area. It also doesn't state its relationship to the 1980 Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act or to OCSLA, leaving possible conflicts and no conforming amendments.

26. **Sec. 7(a):** "Serious harm" is undefined, so the standard for lifting the ban is open-ended. The bill also sets no deadline for the National Academies report, no funding, and no requirement that anyone commission it, so the lifting mechanism depends on a report that may never be produced.

27. **Sec. 7(a):** The joint-resolution mechanism doesn't say whether the resolution is presented to the President or whether expedited procedures apply. If it isn't presented, it raises *Chadha* problems. If it is, it would be subject to ordinary Senate procedure, including the filibuster, which would make the ban close to permanent in practice.

28. **Sec. 7(a) and Sec. 7(b):** The two subsections conflict. Subsection (a) keeps the prohibition in effect until Congress acts, while subsection (b) ends the entire Act 10 years after enactment. It's unclear whether the prohibition survives the sunset.

29. **Sec. 7(b):** The bill has no savings clause. It doesn't say whether pending enforcement actions, penalties for violations that occurred during the term, or registry duties survive expiration.

30. **General:** The bill has no severability clause. If a court strikes a provision, such as the Secretary's discretion in Sec. 2(1)(B) or the joint-resolution mechanism in Sec. 7(a), the whole Act could fall, or courts could parse what remains unpredictably.

31. **General:** The bill has no judicial review or citizen-suit provision and doesn't specify who has standing to sue. It also provides no appropriation for NOAA to run the registry, so the monitoring provisions may be unfunded.

**Items most likely to matter for the explainer:** the single-vessel threshold (items 1–2), the unenforceable registry (items 14–15), the open-ended assessment exemption (item 13), the Sec. 7(a)/(b) conflict (item 28), and the Sec. 5/Sec. 8 timing mismatch (item 22). These are drafting observations, not positions on the bill's merits, so the explainer can present them as questions for the markup and as points the committee may want to address in amendments.
