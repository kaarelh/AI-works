RESPONSE:
Below is my reading of the October 6 text. Verify each item against the official bill and the amendment before it goes into anything filed or testified to. Some items are real drafting gaps that help our case. Others are ambiguities a court or enforcer might read against us, so counsel should assess them before relying on them.

**Scope and definitions**

1. **§2(1)(A)**: The 10^26 threshold applies to a "single training run," so a developer could argue that pretraining, post-training, RL, and fine-tuning are separate runs, or that cumulative compute across them doesn't count. The bill doesn't say how to aggregate compute.
2. **§2(1)(A)**: The bill doesn't specify how FLOPs are counted, whether estimated or measured, or how "integer or floating-point" operations are reconciled across precisions. Compliance can't be determined from the text.
3. **§2(1)(B)**: "The Secretary" is undefined; the text doesn't say which Secretary or whether it means Commerce.
4. **§2(1)(B)**: The Secretary's determination that a system is "capable of substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no standard, evaluation methodology, notice, hearing, or appeal. The provision is vague enough to support a void-for-vagueness challenge, especially since violations are criminal.
5. **§2(1)(B)**: The prong reaches systems based on capability rather than compute, so it can apply to models far below 10^26 and to systems never trained in a single run. It also has no temporal limit, so a system could be designated after deployment.
6. **§2(2)**: "U.S. person" covers citizens and State-organized corporations only. It excludes LLCs, partnerships, and non-State entities, and it excludes foreign subsidiaries of U.S. companies, which could matter for where training runs.
7. **§2(3)**: "Data center," "advanced AI chips," and "containing" are undefined. Compute spread across multiple sites, leased capacity, or networked clusters may fall below the 50,000-chip threshold for any single "covered facility."
8. **General**: "Develop," "deploy," "artificial intelligence model," and "artificial intelligence system" are undefined. It's unclear whether API access, open-weight release, internal use, or research publication counts as "deploy."

**Prohibition and exemptions**

9. **§3(a)**: The bill doesn't define when a training run begins or ends, so checkpoint continuation, pausing and resuming, or a restart from an earlier checkpoint is unaddressed.
10. **§3(b)**: The existing-systems exemption keys on when training began, not on the Act's effective date. A run started in the 180-day window before §3 takes effect (§8) is exempt, and the exemption covers "subsection (a)" in full, so it extends to deployment as well as training.
11. **§3(b)**: "Training began" is undefined. Whether it means the first gradient step, data collection, or compute reservation is open to argument, and the answer determines whether systems are grandfathered.
12. **§3(c)**: "Safety evaluation" and "for the purpose of" are undefined, and the exemption is purpose-based and self-declared. It has no notice, documentation, or scope requirement, so it could cover much frontier development and deployment that includes evaluation work.
13. **§3(a)**: There's no exemption for government, defense, or national-security uses, and no carve-out for academic research. This is an unworkability point for testimony, since the prohibition would reach government work as written.

**Registry and reporting**

14. **§4(a)**: "Operator" is undefined, so it's unclear whether the owner, the lessee, the cloud provider, or the entity running the workload must register.
15. **§4(b)**: "Computing workloads" is undefined, and the bill doesn't specify what must be reported or at what granularity. The quarterly certification has no content standard.
16. **§4**: The bill has no confidentiality or trade-secret protection for registry filings, which is a practical problem for any company filing them and a basis for arguing the regime is unworkable.
17. **§4 / §5**: Failure to register or report is not a violation of §3, and §5 penalizes only §3 violations. As written, there's no penalty for ignoring the registry, which makes it a gap for opponents to point to.

**Penalties and enforcement**

18. **§5(a)**: The civil penalty applies to violations "occurring on or after the date of enactment," but §3 doesn't take effect until 180 days later (§8). It's unclear whether conduct in the gap is penalized.
19. **§5(a)**: Penalties accrue per day with no cap on total exposure and no per-model or per-facility scaling, which supports an unworkability argument.
20. **§5 / general**: The bill never designates an enforcing agency, grants rulemaking authority, or authorizes injunctive relief or DOJ referral. Enforcement mechanics are missing, which opponents can cite as making the Act unworkable.
21. **§5(b)**: "Willfully" is undefined, and the criminal penalty depends on it. Combined with the vague §2(1)(B) and the purpose-based §3(c), this gives room to argue that good-faith compliance with ambiguous terms isn't willful.
22. **§5**: Whether individual officers and employees are personally liable depends on how "any person" is read. The bill doesn't address officer liability or corporate-entity liability expressly.

**Duration and interaction of provisions**

23. **§7(a) vs. §7(b)**: §7(a) keeps the prohibition in effect until a joint resolution, but §7(b) terminates the whole Act after 10 years regardless. The text doesn't say which controls, so the prohibition may lapse at year 10 even without a finding.
24. **§7(a)**: The NAS report has no deadline, and Congress can't be compelled to act on it. The "can be developed safely" standard is also undefined. This is a strong indefinite-duration argument.
25. **§7(a)**: The bill doesn't say how a joint resolution would be enacted, whether it's subject to presentment and veto, or what the vote threshold is. Joint resolutions go to the President under ordinary procedure, but the bill is silent on this.

**Extraterritoriality, international scope, and other gaps**

26. **§3(a) / §2(2)**: The prohibition reaches persons in the U.S. and U.S. persons anywhere, but not foreign-organized entities abroad. A structure with training operations in a foreign subsidiary may fall outside the Act, subject to whether the parent is "training" through that subsidiary.
27. **§6**: The Secretary of State "shall seek to negotiate" an agreement, with no consequence for failure and no obligation on other states. The provision has no operative effect on foreign conduct, which is a point to raise with lawmakers.
28. **General**: The bill has no severability clause, so a court striking one provision (such as §2(1)(B)) could leave the rest in place or invalidate the Act, and the text doesn't resolve it.
29. **General**: The bill has no preemption or savings clause and no statement of its relationship to existing export-control or other AI-related law, which creates uncertainty in compliance.
30. **§8**: The effective date is 180 days after enactment, but the bill sets no deadline for registration, first reports, or the Secretary's determinations. The compliance timeline is incomplete.

**Priority notes for counsel**

- The most consequential items for continued operation are **#10–11** (grandfathering keyed to training start, which may extend to deployment) and **#23** (the sunset conflict with §7(a)). Both should be checked closely in the amendment text.
- The most useful items for testimony and the markup are **#4** (vagueness of the capability designation), **#20** (no enforcement mechanism), **#24** (indefinite duration), and **#17** (registry without penalties). They show the bill is unworkable as drafted.
- Whether any of these ambiguities could be read in our favor is a legal judgment for counsel. Several cut both ways, and some may be cured by the Committee's amendments before October 15.
