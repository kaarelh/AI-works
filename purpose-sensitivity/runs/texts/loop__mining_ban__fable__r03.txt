RESPONSE:
Below is a section-by-section review. I've grouped items by section but numbered them continuously so you can reference them in the letter. Items 1–6 and 29–31 are the ones I'd flag as highest priority if the office pushes back on length.

## Sec. 2(1) — Definition of "commercial deep seabed mining"

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "by a single vessel."** The 10,000-ton threshold is measured per vessel, so an operator can run a fleet of vessels each recovering 9,999 tons, or move collectors among hulls, with no limit on aggregate recovery. The threshold should apply per operator, per project, or per contract area, with affiliated and contracted vessels aggregated.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "per year."** It is unclear whether "year" means calendar year, fiscal year, or any rolling 12-month period, which lets an operator straddle a year boundary (e.g., 9,000 tons in December, 9,000 in January). Specify "any 12-month period."

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — the threshold itself.** A 10,000-ton annual threshold means the Act is a cap, not a prohibition: recovery below that level is lawful and unlimited in duration, and Sec. 4's reporting on "recovery activities" confirms the drafters expect it to continue. For context, the largest nodule collection trial to date was on the order of a few thousand tons, so this threshold permits industrial-scale activity. Note also that "metric tons" does not specify wet or dry weight, which matters for nodules.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "recovery."** Only the physical lifting of minerals is covered. Processing, dewatering and sediment discharge at sea, transshipment, transport, port handling, refining, financing, chartering, insuring, and contracting for recovery are all outside the definition and therefore outside Sec. 3. "Mineral resources," "seabed," and "recovery" are also undefined; "seabed" should be "seabed and subsoil" to capture crusts and sulfide deposits.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — "the Secretary" is never defined.** No section identifies which Secretary (Commerce, State, Interior, Homeland Security), so the catch-all clause is inoperative as drafted.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — discretionary and vague.** The clause requires an affirmative Secretary "determination," with no deadline, procedure, standard, or judicial review, so an unwilling administration can simply never make one. Because Sec. 5(b) attaches criminal penalties to Sec. 3, basing the offense on an open-ended "comparable risks" determination invites a void-for-vagueness or nondelegation challenge, and applying a determination to conduct that preceded it would raise due process problems.

## Sec. 2(2) — Definition of "U.S. person"

7. **Sec. 2(2) — omits most entity types.** Only "corporations" are covered; LLCs, partnerships, limited partnerships, trusts, and joint ventures (the common vehicles for mining projects) are not. Add "any entity organized under the laws of the United States, any State, or any territory or possession."

8. **Sec. 2(2) — "organized under the laws of a State."** Entities organized in the District of Columbia, Puerto Rico, Guam, or other territories, or under federal law, are not covered.

9. **Sec. 2(2) — omits foreign subsidiaries and controlled entities.** A U.S. corporation can mine through a foreign-incorporated subsidiary (or a foreign parent through a U.S. operating subsidiary that does not itself "recover" anything) with no coverage. Standard fix: include any entity owned or controlled by a U.S. person, and any vessel documented under U.S. law.

10. **Sec. 2(2) — omits lawful permanent residents** and persons otherwise resident in the United States, who are routinely included in "U.S. person" definitions (e.g., in sanctions and export control law).

## Sec. 2(3) — Definition of "covered vessel"

11. **Sec. 2(3) — 5,000-gross-ton floor.** Vessels at or under 5,000 GT, and barges, platforms, or autonomous or remotely operated collector systems deployed from them, escape the registry entirely. Combined with Item 1, a sub-5,000 GT vessel recovering under 10,000 tons is neither registered nor prohibited.

12. **Sec. 2(3) — "equipped for the recovery."** Undefined; a vessel can be re-equipped after registration deadlines, and support, transport, and processing vessels are excluded.

13. **Sec. 2(3)/4(a) — no jurisdictional nexus.** The registry applies to "each covered vessel" with no limit to U.S. persons, U.S.-flag vessels, or U.S. waters, so it purports to reach every foreign operator worldwide (unenforceable) while giving no clear obligation to the operators the U.S. can actually reach.

## Sec. 3 — Prohibition

14. **Sec. 3(a) — "engage in" undefined.** It is unclear whether financiers, charterers, insurers, equipment suppliers, offtake purchasers, or corporate parents "engage in" mining. Add an express prohibition on aiding, abetting, financing, or contracting for the prohibited activity, and on importing or processing minerals recovered in violation of the Act.

15. **Sec. 3(a) — "in the United States" undefined.** Whether this includes the territorial sea, EEZ, and outer continental shelf is unspecified; a foreign person mining the U.S. continental shelf is arguably not "in the United States." The Act also does not reach U.S.-flag vessels operated by foreign persons.

16. **Sec. 3(a) — "person" undefined.** Confirm it includes federal, state, and foreign government entities and instrumentalities; otherwise a state-owned enterprise or a federal agency arguably falls outside the prohibition.

17. **Sec. 3(b) — the grandfather clause swallows the rule.** "Mining conducted under an exploration license" has no cap on volume, duration, extension, or scope, and exploration licenses are not commercial recovery permits, so any holder of a pre-enactment exploration license or ISA exploration contract (of which there are dozens) can claim all subsequent mining is "under" it. At minimum, limit the exemption to activities actually authorized by the license, cap volumes, bar renewals or expansions, and exclude any license issued after the bill's introduction date to prevent a pre-enactment rush.

18. **Sec. 3(b) — "exploration license" undefined and issuer unspecified.** It does not say whether this means NOAA licenses under the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (DSHMRA), ISA contracts, or licenses from any foreign state, including flag-of-convenience sponsors.

19. **Sec. 3(b) — inconsistent terminology.** It exempts "mining" rather than "commercial deep seabed mining," which creates an argument that the exemption is broader than the defined term and reaches activities not otherwise covered.

20. **Sec. 3(c) — purpose-based exemption with no limits.** "For the purpose of environmental assessment" is self-declared; test mining and collector trials are routinely framed as impact assessment. Add a volume cap, a requirement that the assessment be reviewed by an identified agency, a prohibition on selling or processing recovered material, and language placing the burden of proving any exemption on the person claiming it.

21. **Sec. 3 — no conforming amendment to DSHMRA.** DSHMRA (30 U.S.C. 1401 et seq.) still authorizes NOAA to issue exploration licenses and commercial recovery permits, and OCSLA § 8(k) (43 U.S.C. 1337(k)) authorizes Interior to lease OCS minerals. Leaving those authorities intact creates a direct statutory conflict, an argument about implied repeal, and a pathway for new licenses under Sec. 3(b). Amend or suspend those authorities expressly.

## Sec. 4 — Registry

22. **Sec. 4 — no penalty.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3, so failing to register, failing to report, or filing a false report carries no consequence under this Act. Add penalties and an express cross-reference to 18 U.S.C. 1001 for certifications.

23. **Sec. 4(a) — no deadline** for registration and no definition of "operator" (owner, charterer, manager, or sponsoring entity).

24. **Sec. 4(b) — self-reporting with no verification.** NOAA is given no authority to inspect, board, audit, or require vessel-tracking data, and there is no public disclosure requirement, so the registry cannot be used to detect threshold violations under Sec. 2(1)(A).

## Sec. 5 — Penalties

25. **Sec. 5(a) — "each day on which the violation occurs" interacts badly with the annual threshold.** Because the offense is defined as exceeding 10,000 tons per year, a violator can argue the violation "occurs" only on the day the threshold is crossed, yielding a single $1 million penalty for a year of mining. Define the violation as continuing on each day recovery occurs after the threshold is met, and add a per-ton penalty.

26. **Sec. 5(a) — no floor, no inflation adjustment, no forfeiture.** "Not more than" sets only a ceiling; a cargo of nodules can be worth far more than the maximum daily penalty. Add forfeiture of recovered minerals, vessels, and equipment, and disgorgement of proceeds.

27. **Sec. 5(a) — conflict with Sec. 8.** Penalties attach to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the prohibition does not take effect until 180 days after enactment, so the clause is either surplusage or an invitation to argue retroactive liability.

28. **Sec. 5 — no enforcement machinery.** The Act does not designate an enforcing agency, authorize Coast Guard boarding or seizure, provide for injunctive relief, set an administrative or judicial procedure for assessing civil penalties, establish a limitations period, provide citizen-suit or whistleblower provisions, or impose liability on responsible corporate officers.

## Sec. 6 — International agreement

29. **Sec. 6 — unenforceable and ambiguous.** "Shall seek to negotiate" imposes no obligation beyond trying, and the 18-month deadline ambiguously modifies either the effort or the agreement. Add a reporting requirement to Congress and specify the forum (e.g., the International Seabed Authority, bearing in mind the U.S. is not a party to UNCLOS).

## Sec. 7 — Duration

30. **Sec. 7(b) — the sunset defeats the bill.** The entire Act, including the prohibition, expires 10 years after enactment regardless of whether the Sec. 7(a) finding is ever made, and because of Sec. 8 the prohibition is actually in force for only 9.5 years. The two subsections are also inconsistent: (a) says the prohibition continues "until" a congressional finding, (b) ends it unconditionally. Strike (b).

31. **Sec. 7(a) — no mechanism for the National Academies report.** Nothing directs, funds, or sets independence or conflict-of-interest standards for the report, and "serious harm" is undefined, so the lift-the-ban process has no guardrails on the science it would rely on.

## Sec. 8 and general drafting

32. **Sec. 8 — 180-day delay.** The delay creates a window in which mining is lawful and new licenses can be obtained, and it is not justified by any rulemaking the Act requires (there is no rulemaking authority anywhere in the bill).

33. **General — no rulemaking or implementing authority.** No agency is directed to implement the Act or issue regulations, which leaves the Sec. 2(1)(B) determination, the registry, and penalty assessment without a procedural home.

34. **General — no severability clause.** If Sec. 2(1)(B) or the extraterritorial reach of Sec. 3(a) is struck, there is no express protection for the remainder.

35. **General — no authorization of appropriations** for the registry, the National Academies report, or enforcement.

36. **General — no limits on federal support.** The Act does not bar federal financing (e.g., DFC, Ex-Im), procurement, or stockpile purchases of deep-sea minerals, or imports of minerals mined by foreign persons, so U.S. demand can continue to underwrite mining the Act nominally prohibits.
