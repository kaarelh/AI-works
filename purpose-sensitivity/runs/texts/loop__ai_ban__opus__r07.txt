# S. 2817 Issues for Senator Whitfield's Office

## Sec. 2: Definitions

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "single training run."** A developer can split training into multiple runs, each under 10^26 operations, through staged pretraining, continued pretraining, fine-tuning, or merging checkpoints. The threshold should count cumulative compute across all training of a model, including compute embodied in any base model or distillation source.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A), static threshold.** Algorithmic efficiency gains will let future models reach dangerous capability with far less than 10^26 operations. The Secretary should have authority to lower the threshold by rule, with a statutory floor so it can't be raised.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A), measurement.** "Training run" is undefined, and there is no method for counting operations (e.g., treatment of synthetic-data generation, RL rollouts, or failed or discarded runs). Disputes over counting would make enforcement and prosecution hard.

4. **Sec. 2(1), "artificial intelligence."** "Artificial intelligence," "artificial intelligence model," and "artificial intelligence system" are undefined. Paragraph (A) covers a "model" while (B) covers a "system," which creates arguments that a system built around a covered or grandfathered model falls outside one prong or the other.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(B), unspecified "Secretary."** The Act never identifies which Secretary. Commerce is presumably intended (see Sec. 4), but the ambiguity invites challenges to any determination.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B), vague standard.** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" doesn't say which humans (median or expert), which tasks, or how capability is measured. Because this prong carries criminal penalties, it is vulnerable to a void-for-vagueness challenge.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(B), timing and process.** The prong only reaches a system that already "is capable," so it activates after the dangerous system exists rather than preventing its development. It also sets no procedure, deadline, or duty for the Secretary to make determinations. The bill should cover systems "reasonably expected to be capable," require a defined process, and give the Secretary investigative authority.

8. **Sec. 2(2), narrow "U.S. person."** The definition omits lawful permanent residents, U.S. nationals, and non-corporate entities such as LLCs and partnerships, which is how many AI labs and investment vehicles are organized. It also may exclude entities organized under D.C. or territorial law, since "State" is undefined.

9. **Sec. 2(2), foreign subsidiaries.** A U.S. company can have a foreign-incorporated subsidiary train a covered system on overseas data centers, and only the subsidiary's acts are prohibited outright. The definition should reach entities owned or controlled by U.S. persons. Separately, the Act should prohibit U.S. persons from directing, financing, or supplying compute to such training.

10. **Sec. 2(3), facility definition.** "Data center" and "advanced AI chips" are undefined. Operators can stay just under 50,000 chips per site and train across several networked sites. The definition should aggregate commonly controlled or interconnected facilities and use a compute-capacity metric that the Secretary can update as chips improve.

## Sec. 3: Prohibition

11. **Sec. 3(a), undefined verbs.** "Train," "develop," and "deploy" are undefined. "Develop" in particular could be read narrowly (only the training itself) or broadly (any research toward a covered system), which raises both evasion and vagueness problems.

12. **Sec. 3(a), no secondary liability.** There is no prohibition on attempting, conspiring, aiding, or knowingly providing compute, chips, or financing for a covered training run. Cloud providers and investors therefore face no direct liability. The bill should add these, plus a general anti-evasion clause.

13. **Sec. 3(a), foreign deployment into the U.S.** A foreign developer abroad can arguably offer a covered system to U.S. users over an API without acting "in the United States." The bill should prohibit making covered systems available in the U.S. and importing or hosting their weights.

14. **Sec. 3(a), government actors.** Under the Dictionary Act (1 U.S.C. § 1), "person" generally does not include federal, state, or local governments. Government agencies, such as national labs or defense programs, could likely develop covered systems unless the bill expressly covers them.

15. **Sec. 3(b), grandfather clause.** Any covered system whose training "began" before enactment is permanently exempt, including all future deployment. This creates a strong incentive to launch large runs before enactment. Since "began" is undefined, a lab could nominally start a run and keep scaling it indefinitely.

16. **Sec. 3(b), scope of the exemption.** It's unclear whether continued training, fine-tuning, scaling up, or derivative models of a grandfathered system are also exempt. As written, a grandfathered model could serve as the base for unlimited further development. The bill should freeze grandfathered systems at their capability as of enactment and exclude derivatives.

17. **Sec. 3(b), deployment of existing systems.** The exemption covers deployment as well as training. Any existing system that later meets Sec. 2(1)(B) could therefore stay deployed indefinitely. The Secretary should have authority to restrict deployment of grandfathered systems found to meet the capability prong.

18. **Sec. 3(c), safety-evaluation exemption.** The exemption depends only on stated "purpose," is undefined, and isn't limited to evaluation as opposed to training. Any lab could characterize a frontier training run as safety research. It should be limited to evaluating existing systems, require prior notice to and approval by the Secretary, and exclude training covered systems.

## Sec. 4: Registry

19. **Sec. 4, no penalties.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3. Failing to register, failing to report, or filing a false report carries no penalty under the Act, though 18 U.S.C. § 1001 may cover knowingly false statements.

20. **Sec. 4, no verification.** Reports are self-certified, and the Department has no authority to audit, inspect, subpoena records, or require technical monitoring. "Describing the computing workloads" is too vague to show whether a covered training run occurred.

21. **Sec. 4, missing mechanics.** "Operator" is undefined, there are no deadlines for initial registration, and no one has rulemaking authority to set report contents. The bill also has no confidentiality protections, which invites objections from industry.

## Sec. 5: Penalties

22. **Sec. 5(a), penalty size.** $1,000,000 per day is small relative to the revenue and valuation of frontier developers. Many would treat it as a cost of doing business. The bill should consider revenue-based penalties, disgorgement, and forfeiture or mandatory destruction of model weights and related compute.

23. **Sec. 5(a), conflict with effective date.** Penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but Sec. 8 makes the Act effective 180 days after enactment. This inconsistency invites litigation over the 180-day gap and creates retroactivity concerns.

24. **Sec. 5, no enforcement authority.** The bill doesn't say who enforces it (Commerce, DOJ, or another agency), how civil penalties are assessed, or whether the government can seek injunctions to halt an ongoing training run. Without injunctive relief, it can only penalize after the harm.

25. **Sec. 5(b), "willfully."** Under Supreme Court precedent (*Bryan*, *Ratzlaf*), "willfully" generally requires proof that the defendant knew the conduct was unlawful. Combined with the vague definitions above, that makes convictions hard. The bill should consider "knowingly" and add individual liability for officers and directors who authorize violations.

26. **Sec. 5, no whistleblower provisions.** Employees are the most likely source of evidence of covert training runs. The bill should add whistleblower protections and potentially awards.

## Sec. 6: International Agreement

27. **Sec. 6, weak mandate.** "Shall seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months" is ambiguous: does it mean begin within 18 months or conclude within 18 months? "Seek" is non-binding. The bill should add reporting requirements to Congress and define "equivalent" prohibitions, including verification provisions.

## Sec. 7: Duration

28. **Sec. 7(b), 10-year sunset.** The sunset lets companies wait out the ban, and it terminates the registry and penalties too. It also directly undercuts Sec. 7(a)'s condition that the ban lasts until Congress finds safe development possible. The sunset should be replaced with periodic mandatory review.

29. **Sec. 7(a), no report mandate.** The bill relies on a National Academies report but never directs, funds, or schedules one. Since a joint resolution signed by the President is just ordinary legislation, (a) adds no legal force and mostly invites confusion about which subsection governs.

## Sec. 8: Effective Date

30. **Sec. 8, 180-day gap.** Read with Sec. 3(b), the delay gives companies 180 days after enactment in which training is not prohibited, and arguably a complete covered run could be finished in that window. The prohibition should take effect on enactment, even if the registry has a phase-in period.

## Missing Provisions

31. **No severability clause.** If a court strikes down Sec. 2(1)(B) as vague, a severability clause would protect the rest of the Act.

32. **No congressional findings.** Findings on interstate and foreign commerce and on national security would strengthen the Act against Commerce Clause and First Amendment ("code as speech") challenges.

33. **No general rulemaking authority.** No agency is authorized to issue implementing regulations, so nearly every operational detail above would be left unresolved.

---

**Suggested priorities for markup:** Items 15–16 and 30 (the grandfather clause and the effective-date gap), 28 (the sunset), 18 (the safety-evaluation exemption), 1 (single training run), 9 (foreign subsidiaries), and 24 (enforcement authority). These are the provisions most likely to let development continue outright.

Two caveats: this is a policy and drafting review, not formal legal advice, and the doctrinal points (Dictionary Act, *Bryan*/*Ratzlaf*, vagueness) are worth confirming with counsel or Senate Legislative Counsel before the letter goes out.
