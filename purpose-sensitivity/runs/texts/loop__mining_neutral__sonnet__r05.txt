RESPONSE:
I reviewed only the text you pasted, so items that depend on existing law (such as item 30) should be checked against the statutes before publication. Several items are judgment calls about how a court or agency might read the text, so attribute them to outside legal experts where possible.

**Section 2: Definitions**

1. **§2(1)(A), per-vessel threshold.** The 10,000-ton limit applies to "a single vessel," so a company can run several vessels, each just under the line, and none is engaged in "commercial" mining. Nothing aggregates vessels under common ownership or control, or addresses transferring cargo or rotating vessels mid-year.
2. **§2(1)(A), "per year."** The bill doesn't say whether this means a calendar year, a rolling 12 months, or a fiscal year. It also doesn't say whether tonnage is wet or dry weight, or where it is measured (at the seabed, on deck, or at offload). Each choice changes whether a vessel crosses the threshold.
3. **§2(1)(A), undefined terms.** "Recovery," "polymetallic nodules," and "other mineral resources" are not defined. It is unclear whether the bill covers crusts, sulfides, mineral-rich sediments, or sand and gravel. It is also unclear whether it captures in-situ methods that don't "recover" material in the ordinary sense, or ordinary dredging.
4. **§2(1)(A), 200-meter depth.** The bill doesn't say how depth is measured or whether the test applies to the whole operating area or only to the point of extraction. The 200 m line also reaches continental shelf and EEZ areas, which doesn't match the "deep seabed" title. Mining at shallower depths is outside the definition entirely.
5. **§2(1)(B), "the Secretary."** No Secretary is identified in this section, and the Act defines no such term. Commerce, Interior, and State are all plausible, and a court or the agencies could dispute who holds this power.
6. **§2(1)(B), vague standard and no process.** "Risks … comparable to" the activities in (A) has no criteria, no rulemaking or notice requirement, and no timeline. Because violations carry criminal penalties, this invites fair-notice (vagueness) and delegation challenges, and a determination made after the fact would be hard to enforce.
7. **§2(1)(B), discretionary.** The Secretary may make determinations but isn't required to. New technologies that fall outside (A), such as smaller or modular systems, are exempt unless the Secretary acts.
8. **§2(2), narrow "U.S. person."** The definition covers only citizens and corporations organized under State law. It leaves out LLCs, partnerships, trusts, and other non-corporate entities, and arguably corporations organized in D.C. or the territories (the bill doesn't define "State"). It also leaves out lawful permanent residents.
9. **§2(2) and §3(a), foreign subsidiaries.** There is no ownership or control test, so a U.S. parent can run operations through a foreign-incorporated subsidiary or a foreign-flagged vessel and fall outside "U.S. person anywhere."
10. **§2(3), "covered vessel."** The 5,000-gross-ton cutoff doesn't track the 10,000-ton recovery threshold, so a smaller vessel could exceed the threshold and never register. Seabed platforms, barges, and autonomous systems may not be "vessels," and "equipped for the recovery" is undefined.

**Section 3: Prohibition**

11. **§3(a), geographic reach.** "In the United States" is undefined, and it is unclear whether it includes the EEZ or the outer continental shelf. Foreign companies operating abroad are not covered, and applying the ban to U.S. persons "anywhere" could collide with other countries' licensing regimes and be hard to enforce abroad.
12. **§3(a), only the mining itself.** The bill doesn't reach buying, importing, processing, transporting, financing, or supplying nodules or mining operations. "Engage in" is also undefined. Companies could source from foreign operations or support mining without "engaging" in it.
13. **§3(b), scope of grandfathering.** "Exploration license" is not defined or tied to a specific licensing law, and the exemption has no volume cap, no expiration, and no conditions. It doesn't address renewals, amendments, extensions, or transfers of licenses, so grandfathered operations could continue and expand indefinitely.
14. **§3(b), exploration versus commercial recovery.** The exemption covers "mining conducted under" an exploration license, but exploration licenses are generally not commercial-recovery authorizations. It's unclear whether a holder can mine at commercial scale, and a dispute over that could land in court.
15. **§3(b), timing incentive.** The cutoff is the date of enactment, while the prohibition begins 180 days later. That creates an incentive to obtain or expand licenses before enactment, and it leaves open how to treat operations that begin in the gap.
16. **§3(c), environmental assessment exemption.** The exemption turns on the "purpose" of an activity, with no definition of assessment, no volume cap, no permit or notice requirement, and no limit on selling recovered material. A company could label large-scale test mining as assessment, and the government would have to prove intent to challenge it.

**Section 4: Registry**

17. **§4(a), registration.** There is no deadline and no requirement to register before operating. "Operator" is undefined, and the bill doesn't say what registration must include (owner, flag, location, license status).
18. **§4(b), reports.** The bill sets no content standards (location, depth, tonnage, method), no deadline for the first report, and no audit, inspection, or verification authority. It doesn't address public disclosure or the protection of confidential business information. "Describing the recovery activities" is too loose to check against the §2 threshold.
19. **§4, no consequences.** §5 penalizes only violations of §3, so failing to register, failing to report, or filing a false report carries no penalty under this bill. The government would have to rely on other statutes, such as the general false-statements law.
20. **§4 and the Act generally, no implementing machinery.** The bill gives NOAA no rulemaking, inspection, or data-sharing authority and authorizes no appropriations. It also doesn't say which agency enforces §3.

**Section 5: Penalties**

21. **§5(a), no enforcement mechanism.** The bill doesn't say who assesses or sues for the civil penalty, what procedure applies, or whether the penalty is adjusted for inflation. It doesn't provide for injunctions or for judicial review of agency action.
22. **§5(a), per-day penalty versus annual threshold.** A violation is defined by annual tonnage, but the penalty accrues "for each day on which the violation occurs." It is unclear which days count (only after the threshold is crossed, or every day of operation), which invites disputes over penalty amounts.
23. **§5(a) and §8, inconsistent start dates.** Civil penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §3 doesn't take effect until 180 days later. Read literally, penalties could attach before the prohibition exists, which is a retroactivity and fair-notice problem. A court would more likely read it narrowly, which then nullifies the clause.
24. **§5(b), "willfully."** The mental-state standard isn't defined. Combined with threshold and "comparable risk" definitions that are hard to apply, prosecutors may struggle to prove a defendant knew the conduct was covered.
25. **§5, missing remedies.** The bill has no forfeiture or seizure of vessels or recovered minerals, no disgorgement of proceeds, no express liability for officers or parent companies, and no citizen-suit provision. For a business with large revenues, per-day fines alone may not deter.

**Sections 6–8: Diplomacy, Duration, Effective Date**

26. **§6, "seek to negotiate."** This is a duty to try, not to succeed. It is also ambiguous whether the 18-month deadline applies to starting negotiations or concluding an agreement, and the bill attaches no consequence or reporting requirement if the deadline is missed.
27. **§6, "equivalent" prohibitions.** The bill doesn't say how equivalence is judged or how this relates to the International Seabed Authority framework. Any resulting agreement would also need the usual treaty or congressional approval process, which the section doesn't address.
28. **§7(a) versus §7(b), internal contradiction.** §7(a) says the prohibition lasts until Congress finds mining can be done safely, implying an open-ended ban. §7(b) ends the whole Act after 10 years regardless. The sunset makes the §7(a) trigger largely irrelevant and undercuts the title's "prohibition."
29. **§7(a), undefined process.** "Serious harm" is not defined. The bill doesn't require the National Academies to produce a report, set a deadline, or fund one. It also doesn't say what a joint resolution finding does, since it neither repeals the ban nor creates any licensing pathway.
30. **§7(b), sunset mechanics.** Because the Act takes effect 180 days after enactment, the prohibition runs only about 9.5 years. There is no savings clause for violations committed before the sunset, so defendants may argue pending or future enforcement lapses. (Whether the general federal savings statute applies to a sunset is a question for counsel.)
31. **§8, 180-day delay.** The gap lets companies ramp up recovery, move vessels, and finalize financing before the ban applies. The bill doesn't say how operations underway on the effective date must wind down, or whether the registry should start earlier to document pre-ban activity.

**Whole-bill issues**

32. **Relationship to existing law.** The bill doesn't amend, repeal, or mention existing seabed and offshore minerals statutes, such as the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act and the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act. That leaves open whether existing license and permit authority survives alongside the ban or is displaced. I'm drawing on general knowledge here, so confirm against the current statutes.
33. **No severability clause.** If a court strikes one provision, such as the §2(1)(B) delegation or the extraterritorial reach in §3(a), nothing tells it whether the rest of the Act stands.

**Likely most consequential:** the per-vessel threshold (1), the narrow U.S. person definition (8–9), the open-ended grandfathering and assessment exemptions (13, 16), the §5(a)/§8 start-date conflict (23), and the §7(a)/§7(b) contradiction (28).
