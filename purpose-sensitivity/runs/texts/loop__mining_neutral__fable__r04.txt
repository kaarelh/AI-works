RESPONSE:
Here's a section-by-section list. I've ordered items by where they appear in the bill, and flagged the ones I'd consider most consequential with a ★.

**Section 2 — Definitions**

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — Per-vessel tonnage threshold invites fleet-splitting.** ★ The 10,000-ton ceiling is measured "by a single vessel," so an operator can run several vessels each recovering 9,999 tons and never trigger the prohibition. There is no aggregation across vessels, affiliates, or a common operator.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "Per year" is undefined.** The bill doesn't say calendar year, fiscal year, rolling 12 months, or license year, which both creates enforcement ambiguity and lets operators time recovery across year boundaries (e.g., 9,000 tons in December and 9,000 in January).

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — Only "recovery" is covered.** The definition reaches physically lifting material from the seabed but not processing, transporting, importing, selling, financing, or providing services to seabed mining. A U.S. company could own the equipment, buy the nodules, and process them domestically without "engaging in" mining.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "Vessel" may not capture all platforms.** Recovery systems can be deployed from fixed or floating platforms, subsea crawlers, or riser systems that may not be "vessels" in the legal sense; the definition and the registry (Sec. 2(3)) both hinge on that word.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "Other mineral resources" is undefined and potentially overbroad.** ★ Read literally, it could sweep in deepwater oil and gas, sand and gravel, or methane hydrates recovered below 200 meters. Expect industry and some senators to raise this at markup; the 1980 Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (DSHMRA) avoided the problem by defining "hard mineral resources."

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — "The Secretary" is never identified.** ★ The bill nowhere says which Secretary (Commerce, State, Interior) makes the "comparable risk" determination, even though that determination can create criminal liability. Sec. 6 refers only to the Secretary of State and Sec. 4 to NOAA, so this is a genuine drafting gap, not an implied reference.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — Open-ended, standardless delegation.** The Secretary can expand the definition of a crime with no procedure (no rulemaking, notice, public comment, or judicial review provision) and no defined meaning of "comparable." This is vulnerable to vagueness/fair-notice and nondelegation challenges, especially as applied to the 10-year criminal penalty in Sec. 5(b).

8. **Sec. 2(2) — "U.S. person" is narrowly drawn.** ★ It covers only citizens and corporations "organized under the laws of a State." It omits LLCs, partnerships, trusts, non-citizen U.S. nationals, lawful permanent residents, and entities organized under the laws of D.C., territories, or tribal law, and it does not reach foreign subsidiaries owned or controlled by U.S. persons. A U.S. corporation can mine through a Bermuda or Singapore subsidiary with a foreign-flagged vessel and fall entirely outside Sec. 3(a).

9. **Sec. 2(3) — "Covered vessel" doesn't line up with the prohibition.** The registry trigger (5,000 gross tons, "equipped for" recovery) is unrelated to the 10,000-ton activity threshold. Smaller vessels, vessels that launch recovery equipment from a separate platform, or vessels that are "equipped" only after registration deadlines pass all fall outside the registry.

**Section 3 — Prohibition**

10. **Sec. 3(a) — Jurisdictional reach is unclear for U.S. waters.** "Person in the United States" is not defined to include the territorial sea, EEZ, or outer continental shelf, so a foreign operator mining in the U.S. EEZ with a foreign vessel and no U.S. incorporation may not be "in the United States" for purposes of the ban. Combined with item 8, the bill may fail to cover foreign-flagged mining both in and outside U.S. waters.

11. **Sec. 3(a) — No conforming amendment to DSHMRA.** NOAA already has authority under the 1980 Act to issue exploration licenses and commercial recovery permits. The bill neither repeals nor reconciles that regime, leaving two conflicting statutes on the books and an argument that a DSHMRA permit is still lawful authority.

12. **Sec. 3(b) — The grandfather clause is broader than it looks.** ★ It exempts "mining conducted under an exploration license," but exploration licenses (under DSHMRA or the International Seabed Authority) authorize exploration, not commercial recovery; the text nonetheless appears to allow full commercial mining under them. It also doesn't say who must have issued the license (any nation, the ISA, NOAA), creating a path to shelter operations under a foreign or ISA license.

13. **Sec. 3(b) — No limits on grandfathered operations.** There is no cap on tonnage, no expiration, no restriction on transferring, renewing, amending, or expanding a pre-enactment license, and no clarity on whether a license "issued before enactment" but renewed afterward still qualifies. This creates a race to obtain licenses before enactment (and the bill offers no cutoff tied to introduction or markup).

14. **Sec. 3(c) — Purpose-based exemption with no guardrails.** "For the purpose of environmental assessment" has no tonnage cap, no approval or notification requirement, no prohibition on selling recovered material, and no agency to decide what qualifies. Test-mining at commercial scale could plausibly be characterized as assessment.

15. **Sec. 3 generally — Inconsistent terminology.** The bill uses "commercial deep seabed mining" (Sec. 2(1)), "mining" (Sec. 3(b)), and "recovery activities" (Sec. 4(b)) without saying whether these are the same thing, which gives defendants room to argue the exemption or registry language is broader or narrower than the defined term.

**Section 4 — Registry**

16. **Sec. 4 — No penalty for noncompliance.** ★ Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3, so failing to register, failing to report, or filing an inaccurate report carries no sanction under this Act (18 U.S.C. § 1001 might reach knowingly false certifications, but the bill doesn't say so).

17. **Sec. 4 — No deadlines, content requirements, or verification authority.** The section sets no registration date, no reporting deadline, no required contents, and gives NOAA no inspection, subpoena, audit, or rulemaking authority. "Operator" is undefined, so it's unclear whether the owner, charterer, or contractor must register.

18. **Sec. 4(a) — Scope is either unlimited or unclear.** "The operator of each covered vessel" has no jurisdictional qualifier, so it purports to cover every qualifying vessel in the world (unenforceable) or must be read to cover only U.S. persons/vessels (unstated). Either way, foreign operators have no obvious obligation to register.

**Section 5 — Penalties**

19. **Sec. 5(a) vs. Sec. 8 — Penalty start date conflicts with effective date.** ★ Civil penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the Act doesn't take effect until 180 days later. Either the penalty clause is surplusage for the first 180 days or it purports to punish conduct that wasn't yet unlawful, inviting a retroactivity challenge.

20. **Sec. 5(a) — "Each day on which the violation occurs" doesn't fit an annual threshold.** Because the violation is defined by exceeding 10,000 tons per year, it's unclear whether the violation "occurs" only on the day the threshold is crossed, on every day of recovery thereafter, or on every day of the year. This could make the daily penalty effectively a one-day penalty.

21. **Sec. 5 — No enforcement machinery.** The bill doesn't designate who assesses civil penalties or how (no administrative process, no referral to DOJ, no injunctive authority, no vessel or mineral forfeiture, no in rem jurisdiction). Vessel forfeiture is the standard tool in maritime enforcement and its absence is notable.

22. **Sec. 5(b) — "Willfully" plus a vague definition.** The high mens rea standard, combined with the undefined "comparable risk" determination in Sec. 2(1)(B) and the undefined "other mineral resources," makes criminal prosecution difficult and vulnerable to vagueness defenses. There's also no provision for corporate officer liability.

**Section 6 — International Agreement**

23. **Sec. 6 — Aspirational with no consequences.** "Seek to negotiate" imposes no obligation to conclude an agreement, and the 18-month deadline is ambiguous as to whether it applies to beginning negotiations or finishing them. There is no reporting requirement to Congress and no consequence if nothing happens.

**Section 7 — Duration**

24. **Sec. 7(a) vs. 7(b) — Internal conflict.** ★ Subsection (a) says the prohibition lasts until Congress finds by joint resolution that mining is safe; subsection (b) says the whole Act expires after 10 years regardless. The sunset controls as a practical matter, which makes (a) largely surplusage and means the "prohibition" is really a 10-year moratorium.

25. **Sec. 7(a) — No one is directed to produce the NAS report.** The bill conditions lifting the ban on a National Academies report but never commissions, funds, or sets a deadline for one. "Serious harm" is also undefined.

26. **Sec. 7(b) — No savings clause.** When the Act "ceases to have effect," pending enforcement actions, accrued penalties, and registry obligations arguably lapse with it. Standard practice is to preserve liability for pre-sunset violations.

**Section 8 — Effective Date**

27. **Sec. 8 — The 180-day delay is a recovery window.** Nothing restrains operators from accelerating recovery or obtaining exploration licenses (see item 13) during the period between enactment and effectiveness, and no agency has interim authority to issue implementing rules.

**Cross-cutting**

28. **No rulemaking authority anywhere.** No agency is authorized to issue regulations defining terms, setting registry procedures, or administering the Secretary's determination, which leaves most of the ambiguities above with no administrative fix.

29. **No severability clause.** If a court strikes the Sec. 2(1)(B) delegation or the criminal penalty, there's no textual signal that the rest of the Act should survive.

30. **No import or market provision.** The bill regulates conduct, not commerce in the product. U.S. ports, refiners, and buyers can receive and process nodules mined by foreign operators, which limits the practical effect of a U.S.-only ban pending the Sec. 6 agreement.

If it's useful for the explainer, the items I'd expect to draw amendments at Thursday's markup are 1, 5, 6, 8, 12, 19, and 24; those are the ones that go to whether the ban works at all rather than how cleanly it's enforced.
