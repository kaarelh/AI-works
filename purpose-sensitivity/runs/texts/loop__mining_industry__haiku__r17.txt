# Drafting Issues and Loopholes in S. 2817 (as amended October 6, 2026)

This list is based only on the text you provided. Counsel should check the cross-references to existing law, particularly the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act, against the current statute.

## Definitions (§2)

1. **§2(1)(A), "by a single vessel":** The 10,000-ton threshold applies per vessel, so an operation could divide recovery among several vessels, each below the threshold, and fall outside the definition. Whether that reading holds depends on how the committee treats aggregation, so expect pushback.

2. **§2(1)(A), "recovery" and "per year":** The bill does not say whether tonnage is measured wet or dry, gross or net of sediment and water, or at what point recovery is complete. Each choice changes whether an operation crosses the threshold.

3. **§2(1)(A), "greater than 200 meters":** The bill gives no reference point or measurement method for depth, and does not address operations that span depths above and below 200 meters.

4. **§2(1)(A), "other mineral resources":** "Mineral resources" is undefined, so the bill's scope over crusts, sulfides, or sediment-based extraction is unclear.

5. **§2(1)(B), "any activity that the Secretary determines":** The catch-all depends on a discretionary "comparable risk" finding with no stated standard, procedure, notice, record, or review. This supports both a vagueness argument and a due-process argument, and it means no operator can know in advance whether it is covered.

6. **§2 generally, "Secretary":** "Secretary" appears in §2(1)(B) without definition, while §6 separately refers to the "Secretary of State." The bill never identifies which official or agency administers §§2 through 5.

7. **§2(2), "U.S. person":** The definition covers only citizens and corporations organized under State law. It excludes LLCs, partnerships, entities organized under federal law or in D.C. or the territories, and foreign-organized affiliates, which narrows the "U.S. person anywhere" reach in §3(a). Our legal team should review our own entity structure against this definition. Any structuring decision should be made on its merits, since committee staff may tighten the definition if they see it as a workaround.

8. **§2(3), "covered vessel":** This term is used only in §4 (registry and reporting), so vessels at or below 5,000 gross tons are outside registration and reporting entirely. "Equipped for the recovery of seabed mineral resources" is also undefined.

9. **§2 generally, "State":** "State" is undefined, which affects the scope of §2(2).

## Scope of the Prohibition (§3)

10. **§3(a), "engage in":** The bill does not say whether financing, chartering, equipment supply, or processing counts as engaging in mining. Because the prohibition is framed around activity, these supporting roles may fall outside it.

11. **§3(a), "in the United States" and "deep seabed":** The bill does not say whether "in the United States" includes the EEZ, the continental shelf, or the international Area. Combined with the "U.S. person anywhere" limit, this leaves foreign operators and foreign-organized entities largely outside the prohibition.

12. **§3(a), no import or sale provision:** The prohibition covers mining only. It does not restrict importing, processing, or selling minerals recovered from the seabed elsewhere, so the bill does not address the market for such minerals.

## Existing Operations Exemption (§3(b))

13. **§3(b), "exploration license":** Only exploration licenses are exempted. Commercial recovery permits issued before enactment are not expressly protected, and the bill does not address pending applications. This is potentially the largest gap in the existing-operations protection, though its scope depends on the current DSHMRA permit structure, which counsel should confirm.

14. **§3(b), "mining conducted under":** It is arguable that commercial-scale recovery conducted under an exploration license is exempt, but the text does not say so. The exemption also does not address renewals, amendments, expansions in area or tonnage, or transfers to successor entities.

15. **§3(b) and §8, timing mismatch:** The exemption is keyed to the date of enactment, but the prohibition takes effect 180 days later under §8. Licenses issued during that window are neither clearly exempt nor subject to a prohibition already in force.

16. **§3(b), foreign authorizations:** The bill does not address whether authorizations under international frameworks, such as International Seabed Authority contracts, held by U.S. persons are covered or exempt.

## Environmental Assessment Exemption (§3(c))

17. **§3(c), "for the purpose of environmental assessment":** "Purpose" is undefined and is not tied to any approval process, plan, volume cap, or duration limit. The bill also does not restrict the sale or use of material recovered during assessment, so the exemption could be read to cover commercial-scale recovery labeled as assessment. Expect scrutiny of this argument, since it may look like pretext.

## Registry and Reporting (§4)

18. **§4(a), "operator" and "register":** "Operator" is undefined, and the bill sets no registration forms, standards, deadlines, or rulemaking authority for NOAA.

19. **§4(b), quarterly report:** The required content ("describing the recovery activities") is unspecified. The bill also has no data fields, confidentiality protections, public-disclosure rules, or deadline for the first report.

20. **§4 and §5, no penalty for registry or reporting violations:** §5 penalizes only violations of §3. Failing to register a vessel or file a report carries no stated penalty.

21. **§4, relationship to the prohibition:** Registration does not authorize activity or create any exemption under §3. Registration therefore does not serve as a compliance pathway, and the bill does not say how the registry relates to the prohibition or to existing permits.

## Penalties (§5)

22. **§5(a), "date of enactment" versus §8:** The civil penalty applies to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §3 does not take effect until 180 days later. Since no §3 violation can occur before the prohibition is in force, the two provisions are inconsistent, and the bill is unclear about exposure during the 180-day window.

23. **§5(a), "for each day":** The bill defines neither "violation" nor how violations are counted, whether per vessel, per ton, or per day of operation. It also sets no aggregate cap and no inflation adjustment, so the calculation of penalties is open to dispute.

24. **§5(a), enforcement mechanics:** The bill names no enforcing agency and sets no procedure for notice, hearing, administrative assessment, limitations period, or judicial review. It also does not authorize injunctive relief.

25. **§5(b), "willfully" and the fine:** "Willfully" is undefined, and the fine is set only by reference to title 18 without a specific amount. The bill also does not address corporate liability or the liability of officers and directors.

## International Agreement (§6)

26. **§6, "shall seek to negotiate":** This is an aspirational duty. It has no consequence if the agreement is not reached, no reporting requirement, and no provision requiring reevaluation of the Act. The 18-month deadline is a deadline to seek negotiations, not to conclude an agreement.

## Duration (§7)

27. **§7(a), "serious harm":** The term is undefined, and the bill does not set the scope of the NASEM report, a deadline for it, or a requirement that it be transmitted to Congress. It also does not say whether a finding applies to the activity category or to specific operations.

28. **§7(a), joint resolution:** The bill does not state whether the joint resolution is presented to the President, whether it receives expedited procedures, or what happens if Congress does not act. The mechanism for lifting the prohibition is therefore unclear and may be practically unavailable.

29. **§7(a) versus §7(b), internal conflict:** §7(a) keeps the prohibition in force until a joint resolution is adopted, but §7(b) ends the entire Act 10 years after enactment regardless of any NASEM finding or resolution. The bill does not say which provision controls.

30. **§7(b), sunset measured from enactment:** The 10-year period runs from enactment, not from the §8 effective date, so the prohibition would be in force for roughly 9.5 years. The sunset also does not say whether penalties for violations before the sunset remain enforceable afterward.

## Effective Date (§8)

31. **§8, no transition provisions:** The 180-day period has no wind-down provisions for vessels in transit, ongoing operations, existing contracts, or pending permit applications. There is also no savings clause preserving rights that accrued before the effective date.

## Structural and Interpretive Issues

32. **Interaction with DSHMRA:** The bill does not amend, repeal, or reference the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act, which provides the existing licensing and permitting framework. It does not say whether the prohibition overrides existing DSHMRA permits or how the two regimes interact, which is a major source of ambiguity.

33. **No severability, savings, or preemption clauses:** The bill has no severability clause, no savings clause for existing rights, and no statement on preemption of State law. Each absence adds uncertainty about what survives if a provision is struck down.

34. **No implementing authority:** The bill grants no rulemaking authority, so the Secretary's determinations under §2(1)(B) and NOAA's registry requirements under §4 lack a stated procedure. This creates both an implementation gap and a potential basis for challenges under the Administrative Procedure Act.

---

**Priorities for the markup:** The points with the clearest textual support are the internal conflicts (items 22, 29, and 15), the missing penalty for registry violations (item 20), the commercial-permit gap (item 13), the vagueness of the §2(1)(B) determination (item 5), and the unclear DSHMRA relationship (item 32). Items 1, 17, and 7 are the most likely to be seen as loophole-seeking, so I'd recommend framing them as drafting problems to be fixed rather than as arguments for exemptions.
