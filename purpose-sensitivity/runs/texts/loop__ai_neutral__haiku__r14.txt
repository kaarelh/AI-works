RESPONSE:
**Capitol Policy Review: Drafting Weak Spots in S. 2817 (Manager's Amendment, Oct. 6, 2026)**

1. **§2(1)(B):** "The Secretary" is never defined, so the bill doesn't say which official (Commerce, State, or another) makes the covered-system determination. Section 4 points to Commerce and Section 6 names the Secretary of State, so the omission is not obviously a cross-reference error.

2. **§2(1)(B):** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no benchmark, measurement method, or definition of "most." That gives developers no notice of what is prohibited and invites vagueness and nondelegation challenges.

3. **§2(1)(B):** The bill sets no procedure for the determination (notice, comment, evidentiary standard, deadline, or judicial review). It also doesn't say whether a determination can apply to systems already in use, or whether developers can know their status before training.

4. **§2(1)(A):** "Single training run" is undefined. Developers could split a run into segments or treat continued pretraining, fine-tuning, or post-training as separate runs to stay under 10^26.

5. **§2(1)(A):** The bill has no counting rules for FLOPs: no treatment of precision (integer and floating-point operations are lumped together), no rule on whether inference or synthetic-data generation counts, and no rule on whether estimates or audited figures control. That makes the threshold hard to measure and easy to dispute.

6. **§2(1)(A):** The 10^26 threshold is fixed in statute with no mechanism to update it as training efficiency improves. Over time, the threshold may cover fewer systems than intended.

7. **§2(1):** "Artificial intelligence model" (A) and "artificial intelligence system" (B) are used without distinction. A system built from several sub-threshold models or wrapped in agent scaffolding may fall outside (A), and nothing automatically triggers (B) review.

8. **§2(2):** "U.S. person" covers only citizens and corporations organized under state law. It excludes LLCs and partnerships (common structures for AI companies), permanent residents, and entities organized under federal, D.C., or territorial law.

9. **§2(2) and §3(a):** The bill doesn't say whether a U.S. parent is responsible for training carried out by a foreign subsidiary or branch. There is no control-or-direction standard, so the reach of "any U.S. person anywhere" over corporate groups is unclear.

10. **§3(a):** Non-U.S. persons developing covered systems abroad are outside the prohibition entirely until a §6 agreement exists. This is the bill's largest territorial gap.

11. **§3(a):** "Train," "develop," and "deploy" are undefined. "Develop" could be read to cover research short of training. It's unclear whether "deploy" covers API access, internal use, or open-weight release, and the bill does not address distribution, hosting, sale, or export of model weights.

12. **§3(a) and §5:** The prohibition has no aiding-or-abetting or assistance clause. Chip suppliers, cloud providers, and data center operators that supply compute to a covered system are not clearly liable, and §5 penalties attach only to a person who violates §3.

13. **§3(b):** The grandfather clause for systems whose "training began before the date of enactment" has no end date. Because it covers the *system*, not just the run, deployment of a grandfathered system and later training on it may be exempt indefinitely.

14. **§3(b):** "Training began" is undefined. A developer could start a preliminary run before enactment and claim the full run is the same grandfathered system. The bill also requires no documentation or registration of grandfathered systems, so the exemption cannot be verified.

15. **§3(c):** "Safety evaluation" is undefined, and the purpose-based exemption requires no registration, notice, compute cap, or oversight. A developer could train or deploy a system and describe the activity as evaluation, and the exemption may cover external deployment for testing.

16. **§2(3) and §4:** The registry is disconnected from the prohibition. Section 3 never mentions covered facilities, and §4 requires no disclosure of which systems are trained or deployed. The 50,000-chip threshold counts hardware, not compute: at plausible cluster efficiency, a 10^26 run could finish on a cluster well below 50,000 chips over several months, so the two thresholds don't line up. (This is a rough estimate that staff should verify with a technical source.)

17. **§2(3):** "Advanced AI chips," "data center," and "containing" are all undefined. A facility can be split across buildings or sites, leased cloud capacity may not count, and it's unclear who is the facility's "operator."

18. **§4(a):** "Operator" is undefined, and the bill sets no registration deadline, no required content beyond the facility's existence, and no public disclosure. §5 imposes no penalty for failing to register.

19. **§4(b):** Quarterly reports must describe "computing workloads" with no content standard. Nothing requires an operator to state whether a workload is a covered system or to report its FLOP count, and the reports are self-certified with no audit or inspection authority.

20. **§4:** The bill doesn't say whether registry reports are public, exempt from FOIA, or shared with other agencies. It also has no confidentiality or trade-secret rules, which could lead to disputes and withholding.

21. **§5(a):** "Each day on which the violation occurs" is not defined as a continuing violation. It's unclear whether a multi-month training run counts as one violation per day or as a single violation, and the bill has no aggregate cap or per-system calculation.

22. **§5(a) and §8:** The civil penalty applies to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §3 doesn't take effect until 180 days later. The text doesn't say whether conduct in that window is penalized, and the mismatch invites challenge.

23. **§5:** The bill names no enforcing agency and no civil enforcement process. It doesn't say whether the Attorney General, Commerce, or a private party brings suit, and it grants no injunctive authority, no power to halt a training run, no authority to seize chips or facilities, and no disgorgement.

24. **§5(b):** "Willfully" is undefined, and the bill sets no knowledge standard for corporate officers. Criminal enforcement also depends on DOJ priorities that the bill doesn't address.

25. **General (§§4–5):** The bill has no whistleblower protections, no subpoena or information-gathering authority, and no recordkeeping requirement for training runs. As drafted, the agency has few tools to verify compliance.

26. **§6:** The Secretary of State "shall seek to negotiate" an agreement, which sets no required outcome, reporting requirement, consequence for failure, or revisit date. "Equivalent prohibitions" is undefined, so what counts as success is unclear.

27. **§7(a):** The prohibition lifts only when Congress finds by joint resolution, on a NASEM report, that covered systems "can be developed safely." The bill sets no deadline for the NASEM report, gives the joint resolution no expedited procedures, leaves "developed safely" undefined, and doesn't say whether presidential signature is required.

28. **§7(a) and §7(b):** The two subsections conflict. Section 7(a) keeps the prohibition in effect until Congress acts, while §7(b) says the Act "shall cease to have effect" after 10 years. The bill doesn't say which controls, and it has no savings clause for penalties accrued before sunset.

29. **§8 and §3(b):** The grandfather is keyed to enactment, but the prohibition takes effect 180 days later. The bill has no transition rule for runs in progress on the effective date, so it's unclear whether a run that began during the 180-day window must stop.

30. **General:** The bill grants no rulemaking authority. Nothing directs an agency to define terms, set the FLOP-measurement method, design registry forms, or implement the §2(1)(B) determination, so the operative details depend on implied authority.

31. **General:** The bill has no severability clause, judicial-review provision, savings clause, or preemption language. Without severability, a successful challenge to §2(1)(B) could take down the whole prohibition, or leave the 10^26 prong standing with no determination mechanism.

32. **General:** Several terms are undefined and have no default meaning, including "person" (though 1 U.S.C. §1 may supply one), "Secretary," "operator," "deploy," "safety evaluation," and "training began."
