RESPONSE:
Here are the issues, in section order. Items 1, 8, 10, 13, 14, 17, and 21 are probably the most consequential. Constitutional points (items 27-30) should go to counsel before they go to the Senator's office.

**Section 2 (Definitions)**

1. **§2(1)(A), "single training run."** The term is undefined, so a developer can split training into staged runs, each under 10^26 FLOP, or use continued pretraining, fine-tuning, distillation, or RL as separate runs. The bill needs aggregation language, such as cumulative compute in a model lineage or compute contributing to a model's final weights.
2. **§2(1)(A), a fixed compute threshold.** Algorithmic efficiency gains could produce dangerous capability below 10^26. The bill has no mechanism to lower the threshold, and no measurement or reporting standard for counting operations (e.g., treatment of failed runs, synthetic data generation, or inference-time compute).
3. **§2(1)(A), overbreadth.** A compute-only test captures narrow systems that are not superintelligent (e.g., a large biology or weather model) while saying nothing about capability. This weakens the bill's rationale and invites as-applied challenges and carve-out pressure.
4. **§2(1)(A)-(B), "model" vs. "system."** Subparagraph (A) covers "models" and (B) covers "systems," and neither term is defined. Developers could argue that scaffolding, agent frameworks, or ensembles of sub-threshold models are not covered.
5. **§2(1)(B), undefined "Secretary."** The bill never says which Secretary. Section 4 names Commerce and §6 says "Secretary of State" explicitly, which makes the bare "Secretary" more conspicuous. It should be named.
6. **§2(1)(B), no standard or process.** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no benchmark, testing method, deadline, notice-and-comment requirement, or burden of proof. A developer cannot know in advance whether it is covered, which is both an enforcement problem and a gift to litigants.
7. **§2(1)(B), timing of the determination.** The bill does not say whether a determination applies retroactively, or whether training that began before the determination is a violation. As written, the Secretary could only act after a system has already been built, which defeats a prohibition on training.
8. **§2(2), "U.S. person" is too narrow.** It omits permanent residents, LLCs, partnerships, unincorporated associations, trusts, and nonprofits. It also omits entities organized in D.C. or a territory ("State" is not defined to include them) and foreign subsidiaries of U.S. firms. Moving a training operation into a foreign-incorporated subsidiary is an obvious workaround, so the definition should cover ownership and control.
9. **§2(3), "advanced AI chips" and "data center" undefined.** There is no performance threshold or cross-reference to export-control classifications, and no rule for how chips are counted (owned, leased, or networked).
10. **§2(3), the facility threshold is easy to game.** An operator can run multiple sites with 49,999 chips each, or train across sites. The definition needs aggregation across affiliated or jointly operated facilities, and a trigger tied to the entity's total compute rather than a single building.

**Section 3 (Prohibition)**

11. **§3(a), "train, develop, or deploy" undefined.** "Develop" is vague, especially in a criminal statute, and "deploy" does not clearly reach internal use, API access, open-weight release, or licensing. The prohibition also omits possessing or transferring weights, and it does not clearly reach funding, supplying compute, or knowingly facilitating others.
12. **§3(a), the foreign-actor gap.** It reaches persons in the U.S. and U.S. persons abroad. A foreign company with no U.S. presence could still train abroad and serve U.S. users, and the bill does not reach foreign cloud customers using U.S. compute or U.S. providers renting compute abroad.
13. **§3(b), the grandfather clause is open-ended.** It exempts any system whose "training began" before enactment, with no limit on continued training, scaling, fine-tuning, successor versions, or deployment. A developer could call every later model a continuation of an existing run. Define "began," bar derivative or successor systems from the exemption, and consider capability or compute caps on exempted systems.
14. **§3(b), a race-to-start incentive.** Combined with the 180-day delay in §8, developers have a strong reason to start the largest possible runs before enactment. The bill should address this, for example with a lookback date earlier than enactment or a registration requirement for existing runs.
15. **§3(b), no registration of grandfathered systems.** Nothing requires developers to identify which systems qualify or when training started, so the exemption would rest on unverifiable self-reporting.
16. **§3(c), the safety-evaluation exemption is unbounded.** "Activities conducted for the purpose of safety evaluation" turns on subjective purpose, covers training and development as well as testing, and has no limits on compute, containment, independence, or oversight. Any developer can claim it. It should be narrowed to testing of existing systems under a licensing or approval process with security requirements.

**Section 4 (Registry)**

17. **§4, no penalty for violations.** Section 5 penalizes only violations of §3, so failing to register or filing a false report carries no express consequence, apart from whatever general false-statement law might apply. This is likely the most important enforcement fix in the section.
18. **§4(a), no deadline or enforcement mechanism.** There is no registration deadline, no definition of what registration requires, and no designated enforcement office.
19. **§4(b), reports are too thin.** "Describing the computing workloads" doesn't require identifying training runs, compute used, model lineage, customers, or whether any run approaches the §2(1)(A) threshold. Quarterly timing also allows up to three months of undetected violation.
20. **§4, no verification authority.** Commerce has no audit, inspection, subpoena, or data-access power, so certified reports can't be checked. The bill also has no whistleblower protection, no cloud-provider know-your-customer duty, and no chip-acquisition reporting.
21. **§4, no confidentiality or data-sharing provisions.** Trade-secret and FOIA treatment is unaddressed, which invites objections and litigation. The bill also doesn't authorize sharing registry data with DOJ or other enforcers.

**Section 5 (Penalties)**

22. **§5(a), the date conflict.** It applies to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §8 makes the Act effective 180 days later. This invites arguments that the penalty provision applies before the prohibition does, or raises ex post facto and fair-notice objections.
23. **§5(a), the penalty is weak relative to the stakes.** A "not more than" $1M per day cap is small for firms spending billions, and judges have discretion to go lower. There is no disgorgement, no injunctive relief, no asset forfeiture, and no authority to order destruction or sequestration of weights.
24. **§5, no enforcer is designated.** The bill doesn't say who brings civil actions (DOJ, Commerce, or both), whether administrative enforcement is available, or whether there is a private or state-AG right of action. It also has no statute of limitations provision.
25. **§5(b), "willfully" combined with §2(1)(B).** Proving a defendant knew its system was covered will be hard when coverage depends on a later and vague Secretarial determination. The bill also does not address officer liability, attempt, or conspiracy.

**Sections 6-8**

26. **§6, no teeth.** The Secretary of State only has to "seek to negotiate." There is no reporting to Congress, no definition of "equivalent," no verification standard, no consequences for failure, and no link to export controls or other leverage.
27. **§7(a) vs. §7(b), conflicting duration clauses.** Subsection (a) implies an indefinite prohibition lasting until Congress acts, and (b) terminates the Act after 10 years regardless (effectively about 9.5 years of operation, given §8). The sunset undercuts the core goal and should be removed or made renewable by default.
28. **§7(a), vague off-ramp.** There's no deadline or requester for the National Academies report, no definition of "safely," and no statement of whether Congress is bound by the report. It also lifts the whole prohibition at once, with no option to allow specific systems.
29. **§8, the 180-day delay.** It creates an open window for training starts and compute buildup (see items 14 and 22), and the interaction with §3(b) and §5(a) is ambiguous.

**Cross-cutting**

30. **No findings or jurisdictional hook.** The bill has no congressional findings on the Commerce Clause or national security basis, and no interstate-commerce element. This weakens its defense against challenges to its reach over purely intrastate activity.
31. **Void-for-vagueness and nondelegation exposure.** Criminal liability that hinges on an undefined Secretarial determination (§2(1)(B)) and vague verbs (§3(a)) is the bill's most obvious constitutional target.
32. **First Amendment and takings arguments.** Developers may argue that restricting code and weights is a speech restriction, and that banning use of compute and training investments is a regulatory taking. A findings section, severability clause, and narrow tailoring would help.
33. **No severability clause.** If one provision, such as §2(1)(B), is struck down, the whole Act could be at risk.
34. **No rulemaking authority or funding.** The bill gives no regulatory authority, appropriations, or implementation deadlines for the agencies it relies on.
35. **Government actors are unaddressed.** The bill doesn't say whether federal agencies, the military, or contractors are covered. Either answer is a policy choice, but the silence will be exploited, and the bill should state it.
36. **No preemption or savings clause.** State law, existing export controls, and other federal authorities are not addressed, which leaves open conflicts and inconsistent enforcement.
