# S. 2817: Loopholes, Ambiguities, and Drafting Problems

This review covers the text as amended by the October 6 manager's amendment. Items are grouped by section. Each item notes whether it mainly **narrows coverage** (a possible path to keep operating) or mainly **creates unworkability or legal vulnerability**.

## Sec. 2: Definitions

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A): per-vessel threshold (narrows coverage).** The 10,000-ton trigger applies "by a single vessel," so an operator running several vessels, each under 10,000 tons a year, appears to fall outside subparagraph (A) no matter the total output. Nothing aggregates tonnage by operator, project, or license area.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A): wet vs. dry tonnage (ambiguity).** "Metric tons" doesn't say whether tonnage is measured wet (as lifted) or dry. Nodules carry substantial water at recovery, so the choice could move an operation above or below the threshold.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A): "recovery" by "a vessel" (ambiguity).** The bill doesn't say whether "recovery" happens at the seabed collector, at the riser, or on deck. Nor does it say whether a separate collector vehicle or a transshipment vessel is the vessel doing the recovering. Collection by one craft and transfer to others could split or obscure attribution.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A): "per year" undefined (ambiguity).** It's unclear whether this means calendar year, fiscal year, or any rolling 12-month period. That affects when a violation occurs and whether output can be timed to stay under the threshold.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(A): "other mineral resources" and "seabed" undefined (overbreadth/ambiguity).** Read literally, this could reach sand, gravel, or other materials taken from below 200 meters, including on the U.S. outer continental shelf. That overlaps BOEM's marine-minerals authority under the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act, and the bill doesn't reconcile the two.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(A): no commercial-purpose element (drafting problem).** "Commercial" deep seabed mining is defined purely by tonnage, with no reference to sale or profit. The label and the definition don't match, which invites argument over legislative intent.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(B): "the Secretary" undefined (drafting problem).** The Act never says which Secretary. Commerce (NOAA), Interior (BOEM), and others are all plausible, and only Sec. 6 names a specific Secretary (State).

8. **Sec. 2(1)(B): open-ended administrative expansion of a criminal prohibition (legal vulnerability).** A Secretary can extend the prohibition to any activity posing "comparable" risks. There is no rulemaking requirement, procedure, or standard for "comparable." Because Sec. 5(b) attaches up to 10 years' imprisonment, this invites vagueness (due process) and delegation challenges.

9. **Sec. 2(2): narrow "U.S. person" definition (narrows coverage).** Only citizens and corporations "organized under the laws of a State" qualify. That leaves out lawful permanent residents, and arguably LLCs, partnerships, and other non-corporate entities.

10. **Sec. 2(2): "State" undefined (narrows coverage).** Without a definition, "State" may not include D.C., Puerto Rico, or the territories, so entities organized there may not be U.S. persons.

11. **Sec. 2(2): foreign subsidiaries excluded (narrows coverage).** A foreign-incorporated subsidiary of a U.S. company is not a U.S. person. Nothing reaches U.S.-owned or U.S.-controlled foreign entities, or U.S. persons who direct, finance, or facilitate foreign operations.

12. **Sec. 2(3): covered-vessel threshold mismatch (drafting problem).** The 5,000-gross-ton registry threshold has no connection to the 10,000-ton mining threshold. Vessels under 5,000 GT escape registration even if they mine, and the bill doesn't say which tonnage measurement convention applies.

13. **Sec. 2(3): "equipped for" undefined (ambiguity).** It's unclear whether removable or modular recovery equipment, or equipment installed only between voyages, makes a vessel "covered."

## Sec. 3: Prohibition

14. **Sec. 3(a): "in the United States" undefined (ambiguity).** It's unclear whether this covers the territorial sea, the EEZ, the outer continental shelf, territories, or U.S.-flagged vessels. Foreign persons operating outside U.S. territory, including from U.S.-flagged ships, may not be covered.

15. **Sec. 3(a): conduct-only prohibition (narrows coverage).** The section bans "engag[ing] in" mining. It does not ban financing, sponsoring, chartering, purchasing, processing, or importing seabed minerals, so supporting or buying from foreign-conducted mining appears unaffected.

16. **Sec. 3(b): grandfathered "mining" under an exploration license (major loophole).** The exemption covers "mining conducted under an exploration license" issued before enactment, with no tonnage cap and no end date short of the sunset. Read literally, any pre-enactment licensee could conduct commercial-scale recovery indefinitely.

17. **Sec. 3(b): conflict with the DSHMRA framework (drafting problem).** Under the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act, exploration licenses and commercial recovery permits are separate instruments, and an exploration license does not authorize commercial recovery. The exemption therefore either does nothing or silently expands what exploration licenses allow. Counsel should confirm against current statute and NOAA regulations.

18. **Sec. 3(b): "exploration license" undefined (ambiguity).** The bill doesn't say whether this means only NOAA licenses under DSHMRA, or also ISA exploration contracts or licenses from foreign sponsoring states.

19. **Sec. 3(b): renewals, extensions, transfers, and amendments (ambiguity).** The bill doesn't say whether a pre-enactment license keeps its exemption after renewal, extension, transfer to a new holder, or a change in area or terms. It also doesn't say whether contractors operating "under" a licensee's authority are covered.

20. **Sec. 3(c): environmental assessment exemption unbounded (major loophole).** "Activities conducted for the purpose of environmental assessment" has no tonnage limit, no approval or permit requirement, and no definition. Nothing bars selling material recovered during an assessment. A subjective purpose test is also hard to enforce against dual-purpose operations.

21. **Sec. 3 generally: no administering agency or rulemaking authority (unworkability).** No agency is charged with implementing or interpreting the prohibition, issuing regulations, or determining compliance.

22. **Sec. 3 generally: no reconciliation with existing statutes (unworkability).** The bill doesn't amend or repeal DSHMRA or address OCSLA. It's unclear whether NOAA must keep processing license and permit applications, or what happens to permits that would otherwise issue.

## Sec. 4: Registry

23. **Sec. 4(a): no deadline and no geographic or flag scope (ambiguity).** Registration has no deadline. The section doesn't say whether foreign-flagged vessels or foreign operators must register, and "operator" is undefined.

24. **Sec. 4(a)–(b): no penalty for non-compliance (unworkability).** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3. Failing to register or report carries no penalty under this Act, though 18 U.S.C. § 1001 would still reach knowingly false statements to a federal agency.

25. **Sec. 4(b): report content and timing unspecified (ambiguity).** "Describing the recovery activities" sets no required data such as tonnage, location, or depth. There are no quarter deadlines and no verification mechanism, so the reports may not be able to establish whether the Sec. 2(1)(A) threshold was crossed.

26. **Sec. 4 vs. Sec. 2(1)(B): inconsistent agency references (drafting problem).** Sec. 4 names NOAA while Sec. 2 refers to an unidentified "Secretary," with no link between the registry and enforcement of Sec. 3.

## Sec. 5: Penalties

27. **Sec. 5(a): per-day penalty vs. per-year violation (ambiguity).** The violation is defined by annual tonnage, but the penalty runs "for each day on which the violation occurs." It's unclear which days count: every day of operation, only days after the threshold is crossed, or every day of the year.

28. **Sec. 5(a) vs. Sec. 8: effective-date conflict (drafting problem).** Penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the Act takes effect 180 days after enactment. This creates a contradiction over whether conduct in that window is punishable. Operators could argue it isn't, since the prohibition wasn't yet in effect.

29. **Sec. 5(a): no enforcement machinery (unworkability).** There is no designated assessing agency, no notice-and-hearing procedure, no judicial review provision, no injunctive authority, and no seizure or forfeiture authority. The general 5-year limitations period of 28 U.S.C. § 2462 would likely fill the timing gap, but the rest isn't supplied.

30. **Sec. 5(b): criminal liability built on vague and delegated terms (legal vulnerability).** Criminal exposure attaches to Sec. 2(1)(B) secretarial determinations and to undefined terms like "seabed," "recovery," and "in the United States." That strengthens vagueness and fair-notice arguments, especially for extraterritorial conduct.

## Sec. 6: International Agreement

31. **Sec. 6: hortatory only (non-issue for operations, but notable).** "Shall seek to negotiate" imposes no binding result, and the 18-month deadline has no consequence if missed. Any resulting treaty would also need Senate advice and consent. The section doesn't mention the ISA or address the U.S. non-party status under UNCLOS.

## Sec. 7: Duration

32. **Sec. 7(a) vs. 7(b): conflicting termination mechanisms (drafting problem).** Subsection (a) says the prohibition lasts until a joint resolution, while (b) ends the whole Act at 10 years. The bill doesn't say which controls or whether (a) has any work to do if (b) arrives first.

33. **Sec. 7(a): no NAS report is actually commissioned (drafting problem).** Congress must act "on a report" of the National Academies, but nothing directs or funds such a report, and "serious harm" is undefined. A joint resolution is ordinary legislation anyway, so this subsection adds little legal effect.

34. **Sec. 7(b): fate of pending enforcement at sunset (ambiguity).** It's unclear whether penalties for pre-sunset violations survive expiration. The general savings statute, 1 U.S.C. § 109, addresses repeal, and its application to self-expiring statutes is less settled, so this is worth counsel's review.

35. **Sec. 7(b) vs. Sec. 8: shortened effective life (drafting problem).** The sunset runs from enactment while the effective date is 180 days later, so the prohibition operates for about 9.5 years.

## Sec. 8: Effective Date

36. **Sec. 8: 180-day gap (narrows coverage, timing).** Operations between enactment and the effective date appear lawful under Sec. 3. Combined with item 28, this creates a window of uncertain exposure rather than clear prohibition.

37. **Sec. 8 vs. Sec. 3(b): mismatched reference dates (drafting problem).** The grandfather clause keys to enactment, but the prohibition keys to the effective date. That mismatch invites dispute over licenses whose status changes in between.

## General

38. **No severability clause (minor).** If Sec. 2(1)(B) or another provision fails, courts would likely sever it anyway under the default presumption (*Barr v. AAPC*, 2020). The omission is weak as an "unworkability" argument but may be worth noting.

39. **No import or market provisions (narrows coverage).** Foreign-mined nodules and the metals refined from them can enter U.S. commerce freely, which also undercuts the bill's stated rationale.

## Strategic Notes for Legal

- **Strongest items for continued operation:** 1 (per-vessel threshold), 11 (foreign subsidiaries), 16–19 (grandfather clause), and 20 (environmental-assessment exemption). These depend heavily on facts: our license status, vessel configuration, and corporate structure.
- **Strongest "unworkable" arguments:** 7–8, 21–22, 27–30, and 32. These are about undefined administration, vagueness in the criminal provisions, and statutory conflicts.
- **Markup risk:** Raising technical defects at markup often prompts a clarifying amendment that closes them, particularly the per-vessel threshold, the grandfather clause, and the effective-date conflict. Legal and the policy team may want to decide which items to raise publicly and which to reserve for post-enactment compliance planning or litigation.
