**Section 2: Definitions**

1. **§2(1)(A), per-vessel threshold.** The 10,000-ton test applies to "a single vessel," with no aggregation across vessels, affiliates, or a single project. An operator could spread recovery across several vessels, each under the line, and stay outside the ban.

2. **§2(1)(A), measurement gaps.** The text doesn't say whether tonnage is wet or dry weight, whether water and sediment in the slurry count, or what "per year" means (calendar year, rolling 12 months, or prorated for partial-year operations). It also doesn't define "recovery," so it's unclear whether the count is at the seabed or on deck. Each gap lets operators report below the threshold and gives defendants grounds to contest a violation.

3. **§2(1)(A), depth test.** The text doesn't say where the 200-meter depth is measured (the vessel, the collector, or the mine site) or how sloping terrain is handled. Anything at or shallower than 200 meters falls outside the definition entirely, despite the "deep seabed" title.

4. **§2(1)(A), "other mineral resources."** Only nodules are named, and nothing defines whether crusts, sulfide deposits, rare-earth muds, or brines count. Operators targeting non-nodule resources could argue they are outside the definition.

5. **§2(1)(B), undefined "Secretary."** Section 6 specifies "Secretary of State," but §2(1)(B) just says "the Secretary." It is unclear which department holds this power, and a challenger could argue the delegation is invalid for that reason.

6. **§2(1)(B), standard and procedure.** The "risks … comparable to" test has no criteria, no required rulemaking or notice, and no timing. Because §3 and the criminal penalty in §5(b) depend on this definition, defendants can raise vagueness, fair-notice, and nondelegation challenges, and the Secretary could expand or narrow the ban at will.

7. **§2(2), narrow "U.S. person."** The definition covers only citizens and corporations organized under state law. It leaves out permanent residents, LLCs, partnerships, trusts, nonprofits, and unincorporated groups. "State" is also undefined, so DC- or territory-chartered entities are unclear.

8. **§2(2), foreign structures.** There is no ownership or control test, so a foreign-incorporated subsidiary of a U.S. company, or a foreign vehicle with U.S. financing, may fall outside "U.S. person." The extraterritorial reach in §3(a) can be avoided through corporate structuring.

9. **§2(3), registry and ban don't match.** The registry covers only vessels over 5,000 gross tons, but the ban can be triggered by any single vessel recovering over 10,000 tons. A smaller vessel could mine above the threshold without ever being registered or reporting. The text also doesn't say which gross tonnage measure applies.

10. **§2(3), "equipped for recovery."** Status depends on a vessel's current equipment, so equipment installed or removed between reporting periods could move a vessel in and out of coverage. Support vessels, barges, and seafloor collectors that aren't vessels aren't addressed.

**Section 3: Prohibition**

11. **§3(a), "in the United States."** The term is undefined, so it's unclear whether it reaches the territorial sea, the EEZ, the continental shelf, or U.S.-flag vessels at sea. Because mining happens offshore, this affects who can be reached. Foreign persons operating abroad are outside the ban entirely.

12. **§3(a), conduct covered.** Only "engag[ing] in" commercial mining is banned. Nothing prohibits financing, processing, importing, purchasing, or transporting seabed minerals, and nothing addresses attempt, conspiracy, or aiding and abetting. Sub-threshold exploration and test mining stay lawful, with no cumulative cap.

13. **§3(b), grandfathering mismatch.** The heading says "existing operations," but the exemption turns on whether an exploration license predates enactment. The text doesn't say whether a license authorizing exploration also covers commercial-scale recovery, nor what "exploration license" means or which authority (U.S., foreign, or international) must have issued it.

14. **§3(b), no limits.** There is no volume cap, expiration, or bar on transfers, renewals, extensions, or expanded areas, and the sunset in §7(b) means holders may be able to wait out the ban. The enactment-date cutoff could also prompt a rush of license applications before the bill passes.

15. **§3(b), commercial recovery permits.** Only exploration licenses are grandfathered. If any holder has a commercial recovery permit or similar authorization, the bill doesn't protect it, which invites takings and due-process litigation.

16. **§3(c), environmental assessment exemption.** The exemption turns on the "purpose" of the activity, with no volume limit, permit, approval, oversight, or restriction on selling recovered material. Operators could label pilot or test extraction as assessment work, and the exemption overrides the 10,000-ton threshold entirely.

**Section 4: Registry**

17. **§4(a), registration.** There is no deadline, no definition of "operator," and no rulemaking authority for NOAA. The registry applies to vessels regardless of flag or ownership, which exceeds the reach of §3.

18. **§4(b), reports.** The text requires only a description of "recovery activities." It doesn't require quantities, locations, depths, resource types, or whether the vessel is operating under a license or the assessment exemption. It also sets no due dates, no standard for the operator's certification, and no audit, inspection, or verification power. It says nothing about public access to reports or protection of confidential business information.

19. **§4 and §5, no penalty for registry violations.** §5 penalizes only violations of §3. Failing to register, failing to report, or filing false reports has no stated consequence. The text also doesn't require NOAA to share registry data with an enforcement agency.

**Section 5: Penalties**

20. **§5(a) vs. §8, timing conflict.** Civil penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the Act doesn't take effect until 180 days later. The text doesn't resolve whether conduct in that window is penalized, which invites fair-notice and retroactivity challenges.

21. **§5(a), no enforcement machinery.** The bill names no enforcing agency and sets no procedures for notice, hearings, or penalty factors. It provides no injunctive relief, no forfeiture or seizure of vessels or minerals, no citizen suits, and no definition of "each day" (for example, per vessel or per site).

22. **§5, extraterritorial enforcement.** The text has no provisions for foreign-flag vessels, evidence-gathering at sea, or venue for offenses outside any judicial district. Reach over U.S. persons "anywhere" may be hard to use in practice.

23. **§5(b), "willfully."** The mental state is undefined. Defendants can argue they didn't know they exceeded the threshold or that the Secretary's determination under §2(1)(B) applied to them, and the vagueness in the definition makes willfulness hard to prove.

**Sections 6–8: International, duration, effective date**

24. **§6, "seek to negotiate."** The duty is only to try, and the text doesn't say whether the 18-month deadline applies to starting talks or finishing an agreement. There is no reporting requirement to Congress and no consequence if the deadline is missed.

25. **§6, "equivalent" prohibitions.** The bill doesn't define equivalence or say how a deal would relate to the existing international regime. An agreement with weaker or different terms could still arguably satisfy the section.

26. **§7(a), vague trigger.** Lifting the ban requires a joint resolution finding that mining can be done "without serious harm." "Serious harm" is undefined, nothing requires the National Academies to produce a report or sets a deadline, and no expedited procedure exists, so the mechanism may never operate as written.

27. **§7(a) vs. §7(b), internal conflict.** §7(a) implies the ban lasts until Congress acts, while §7(b) ends the whole Act after 10 years regardless. Since the clock starts at enactment and the ban begins 180 days later, the actual prohibition lasts about 9.5 years, and the §7(a) process becomes largely irrelevant.

28. **§7(b), no savings clause.** The bill doesn't say whether violations committed before the sunset can still be penalized afterward. The general federal savings statute (1 U.S.C. 109) may help for criminal liability, but it's unclear whether it covers civil penalties or sunset-driven expiration.

29. **§8, 180-day gap.** Operators can start or ramp up activity before the prohibition takes effect, and the text doesn't bar agencies from issuing new authorizations during that window. Meanwhile §3(b), §5(a), §6, and §7(b) are all keyed to enactment rather than the effective date.

**Cross-cutting**

30. **Missing implementation provisions.** The text doesn't designate a lead agency, grant rulemaking authority, authorize appropriations, or include a severability clause. If a court struck down a provision like §2(1)(B), it would be unclear whether the rest stands.

31. **Relationship to existing law.** The bill doesn't amend, repeal, or reference the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act of 1980, which NOAA administers and which authorizes licensing and permitting, or any outer continental shelf mineral-leasing authority. That leaves conflicting statutory regimes and arguments about which controls. Please verify the current status of those statutes before publishing.
