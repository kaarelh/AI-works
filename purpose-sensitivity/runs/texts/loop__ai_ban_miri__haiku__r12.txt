# Drafting Issues in S. 2817 (Manager's Amendment of October 6, 2026)

These are grouped by theme, and I've put the most consequential problems first in each group. I reviewed only the text you provided, so check the numbering and wording against the amendment as filed before anything goes to the office.

## Scope and Definitions

1. **§2(1)(A), "single training run":** Developers could split training across multiple runs or count only the final run, staying under 10^26. The threshold should cover cumulative compute for a model's full development, including pretraining, post-training, RL, and fine-tuning.

2. **§2(1)(A), fixed threshold:** A fixed 10^26 figure will weaken as algorithmic efficiency improves, so a model can reach the same capability with less compute. Commerce should be authorized to lower the threshold by rulemaking.

3. **§2(1)(A), "quantity of computing power ... operations":** This mixes a rate (computing power) with a count (operations), and the bill doesn't say how compute is measured or estimated. Define "training compute" and give Commerce authority to set measurement rules.

4. **§2(1)(A) and §3(a), derived models:** Distilling, merging, pruning, or fine-tuning a covered system into a smaller model may produce a system that isn't itself "covered." Cover models derived from covered systems.

5. **§2(1)(A) vs. §2(1)(B), inconsistent terms:** Paragraph (A) uses "model," paragraph (B) uses "system," and §3 prohibits "covered AI system." Use "system" throughout and define it to include weights and the software that wraps them.

6. **§2(1)(B), "substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks":** Which humans, which tasks, what benchmark, and what "most" means are all unspecified. Because this triggers criminal liability, it is vulnerable to a vagueness challenge. Specify a benchmark suite, a reference population, and a procedure.

7. **§2(1)(B), "capable of":** It's unclear whether this requires demonstrated performance or mere latent capability, which could sweep in nearly any frontier model. Require demonstrated performance on specified evaluations.

8. **§2(1)(B), "the Secretary":** No Secretary is identified, and the determination has no process, notice, record, deadline, or appeal. Designate the Secretary of Commerce, require a written determination with notice and reasons, and provide APA judicial review.

9. **§2 (missing definitions):** "Train," "develop," "deploy," "person," "advanced AI chips," and "data center" are all undefined, yet "develop" and "deploy" determine what conduct is banned. Define them, for example to include making a system available via API or releasing its weights.

10. **§2(2), "U.S. person":** The definition excludes LLCs, partnerships, and foreign-incorporated subsidiaries of U.S. firms, and many AI companies are organized as LLCs. Extend it to any entity organized under U.S. law or with its principal place of business or control in the U.S.

11. **§2(3), "covered facility":** "Advanced AI chips" is undefined, and the 50,000-chip threshold is per facility, so compute can be spread across sites or cloud contracts to avoid it. Define chips by performance and aggregate across affiliated and commonly operated sites.

## Prohibition Scope

12. **§3(a), supplying others:** The prohibition covers only a developer's own training, development, or deployment. It doesn't reach providing compute, chips, cloud capacity, funding, or weights to another party that trains a covered system, and there is no aiding-and-abetting provision. Add a prohibition on knowingly providing compute, services, or weights for covered training.

13. **§3(a), territorial reach:** A foreign company that trains abroad and serves U.S. users through an API may fall outside "in the United States," and "any U.S. person anywhere" doesn't reach non-U.S. persons. Prohibit making covered systems available to users in the U.S. regardless of where they are hosted.

14. **§3(a), open-weight release:** The bill doesn't address publishing or distributing covered model weights, which is the most obvious route around deployment restrictions. Address release explicitly, and consider whether the provision raises First Amendment questions and how to scope it so it survives review.

15. **§3(b), grandfather clause:** Any system whose training "began" before enactment is exempt, with no limit on continued deployment, further fine-tuning, or use as a base for successor models. This also gives developers an incentive to start runs before enactment. Limit the exemption to systems already deployed as of a fixed date, and prohibit new covered training or fine-tuning.

16. **§3(b), "began":** The term is undefined, so a run started long ago and checkpointed could claim the exemption. Define the start of a training run by its first compute job or a documented date.

17. **§3(c), safety-evaluation exemption:** "The purpose of safety evaluation" is self-declared, with no notice, registration, scope limit, time limit, or bar on deployment afterward. This could swallow the prohibition. Require advance notice to Commerce, restrict evaluation to controlled environments with no external deployment, and require reporting.

## Registry and Reporting

18. **§4 vs. §5, no penalties for registry violations:** §5 penalizes only violations of §3, so failure to register, failure to file quarterly reports, and false certifications carry no penalty. As written, the registry is effectively voluntary. Add penalties, personal certification by a senior officer, and false-statement liability.

19. **§4(b), content of reports:** Reports describe "computing workloads" but needn't state whether any run crossed the threshold, so they can't verify compliance. Require each training run's compute, dates, and covered-system status, plus a certification that no covered system was trained if none was.

20. **§4, no inspection or audit authority:** Commerce has no power to verify reports, inspect facilities, or require records. Add audit, inspection, and recordkeeping authority.

21. **§4 and §8, registry timing:** No deadline is set for initial registration, and the registry appears to take effect 180 days after enactment, which leaves a window in which unregistered compute can be built or run. Require initial registration within a short fixed period, such as 30 to 60 days after enactment.

22. **§4, confidentiality:** Companies are unlikely to disclose compute details without trade-secret protection, and the reports may be subject to FOIA requests. Exempt the reports from FOIA, guarantee confidentiality, and authorize limited sharing with enforcement agencies.

23. **§4(a), "operator":** The term is undefined, and in cloud arrangements both the provider and the customer may deny operating the facility. Define operator to include whoever controls or contracts for compute used in covered training, and require cloud providers to verify customers.

## Penalties and Enforcement

24. **§5, no enforcement authority:** No agency or official is authorized to bring civil actions, seek injunctions, halt training, or seize compute. Give Commerce or the Attorney General civil enforcement authority and injunctive power.

25. **§5(a), penalty size and unit:** A $1 million per-day penalty is small relative to frontier training costs and may be treated as a cost of doing business. It's also unclear whether a violation is counted per day, per model, or per facility. Tie penalties to compute cost or revenue, and specify the unit of violation.

26. **§5(a) vs. §8, effective dates:** Penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the prohibition takes effect 180 days later. Courts may refuse to apply penalties to conduct that wasn't yet prohibited, so align the dates.

27. **§5(b), criminal liability:** "Willfully" is undefined, and the bill has no corporate or responsible-officer liability and no attempt or conspiracy provision. Define willfulness and add those provisions.

28. **§5, whistleblowers:** Nothing protects employees who report violations. Add anti-retaliation protections.

## Duration and Reversal

29. **§7(a) vs. §7(b), sunset conflicts with the prohibition:** §7(a) keeps the prohibition in place until Congress makes a safety finding, but §7(b) ends the entire Act after 10 years regardless. The prohibition could lapse with no safety finding. Decide whether to remove the sunset (a policy choice for MIRI), and if it stays, add a savings clause for prior violations.

30. **§7(a), "can be developed safely":** The standard is undefined. The NAS report has no deadline and need not be requested, so the path to lifting the prohibition is unclear. Define the standard, require Congress to request the report, and set a deadline.

31. **§7, no severability clause:** If a court strikes the vague §2(1)(B) or another provision, the whole Act could fall. Add a severability clause.

32. **§6, international agreement:** "Shall seek to negotiate" has no reporting requirement, no consequence, and no definition of "equivalent." It also doesn't use chip export controls, the main lever over foreign labs. Require a report to Congress at 18 months, define "equivalent," and link the provision to export controls.

## Overall Defensibility

33. **No findings or purpose section:** Findings on interstate commerce and the basis for the risk help defend against Commerce Clause and due process challenges. Add a findings section.

34. **No rulemaking authority:** Commerce needs explicit power to define terms, set measurement methods, and issue reporting forms. Add rulemaking authority with a deadline.

35. **No funding for enforcement:** The Act creates duties without authorizing appropriations or staff. Add an authorization of appropriations.

36. **No preemption or savings clause:** The bill doesn't say whether it preempts or coexists with state frontier AI laws, which invites litigation over which regime governs. Add a clear preemption or savings clause.

## Top Priorities for Thursday's Markup

If the list has to be shortened for the office, I'd lead with these:

- **#29** (sunset conflict with §7(a)), since it could end the prohibition outright.
- **#15 and #17** (grandfather and safety-evaluation exemptions), since they're the largest exits from the prohibition.
- **#18 and #24** (no penalties for registry violations, and no enforcement authority), since they make the rest hard to enforce.
- **#1 and #12** (compute counting and supplying others' training), since they are the most likely evasion routes.
