# Issues in S. 2817 (as amended October 6, 2026)

This review is based only on the bill text provided. Legislative counsel should check these items against the full amendment and the cross-referenced statutes, such as 1 U.S.C. § 1, which supplies a default definition of "person."

**Definitions and scope**

1. **§2(1)(A)**: "in a single training run" invites decomposition. A developer could split one effort into sequential or parallel runs, or use continued pretraining, so that no single run exceeds 10^26 operations. The threshold should count cumulative compute for a model lineage.
2. **§2(1)(A)**: The bill does not say whether post-training, RL, fine-tuning, or distillation count toward the threshold, or how operations are measured (precision, sparsity, dense vs. effective). Different accounting methods can move a system across the line.
3. **§2(1)(A)**: The 10^26 threshold is fixed, and nothing authorizes the Secretary to update it. Algorithmic efficiency gains will allow dangerous systems to be trained below the threshold over time.
4. **§2(1)(B)**: "the Secretary" has no antecedent, and the bill does not name the office. The determination has no standard (which tasks, what human baseline, what "substantially" means), no procedure, no notice, and no appeal, which creates vagueness and nondelegation challenges.
5. **§2(1)(B)**: "any artificial intelligence system" is not limited to machine learning models or general-purpose capability. Read literally, it could reach narrow superhuman tools such as game engines or scientific software, inviting overbreadth challenges. Case-by-case determinations also leave a gap until a determination is issued.
6. **§2(1)**: Subsection (A) defines a "model," subsection (B) refers to a "system," and §3 prohibits "covered AI system[s]." It is unclear whether applications, agents, or products built on a covered model are separately covered.
7. **§2(2)**: "U.S. person" excludes LLCs, partnerships, unincorporated entities, foreign subsidiaries of U.S. companies, and lawful permanent residents. "State" is also undefined, so it is unclear whether D.C. and the territories are included.
8. **§2(3)**: "advanced AI chip" is undefined, so the 50,000-chip registry trigger can be avoided by using hardware that appears on no list.
9. **§2(3)**: "facility" and "containing" are undefined. Distributed training across multiple sites, or leased capacity, can keep each site under 50,000 chips, and it is unclear who is the "operator" when a cloud provider hosts a customer's chips.
10. **§2(3) and §4**: The 50,000-chip facility trigger has no relationship to the 10^26 model threshold. A covered model can be trained at a facility below 50,000 chips and never be reported.

**Prohibition (§3)**

11. **§3(a)**: "train," "develop," and "deploy" are undefined. It is unclear whether inference, API access, fine-tuning of covered models, open-weight release, or research counts. Narrow readings will be argued by industry, and any reach into publishing weights or code may draw First Amendment challenges.
12. **§3(a)**: The bill does not prohibit enabling conduct. Providing chips, cloud compute, financing, or model weights to a foreign party that trains a covered system is not addressed, and the "anywhere" language reaches only U.S. persons who do the training.
13. **§3(a)**: A foreign developer that trains and serves users entirely outside the United States falls outside the statute. The international agreement in §6 is the only route to those actors.

**Exemptions (§3(b)–(c))**

14. **§3(b)**: "training began" is undefined. An initial checkpoint or preliminary run before enactment could be used to claim grandfathered status, allowing a system to be trained further and deployed indefinitely. The bill also requires no registration of grandfathered systems, so regulators cannot verify which systems are exempt.
15. **§3(b) and §8**: Runs started between enactment and the 180-day effective date are not grandfathered under §3(b), but the bill does not say whether they become covered once §3 takes effect. This creates a window in which developers can start runs and continue them.
16. **§3(c)**: "activities conducted for the purpose of safety evaluation" is undefined and is determined by the operator's stated purpose. There are no limits on compute, scope, or public deployment, and no notice requirement. This is likely the largest exemption risk, since capability training can be relabeled as safety evaluation.
17. **§3(c)**: The exemption covers "activities" generally, including training and deployment, and does not say whether evaluation activity must be reported under §4.

**Registry (§4)**

18. **§4(a)**: Registration is triggered by facility size, not by training a covered system. Commerce therefore does not learn which covered systems exist, and no one is required to register a covered model.
19. **§4(b)**: "describing the computing workloads" does not specify required content such as model identity, compute used, purpose, or checkpoints, so reports can be vague and unverifiable. Certification is by "the operator," with no responsible officer named.
20. **§4**: There are no deadlines for initial registration or the first quarterly report, no effective date for the section, no rulemaking authority, and no confidentiality protection. Without confidentiality protection, FOIA requests and litigation could expose reported information.
21. **§4 and §5**: Failure to register, late reports, and false certifications carry no penalty, because §5 penalizes only violations of §3. In practice, the reporting requirement is voluntary.
22. **§4**: The bill provides no inspection, audit, or access authority. Commerce must accept certifications at face value, which weakens the compute-based regime.

**Penalties and enforcement (§5)**

23. **§5(a)**: The bill does not name an enforcing agency or procedure for civil penalties (Attorney General or Commerce; administrative or judicial). It also gives no authority to seek injunctions, halt training, or seize chips or weights, so a fine alone may not stop an ongoing run.
24. **§5(a)**: A $1 million-per-day penalty may be treated as a cost of doing business for large developers. The bill has no cap, escalation for repeat violations, or penalty tied to compute or revenue.
25. **§5(a) and §8**: The civil penalty applies to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §3 takes effect 180 days later. The penalty clause and the effective-date clause conflict.
26. **§5(b)**: "willfully" requires proof of knowledge. Because coverage depends on FLOP accounting and a Secretary determination, defendants will claim they did not know a system was covered. The bill should consider a knowledge-of-facts or recklessness standard and add attempt and conspiracy language.
27. **§5**: The bill has no whistleblower protections and no individual liability for responsible officers in the civil penalty provision. Both matter because the government will have limited visibility into training runs.

**International agreement (§6)**

28. **§6**: "seek to negotiate" is satisfied by opening talks. The bill does not require the agreement to be concluded, reported to Congress, or followed by any domestic adjustment if talks fail.
29. **§6**: "equivalent" is undefined, so there is no standard for what a foreign regime must contain to count, and no verification or enforcement mechanism is specified.

**Duration and effective date (§§7–8)**

30. **§7(a) and §7(b)**: §7(a) keeps the prohibition in effect "until" Congress finds otherwise, but §7(b) ends the entire Act after 10 years regardless of any finding. This is a direct internal conflict. The sunset also tells developers that the pause ends in roughly 2036.
31. **§7(a)**: "can be developed safely" has no standard. The NASEM report has no deadline, scope, or funding, and NASEM is a private nonprofit, so the Act would need to direct and fund the study.
32. **§7(a)**: The bill does not specify the joint-resolution procedure, including presentment to the President or any expedited consideration. Without this, the finding mechanism may be contested.
33. **§7(b)**: The sunset has no savings clause. It is unclear whether violations before the sunset remain prosecutable, and whether systems trained under the Act may be deployed afterward.
34. **§8**: The 180-day delay creates a window for training to proceed, and the bill does not say whether the registry or reporting obligations begin at enactment or at the effective date.

**Structural and legal vulnerabilities**

35. **No severability clause**: If §2(1)(B) is struck down for vagueness or nondelegation, the whole Act could fall.
36. **No judicial review standard**: Secretary determinations are likely reviewable under the APA, but the bill sets no record requirement, standard of review, or procedure, which invites litigation.
37. **No rulemaking or appropriations authority**: Commerce cannot implement the registry, issue guidance, or make determinations without implementing regulations and funding, and the bill provides neither.
38. **No findings or purpose statement**: Findings tying the regulation to the interstate commerce power, national security, and the chip supply chain would strengthen the Act's constitutional footing and help courts interpret ambiguous terms.

**Recommended priorities for the Thursday markup**

If staff must pick the most important fixes, I suggest: (1) resolve the §7(a)/§7(b) conflict; (2) define "safety evaluation" and bound §3(c); (3) add cumulative-compute counting and an update mechanism to §2(1)(A); (4) make §4 enforceable and tie registration to covered systems; and (5) add injunctive authority and a named enforcing agency to §5.

I can draft specific amendment language for any of these items if that would help the staff prepare for the markup.
