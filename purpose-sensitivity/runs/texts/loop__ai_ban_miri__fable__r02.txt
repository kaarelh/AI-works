RESPONSE:
Here's a section-by-section review. I've grouped items by section and flagged the ones I'd put at the top of the list for Senator Whitfield's staff at the end.

## Sec. 2(1) — "Covered AI system"

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "in a single training run."** This is the biggest hole in the definitional prong. A developer can split training into multiple runs (pretrain, continued pretrain, RL post-training, fine-tune), each under 10^26, or merge/ensemble sub-threshold models; the definition should count cumulative compute across all stages contributing to a model, including compute used to generate its training data.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "training run" and "training" are undefined.** It's unclear whether RL post-training, distillation from a larger model, synthetic-data generation, or inference-time compute scaling count as "training." Given that post-training is now a large share of frontier compute, this ambiguity is exploitable.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — static threshold.** 10^26 is hard-coded with no mechanism to lower it as algorithmic efficiency improves; the bill should give the Secretary authority to adjust the threshold downward (and arguably bar adjusting it upward without congressional action).

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — no measurement rule.** The bill doesn't specify how operations are counted, who counts them, or what records must be kept, which makes the threshold hard to prove in an enforcement action. Add a counting methodology (or delegate one by rule) and a record-keeping requirement.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(A) vs. (B) — "model" vs. "system."** Prong (A) covers a "model," prong (B) a "system," and Sec. 3(a) prohibits training a "system." A developer could argue a trained model is not a "system" until integrated with tooling, or that an agentic scaffold of sub-threshold models is a "system" but no "model" crosses the line. Use one defined term consistently, and cover scaffolds/ensembles explicitly.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — "the Secretary" is never defined.** Secretaries of Commerce and State both appear elsewhere; a court can't tell who has the determination authority. Define "Secretary" in Sec. 2.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — coverage depends on an affirmative determination that is purely discretionary.** Until the Secretary acts, a system beyond human performance is not covered, so inaction (or a hostile administration) nullifies the prong. Add a mandatory duty to assess, deadlines, a petition process, and a presumption that systems meeting specified capability indicators are covered unless the Secretary finds otherwise.

8. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — the determination comes after the fact.** The Secretary can only "determine" a system is superintelligent once it exists, by which point the harm has occurred; the prong has no anticipatory reach to training runs reasonably expected to produce such a system. Tie coverage to systems "that are or are reasonably likely to be" capable.

9. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — vagueness invites challenge.** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no benchmark, baseline human population, or measurement standard, and because the bill carries 10-year criminal penalties, a void-for-vagueness challenge is foreseeable. Specify criteria by rule and limit criminal liability to the bright-line prong (A) or to knowing violations after a published determination.

10. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — no procedure or judicial-review provision.** There's no notice, hearing, or standard of review for determinations, so they will be litigated under default APA rules; specify a procedure and an expedited, deferential review standard.

11. **Sec. 2 generally — "artificial intelligence" and "artificial intelligence model" are undefined.** Cross-reference an existing definition (e.g., 15 U.S.C. 9401(3)) to avoid litigation over whether a given system is "AI" at all.

## Sec. 2(2) — "U.S. person"

12. **Sec. 2(2) — omits most legal entities.** "Corporation organized under the laws of a State" excludes LLCs, partnerships, trusts, nonprofits, and entities organized under federal law; most AI labs' operating entities are LLCs. Use the standard formulation: any entity organized under the laws of the United States, any State, or any jurisdiction within the United States.

13. **Sec. 2(2) — omits lawful permanent residents and foreign subsidiaries.** The term covers citizens but not green-card holders, and does not reach foreign entities owned or controlled by U.S. persons, so a U.S. lab can offshore training through a foreign subsidiary. Add LPRs and entities controlled by U.S. persons (as in OFAC/export-control definitions).

14. **Sec. 2(2) — "a State" is undefined.** Without a definition that includes D.C., Puerto Rico, and the territories, entities organized there may fall outside the Act.

## Sec. 2(3) — "Covered facility"

15. **Sec. 2(3) — "advanced AI chips" is undefined.** The registry has no operative trigger until this is defined; cross-reference the export-control thresholds (ECCN 3A090/4A090) or delegate a definition by rule with authority to update it.

16. **Sec. 2(3) — single-site threshold is easily evaded.** A company can run 49,000 chips at each of several sites and train across them; the definition should aggregate chips under common ownership, control, or interconnection, and "data center" should be defined.

17. **Sec. 2(3) — static chip count.** As chips grow more capable, 50,000 chips will represent far more compute than today; tie the threshold to aggregate compute capacity (FLOP/s) or allow downward adjustment.

18. **Sec. 2(3)–Sec. 4 — "operator" is undefined.** It's unclear whether a colocation landlord, the cloud provider, or the tenant running the workload must register and report; define it to capture whoever controls the compute and whoever uses it.

## Sec. 3 — Prohibition

19. **Sec. 3(a) — "develop" is undefined and overbroad in a way that invites First Amendment challenge.** Read broadly it reaches research, writing code, or publishing papers; read narrowly it adds nothing to "train." Define it in terms of conduct (allocating compute, assembling training data, executing training) rather than speech.

20. **Sec. 3(a) — missing verbs.** The prohibition does not cover possessing, transferring, exporting, selling, licensing, importing, providing access to, or using a covered system, nor providing compute, chips, funding, or other material support to a covered training run. A U.S. cloud provider could lawfully host a foreign lab's covered run, and a U.S. company could lawfully offer a foreign-trained superintelligence to U.S. customers via API.

21. **Sec. 3(a) — no attempt, conspiracy, or aiding-and-abetting liability.** Enforcement can only begin after a completed violation; add attempt and conspiracy, and liability for knowingly facilitating a violation.

22. **Sec. 3(a) — unclear application to the federal government.** "Person" by default does not include the United States, so a court could read the Act as not binding federal agencies or contractors acting on their behalf. State explicitly whether the prohibition applies to the government (and if any national-security carve-out is intended, draft it narrowly rather than leaving it to implication).

23. **Sec. 3(a) — no scienter standard for the civil violation.** Without a stated standard, defendants will argue they could not know a run would cross 10^26 or that a system would be "determined" covered; specify strict liability for the compute prong and knowing/reckless liability for the capability prong.

24. **Sec. 3(b) — grandfather clause swallows the prohibition.** Any system "whose training began" before enactment is exempt from the entire prohibition, including deployment, forever. A developer can start a run the day before enactment and continue it indefinitely, or claim any later work is "continued training" of a grandfathered model. At minimum, limit the exemption to deployment of models whose training *completed* before enactment, bar further training of those models, and exclude systems covered under prong (B).

25. **Sec. 3(b) — "training began" is ambiguous and unverifiable.** There's no definition of when training "begins" and no documentation requirement, so the date will be contested in every case; require contemporaneous records and shift the burden of proof to the party claiming the exemption.

26. **Sec. 3(c) — the safety-evaluation exception is unbounded.** It is purpose-based, undefined, available to anyone, and requires no authorization or disclosure, so any lab can label a frontier training run "safety evaluation." Narrow it to evaluation of *existing* systems (not training new ones), require prior written authorization from the Secretary with scope and compute limits, and require reporting.

27. **Sec. 3(c) — no limit on deployment under the exception.** As written, "deploy" for the purpose of safety evaluation is also exempt, which could cover deployment to a large user base framed as a "study."

## Sec. 4 — Registry

28. **Sec. 4 — no penalty for noncompliance.** Section 5 penalizes only violations of Section 3, so failing to register or filing a false report carries no penalty under this Act (18 U.S.C. 1001 may apply to false statements, but not to silence). Add civil and criminal penalties for Section 4 violations.

29. **Sec. 4(a) — no registration deadline or content requirements.** Specify when registration is due (including for facilities that cross the threshold later), what information must be provided, and an update obligation.

30. **Sec. 4(b) — reports are vague and unverifiable.** "Describing the computing workloads" doesn't require identifying training runs, their compute totals, the models trained, or the customers running them, and there is no audit, inspection, or verification authority. Require run-level reporting against the 10^26 threshold and give Commerce inspection and subpoena power.

31. **Sec. 4(b) — "certified by the operator" lacks teeth.** Specify certification by a senior officer under penalty of perjury.

32. **Sec. 4 — registry covers facilities, not developers.** There is no obligation on developers to report planned or ongoing covered training runs, or to report compute used across facilities, so the government's only visibility is through data-center operators who may not know what their tenants are doing. Add a developer-side notification requirement for any run expected to exceed a lower threshold (e.g., 10^25).

33. **Sec. 4 — no chip tracking.** The registry is keyed to facilities, not to chips, so chips moved out of registered facilities (or never placed in one) are invisible; consider a chip-level reporting or export-control linkage.

## Sec. 5 — Penalties

34. **Sec. 5(a) — penalty is immaterial to the likely violators.** $1,000,000 per day caps annual exposure at $365M, a rounding error for a frontier lab; add a penalty tied to a percentage of global revenue or multiple of the compute cost of the run, plus disgorgement of profits.

35. **Sec. 5(a) — no non-monetary remedies.** There is no injunctive relief, no authority to order a run halted, and no seizure or forfeiture of model weights, training data, or chips. A violator can pay the fine and keep the model; add mandatory destruction/forfeiture of weights produced in violation and seizure of the hardware.

36. **Sec. 5(a) — no enforcement authority designated.** The bill does not say who brings civil actions (DOJ? Commerce?), in what court, or under what procedure, nor does it authorize a private right of action or whistleblower rewards.

37. **Sec. 5(a) — "on or after the date of enactment" conflicts with Sec. 8.** The Act doesn't take effect until 180 days after enactment, so penalizing violations "on or after the date of enactment" is either surplusage or an unconstitutional retroactive penalty; conform the language to the effective date (or make Section 3 effective on enactment — see item 44).

38. **Sec. 5(a) — "each day on which the violation occurs" may undercount.** A single deployment or a training run that completes in one day could be argued to be a one-day violation; make clear that each day a covered system is trained, possessed, or deployed is a separate violation.

39. **Sec. 5(b) — "willfully" sets a high bar.** Willfulness generally requires proof the defendant knew the conduct was unlawful; consider "knowingly" for prong (A) violations, where the compute threshold is objective.

40. **Sec. 5(b) — organizational fines are capped by Title 18.** Under 18 U.S.C. 3571 an organization's fine is generally capped at $500,000 per count (or twice the gain), which is trivial here; specify a higher organizational fine and add individual liability for responsible corporate officers who authorize or direct a violation.

41. **Sec. 5 — no statute of limitations or venue provision**, and no mechanism for enforcing against U.S. persons acting abroad (e.g., no extraterritorial venue, asset-freeze, or sanctions linkage).

## Sec. 6 — International agreement

42. **Sec. 6 — purely hortatory.** "Seek to negotiate" creates no enforceable obligation, and it is ambiguous whether 18 months is the deadline to begin negotiating or to conclude an agreement. Require periodic reports to Congress, specify the agreement's required elements (verification, enforcement), and authorize the executive branch to use trade or export tools against non-participating jurisdictions.

## Sec. 7 — Duration

43. **Sec. 7(a) and 7(b) are contradictory.** Subsection (a) says the prohibition lasts until Congress affirmatively finds it can be lifted; subsection (b) sunsets the entire Act (definitions, registry, and all) after 10 years regardless. A 10-year sunset also signals to developers that they need only wait, and it would undercut any "equivalent" international agreement under Sec. 6. Strike (b); if a review point is politically necessary, make it a mandatory report to Congress rather than an automatic expiration.

44. **Sec. 7(a) — no mechanism for the National Academies report.** Nothing commissions, funds, or schedules the report, defines what "developed safely" means, or specifies what evidence the finding must rest on; add those elements so the lifting pathway can't be satisfied by a thin or politically driven report.

## Sec. 8 — Effective date

45. **Sec. 8 — the 180-day delay creates a rush window.** Combined with Sec. 3(b), the bill gives developers notice to start (and under 3(b), to grandfather) runs before enactment, and then another 180 days during which training is lawful. Make Section 3 effective on enactment (or very shortly after), and apply the delay only to the registry's administrative provisions if needed.

46. **Sec. 8 — status of runs ongoing at the effective date is unclear.** A run started after enactment but before the effective date is not grandfathered under 3(b), but the bill doesn't say whether continuing it after the effective date is a violation; state explicitly that it is.

## Cross-cutting omissions

47. **No rulemaking authority.** Nothing authorizes Commerce (or anyone) to issue implementing regulations, which leaves the undefined terms above with no administrative fix and weakens the government's position in litigation.

48. **No appropriations or administrative capacity.** The registry and enforcement functions have no funding or staffing authorization.

49. **No severability clause.** Given the vagueness exposure in Sec. 2(1)(B), a successful challenge to one provision could take down the whole Act.

50. **No preemption statement.** The bill doesn't say whether it preempts stricter state laws (it shouldn't); silence will be litigated.

51. **No whistleblower protection.** Insiders at labs are the most likely source of evidence of covert runs; add anti-retaliation protection and a reward provision.
