I worked only from the text you pasted. Each item is tagged **[Evasion]** (lets companies keep going), **[Enforcement]** (hard to administer or prove), or **[Challenge]** (easy to attack legally).

## Section 2: Definitions

1. **§2(1)(A), "single training run."** The term is undefined, so a developer could split work into sub-threshold runs or build a model through sequential runs, continued pretraining, or fine-tuning. Total compute could far exceed 10^26 without any one run crossing it. **[Evasion]**

2. **§2(1)(A), compute measurement.** The bill doesn't say who counts operations or how, or whether compute from earlier models, synthetic-data generation, or restarted runs counts. There is also no verification mechanism. **[Enforcement]**

3. **§2(1)(A), timing.** A model is covered once it has been "trained using" more than 10^26 operations, which is only true when a run crosses the line. It is unclear whether a run is unlawful from the start or only upon crossing, which would force regulators to act mid-run or prosecute after the fact. **[Enforcement]**

4. **§2(1)(A), compute as a proxy for "superintelligence."** The title targets superintelligence, but prong (A) captures models by compute alone. It is over-inclusive (powerful but not superintelligent models) and under-inclusive (more efficient or distilled models that reach high capability below the threshold). **[Challenge] [Evasion]**

5. **§2(1)(A), fixed threshold.** The number is hard-coded with no authority to adjust it. Algorithmic efficiency gains will erode its meaning over time. **[Evasion]**

6. **§2(1)(B), "the Secretary."** The Act never defines which Secretary. Section 6 says "Secretary of State" explicitly, so the reader can't tell whether this means Commerce or someone else. **[Enforcement]**

7. **§2(1)(B), the capability standard.** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no benchmark, and "substantially," "most," and "cognitive tasks" are all undefined. With criminal penalties attached, this invites a vagueness challenge. **[Challenge]**

8. **§2(1)(B), no process.** There is no notice, publication, comment period, deadline, appeal, or judicial review for determinations. It is also unclear whether a determination applies prospectively, so developers can't know in advance whether a system is covered. That raises fair-notice and nondelegation arguments. **[Challenge]**

9. **§2(1), "model" vs. "system."** Prong (A) covers a "model" and prong (B) a "system," and neither "artificial intelligence" nor either term is defined. Ensembles or agentic scaffolds built from sub-threshold models fall into a gray zone, reachable only through a discretionary (B) determination. **[Evasion]**

10. **§2(2), narrow "U.S. person."** The definition omits lawful permanent residents, LLCs, partnerships, nonprofits, universities, trusts, and federally chartered entities. "State" is undefined, so it's unclear whether D.C. or territorial entities count. **[Evasion]**

11. **§2(2), foreign subsidiaries.** A foreign subsidiary of a U.S. company is organized under foreign law and so is not a "U.S. person." Offshore training escapes the "anywhere" prong unless the parent can be tied to the conduct. **[Evasion]**

12. **§2(3), "advanced AI chip" and "data center."** Neither term is defined. A raw chip count also ignores performance differences across chip generations. **[Enforcement]**

13. **§2(3), the 50,000-chip threshold.** Operators can stay under it by splitting capacity across sites or affiliates, or by leasing capacity, and the bill has no aggregation rule. A 10^26-operation run is also feasible on a smaller cluster running longer. **[Evasion]**

14. **§2(3) vs. §2(1), mismatched thresholds.** The registry is keyed to facility size and the prohibition to compute or capability. A covered system could be trained entirely outside any registered facility, so the registry doesn't map onto the thing being banned. **[Enforcement]**

## Section 3: Prohibition

15. **§3(a), undefined verbs.** "Train, develop, or deploy" are undefined. The bill doesn't reach use, sale, distribution, open-weight release, hosting, API access, possession, or import, and "develop" overlaps with "train." **[Evasion]**

16. **§3(a), "in the United States."** "United States" is undefined, and it's unclear how the clause applies to remote activity, such as a foreign firm directing training from abroad on U.S.-hosted servers, or the reverse. **[Enforcement]**

17. **§3(a), no secondary liability.** Nothing addresses cloud providers, chip suppliers, facility operators, or investors who enable a violation, and there are no know-your-customer duties. Only general criminal aiding-and-abetting law could reach them. **[Evasion]**

18. **§3(a), government actors.** The bill doesn't say whether federal (including military and intelligence) or state entities are covered. "Person" is generally read not to include the federal government, so there is an ambiguity either way, and a national-security carve-out question goes unaddressed. **[Enforcement] [Challenge]**

19. **§3(b), open-ended grandfathering.** The exemption has no limits on continued training, scaling, fine-tuning, or successor versions, and it's unclear whether derivative models or new checkpoints count as the same "system." It also rewards starting runs before enactment. **[Evasion]**

20. **§3(b) vs. §8, wrong trigger date.** The exemption keys to enactment, but the Act takes effect 180 days later. A run started in that window isn't exempt but isn't yet prohibited, and it's unclear whether continuing it past day 180 is a violation. That invites reliance and retroactivity arguments. **[Evasion] [Challenge]**

21. **§3(c), "safety evaluation."** The term is undefined and the exemption turns on subjective "purpose," with no limits on compute, duration, or scope. Mixed-purpose activity is hard to police, and "activities" could be read to include training itself. **[Evasion]**

22. **§3(c), no safeguards.** The exemption requires no independent or government oversight, authorization, reporting, or containment. Nothing bars a developer from keeping or later deploying a system built "for evaluation." **[Evasion]**

## Section 4: Registry

23. **§4(a), thin registration rules.** There is no deadline, no required content, and no rulemaking authority for Commerce. "Operator" is undefined, so it's unclear whether the owner, a lessee, or a cloud tenant registers. **[Enforcement]**

24. **§4(b), "describing the computing workloads."** The bill doesn't require details such as compute used, model or customer identity, or duration. Operators of multi-tenant facilities may not know what customers run, and quarterly reporting lets a run finish between reports. **[Enforcement]**

25. **§4, no verification.** Reports are self-certified, with no audit, inspection, or data-access authority. **[Enforcement]**

26. **§4, no penalty.** Section 5 penalizes only violations of section 3, so failing to register or filing a false report carries no specified penalty. The only fallback would be general false-statement law. **[Enforcement]**

27. **§4, confidentiality and sharing.** The bill has no trade-secret or FOIA protections for reported data and no provision for sharing it with enforcers. Both gaps invite industry resistance and legal challenge. **[Challenge] [Enforcement]**

28. **§4, no foreign coverage.** Only facilities (presumably domestic) are registered, with nothing on overseas facilities used by U.S. persons. Training abroad would not appear in the registry. **[Evasion]**

## Section 5: Penalties

29. **§5(a), no enforcer.** The section never says who assesses or sues for civil penalties (Attorney General, Commerce, another agency). It sets no procedure, no hearing rights, no injunctive authority, and no private right of action. **[Enforcement]**

30. **§5(a), the $1,000,000/day cap.** The amount is small relative to frontier training budgets and could be treated as a cost of doing business. It has no minimum, no inflation adjustment, and no scaling to revenue or compute. **[Evasion]**

31. **§5(a), "each day on which the violation occurs."** It's unclear how to count days for a one-time act, such as starting a run, versus continuing conduct like training or deployment. **[Enforcement]**

32. **§5(a) vs. §8, conflicting dates.** Penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the prohibition doesn't take effect for 180 days. The language is either meaningless or reaches conduct that wasn't yet unlawful, which is a retroactivity argument for defendants. **[Challenge]**

33. **§5(b), "willfully."** The bill doesn't say whether the defendant must know the system is covered. Combined with discretionary (B) designations and compute thresholds, willfulness would be very hard to prove. **[Enforcement]**

34. **§5(b), individual liability.** Nothing addresses officers, directors, or engineers, or says how liability attaches to a corporation's conduct. **[Enforcement]**

35. **§5(b), "fined under title 18."** This incorporates the general fine caps, which for organizations are typically lower per felony count than $1,000,000 per day in civil penalties (subject to an alternative gain-or-loss fine). The criminal fine could therefore be the weaker deterrent for a company. **[Evasion]**

36. **§5, no other remedies.** There is no cease-and-desist authority, forfeiture or seizure of hardware or model weights, or whistleblower protection. **[Enforcement]**

## Section 6: International Agreement

37. **§6, "shall seek to negotiate."** This requires effort, not results. It's ambiguous whether the 18-month deadline applies to starting talks or concluding an agreement, and there is no consequence for missing it and no reporting requirement to Congress. **[Enforcement]**

38. **§6, "equivalent" prohibitions.** The term is undefined. The bill is silent on the form of agreement (treaty or executive agreement), Senate role, verification, and any link to export controls or sanctions. In the meantime nothing stops development from moving offshore. **[Evasion]**

## Section 7: Duration

39. **§7(a) vs. §7(b), internal contradiction.** Subsection (a) implies the ban lasts until Congress acts, while (b) ends the whole Act after 10 years regardless. It's unclear which controls. **[Challenge] [Enforcement]**

40. **§7(a), the lifting mechanism.** A "joint resolution" needs presidential signature or a veto override, yet the text says Congress "finds." The bill doesn't require NASEM to produce a report and sets no deadline, funding, or criteria, and "developed safely" is undefined. The lift is also all-or-nothing, with no partial or conditional relief. **[Enforcement]**

41. **§7(b), sunset timing and savings.** The 10 years run from enactment, not the effective date, so the ban lasts about 9.5 years. There is no savings clause for pending cases or accrued penalties, and the sunset gives companies an incentive to stockpile chips and prepare to train the day it expires. **[Evasion] [Enforcement]**

## Section 8 and Cross-Cutting Issues

42. **§8, the 180-day gap.** There are no interim measures, such as a freeze on new training starts or early registration. Combined with §3(b), this creates a rush-to-start window. **[Evasion]**

43. **No implementation architecture.** The bill designates no lead agency, rulemaking authority, appropriations, interagency coordination (Commerce, DOJ, State), or congressional reporting. **[Enforcement]**

44. **No severability clause.** If a court strikes a provision such as the §2(1)(B) designation power, the remaining text's survival would turn on judicial inference rather than express language. **[Challenge]**

45. **Likely constitutional arguments.** Challengers can be expected to raise vagueness and due process (§2(1)(B), §5(b)), nondelegation (§2(1)(B)), First Amendment claims over code and model weights as expression, and takings or ex post facto arguments tied to §3(b) and §5(a). I'd frame these as arguments opponents may make, not predictions of outcome. **[Challenge]**
