RESPONSE:
**Highest-priority items:** #30 (sunset conflict), #13 and #14 (grandfathering and the enactment-to-effective-date gap), #17 (safety-evaluation exemption), #24 (no injunctive remedy), and #1 (undefined "Secretary" and capability determination).

**Definitions (Sec. 2)**

1. **Sec. 2(1)(B):** "The Secretary" is never defined, and the bill doesn't say how capability determinations are made, what notice is given, whether there's a deadline, or whether the determination can be reviewed. This prong is the only coverage for dangerous systems trained below 10^26 operations, so it needs a defined process.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(B):** "Substantially exceeding," "most," and "cognitive tasks" are undefined, which invites vagueness challenges and lets developers argue their systems don't qualify. The standard should tie to measurable benchmarks or a published Secretary methodology.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A):** The threshold counts only compute in a "single training run," so a developer can split one effort into several runs or count pretraining, fine-tuning, and RL separately while staying under 10^26. Coverage should be based on cumulative training compute per model lineage.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A):** The bill doesn't say how operations are counted (precision, sparsity, synthetic-data generation, RL rollouts) or who measures them, leaving room to dispute whether the line was crossed. A Commerce measurement standard with a reporting duty would close this.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(A):** Coverage attaches to a single model, so combined systems of sub-threshold models (routing, ensembles, agent scaffolds) could reach covered-level capability without any one model being covered. Distilled "student" models trained from a covered model with less compute are also not addressed.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(A):** The compute threshold is fixed in statute while algorithmic efficiency improves, and nothing lets the Secretary adjust it. Over a 10-year horizon the line could become easy to evade, so the Secretary should have authority to update it.

7. **Sec. 2(2):** "U.S. person" covers only citizens and corporations organized under a State's law, which excludes LLCs, partnerships, and foreign-organized subsidiaries of U.S. companies. "State" is also undefined as to D.C. and the territories.

8. **Sec. 2(3):** "Advanced AI chips" is undefined, so developers could argue older or differently classified accelerators fall outside the 50,000-chip trigger. "Data center" and "facility" are also undefined, leaving open whether multi-site clusters and cloud regions are aggregated.

9. **Sec. 2 (general):** "Person," "train," "develop," "deploy," "operator," and "artificial intelligence" are all undefined. "Artificial intelligence" could cross-reference the existing statutory definition in 15 U.S.C. § 9401 (verify with Legislative Counsel), and "person" should expressly include entities.

**Prohibition (Sec. 3)**

10. **Sec. 3(a):** "Deploy" is undefined and may not reach open-weight publication, weight licensing, or hosted API access, which are the main ways a frontier model reaches the public. The prohibition should expressly cover releasing or transferring weights.

11. **Sec. 3(a):** The ban doesn't reach those who supply compute, chips, or financing for a covered training run, or those who knowingly facilitate one. Aiding-and-abetting language and duties for compute providers are needed.

12. **Sec. 3(a):** The ban applies to "any person in the United States" and "U.S. person[s]," so a foreign-organized developer training abroad with no U.S. nexus is outside it. A rule barring U.S. deployment of covered systems regardless of where they were trained would close this gap.

13. **Sec. 3(b):** Grandfathered systems are exempt from all of Section 3, so their training, further development, and deployment remain legal indefinitely. Grandfathered systems should face deployment limits or a sunset.

14. **Sec. 3(b) and Sec. 8:** The grandfather date is enactment, but the prohibition takes effect 180 days later, which creates a 180-day window in which developers can start runs without a legal bar and with an incentive to rush. It's also unclear whether a run started during that window is prohibited once the Act takes effect.

15. **Sec. 3(b):** "Training began" is undefined, so it's unclear whether resuming a checkpoint, or training a successor version from a grandfathered base, counts as a new system or a continuation of an exempt one.

16. **Sec. 3(b):** Grandfathered systems have no disclosure requirement, so the government cannot verify which systems qualify. A one-time attested disclosure of compute and training start dates should be required shortly after enactment.

17. **Sec. 3(c):** The safety-evaluation exemption has no limits on scale, duration, public deployment, or who conducts the evaluation, so a developer could label full-scale training or user-facing release as "safety evaluation." The "for the purpose of" test is also a subjective intent standard that is hard to disprove. Evaluations should require government authorization, bar public deployment, and require reporting of results.

**Registry (Sec. 4)**

18. **Sec. 4(a):** "Operator" is undefined. For cloud compute, it's unclear whether the registrant is the facility owner, the operator, or the customer running the training, so the registry could miss the actual developer.

19. **Sec. 4 (general):** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3, so failing to register or file reports carries no consequence. The registry is effectively voluntary.

20. **Sec. 4(b):** "Describing the computing workloads" doesn't require per-run compute totals, model identifiers, or start and end dates, so the Secretary can't check the Sec. 2(1)(A) threshold from the reports.

21. **Sec. 4(b):** Reports are certified by the operator but have no false-statement penalty, no audit or inspection authority, and no requirement that Commerce verify them. Without these, the registry depends entirely on good faith.

22. **Sec. 4 (general):** The bill has no confidentiality provision for reported information and no authority for Commerce to share it with enforcement agencies such as DOJ. Companies may resist reporting or litigate over disclosure, and enforcement coordination is unclear.

**Penalties (Sec. 5)**

23. **Sec. 5 (general):** No agency is designated to bring civil enforcement, and the bill has no express authority for injunctions or temporary restraining orders. Penalties after the fact cannot stop an ongoing training run, which is the core harm.

24. **Sec. 5(a):** The bill doesn't say whether a violation is counted per day, per run, per chip, or per model. At $1,000,000 per day, the penalty is small relative to frontier training budgets and may be treated as a cost of doing business. Penalties should scale with compute spend or the value of the violating activity.

25. **Sec. 5(a):** The penalty applies to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but Section 3 doesn't take effect until 180 days later under Sec. 8. The penalty trigger should match the effective date.

26. **Sec. 5 (general):** The bill has no forfeiture or destruction provisions for covered model weights, compute, or facilities, so a violating system can survive a penalty and be redeployed. Seizure and destruction authority is needed.

27. **Sec. 5(b):** "Willfully" is undefined, and the bill doesn't address whether corporate officers are personally liable. A responsible-corporate-officer provision and a definition of knowledge (for example, knowledge triggered by written notice from the Secretary) would make prosecution feasible.

28. **Sec. 5 (general):** The bill has no whistleblower protections, even though employees at AI developers are the most likely source of evidence of covered training. Protections for reporting violations should be added.

29. **Sec. 5 (general):** The bill doesn't specify whether attempts, conspiracies, or preparatory acts (such as acquiring or assembling a training cluster) are covered. Attempt and preparation language would let enforcement act before a run completes.

**Duration and sunset (Sec. 7)**

30. **Sec. 7(a) vs. Sec. 7(b):** The two provisions conflict. Sec. 7(a) keeps the prohibition in force until Congress finds, by joint resolution, that covered systems can be developed safely, while Sec. 7(b) ends the entire Act after 10 years regardless. The bill must say whether the prohibition is indefinite or expires at year 10, and this is the most urgent fix.

31. **Sec. 7(a):** "Can be developed safely" is undefined, and the NAS report has no deadline, scope, funding source, or requirement to issue at all. The trigger may never occur, and the bill doesn't say what happens if the report is never delivered.

**International agreement (Sec. 6)**

32. **Sec. 6:** "Shall seek to negotiate" creates no obligation to reach an agreement, report to Congress, or revisit domestic law if talks fail. "Equivalent" is also undefined, so a weak agreement could be treated as satisfying the requirement. A required report to Congress at 18 months and a defined equivalence standard would help.

**Structural and litigation risk**

33. **General:** The bill assigns the Secretary several determinations but gives no general rulemaking authority to define terms, set measurement methods, or update thresholds. It also doesn't authorize appropriations for enforcement capacity.

34. **General:** The bill has no severability clause. If a court strikes one provision (for example, Sec. 2(1)(B) on vagueness or nondelegation grounds, or the registry), it could treat the whole Act as invalid.

35. **General:** The bill has no express preemption or savings clause, so it's unclear whether it displaces state AI laws or leaves them in place. That ambiguity invites industry litigation and state-level confusion.

36. **General (litigation exposure):** Several vague terms (items 2, 9, 17, and 27) create vagueness and due-process risk. Delegating capability determinations to a Secretary without a defined standard invites a nondelegation challenge, and restrictions on releasing weights may draw First Amendment challenges. I recommend Legislative Counsel review these before the markup.
