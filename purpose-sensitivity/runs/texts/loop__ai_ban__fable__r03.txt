RESPONSE:
Here's a full pass through the bill, section by section. I've flagged every loophole, ambiguity, enforcement gap, and litigation vulnerability I could find. Items marked ★ are the ones I'd treat as must-fix before markup.

## Sec. 2(1)(A) — Compute threshold

1. ★ **Sec. 2(1)(A), "in a single training run."** A lab can split training into several runs each under 10^26 (pretraining in stages, then continued pretraining, then RL/post-training), or train several sub-threshold models and merge or distill them. Compute should be counted cumulatively across all phases that contribute to a model, including training of parent/teacher models and synthetic-data generation.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A), no measurement standard.** "Integer or floating-point operations" doesn't say how to count (dense vs. sparse ops, precision, whether to include evaluation passes), who counts, or what records must be kept. Without a defined methodology and record-keeping duty, every enforcement action turns into a battle of expert accountants.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A), fixed threshold.** 10^26 is hard-coded with no authority to lower it as algorithmic efficiency improves. Within a few years, systems far below the threshold could have the capabilities the bill targets, leaving only the vague prong (B) to catch them. Add rulemaking authority to adjust the threshold (downward only, or with congressional notice).

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A), ignores inference-time compute and composite systems.** A system built from many sub-threshold models orchestrated together, or one that gains capability through heavy test-time compute, is not "a model trained using" the threshold compute. The definition should reach systems and ensembles, not just individual models.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(A)–(B), "model" vs. "system."** Prong (A) covers an "artificial intelligence model"; prong (B) and Sec. 3 cover a "system." Neither term is defined, and the inconsistency invites arguments that a deployed product (system) built on a covered model is not itself covered.

## Sec. 2(1)(B) — Capability prong

6. ★ **Sec. 2(1)(B), "the Secretary" is undefined.** The bill mentions the Department of Commerce (Sec. 4) and the Secretary of State (Sec. 6), but never says which Secretary makes capability determinations. This is a fatal drafting gap: no one has the authority.

7. ★ **Sec. 2(1)(B), no process for determinations.** There is no procedure, standard of evidence, notice, deadline, or judicial-review provision for the determination. Determinations will default to the APA, be challenged as arbitrary, and leave companies arguing they had no notice that their system was covered.

8. ★ **Sec. 2(1)(B), void-for-vagueness exposure.** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" (which humans? which tasks? measured how?) is the sole trigger for a 10-year felony under Sec. 5(b). Criminal liability resting on an undefined standard applied after the fact is the single most likely basis for a constitutional challenge. At minimum, the capability prong should apply prospectively from the date of a published determination, with criminal penalties only for conduct after notice.

9. **Sec. 2(1)(B), timing problem.** A determination can only be made about a system that already exists, but Sec. 3 prohibits *training*. By the time the Secretary determines a system is covered, training is done and the only remaining prohibited act is deployment. Prong (B) should also authorize the Secretary to halt ongoing training runs and designate classes of systems or approaches, not just finished models.

## Sec. 2(2) — "U.S. person"

10. ★ **Sec. 2(2), entities not covered.** The definition reaches only "a corporation organized under the laws of a State." LLCs, partnerships, LPs, trusts, nonprofits, and public-benefit corporations (the form several frontier labs actually use) are arguably excluded. Use the standard formulation: any entity organized under the laws of the United States or any jurisdiction within it.

11. ★ **Sec. 2(2), foreign subsidiaries.** A Delaware parent's Cayman, Singapore, or UAE subsidiary is not a "U.S. person," so the parent can direct a covered training run abroad through a subsidiary it owns 100%. Add "any entity owned or controlled by a U.S. person," as export-control law does.

12. **Sec. 2(2), individuals.** Only "citizens" are covered, so lawful permanent residents and other foreign nationals living in the U.S. are reached only when they are physically "in the United States" under Sec. 3(a). Standard practice is to include permanent residents.

13. **Sec. 2(2), "State" undefined.** It is unclear whether corporations organized in D.C., Puerto Rico, or other territories are covered.

## Sec. 2(3) — "Covered facility"

14. **Sec. 2(3), "advanced AI chips" and "data center" undefined.** The registry turns entirely on these terms, and the bill never says what qualifies. Cross-reference an existing standard (e.g., the BIS ECCN classifications for advanced computing items) or give Commerce rulemaking authority.

15. ★ **Sec. 2(3), chip-count threshold is easy to evade.** Operators can split capacity across multiple buildings or sites each holding 49,999 chips and run distributed training across them. A performance-based threshold (aggregate compute capacity) and an aggregation rule for commonly controlled or networked facilities would close this.

16. **Sec. 2(3)/Sec. 4, "operator" undefined.** In cloud settings the facility owner, the cloud provider, and the tenant running the workload are often different entities. The bill doesn't say who registers or who must know what workloads are running.

## Sec. 3 — Prohibition

17. ★ **Sec. 3(a), narrow verbs.** "Train, develop, or deploy" omits: possessing or storing weights, transferring or selling weights, releasing weights openly, importing a foreign-trained covered system, providing compute to someone training one, financing or procuring training (contracting a foreign lab to do it), and attempting or conspiring to do any of these. Add these, plus an aiding-and-abetting provision and an anti-circumvention clause.

18. **Sec. 3(a), "develop" and "deploy" undefined.** It is unclear whether fine-tuning a covered model, building products on it, or running internal research with it counts as "developing"; and whether offering API access, open-weight release, or purely internal use counts as "deploying."

19. **Sec. 3(a), foreign companies serving U.S. users.** A foreign lab deploying a covered system to U.S. customers from servers abroad is arguably not "in the United States." Add language covering any person who makes a covered system available to persons in the United States.

20. **Sec. 3(a), federal government coverage unclear.** "Person" in federal statutes generally does not include the United States itself. The bill is silent on whether federal agencies, the military, and government contractors are covered. Whatever the intended policy, the current silence will be read as a de facto government exemption, and if an exemption is intended it should be explicit and bounded.

21. ★ **Sec. 3(b), grandfather clause swallows the rule.** Any run that "began" before enactment is exempt forever, with no cap on how long it continues, no limit on continued training, and no restriction on deploying the result. Since the bill is public now, every lab has an incentive to start a very large run before signature. At minimum: tie the cutoff to a date before introduction, require existing runs to be reported and completed within a fixed window, and keep the deployment prohibition (and the prong (B) prohibition) applicable to existing systems.

22. ★ **Sec. 3(c), safety-evaluation exemption swallows the rule.** You cannot evaluate a system without training it, so any lab can characterize any training run as "for the purpose of safety evaluation." "Safety evaluation" is undefined and there is no licensing, compute cap, oversight, or bar on deploying the result. Either strike it or convert it to a narrow licensed exception with Secretary approval, compute limits, government access, and a no-deployment condition.

## Sec. 4 — Registry

23. ★ **Sec. 4, no penalty for noncompliance.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3. Failing to register, failing to report, or filing a false quarterly report carries no sanction under this Act (18 U.S.C. § 1001 may apply to false statements, but that's not stated and doesn't cover silence).

24. **Sec. 4, no deadline.** Registration has no due date and reports have no specified filing schedule or format.

25. ★ **Sec. 4, no verification powers.** There is no audit, inspection, subpoena, or records-retention authority. Self-certified quarterly reports with no ability to check them cannot support enforcement of a compute threshold. The Department also has no stated authority to issue regulations.

26. **Sec. 4(b), "certified by the operator" is vague.** It doesn't say who signs (a senior officer?), under what standard (penalty of perjury?), or what the report must contain (compute per training run, which is what enforcement actually needs).

27. **Sec. 4, registry covers facilities, not training runs or models.** The thing the bill prohibits is a training run; the thing it registers is a building. Add pre-notification of training runs above a lower threshold (e.g., 10^25) so the government knows before, not after.

28. **Sec. 4, no confidentiality or whistleblower provisions.** Operators will resist reporting trade-secret workload data without FOIA/confidentiality protections, and employees who know about a hidden run have no protection for reporting it.

## Sec. 5 — Penalties

29. ★ **Sec. 5(a), penalty is immaterial to the targets.** $1M/day is $365M/year against companies spending tens of billions on compute. Add disgorgement of revenue, penalties scaled to a percentage of global revenue, and forfeiture or mandatory deletion of the model weights and seizure of chips used in the violation.

30. **Sec. 5, no enforcement mechanism.** The bill doesn't say who enforces (DOJ? Commerce? the FTC?), doesn't authorize injunctions or emergency orders to halt a training run in progress, provides no statute of limitations, and provides no debarment or private right of action.

31. ★ **Sec. 5(a) conflicts with Sec. 8.** Civil penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but under Sec. 8 nothing is unlawful until 180 days after enactment. The clause is either a nullity or an ex post facto trap; conform it to the effective date.

32. **Sec. 5(b), "willfully" plus a vague definition.** The willfulness standard, combined with the undefined prong (B), means prosecutors would have to prove the defendant knew its system met an undefined capability test. Expect near-zero criminal enforcement unless prong (B) is tied to published determinations.

33. **Sec. 5(b), corporate fines.** "Fined under title 18" caps organizational fines at $500,000 per offense (18 U.S.C. § 3571(c)) absent a stated alternative. Specify a higher figure or the alternative-fine provision.

## Sec. 6 — International agreement

34. **Sec. 6, hortatory and internally illogical.** "Shall seek to negotiate" imposes no obligation to conclude anything, and the 18-month deadline attaches to "seeking," which is meaningless. Add a reporting requirement to Congress and consider tying trade or export measures to foreign non-participation.

## Sec. 7 — Duration

35. ★ **Sec. 7(a) and 7(b) contradict each other.** Subsection (a) says the prohibition lasts until Congress affirmatively finds development is safe; subsection (b) repeals the whole Act after 10 years regardless. Courts will give effect to the sunset, which means the "prohibition until safe" is really a 10-year moratorium with a known expiration that labs can plan around.

36. **Sec. 7(a), no mechanism for the NAS report.** Nothing commissions, funds, or schedules the National Academies report that is the predicate for lifting the ban, and the finding is all-or-nothing ("covered AI systems can be developed safely") with no partial or conditional path.

37. **Sec. 7(b), sunset repeals the registry and penalties too.** Even if a sunset on the prohibition were intended, sunsetting the entire Act eliminates the registry and any pending enforcement actions.

## Sec. 8 — Effective date

38. ★ **Sec. 8, 180-day window interacts badly with Sec. 3(b).** Training that begins between enactment and the effective date is neither grandfathered (began after enactment) nor prohibited at the time it starts. Whether it becomes unlawful on day 181 is unclear, and labs will argue it doesn't. Fix by tying the grandfather date to a date certain before introduction and stating that the prohibition applies to ongoing training as of the effective date.

## Missing provisions (bill-wide)

39. **No definitions for core terms:** "artificial intelligence," "train," "training run," "person," "State," "Secretary," "advanced AI chips," "data center," "operator," "safety evaluation."

40. **No rulemaking authority** for any agency to define terms, set measurement methods, adjust thresholds, or administer the registry.

41. **No severability clause.** If prong (B) or the criminal provision falls to a vagueness challenge, the challenger will argue the whole Act falls with it.

42. **No preemption or savings clause** addressing state AI laws (several states have or are considering frontier-model statutes), creating uncertainty about whether the Act displaces stronger state rules.

43. **No appropriation or staffing authorization** for Commerce to run the registry or for enforcement.

44. **No anti-circumvention or structuring provision** making it unlawful to arrange training, corporate structure, or facility layout for the purpose of evading the Act's thresholds. Given how many of the loopholes above are threshold-gaming, a general anti-evasion clause is the most efficient backstop.
