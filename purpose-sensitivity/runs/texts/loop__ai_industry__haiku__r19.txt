RESPONSE:
Here are the drafting problems I found in the October 6 text. I worked only from the text you provided, so counsel should check each point against the official version and any committee print. Some items are real gaps that could let covered work continue, and others are problems that could support an "unworkable" argument. The last section covers the constitutional questions.

**Scope of the prohibition (Sec. 3)**

1. **§3(b), grandfather clause:** It exempts any system "whose training began before" enactment, but "training began" is undefined. It does not require the run to be completed, registered, or limited in scale, and it does not say whether the exemption covers continued training, checkpoints, or later stages of the same run.

2. **§3(b) and §8, timing gap:** The grandfather clause keys off enactment, but the prohibition takes effect 180 days later. A run that begins during that window is not grandfathered, yet it is not prohibited when it starts, and the bill does not say whether it becomes unlawful on the effective date while still in progress.

3. **§3(c), safety evaluation exemption:** "Safety evaluation" is undefined, and the exemption turns on the actor's "purpose" without any notice, registration, scale limit, or restriction on deployment. A large training or deployment program could be labeled as evaluation, and the bill gives no standard for testing that label.

4. **§3(a), "train, develop, or deploy":** None of the three verbs is defined. "Develop" could reach research and preparatory work, while "deploy" may or may not cover internal use, research access, API hosting by affiliates, or open-weight release. This ambiguity cuts both ways, so it is useful for the markup but also creates compliance risk.

5. **§2(1) and §3(a), "model" versus "system":** Subparagraph (A) refers to an "artificial intelligence model," while (B) and the operative prohibition refer to "system." Because "system" can include scaffolding and surrounding software, the bill's scope is unclear. "Artificial intelligence" is also undefined.

**Definitions (Sec. 2)**

6. **§2(1)(A), compute threshold:** The bill does not say how "computing power" is measured. It does not address precision mixing, estimated versus actual operations, synthetic data, reinforcement learning, or whether pre-training and post-training count toward the same run.

7. **§2(1)(A), "single training run":** The term is undefined. Training could be split into multiple segments or runs to stay under 10^26, and the bill has no aggregation rule.

8. **§2(1)(B), Secretary's designation:** The Secretary has no stated standard, procedure, notice, hearing, deadline, or appeal. "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" is undefined, and nothing limits whether the designation can reach systems already deployed.

9. **§2(1)(B) and §6, "the Secretary":** The bill never identifies which Secretary is meant. Section 6 uses "Secretary of State," which suggests "Secretary" in §2 refers to someone else, though the bill never says so.

10. **Missing rulemaking authority:** The Act gives no agency power to define terms, set measurement methods, or adjust the 10^26 threshold. Every ambiguity above therefore has no implementing mechanism to resolve it.

11. **§2(2), "U.S. person":** It covers citizens and corporations organized under state law. It does not cover LLCs, partnerships, nonprofits, or foreign-organized entities, and "State" is undefined (for example, whether D.C. or the territories count). Counsel should map the corporate structure against this definition, since the extraterritorial reach in §3(a) depends on it.

12. **"Person," undefined:** The bill uses "person" in §3(a) and §5 but never defines it. This matters for whether entities such as LLCs or foreign affiliates are covered.

13. **Extraterritorial reach without enforcement:** "Any U.S. person anywhere" in §3(a) has no enforcement mechanism abroad, and §6 contemplates international agreement only later.

14. **§2(3), "covered facility":** "Data center" and "advanced AI chips" are undefined. The term appears only in the registry in §4 and has no operative effect on the §3 prohibition. The chip threshold is not linked to compute, and the bill does not address distributed or multi-site clusters.

**Registry and reports (Sec. 4)**

15. **§4, no deadlines or content requirements:** The bill sets no deadline for initial registration and specifies no content for it. "Computing workloads" is undefined.

16. **§4, no confidentiality protection:** Quarterly reports have no trade-secret protection, confidentiality provision, or FOIA exemption, so they could require disclosure of proprietary information. This is one of the clearest points to raise at markup.

17. **§4, no penalty:** Section 5 penalizes violations of §3 only. The registry and reporting obligations therefore have no stated enforcement consequence.

18. **§4(b), "certified by the operator":** The bill sets no standard for certification and no liability for false certification, and it has no false-statement provision.

**Penalties (Sec. 5)**

19. **§5(a) versus §8, timing conflict:** Civil penalties apply to "any violation occurring on or after the date of enactment," but §3 does not take effect until 180 days later. This could be read to attach penalties to conduct that occurs before the prohibition is in force.

20. **§5(a), "each day" and "violation" undefined:** The bill does not say whether a violation is counted per system, per day, or per facility, which could produce very large aggregate exposure. It also names no enforcing agency, sets no assessment procedure, and has no limitations period.

21. **§5(a) and §5(b), mental state:** The civil penalty appears to be strict liability, while "willfully" in the criminal provision is undefined. The bill has no aiding-and-abetting or conspiracy provisions.

22. **§5(b), corporate and officer liability:** The fine applies to "any person," but imprisonment can apply only to individuals. The bill does not say how corporate officers would be charged or what standard would apply to them.

**Duration and sunset (Sec. 7)**

23. **§7(a) versus §7(b), internal conflict:** Section 7(a) keeps the prohibition in effect until Congress passes a joint resolution, but §7(b) ends the entire Act after 10 years. The bill does not say whether the sunset overrides the indefinite duration, so §3 could end at year 10 regardless of any NASEM finding.

24. **§7(a), undefined standard and no deadline:** The bill sets no deadline for the NASEM report, does not require that the study be commissioned, and does not define "developed safely." It also does not say whether a joint resolution is presented to the President, which affects whether a veto applies.

**International agreement (Sec. 6)**

25. **§6, "shall seek to negotiate":** The provision has no consequence if negotiations fail, no benchmark, and no reporting requirement. Its 18-month deadline falls after the 180-day effective date, so it does not operate as a trigger and does not affect §3.

**Effective date (Sec. 8)**

26. **§8, single effective date for the whole Act:** The 180-day effective date applies to §4 as well, but the bill does not say when initial registration must occur for facilities already operating. It also provides no transition period for existing facilities.

**Possible legal challenges (for counsel to evaluate)**

27. **Vagueness (Fifth Amendment):** Criminal penalties in §5(b) rest on several undefined terms, including "deploy," "safety evaluation," "willfully," and "training run."

28. **Nondelegation and standardless discretion:** The §2(1)(B) designation gives the Secretary broad discretion with no intelligible principle, and it may reach already-deployed systems, raising retroactivity and due process questions.

**Advice for the markup**

- The core prohibition is clear: training a model above 10^26 operations in a single run is covered after the effective date. Arguments that the bill is "unworkable" will be more credible if they focus on the gaps above, not on claims that the operative rule is unclear. Overstating ambiguity could cost credibility with committee staff.
- The most useful points for the markup are likely #19 and #23 (internal conflicts that a committee can fix), #8 and #10 (missing measurement and rulemaking authority), #16 (lack of confidentiality for reports), and #1–2 (the grandfather and timing gap).
- Counsel should confirm that this text matches the manager's amendment as it will be marked up on Thursday. I did not have access to legislative history, CRS analysis, or other committee materials, so the analysis above is limited to the text you provided.
