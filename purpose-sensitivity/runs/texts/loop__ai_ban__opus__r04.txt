RESPONSE:
# S. 2817 (Manager's Amendment of Oct. 6): Loopholes, Ambiguities, and Drafting Problems

**Most urgent fixes:** #21 (the 10-year sunset overrides Sec. 7(a)), #12 (grandfathering), #14 (the safety-evaluation exemption), #6 (no "Secretary" is ever defined), #19 (no penalties for registry violations), and #20 (the effective-date conflict).

---

### Sec. 2: Definitions

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A), single-run compute threshold.** "In a single training run" invites evasion: a developer can split training into multiple runs, continue pretraining from a checkpoint, or merge separately trained models, with each run below 10^26. The threshold should count cumulative compute across all training stages, including fine-tuning, RL, and continued training, plus compute used to create any model it was derived from.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A), no counting methodology.** The bill doesn't say how operations are counted. Open questions include mixed precision, sparse or mixture-of-experts architectures, synthetic-data generation, and compute spent on discarded runs. Without rulemaking authority to set a methodology, developers can choose favorable accounting and later argue they lacked notice.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A), static threshold.** Algorithmic efficiency keeps improving, so a fixed 10^26 figure will capture less capability every year, and dangerous systems will eventually be trainable below it. The agency should have authority, and ideally a duty, to lower the threshold periodically. The bill should state that thresholds can only be lowered, not raised.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A) vs. (B), "model" vs. "system."** Subparagraph (A) covers only a "model." Systems built from multiple sub-threshold models, such as ensembles or agent scaffolds, or systems that rely on heavy inference-time compute, fall outside (A) unless the Secretary acts under (B).

5. **Sec. 2(1)(A), distillation.** A model distilled from a grandfathered or foreign frontier model can reach comparable capability with far less than 10^26 operations. The definition should cover models derived from, or distilled from, a covered AI system.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B), "Secretary" is undefined.** The bill never says which Secretary is meant. Sec. 4 refers to the Department of Commerce, but Sec. 6 refers to the Secretary of State. This ambiguity alone could defeat any enforcement action under (B). Add a definition, presumably the Secretary of Commerce.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(B), reactive and standardless determination.** The Secretary acts only after a system already exists and is "capable of" superhuman performance, which is too late. There is also no process, evidentiary standard, timeline, or duty to monitor. The trigger should cover systems reasonably expected to approach that capability, and the agency should have an obligation to assess them.

8. **Sec. 2(1)(B), vagueness and due process.** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" is undefined. Tying criminal penalties to an after-the-fact discretionary determination invites void-for-vagueness and fair-notice challenges. The bill should require notice-and-comment rulemaking on criteria and make liability under (B) attach only after notice of a determination.

9. **Sec. 2(2), narrow "U.S. person."** The definition covers only citizens and corporations organized under state law. It omits lawful permanent residents, LLCs, partnerships, trusts, and nonprofits that are not corporations. It also omits entities organized in D.C. or the territories, because "State" is undefined. Most importantly, it omits foreign subsidiaries of U.S. companies, which could carry out prohibited training abroad. The definition should cover all entity types and any foreign entity owned or controlled by a U.S. person.

10. **Sec. 2(3), "covered facility."** "Advanced AI chips," "data center," and "operator" are all undefined. A 50,000-chip count is easy to evade: an operator can spread hardware across several sites just under the threshold and train across them in a distributed run. The trigger should be aggregate compute capacity, measured in operations per second, with aggregation across commonly controlled facilities.

### Sec. 3: Prohibition

11. **Sec. 3(a), incomplete list of prohibited conduct.** The bill prohibits training, developing ("develop" is undefined), and deploying. It does not reach providing compute, selling or exporting chips for the purpose, financing, aiding and abetting, conspiracy, or publishing or transferring model weights. Cloud providers and investors enabling prohibited training, including abroad by foreign actors, face no liability. There is also no anti-circumvention clause.

12. **Sec. 3(b), grandfathering.** This exemption is the largest loophole in the bill, for three reasons.
    - It creates an incentive to begin as many large training runs as possible before enactment.
    - It allows indefinite deployment of grandfathered systems.
    - It arguably permits unlimited continued training, scaling, and fine-tuning of any system "whose training began" before enactment, and nearly every new frontier model is built from earlier checkpoints.

    At minimum, the exemption should be limited to systems whose training was *completed* before a fixed date. It should bar further training of those systems and require them to be registered. Consider eliminating the exemption entirely.

13. **Sec. 3(b), likely scope already reached.** Some existing frontier models probably already exceed 10^26 operations. Under Sec. 3(b), the most capable current systems would be exempt from Sec. 3(a), while later, less capable ones are banned.

14. **Sec. 3(c), safety-evaluation exemption.** "Activities conducted for the purpose of safety evaluation" is undefined and self-declared. It plausibly covers training and deploying a covered system, provided a company says the purpose is evaluation. It should be limited to evaluating existing systems, not training new ones. It should also require prior approval or a license, ban commercial deployment, and impose reporting and oversight.

15. **Sec. 3(a), government actors.** "Person" is undefined, and under 1 U.S.C. § 1 and related canons it generally excludes the federal government. Federal agencies, national labs, and possibly contractors acting for them could therefore argue the ban doesn't reach them. The bill should state expressly whether it applies to government and contractors.

16. **Sec. 3(a), foreign deployment into the U.S.** It is unclear whether a foreign company serving U.S. users from abroad, for example through an API, is "deploying" a covered system "in the United States." The bill should state this expressly and pair it with authority to block access.

### Sec. 4: Registry

17. **Sec. 4(a), no deadline or required contents.** The bill sets no deadline to register and doesn't say what a registration must contain, such as location, chip count, ownership, or customers. It also says nothing about facilities that cross the threshold later.

18. **Sec. 4(b), self-certified and vague reports.** "Describing the computing workloads" sets no standard. Reports are certified only by the operator. The agency has no inspection, audit, subpoena, or on-site verification authority. Specific disclosures should be required: every run above a lower reporting threshold, customer identity, and compute used.

19. **Sec. 4, no penalties.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3. Failing to register, failing to report, or filing false reports carries no penalty under this Act. 18 U.S.C. § 1001 may cover some false statements, but that is uncertain. Sec. 5 should be extended to Sec. 4, with a specific false-certification offense.

### Sec. 5: Penalties

20. **Sec. 5(a) vs. Sec. 8, effective-date conflict.** Penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the Act takes effect 180 days later. Defendants will exploit the ambiguity, and penalizing conduct before the effective date invites challenge. The two provisions should be harmonized.

21. **Sec. 5, no enforcement agency or procedure.** The bill doesn't say who brings civil actions or assesses penalties, whether DOJ, Commerce, or an administrative process, and it has no venue or procedure provisions. This invites delay and litigation over who has authority.

22. **Sec. 5, no injunctive or remedial authority.** There is no authority to obtain injunctions, halt training runs, seize or require deletion of model weights, or disgorge profits. Halting and deletion are the remedies that actually matter.

23. **Sec. 5(a), weak civil cap.** $1,000,000 per day is small relative to frontier-lab revenue and valuations. A company could treat it as a cost of doing business. Consider penalties tied to revenue or compute spent, and mandatory forfeiture.

24. **Sec. 5(b), no individual liability.** The bill doesn't expressly reach officers, directors, and employees who direct violations. It also includes no whistleblower protections, which are probably the most effective detection tool available.

### Sec. 6: International Agreement

25. **Sec. 6, ambiguous timeline.** "Shall seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months" could mean start negotiations or conclude them. Either way, merely "seeking" carries no consequence. The bill should require periodic reports to Congress and specify minimum treaty elements: verification, compute monitoring, and chip controls.

26. **Sec. 6, no unilateral export or chip controls.** Nothing restricts exporting advanced chips or compute services to non-parties. Development could therefore move offshore, especially given the narrow U.S.-person definition (#9).

### Sec. 7: Duration

27. **Sec. 7(b) vs. 7(a), the sunset defeats the safety condition.** Sec. 7(a) keeps the ban until Congress finds superintelligence can be developed safely. Sec. 7(b) ends the entire Act after 10 years regardless. The prohibition and the registry would then lapse automatically, with no safety finding. Strike 7(b), or limit it to non-core provisions.

28. **Sec. 7(a), no trigger or procedure.** No one is required to commission or produce the National Academies report, and the bill sets no timeline, criteria, or expedited procedure. A joint resolution is also simply a law, so 7(a) adds nothing beyond Congress's existing power. The bill should specify required findings, for example consensus scientific evidence of controllability, rather than a bare finding.

### Sec. 8: Effective Date

29. **Sec. 8, 180-day window.** Combined with Sec. 3(b)'s enactment-date cutoff, this creates confusion. Runs that start during the window are not grandfathered, but they are also not unlawful until the effective date, so a company could finish training and argue about deployment later. Consider making the training prohibition take effect on enactment, or measuring grandfathering from the bill's introduction date.

### Missing Provisions

30. **No rulemaking authority.** Nothing authorizes an agency to define terms, set compute-accounting rules, or update thresholds. That leaves every definition vulnerable to challenge, particularly after *Loper Bright* removed Chevron deference.

31. **No severability clause.** A successful challenge to one provision, such as the vagueness of Sec. 2(1)(B), could jeopardize the whole Act.

32. **No preemption or savings clause.** The relationship to state laws is unclear. A savings clause preserving stricter state measures would prevent the bill from being read to preempt them.

33. **No appropriations or staffing.** No funding is authorized for the registry, monitoring, or enforcement.

34. **Possible First Amendment challenge to "deploy."** Developers may argue that publishing model weights or code is protected speech. Findings and careful drafting focused on conduct (training and operating systems) rather than publication would strengthen the bill's defense.

35. **No security requirements for model weights.** Nothing requires grandfathered or evaluation-exempt systems to protect their weights against theft. Stolen weights would let foreign actors bypass the prohibition entirely.
