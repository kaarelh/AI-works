# S. 2817: Loopholes, Ambiguities, and Drafting Problems

**Sec. 2(1)(A), definition of commercial deep seabed mining**

1. **Per-vessel threshold invites splitting.** The 10,000-metric-ton cap applies "by a single vessel," so an operator could run several vessels, each under the cap, with no limit on combined output. Nothing aggregates tonnage across vessels, affiliates, or contractors.

2. **"Per year" is undefined.** The bill doesn't say whether this means a calendar year, a rolling 12 months, or the operator's fiscal year. That makes it unclear when a violation begins, which matters because penalties accrue daily (see item 22).

3. **"Recovery" is undefined.** The bill doesn't say whether material must be lifted to the surface, or whether collecting, stockpiling on the seabed, or partial processing counts. An operator could argue that collector-vehicle activity is not "recovery" until material reaches the vessel.

4. **Only vessel-based recovery is covered.** Recovery from fixed platforms, seabed installations, or other non-vessel systems falls outside subparagraph (A).

5. **"Other mineral resources" is broad and conflicts with other laws.** Read literally, it could reach sand, gravel, or arguably hydrocarbons on the continental shelf. That could conflict with the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act and existing leasing programs. The bill also never says which waters "the seabed" covers (U.S. waters, the EEZ, the continental shelf, areas beyond national jurisdiction).

6. **No method for measuring depth or tonnage.** The bill doesn't specify how the 200-meter depth is measured (water depth at the collection site?) or whether tonnage is wet or dry weight. Wet nodule weight differs substantially from dry, so this choice changes who crosses the threshold.

**Sec. 2(1)(B), catch-all definition**

7. **"The Secretary" is never identified.** The bill doesn't say whether this means Commerce (which houses NOAA), Interior, or State. That alone could stall implementation and invite challenge.

8. **Open-ended delegation tied to criminal liability.** The Secretary can bring "any activity" into the prohibition with no procedure (no rulemaking, notice, or comment) and only a vague "comparable risks" standard. Because these determinations trigger criminal penalties under Sec. 5(b), they are exposed to vagueness, due-process, and nondelegation challenges.

**Sec. 2(2), definition of U.S. person**

9. **Many entity types are left out.** The definition covers only citizens and "corporation[s] organized under the laws of a State." That arguably excludes LLCs, partnerships, trusts, and entities organized under D.C., territorial, or federal law. Lawful permanent residents are also not covered.

10. **Foreign subsidiaries escape.** A U.S. company could operate through a foreign-incorporated subsidiary, or reincorporate abroad, and fall outside the "anywhere" reach of Sec. 3(a). The bill doesn't address U.S.-owned or U.S.-controlled foreign entities.

**Sec. 3(a), prohibition**

11. **"In the United States" is ambiguous.** The phrase may refer to where the person is or where the mining occurs. Its geographic scope (territorial sea, EEZ) is undefined. A foreign-flagged vessel operated by a foreign company in the U.S. EEZ may not clearly be covered.

12. **Only direct mining is prohibited.** Nothing bars U.S. persons from financing, insuring, sponsoring, processing, or importing minerals from seabed mining by others. "Engage in" is also undefined, so liability for investors, parent companies, and contractors is unclear.

**Sec. 3(b), existing-operations exemption**

13. **Exploration licenses become a path to commercial mining.** The exemption covers "mining conducted under an exploration license," which could let licensees mine at commercial scale. Exploration licenses normally don't authorize commercial recovery.

14. **The exemption has no limits.** It has no expiration, tonnage cap, or limit on renewals, extensions, or transfers. A license could be sold or extended indefinitely.

15. **The license's source is unspecified.** The bill doesn't say whether "exploration license" means only NOAA licenses under the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act of 1980, or also International Seabed Authority contracts and foreign-sponsored licenses.

16. **Incentive to rush licenses before enactment.** Anyone holding an exploration license on enactment day is permanently exempt, which encourages applicants and agencies to issue licenses quickly before the bill becomes law.

**Sec. 3(c), environmental-assessment exemption**

17. **The exemption is undefined and uncapped.** Exemption depends only on stated "purpose," with no definition, permit, tonnage limit, duration, or oversight. Commercial "pilot" or "test mining" operations could be labeled environmental assessment.

**Sec. 4, registry**

18. **The vessel threshold doesn't match the mining definition.** Only vessels over 5,000 gross tons must register, so smaller vessels (the ones best suited to splitting under item 1) go unregistered and unreported. "Operator" and "recovery activities" are also undefined.

19. **No penalty for failing to register or report.** Sec. 5 penalties apply only to violations of Sec. 3. The bill sets no registration deadline, no report contents (tonnage, location, depth), and no false-statement provision. The general federal false-statements statute, 18 U.S.C. 1001, might still apply, but the bill doesn't say so.

20. **Self-certified reports with no verification.** NOAA gets no inspection, observer, vessel-tracking, or audit authority. Only "registered" operators must report, so unregistered operators have no reporting duty at all.

**Sec. 5, penalties**

21. **No enforcing agency or procedure.** The bill doesn't say who assesses civil penalties, whether through administrative or judicial process, or with what enforcement powers (boarding, seizure, subpoena, forfeiture). It also has no citizen-suit provision.

22. **Daily penalties don't fit an annual threshold.** A violation is defined by annual tonnage, but penalties accrue per day. It is unclear whether liability starts on the day the 10,000-ton mark is crossed, covers every operating day that year, or something else. This invites litigation over the size of penalties.

23. **Penalty start date conflicts with the effective date.** Sec. 5(a) reaches violations "on or after the date of enactment," but Sec. 8 makes the Act effective 180 days later. Courts would have to resolve which governs, and applying penalties before the effective date would raise fair-notice problems.

24. **Criminal liability rests on undefined terms.** "Willfully" is undefined, and criminal exposure can arise from Secretary determinations under Sec. 2(1)(B). That strengthens the vagueness challenge in item 8.

**Sec. 6, international agreement**

25. **The negotiation mandate is unenforceable and ambiguous.** "Shall seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months" doesn't say whether the deadline applies to starting or finishing negotiations. There are no reporting requirements or consequences. The bill also doesn't address the U.S. relationship to UNCLOS or the ISA, since the U.S. is not a party.

**Sec. 7, duration**

26. **The two duration rules conflict.** Subsection (a) keeps the prohibition in force until Congress acts, while subsection (b) ends the entire Act after 10 years regardless. A future dispute over which controls is likely.

27. **The off-ramp is hollow.** No one is directed to commission the National Academies report, and "serious harm" is undefined. A joint resolution must be signed by the President like any other law, so subsection (a) gives Congress no power it doesn't already have.

28. **No savings clause at sunset.** When the Act expires, pending enforcement actions could be argued to lapse. The general savings statute (1 U.S.C. 109) may help, but courts have not uniformly applied it to sunsets.

**Sec. 8, effective date**

29. **180-day window.** The delay allows mining to ramp up or proceed in the interim. Because the sunset runs from enactment, the prohibition is in force for only about 9.5 years.

**Structural omissions**

30. **No relationship to existing law.** The bill doesn't amend or repeal the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act. That leaves NOAA's existing licensing and permitting authority, including pending commercial recovery permit applications, in apparent conflict with the prohibition.

31. **No rulemaking authority.** No agency is given general authority to issue implementing regulations, which leaves most of the ambiguities above to litigation.

32. **No severability clause.** If a court strikes a vulnerable provision such as Sec. 2(1)(B), the bill gives no guidance on whether the rest survives.

33. **Takings exposure.** Entities with pending permit applications or investments that the Sec. 3(b) exemption doesn't cover may bring regulatory takings claims.

**For the explainer:** Many of these items work in opposite directions. Some weaken the prohibition (items 1, 10, 13, 17). Others make it broader or harder to defend legally (items 5, 8, 23). For balance, you may want to frame them as "drafting issues the committee may address" and ask both the sponsor's office and opponents for comment. Some items, especially 23, 26, and 28, involve legal interpretation and are worth checking with a legislative-drafting or administrative-law expert before publication.
