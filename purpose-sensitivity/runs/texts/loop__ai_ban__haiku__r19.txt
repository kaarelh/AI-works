RESPONSE:
**Highest priority (fix these first):**
- **Item 1:** The sunset in §7(b) contradicts the "until Congress acts" duration in §7(a).
- **Item 4:** The §3(b) grandfather clause and the §8 effective date leave a window where a frontier run can start legally.
- **Item 5:** The §3(c) safety-evaluation exemption is open-ended.
- **Item 16:** Failing to register or filing false reports under §4 carries no penalty.
- **Item 9:** The U.S. person definition excludes LLCs and foreign subsidiaries, which AI companies commonly use.

**Duration and structure**

1. **§7(a), §7(b):** The prohibition lasts "until" Congress finds by joint resolution that covered systems can be developed safely, but §7(b) sunsets the entire Act after 10 years. Read together, the Act expires regardless of whether safety is established, so the two provisions need to be reconciled, and the sunset should be removed or made conditional.
2. **§7(a):** "Can be developed safely" is undefined and has no burden of proof, and nothing requires the National Academies to be commissioned or to report by a deadline. The joint-resolution procedure also needs to specify expedited floor consideration and whether it is subject to presidential veto.
3. **§8 and §5(a):** Section 5(a) applies penalties to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §8 delays the Act's effective date by 180 days. The penalty start date and the effective date need to match.

**Effective date and grandfathering**

4. **§3(b), §8:** The grandfather clause keys to the date of enactment, but the prohibition does not take effect until 180 days later. Training that starts in that gap is not prohibited, yet also is not grandfathered, and the text does not say whether it must stop when the prohibition takes effect. The grandfather clause should key to the effective date, with a freeze on new runs starting at enactment.
5. **§3(b):** "Training began" is undefined and could mean the first gradient step or the start of procurement, data collection, or staging. Continued pretraining, post-training, or a second phase of an existing model is not addressed and could be treated as an exempt continuation.
6. **§3(b):** Grandfathered systems can be deployed, and arguably scaled, indefinitely with no registration or reporting obligation. The grandfather clause should be limited to existing deployments with disclosure and a cap on further compute.

**Prohibition scope**

7. **§3(c):** "Activities conducted for the purpose of safety evaluation" has no limit on scope, compute, who conducts the work, or whether the model may be deployed or released. Developers could label capability training as evaluation. The exemption needs notice to Commerce, a compute cap, a ban on external release, and a requirement to report results.
8. **§3(a):** "Deploy" and "develop" are undefined. It is unclear whether releasing model weights, making a model available through an API, or hosting a model on rented compute counts.
9. **§2(2):** A "U.S. person" is a citizen or a corporation organized under a state's law. This excludes LLCs and partnerships, which are common in AI startups, and excludes foreign-incorporated subsidiaries of U.S. companies, which would then be free to train abroad.
10. **§3(a):** The prohibition on foreign persons applies only to those "in the United States." A foreign company training outside the U.S. is covered only if it is a U.S. person, so the bill relies on the treaty in §6 to reach the rest.
11. **§3(a):** The bill does not reach aiders and abettors, such as cloud providers, chip suppliers, investors, or contractors who knowingly support a covered training run. Without secondary liability, the most capable enforcement targets sit outside the Act.
12. **§3, generally:** The bill is silent on whether federal agencies, including defense and intelligence programs, are bound. This invites argument in both directions and should be resolved explicitly.

**Definitions**

13. **§2(1)(A):** "Quantity of computing power" should be "total training compute," with a specified counting method covering precision, repeated runs, and inference-time or post-training compute. As written, the threshold can be evaded by splitting a run into several smaller "single training runs."
14. **§2(1)(A):** The 10^26 threshold is fixed in statute, while algorithmic efficiency lets a given capability require less compute over time. The Secretary should have authority to update the threshold by rule, and the bill should include an anti-circumvention clause against structuring runs to fall below it.
15. **§2(1)(B):** "The Secretary" is undefined (§6 uses "Secretary of State," so the referent is unclear). The determination has no procedure, notice, standard of review, or timeline, and "substantially," "most," and "cognitive tasks" are undefined, which invites vagueness challenges. It also has no benchmark for what counts as human performance.
16. **§2(1)(B):** The provision reaches "any artificial intelligence system," not just models. It is unclear whether the prohibition applies to a model embedded in a larger product, and "system" and "model" are used inconsistently across §§2 and 3.
17. **§2(3):** "Data center" and "advanced AI chips" are undefined, and the threshold counts chips rather than compute. A cluster can be split across sites below 50,000 chips per facility.
18. **Title, §2:** The title refers to "superintelligence," but the term is never defined, and the operative definitions are compute-based and a discretionary capability test. The bill should either define superintelligence or align the operative definitions with its stated purpose.
19. **Missing:** The bill has no definition of "person" (the Dictionary Act may supply one, but this should be stated) or of "State" (does it include D.C. and the territories?).

**Registry and reporting**

20. **§4(a):** "Operator" is undefined. It could mean the data center owner, the chip owner, or the tenant training the model, and each reading produces different reporting obligations.
21. **§4(a):** There is no registration deadline and no effective-date rule for the registry, so it is unclear when obligations begin.
22. **§4(b):** "Computing workloads" is vague. The report should require the identity of each model trained, its compute totals, its training start and end dates, and whether it falls under §2(1).
23. **§4, §5:** Section 5 penalizes only violations of §3, so failing to register under §4 or filing a false certification has no penalty under this Act. Only general federal false-statement law might apply, and that is uncertain.
24. **§4:** The bill gives no inspection, audit, or subpoena authority, so Commerce cannot verify certified reports. It also has no confidentiality rule, so operators may claim the reports are trade secrets, and no provision for public or congressional access.
25. **§4, generally:** The registry is organized around facilities, not covered systems, so it does not show whether a prohibited run has occurred. It should require reporting of covered-system training events, not only facility workloads.

**Penalties and enforcement**

26. **§5(a):** The civil penalty is capped at $1 million per day. A frontier training run costs far more over months, so the penalty may be treated as a cost of doing business. Consider a penalty tied to compute cost or revenue, plus disgorgement and an express injunction power.
27. **§5(a):** The Act does not designate the enforcing agency or who may bring civil actions (DOJ, Commerce, or private parties). It also does not say whether "each day" means each day of a single training run or each day of deployment.
28. **§5(b):** "Willfully" is undefined, and the Act does not address corporate liability, individual officer liability, attempt, or conspiracy. Given the 10-year maximum, the bill should specify whether officers can be charged personally.
29. **§5:** There are no whistleblower protections, which means the people most likely to know about a violation have no statutory safe harbor.
30. **§5(a):** The penalty has no inflation adjustment, so its real value will erode over the Act's life.
31. **General:** The bill has no rulemaking authority for the Secretary or Commerce, no appropriation for enforcement, and no savings clause for violations that occur before a sunset.

**Constitutional and litigation exposure**

32. **Missing:** The bill has no severability clause. If a court strikes §2(1)(B) as vague, the entire Act could fall, so every substantive section should be separately severable.
33. **Missing:** The bill has no findings or purpose section. Findings on the risk and the rationale for the threshold would support deference on review and help a court interpret ambiguous terms.
34. **Missing:** The bill does not address judicial review of the Secretary's §2(1)(B) determinations. Without a specified standard, they will be reviewed under the APA, which invites challenges on arbitrary-and-capricious grounds.
35. **Missing:** The bill has no preemption clause, so it is unclear whether state AI laws are displaced or can coexist.
36. **Missing:** The bill does not address First Amendment challenges, such as arguments that model weights or code are speech. Narrowly tailored findings and a clear statement of the government interest would strengthen it against these challenges.

**Treaty and international**

37. **§6:** The Secretary of State "shall seek to negotiate," with no obligation to reach agreement, no report to Congress, and no consequences if talks fail. The bill should require periodic reporting to Congress and authorize trade or export-control measures against non-participating states.
38. **§6:** "Prohibitions equivalent to those in section 3" is undefined. A foreign agreement with weaker definitions could be argued to satisfy the requirement.
