Here's a pass through the text. I've numbered continuously but grouped by section so it's easy to map to the bill. A few items reference existing law from my own knowledge (not the bill text); I've flagged those so the desk can verify before publication.

## Definitions (Sec. 2)

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "by a single vessel."** The 10,000-ton threshold is measured per vessel, not per operator, project, or company. A company can run a fleet of vessels each recovering 9,999 tons and never trigger the ban, no matter the aggregate.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "per year."** The bill doesn't say calendar year, fiscal year, 12-month rolling period, or license year. A vessel could recover 10,000 tons in late December and another 10,000 in early January without exceeding any "year," and the ambiguity invites litigation over when a violation begins.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — no vessel identity rule.** Because the count attaches to "a single vessel," reflagging, renaming, or transferring the vessel mid-year arguably resets the count. Nothing addresses successor vessels or vessels operated in series.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — recovery must be "by a single vessel."** Fixed or floating platforms, seabed crawlers, and subsea collector systems that are not "vessels" arguably fall outside the definition entirely. A defendant could also argue recovery is performed "by" the collector vehicle, not the surface ship.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "mineral resources" is undefined.** Read broadly, it sweeps in offshore oil and gas, sand and gravel dredging, and methane hydrates, which are not the target of the bill. Overbreadth is both a political vulnerability at markup and a litigation argument that the bill is not narrowly drawn.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "recovery" is undefined and narrow.** Only the physical lifting of material is prohibited; transport, at-sea processing, financing, chartering, marketing, and importing recovered minerals are untouched. Also unclear whether tonnage is wet or dry, or whether sediment and tailings count.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "at depths greater than 200 meters."** Activity at or above 200 m is exempt, which is fine for nodules but leaves shallower cobalt-crust and sulfide deposits on some seamount summits and ridges uncovered. Depth measured where—at the collection point, the vessel, or the deposit—is also unstated.

8. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — "the Secretary" is never identified.** No Secretary (Commerce, Interior, State) is defined, so the prong is inoperative on its face until someone resolves it, and NOAA (Sec. 4) sits in Commerce while seabed minerals also implicate Interior.

9. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — open-ended delegation with no process.** The Secretary can expand the definition of a crime by a bare "determination" with no rulemaking, notice, criteria beyond "comparable," judicial review provision, or deadline. That is a strong vagueness/fair-notice challenge given Sec. 5(b)'s 10-year prison term, and a likely nondelegation or major-questions argument.

10. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — retroactivity of determinations.** Nothing says a determination applies only prospectively, so an operator could be penalized for conduct that was lawful until the Secretary acted; conversely, a hostile Secretary could simply never make a determination, leaving sub-threshold mining permanently exempt.

11. **Sec. 2(2) — "U.S. person" omits most business forms.** It covers only citizens and "corporations," excluding LLCs, limited partnerships, trusts, and joint ventures—the structures most likely to hold a mining asset. It also omits lawful permanent residents, non-citizen U.S. nationals, and U.S.-flagged vessels.

12. **Sec. 2(2) — "organized under the laws of a State."** "State" is undefined; D.C., Puerto Rico, Guam, and the other territories are arguably not States, so entities organized there escape the definition.

13. **Sec. 2(2) — no "owned or controlled" language.** A U.S. corporation can conduct mining through a foreign-incorporated subsidiary and fall outside the ban; compare OFAC-style definitions that reach entities "owned or controlled by" U.S. persons. This is probably the single largest hole for the major contractors.

14. **Sec. 2(3) — "covered vessel" is mismatched to the prohibition.** The registry trips at 5,000 gross tons and "equipped for" recovery, while the ban trips at 10,000 tons of material. Vessels under 5,000 GT, or vessels that carry mining gear on a separate support ship, never have to register or report, so the registry won't capture fleet-splitting under item 1.

## Prohibition (Sec. 3)

15. **Sec. 3(a) — no flag-state hook.** The ban applies to persons, not vessels, and never mentions U.S.-flagged vessels. Flag-state jurisdiction is the primary basis for regulating activity on the high seas, and a foreign-owned, U.S.-flagged vessel is not covered.

16. **Sec. 3(a) — "any person in the United States."** Mining happens at sea, so the question is whether a person who finances, directs, or contracts for mining from a U.S. office "engages in" it. The bill has no aiding-and-abetting, conspiracy, facilitation, or "cause to be done" language.

17. **Sec. 3(a) — no import or supply-chain restriction.** Nothing bars importing, purchasing, or processing seabed minerals mined by foreign operators, so U.S. demand continues to support the activity the bill targets.

18. **Sec. 3(b) — "exploration license" is undefined.** It doesn't say who issues it (ISA contracts, NOAA licenses under the 1980 Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act, foreign sponsoring states), and exploration licenses by design do not authorize commercial recovery. Treating "mining conducted under an exploration license" as exempt lets a holder characterize commercial-scale recovery as something done "under" the license.

19. **Sec. 3(b) — the exemption may swallow the rule.** *(From background knowledge—verify.)* Essentially every entity positioned to mine commercially already holds an ISA exploration contract or a NOAA DSHMRA license, so grandfathering all of them exempts the realistic universe of near-term operators.

20. **Sec. 3(b) — perpetual and transferable.** The exemption has no expiration, no cap, no tie to the license's own term or work plan, and no bar on renewals, amendments, area expansions, or assignment to a new owner. An exploration license issued in 2015 could shelter mining in 2035.

21. **Sec. 3(b) — keyed to enactment, not effective date.** Because the Act takes effect 180 days after enactment (Sec. 8), anyone expecting the bill to pass has an incentive to obtain licenses before signing; and the drafting leaves unclear whether a license issued in the 180-day gap is grandfathered.

22. **Sec. 3(c) — the environmental-assessment exemption has no limits.** No tonnage cap, no permit, no agency sign-off, no data-sharing requirement, no definition of "environmental assessment," and a purely subjective "purpose" test. Pilot and test-mining campaigns are routinely framed as assessment, so this exemption can be self-declared at commercial scale.

## Registry (Sec. 4)

23. **Sec. 4 — no penalty for noncompliance.** Section 5 penalizes only violations of Section 3, so failure to register or report carries no consequence. The registry is effectively voluntary.

24. **Sec. 4 — no jurisdictional nexus.** It applies to "the operator of each covered vessel" worldwide with no link to U.S. persons, U.S. flag, or U.S. ports; as written it is either unenforceably broad or will be read down to nothing. "Operator" is also undefined.

25. **Sec. 4(b) — self-certified, unverifiable reports.** No inspection, boarding, audit, data-retention, false-statement, or public-disclosure provisions. NOAA receives whatever operators choose to say, quarterly, with no tools to check it.

## Penalties (Sec. 5)

26. **Sec. 5(a) vs. Sec. 8 — internal conflict on timing.** Civil penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the Act does not take effect until 180 days later, so there can be no violation in the gap. A defendant penalized for gap conduct has a clean retroactivity argument, and the clause invites a challenge to the whole penalty section.

27. **Sec. 5(a) — daily penalty for an annual threshold.** The violation is defined by exceeding 10,000 tons "per year," but the penalty runs "for each day on which the violation occurs." Unclear whether the violation "occurs" only on the day the threshold is crossed, every day thereafter, or every day of the year; expect litigation on penalty calculation.

28. **Sec. 5(a) — no floor, no enforcer, no other remedies.** "Not more than $1,000,000" permits nominal penalties; no agency or the Attorney General is designated to bring actions; and there is no injunctive relief, vessel seizure, forfeiture of recovered minerals, disgorgement, or citizen suit. A well-capitalized operator can treat the maximum as a cost of doing business measured against the value of the ore.

29. **Sec. 5(b) — "willfully" plus vague definitions.** Willfulness is hard to prove when the underlying definition turns on an undefined "Secretary" and "comparable" risk; the criminal prong will rarely be usable. No provision for officer or corporate liability.

30. **Sec. 5 — no boarding, inspection, or Coast Guard authority.** Nothing authorizes stopping a vessel at sea, inspecting cargo, or compelling production of records, so proving tonnage is left to the operator's own Sec. 4 reports.

## International, duration, effective date (Secs. 6–8)

31. **Sec. 6 — hortatory.** "Shall seek to negotiate" imposes no obligation to conclude anything, has no reporting requirement, and the 18-month clause is ambiguous about whether it bounds the start or the finish of negotiations.

32. **Sec. 7(a) vs. 7(b) — direct conflict.** 7(a) says the ban lasts until Congress affirmatively finds mining safe by joint resolution; 7(b) says the entire Act ceases after 10 years regardless. The sunset controls as the more specific provision, which makes 7(a) illusory and is a headline point for the explainer: the "prohibition" is a 10-year moratorium.

33. **Sec. 7(a) — the National Academies report is never commissioned.** No one is directed to request it, no funding or deadline is provided, and "serious harm" is undefined. (Also, one Congress cannot bind a later one, so the joint-resolution condition is legally unenforceable even on its own terms.)

34. **Sec. 7(b) — sunset with no savings clause.** When the Act "ceases to have effect," pending enforcement actions, unpaid penalties, and registry obligations arguably lapse with it, encouraging operators to run out the clock on late-term violations.

35. **Sec. 8 — the 180-day window.** The delayed effective date gives operators six months of unrestricted activity plus time to restructure as foreign subsidiaries or LLCs (items 11–13) and to obtain licenses (item 21).

## Cross-cutting

36. **No interaction with existing law.** *(From background knowledge—verify.)* The bill neither amends nor repeals the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act, under which NOAA can still issue commercial recovery permits, and it doesn't address the April 2025 executive order directing NOAA to expedite those permits. A NOAA permit holder would argue the later-enacted bill was not intended to override an unmentioned permitting regime, especially given Sec. 3(b).

37. **No severability clause.** If a court strikes Sec. 2(1)(B) for vagueness or Sec. 5(a)'s retroactive language, there is no textual guidance on whether the rest survives; courts usually presume severability, but the absence is an easy fix the sponsors skipped.

38. **Undefined terms generally.** "Secretary," "seabed," "recovery," "mineral resources," "operator," "exploration license," "year," "State," and "serious harm" are all undefined. For a bill carrying 10-year criminal exposure, that is the kind of drafting courts punish.

---

If you want a short list for the top of the explainer, the five that most clearly "let companies keep going" are: the per-vessel threshold (1), the corporate/subsidiary gap (11–13), the exploration-license exemption (18–20), the self-declared environmental-assessment exemption (22), and the 10-year sunset that overrides the "until Congress finds it safe" language (32). The ones most likely to be raised in a court challenge are 9, 26, and 27.
