# S. 2817 (as amended Oct. 6): Loopholes, Ambiguities, and Drafting Issues

These are analytical observations about the text as written, not judgments on the bill's merits. Before publication, it's worth asking committee staff and the sponsor's office how they read each one, since some may be answered in report language or a further amendment at markup.

## Sec. 2: Definitions

1. **Sec. 2 (generally): "Artificial intelligence" is never defined.** "Artificial intelligence model" and "artificial intelligence system" are central to the bill but undefined. That leaves the scope open to argument and gives defendants an opening, especially in criminal cases.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(B), and throughout: "Secretary" is undefined.** The bill never says which Secretary makes the capability determination. Sec. 4 points to Commerce, but Sec. 6 refers to the Secretary of State, so the text is internally ambiguous about who holds the bill's most consequential power.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A): "Single training run" invites splitting.** Developers could stay under 10^26 operations per run by breaking training into nominally separate runs, such as continued pretraining, sequential fine-tuning, or merging separately trained models. "Training run" is undefined, and there is no aggregation rule.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A): Unclear how derived models are counted.** The text doesn't say whether compute for fine-tuning or distilling an existing model includes the compute used to train the base model. It also doesn't say whether the derived model is a new model "whose training began" after enactment, which matters for the Sec. 3(b) grandfather clause.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(A): The compute threshold is fixed.** Algorithmic efficiency gains could let highly capable systems be trained below 10^26 operations, and the bill has no mechanism to adjust the threshold. Conversely, the threshold covers any model above the line regardless of capability, which critics may call over-inclusive.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(A) vs. (B): "Model" vs. "system."** Prong (A) covers a "model," while (B) covers a "system." A composite system built from several sub-threshold models, such as ensembles or agent scaffolding, escapes (A) and is reachable only through a discretionary (B) determination.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(B): The capability standard is vague.** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no defined benchmarks, baseline human, or task set. That makes it hard to apply consistently and vulnerable to a void-for-vagueness challenge, particularly because it can trigger criminal liability under Sec. 5(b).

8. **Sec. 2(1)(B): No process for determinations.** The bill provides no notice, criteria, rulemaking, opportunity to contest, or judicial review provision for a Secretary's determination. This invites Administrative Procedure Act and due process challenges, and possibly nondelegation arguments.

9. **Sec. 2(1)(B): Determinations come after the fact.** A system can only be "determined" capable once it exists. Developers therefore can't know in advance whether training is lawful, and it's unclear whether liability attaches to conduct before the determination.

10. **Sec. 2(2): "U.S. person" is narrow.** It covers only citizens and corporations, omitting lawful permanent residents, LLCs, partnerships, and other entity types. A U.S. company could train abroad through a foreign-incorporated subsidiary or an LLC structure outside the definition.

11. **Sec. 2(2): "State" is undefined.** It's unclear whether corporations organized under the laws of D.C., U.S. territories, or federal charters count. "United States" is also undefined for Sec. 3(a), so territorial reach is uncertain.

12. **Sec. 2(3): "Covered facility" relies on undefined terms.** "Advanced AI chips" and "data center" are undefined, so it's unclear which hardware counts.

13. **Sec. 2(3): The registry threshold is easy to avoid.** An operator could stay under 50,000 chips per facility by spreading hardware across multiple sites, including for distributed training runs. The chip-count threshold also isn't tied to the 10^26 compute threshold. As chips improve, a 10^26 run may be feasible in facilities that never have to register.

## Sec. 3: Prohibition

14. **Sec. 3(a): "Develop" and "deploy" are undefined.** "Develop" could reach preliminary research. It's also unclear whether "deploy" covers offering API access, releasing open weights, or a U.S. business merely using a model.

15. **Sec. 3(a): Foreign models are a gap.** A foreign developer with no U.S. presence could train a covered system abroad and serve U.S. users remotely. It's unclear whether that is "deployment" by a "person in the United States," and enforcement against the foreign developer would be difficult.

16. **Sec. 3(a): Cross-border training is unaddressed.** The bill doesn't address a run split between U.S. and foreign facilities, or a non-U.S. person renting compute located in the U.S.

17. **Sec. 3(b): The grandfather clause is broad.** Any system whose training "began" before enactment is exempt indefinitely, including continued training, further development, and deployment. A developer could start a nominal training run just before enactment and keep scaling it afterward.

18. **Sec. 3(b): Grandfathered models could seed successors.** It's unclear whether fine-tuned versions, successors, or distilled derivatives of a grandfathered system inherit the exemption. Without an answer, existing frontier models could serve as a base for continued development.

19. **Sec. 3(c): The safety-evaluation exemption has no limits.** "Safety evaluation" is undefined, and the exemption applies to all of Sec. 3(a), including training and deployment. It has no oversight, approval, scope, or duration limits, and "for the purpose of" turns on the developer's own stated intent, so it could cover substantial development activity.

## Sec. 4: Registry

20. **Sec. 4: No penalty for registry violations.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3. Failing to register or filing an inaccurate report carries no penalty under the Act itself, though general false-statement law (18 U.S.C. § 1001) might apply to false certifications.

21. **Sec. 4(a): No registration deadline.** The bill sets no deadline or process for registration, and "operator" is undefined. It's unclear whether the operator is the facility owner, the colocation provider, or the cloud customer running the workload.

22. **Sec. 4(b): Reports have no defined content.** "Describing the computing workloads" has no required level of detail. Without that, a report need not reveal whether a covered training run occurred.

23. **Sec. 4: Commerce gets no tools.** The bill gives Commerce no inspection, audit, subpoena, or rulemaking authority. Enforcement would rest largely on operators' self-certification.

24. **Sec. 4: No confidentiality protections.** Quarterly workload reports could contain trade secrets, and the bill doesn't address FOIA or confidentiality. That could prompt industry objections or litigation.

25. **Sec. 4: The registry is domestic only.** It covers facilities, not entities, so it doesn't capture U.S. persons training abroad, even though Sec. 3(a) reaches them.

## Sec. 5: Penalties

26. **Sec. 5(a) vs. Sec. 8: Liability starts before the Act takes effect.** Civil penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the Act takes effect 180 days later. This internal conflict leaves unclear whether conduct in that window is penalized, and raises fair-notice and retroactivity concerns.

27. **Sec. 3(b) and Sec. 8: The 180-day gap.** Training begun during the 180 days after enactment is not grandfathered, but the prohibition isn't in effect yet. The bill doesn't say how that training is treated, which could prompt a rush to start runs or produce conflicting interpretations.

28. **Sec. 5: No enforcement authority.** The bill doesn't say which agency assesses civil penalties, by what procedure, or whether DOJ, Commerce, or both enforce. There's also no injunctive relief to stop an ongoing violation.

29. **Sec. 5(a): The penalty may be modest relative to frontier budgets.** The cap of $1,000,000 per day works out to about $365 million per year. Some observers may argue that is modest next to frontier training budgets, which could make penalties a cost of doing business rather than a deterrent. "Each day on which the violation occurs" is also ambiguous for a discrete act like completing training.

30. **Sec. 5(b): "Willfully" plus vague definitions.** The willfulness standard combined with the vague Sec. 2(1)(B) standard and the undefined "develop"/"deploy" strengthens constitutional vagueness challenges to the criminal provision. The bill also doesn't address individual liability of officers or employees acting for a company.

## Sec. 6: International Agreement

31. **Sec. 6: The duty is only to try.** "Shall seek to negotiate" imposes no obligation to conclude an agreement and no consequence for failing. It's also ambiguous whether the 18-month deadline applies to starting or finishing negotiations.

32. **Sec. 6: No guidance on equivalence or approval.** "Equivalent" prohibitions are undefined. The bill doesn't address whether a resulting agreement needs Senate advice and consent, or how domestic rules interact with an agreement once one exists.

## Sec. 7: Duration

33. **Sec. 7(a) vs. 7(b): Contradictory duration.** Subsection (a) says the prohibition lasts "until" Congress acts, implying it is indefinite. Subsection (b) ends the entire Act after 10 years regardless. Because of the 180-day effective date, the prohibition actually operates for about 9.5 years.

34. **Sec. 7(a): The off-ramp adds little legally.** A joint resolution must be presented to the President, so it is effectively a new law. Congress could always do that anyway, so the "off-ramp" adds little legally.

35. **Sec. 7(a): The NASEM report is not required.** The bill doesn't direct or fund the National Academies to produce the report, and it sets no timeline or criteria. The mechanism may never be triggered.

36. **Sec. 7(b): Pending cases at sunset.** It's unclear whether pending prosecutions or penalty actions survive when the Act ceases to have effect. The general savings statute (1 U.S.C. § 109) addresses repeals, but its application to expiring statutes is less settled.

## Structural Omissions

37. **No severability clause.** If a court strikes down a vulnerable provision, such as the Sec. 2(1)(B) determination power, there is no clause signaling Congress's intent to preserve the rest of the bill.

38. **No preemption provision.** The bill is silent on whether it preempts state AI laws or leaves room for stricter or weaker state rules.

39. **No rulemaking authority or appropriations.** No agency is given authority to issue regulations defining key terms, and no funding is authorized for the registry, determinations, or enforcement.

40. **No whistleblower protections.** Given reliance on self-reported, operator-certified data, the absence of protections for employees reporting violations could weaken detection.

**Highest-impact items for the explainer:** #2 (undefined "Secretary"), #19 (safety-evaluation exemption), #17 (grandfather clause), #20 (registry has no penalties), #26 and #27 (enactment vs. effective-date conflicts), #33 (contradictory duration), and #10 (narrow "U.S. person" definition). These are visible on the face of the text and are the most likely subjects of amendments at Thursday's markup.
