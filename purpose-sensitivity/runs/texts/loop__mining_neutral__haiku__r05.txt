# Drafting Issues and Open Questions in S. 2817 (Manager's Amendment, October 6)

**Definitions and scope**

1. **§2(1)(A), "per year" threshold:** The 10,000-metric-ton threshold doesn't specify wet or dry weight, whether it counts material recovered or material processed, or how a "year" is measured. Operators could shape their reporting to fall under the line.

2. **§2(1)(A), "by a single vessel":** The threshold applies per vessel, so an operator could spread recovery across several vessels that each stay under 10,000 tons. The bill doesn't address fleets, sequential use of vessels, or affiliated companies.

3. **§2(1)(A), "other mineral resources" and "recovery":** Neither term is defined, and the bill doesn't list other covered minerals (such as cobalt-rich crusts or seafloor massive sulfides). It's unclear whether seabed disturbance without collection counts as "recovery."

4. **§2(1)(B), "the Secretary":** No agency is identified, so it's unclear whether Commerce, Interior, or another department makes the determination. The provision also sets no procedure, notice requirement, criteria, or judicial review standard.

5. **§2(1)(B), "comparable to":** This catch-all has no measurable standard. It could be read to expand or narrow the bill's scope, and it invites litigation over vagueness and delegation.

6. **§2(2), "U.S. person":** Limiting this term to citizens and State-organized corporations excludes LLCs, partnerships, foreign-organized subsidiaries of U.S. companies, and permanent residents. The bill also doesn't address DC or U.S. territories, and operators could restructure to fall outside the definition.

7. **§2(3), "covered vessel" and "equipped for":** The 5,000-gross-ton threshold has no clear relationship to the 10,000-ton production threshold in §2(1)(A), and "equipped for" is undefined. Vessels below the tonnage line would fall outside the registry and reporting requirements even if they conduct recovery.

**Prohibition (§3)**

8. **§3(a), "engage in" and "person":** Neither term is defined. It's unclear whether financing, equipment procurement, surveys, permit applications, or vessel contracting count as engaging in mining.

9. **§3(a), territorial reach:** "In the United States" doesn't clarify whether it covers the territorial sea, the EEZ, the continental shelf, or the high seas. "Any U.S. person anywhere" raises questions about extraterritorial application under international law and about enforcement against foreign-flagged vessels.

10. **§3(b), exploration-license exemption:** The bill doesn't identify the license or the agency that issues it, and it doesn't reference the existing federal seabed licensing framework. Because the exemption has no time or volume limit, and an exploration license doesn't clearly authorize commercial recovery, its scope is unclear.

11. **§3(b) with §8, licensing window:** The exemption applies to licenses issued before enactment, but the prohibition doesn't take effect until 180 days later. That gap could allow new licenses to be issued and then grandfathered. The bill also doesn't restrict license renewal or transfer.

12. **§3(c), environmental assessment exemption:** "For the purpose of environmental assessment" has no definition, volume cap, duration limit, permit requirement, or reporting obligation. Commercial-scale collection could be labeled as assessment, which makes this a central question for the explainer.

13. **§3(c) with §4, monitoring gap:** Assessment activities by vessels under 5,000 gross tons fall outside the registry and reporting requirements, so the exemption can't be monitored for those vessels.

**Registry and reporting (§4)**

14. **§4(a), "operator" and deadlines:** "Operator" isn't defined (owner, charterer, or manager?). The registration duty has no deadline and no effective date beyond the general one in §8.

15. **§4, no penalty for noncompliance:** Section 5 penalizes only violations of §3. Failing to register or file a report carries no stated penalty.

16. **§4(a), what registration does:** The bill doesn't say whether registration has any legal effect on whether mining is authorized. Registration could be read as implied permission, which is worth clarifying with the bill's sponsors or staff.

17. **§4(b), self-certified reports:** Reports are certified by the operator and have no content requirements, audit authority, or inspection powers. "Recovery activities" is undefined, so reports might not capture meaningful data.

**Penalties and enforcement (§5)**

18. **§5(a) with §8, effective-date conflict:** The civil penalty applies to violations "occurring on or after the date of enactment," but §3 doesn't take effect until 180 days later. A court could read this to attach penalties during the 180-day window or to attach none, so this should be flagged as a drafting conflict.

19. **§5(a), "per day":** The bill doesn't say whether each day of an ongoing operation, each recovery, or each vessel is a separate violation. It also has no inflation adjustment, no limitations period, and no assessment procedure.

20. **§5, no enforcing agency or procedure:** The bill doesn't name an agency to impose civil penalties or describe how they're assessed or collected.

21. **§5, no operational remedies:** The bill has no injunctive relief, cease-and-desist authority, vessel or equipment seizure, forfeiture, suspension of operations, or citizen-suit provision. Mining could continue during enforcement or litigation.

22. **§5(b), criminal liability:** "Willfully" is undefined, and the bill doesn't specify whether individual officers are liable. It also lacks language on conspiracy, attempt, or aiding and financing violations, and doesn't address how criminal provisions apply to foreign-flagged operators.

**International agreement (§6)**

23. **§6, "shall seek to negotiate":** This requires an effort, not a result. The bill sets no reporting requirement to Congress, no consequence if negotiations fail, and no account of how a future agreement would interact with §3. It also doesn't mention the International Seabed Authority or the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea, which likely govern the international framework. Reporters may want to confirm this with experts.

**Duration and sunset (§7)**

24. **§7(a), "serious harm" and the NAS report:** "Serious harm" is undefined, so the standard for lifting the prohibition is unclear. The National Academies report is referenced but not required, and the bill doesn't set a deadline or content requirements for it.

25. **§7(a), joint resolution procedure:** The bill doesn't say whether the joint resolution follows standard bicameral passage and presentment to the President, or expedited procedures. This affects how difficult it is to lift the prohibition.

26. **§7(a) vs. §7(b), conflicting duration:** The prohibition lasts until Congress passes a joint resolution, but the entire Act sunsets 10 years after enactment, about 9.5 years after the effective date. The sunset would override any finding, which may not match what the bill's sponsors intend.

27. **§7(b), no savings clause:** When the Act sunsets, there's no provision preserving pending penalties, ongoing investigations, or accrued liability.

**General drafting**

28. **No severability clause, rulemaking authority, or enforcement funding:** If one provision is struck down, the bill has no fallback. It also doesn't authorize regulations or appropriations.

29. **No relationship to existing law:** The bill doesn't amend, reference, or preempt the existing federal seabed mining statute, the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (30 U.S.C. §1401 et seq.). Overlap and possible conflict with that law are unaddressed. I can't verify the current statutory text from this environment, so please check the citation before publication.

**Priorities for the explainer:** The most defensible points are the internal conflicts: §5(a) against §8 (item 18), §7(a) against §7(b) (item 26), and the licensing window between enactment and the effective date (item 11). These are drafting problems that don't depend on how anyone reads the bill's policy goals. The exemptions in §3(b) and §3(c) are the most consequential questions for coverage.
