RESPONSE:
Below are the issues I found in the October 6 text. They are my reading of the bill, not legal analysis, so counsel should verify each one, especially the points about how courts would likely read ambiguous terms.

**Section 2 (Definitions)**

1. **§2(1)(B), "Secretary":** The term is never defined. Section 4 uses the Department of Commerce and Section 6 uses the Secretary of State, so it is unclear which official makes the capability determination.
2. **§2(1)(B), capability prong:** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no benchmark, procedure, notice requirement, or appeal. This supports vagueness and due-process arguments, and it means no developer can know in advance whether a system is covered.
3. **§2(1)(B), scope of determinations:** The bill doesn't say whether a determination applies to one system or a class, whether it can reach systems built before enactment, or whether it can be made before training begins.
4. **§2(1)(A), "single training run":** The bill doesn't address continued pretraining, fine-tuning, multi-stage runs, or whether compute from successive runs is aggregated. Splitting a run could arguably keep a system below 10^26 operations.
5. **§2(1), "model" vs. "system":** Clause (A) refers to a "model," clause (B) to a "system," and neither is defined. It is unclear whether a product built on a covered model is itself a covered system.
6. **§2(1)(A), static threshold:** The 10^26 figure has no update mechanism, so algorithmic efficiency gains could make it over- or under-inclusive over the 10-year life of the Act.
7. **§2(2), "U.S. person":** It covers only citizens and corporations organized under a State's law. LLCs, partnerships, and entities organized under federal or D.C. law fall outside it, and "a State" is undefined.
8. **§2(2), affiliates:** The bill has no control or attribution rule, so a foreign-organized subsidiary of a U.S. corporation is not a U.S. person, and its parent's responsibility is unaddressed.
9. **§2(3), "data center" and "advanced AI chips":** Neither term is defined. The bill also doesn't say how to count leased, cloud, or multi-tenant capacity.
10. **§2(3), no aggregation rule:** Compute can be spread across facilities each under 50,000 chips, and the bill doesn't aggregate across sites or affiliates.
11. **§2, "person":** The Act doesn't define "person," so the general definition in 1 U.S.C. §1 probably applies. Counsel should confirm.

**Section 3 (Prohibition)**

12. **§3(a), "develop" and "deploy":** "Develop" overlaps with "train," and neither "develop" nor "deploy" is defined. It is unclear whether API access, internal use, or releasing model weights counts as deployment.
13. **§3(a), no liability for compute providers:** The prohibition covers those who train, develop, or deploy, but the bill doesn't address those who supply chips, cloud capacity, or other support, and there is no aiding-and-abetting provision.
14. **§3(b), grandfathering:** The exemption turns on when "training began," which is undefined. It could be read to cover training that continues after enactment, successor runs, or deployment of a grandfathered system, and it could be read to reach preparatory work.
15. **§3(b) and §8, timing mismatch:** The grandfather cutoff is enactment, but the prohibition takes effect 180 days later. Training begun during that window is not grandfathered, yet it is not clearly prohibited either.
16. **§3(c), "safety evaluation":** The term is undefined and purpose-based, with no limits on scale, duration, documentation, or third-party access. Mixed-purpose activity is unresolved, and whether deploying a system to outside evaluators counts is unclear.

**Section 4 (Registry)**

17. **§4(a), "operator":** Who the operator is (owner, tenant, or cloud provider) is undefined. The bill also sets no registration deadline.
18. **§4(b), "computing workloads":** The term is undefined and the bill sets no content standard for the quarterly report.
19. **§4(b), confidentiality:** The bill has no trade-secret protection or FOIA exemption, so workload information filed with Commerce is exposed.
20. **§4, overbreadth:** The reporting duty applies to every covered facility whether or not it runs a covered system, and it isn't tied to any prohibited activity.
21. **§4 vs. §3, no link:** The registry does not require a registered facility to show it is not running covered systems, so it doesn't operate as a compliance mechanism.
22. **§4, no penalty:** Section 5 penalizes only violations of section 3, so failing to register or to file reports carries no penalty under the Act.

**Section 5 (Penalties)**

23. **§5(a), "each day on which the violation occurs":** It is unclear whether one ongoing training run is one violation per day or whether each system or deployment counts separately.
24. **§5(a), no enforcement mechanism:** The Act doesn't say who brings civil actions (DOJ, Commerce, or someone else), whether there is an administrative process, or what standard of proof applies.
25. **§5(a) vs. §8, timing conflict:** Civil penalties apply to "violations occurring on or after the date of enactment," but the prohibition takes effect 180 days later. Either penalties can attach during the 180-day window, or the drafting is inconsistent. Markup opponents will likely raise this.
26. **§5(a), no scienter:** The civil penalty has no knowledge or intent element, so it is effectively strict liability.
27. **§5(b), "willfully":** The term is undefined, and the bill doesn't say how it interacts with the civil penalty or whether civil and criminal penalties can both apply to the same conduct.

**Section 6 (International agreement)**

28. **§6, "shall seek to negotiate":** The duty is to try, with no required result and no consequence if no agreement is reached. "Equivalent prohibitions" is undefined, and the Act doesn't say whether domestic enforcement depends on any agreement.

**Section 7 (Duration)**

29. **§7(a) vs. §7(b), conflict:** Under §7(a) the prohibition lasts until a joint resolution is enacted, but §7(b) ends the entire Act after 10 years. It is unclear whether the prohibition ends at year 10 regardless of any joint resolution.
30. **§7(a), no deadline or standard:** The bill sets no deadline for the NASEM report or for Congress to act, and "can be developed safely" is undefined. It is also unclear whether a joint resolution requires presentment to the President.
31. **§7(a), NASEM role:** The bill doesn't say whether the NASEM report is binding, whether it is funded, or what happens if NASEM never reports.
32. **§7(b), no savings clause:** The Act has no provision preserving liability for violations that occur before the sunset. Whether the general savings statute (1 U.S.C. §109) applies to a sunset is a question for counsel.

**Section 8 and general provisions**

33. **No rulemaking or guidance authority:** The Act doesn't authorize Commerce or any agency to issue regulations, interpretations, or advisory opinions. Without this, the defined terms and §2(1)(B) determinations have no implementing process.
34. **No severability clause:** If a court strikes the vague §2(1)(B) provision, the rest of the Act would rely on default severability doctrine, which is uncertain.

**Highest-priority items for the markup**

- Items 2, 12, and 16: vague capability test, undefined "deploy," and undefined safety-evaluation exemption. These are the core unworkability arguments.
- Items 14–15: grandfathering scope and the timing conflict with §8.
- Item 25: the §5(a)/§8 inconsistency.
- Item 29: the §7(a)/§7(b) conflict.
- Item 33: no rulemaking or advisory process.

Policy arguments on drafting defects tend to land best when framed as specific fixes Congress can make, such as defining terms, adding a determination procedure, and reconciling the effective-date and sunset provisions. I'd also suggest counsel weigh the criminal exposure in §5(b) before the team relies on any of the narrower readings above, since willfulness is the element most likely to be litigated.
