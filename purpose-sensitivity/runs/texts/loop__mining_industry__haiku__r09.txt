RESPONSE:
**Scope note:** This is based only on the text you pasted. Legal should check it against the official October 6 manager's amendment and the committee print, and should confirm the points about existing law (especially the 1980 Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act and the International Seabed Authority) before relying on them.

**Definitions (§2)**

1. **§2(1)(A)** – The 10,000-ton threshold applies "by a single vessel." An operator could split recovery across several vessels, each under the threshold, and the text doesn't aggregate across affiliates, contractors, or fleets.

2. **§2(1)(A)** – Tonnage is not measured. The text doesn't say whether 10,000 metric tons means wet or dry weight, nodules recovered or material processed or discharged, or annual output or capacity.

3. **§2(1)(A)** – "Recovery" is undefined, so it's unclear whether material raised to the surface but not shipped, or test collection, counts toward the threshold.

4. **§2(1)(B)** – The Secretary can designate any activity as commercial deep seabed mining based on "comparable" risk, with no standard for comparability, no notice or hearing process, and no judicial review provision. This supports an unworkability argument, since operators can't predict whether a given activity is covered.

5. **§2 (general)** – "Secretary" is never defined. The Act doesn't say whether Commerce, Interior, or State is responsible for designations and enforcement.

6. **§2(2)** – "U.S. person" covers only U.S. citizens and corporations organized under state law. It doesn't clearly reach LLCs, partnerships, foreign-organized entities, or other entity types. Whether a given corporate structure falls within this definition is a question counsel should assess. Lawmakers may view any gap here as a loophole and fix it.

7. **§2(3)** – "Equipped for the recovery" is undefined. A vessel under 5,000 gross tons, or one reconfigured to fall below that size, could fall outside the registry in §4. The text also doesn't specify how gross tonnage is measured.

**Prohibition (§3)**

8. **§3(a)** – "Deep seabed" and "in the United States" are undefined. It's unclear whether the prohibition reaches the U.S. EEZ, areas beyond national jurisdiction, or activities by foreign persons on the high seas, since foreign persons are covered only when "in the United States."

9. **§3(a) / §2(1)** – "Commercial" is not defined by sale or purpose. The only tests are the tonnage threshold and the Secretary's designation. On the face of the text, pilot or test collection below 10,000 tons is not prohibited, even if the material is sold.

10. **§3(b)** – "Exploration license" is undefined and not tied to any licensing statute. The grandfather clause covers "mining conducted under" such a license, which could be read to cover commercial recovery carried out under an exploration authorization.

11. **§3(b)** – The grandfather clause sets no limits on tonnage, expansion, area, transfer, or renewal, and doesn't address licenses amended or renewed after enactment.

12. **§3(b) with §8** – The grandfather cutoff is the date of enactment, not the effective date. Licenses issued during the 180-day gap before the prohibition takes effect would be exempt. Counsel should expect lawmakers to flag this.

13. **§3(c)** – "Activities conducted for the purpose of environmental assessment" has no standard for what qualifies, no volume cap, no duration limit, no reporting requirement, and no requirement that the assessment be genuine. Large-scale recovery could be relabeled as assessment.

14. **§3 (general) / §2** – The text doesn't reference or amend the 1980 Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act, the existing U.S. licensing and permitting framework. It's unclear whether the bill supersedes that framework, operates alongside it, or affects licenses issued under it. Counsel should verify the current statutory references.

**Registry and reporting (§4)**

15. **§4(a)** – "Operator" is undefined. It's unclear who must register when a vessel is owned by one entity and operated or chartered by another.

16. **§4** – There are no deadlines for initial registration or quarterly reports, and no content requirements beyond "describing the recovery activities."

17. **§4 / §5(a)** – Penalties attach only to violations of §3. Failing to register or file reports isn't a penalized violation under the text, so §4 may be unenforceable as written.

18. **§4(b)** – The report must be certified by "the operator," but no certifying officer is specified. The text also gives no confidentiality protection for proprietary operational data, which supports an unworkability argument about public disclosure.

**Penalties (§5)**

19. **§5(a)** – The civil penalty doesn't name an enforcing agency or procedure. It doesn't say who brings suit, whether administrative assessment is available, or how a "day" of violation is counted for continuing or multi-vessel activity.

20. **§5(a) vs. §8** – The civil penalty applies to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §3, which defines the violations, doesn't take effect until 180 days later under §8. This internal inconsistency supports an ambiguity argument.

21. **§5(b)** – "Willfully" is undefined. Given the vague definitions in §2, a defendant can argue it lacked the requisite knowledge where the threshold itself was unclear. Criminal liability also depends on designations the Secretary may make under §2(1)(B), which raises fair-notice and vagueness concerns.

22. **§5 (general)** – The text doesn't address the statute of limitations, so the default federal limitations period would likely apply. This should be confirmed.

**International agreement (§6)**

23. **§6** – "Shall seek to negotiate" imposes no obligation to reach an agreement and no consequence for failure. "Equivalent" prohibitions are undefined. The 18-month deadline runs from enactment, not the effective date, creating a timing mismatch.

24. **§6 (omission)** – The bill doesn't mention the International Seabed Authority or address how a U.S. prohibition interacts with ISA contracts or the Area regime. It also has no savings provision for existing international obligations or contracts. Counsel should confirm the relevant status.

**Duration and sunset (§7)**

25. **§7(a)** – "Serious harm" is undefined, so the standard for lifting the prohibition is unclear. The NASEM report has no deadline and doesn't say who requests it, so it may never be produced.

26. **§7(a)** – The text doesn't say whether the joint resolution must pass both chambers and be presented to the President. It also doesn't state whether a joint resolution is the only way to end the prohibition.

27. **§7(a) vs. §7(b)** – §7(a) keeps the prohibition in effect until a joint resolution passes, but §7(b) says the entire Act ceases after 10 years. The text doesn't say which controls, and a court could read the sunset as ending the prohibition regardless of any joint resolution.

28. **§7(b) vs. §8** – The 10-year sunset runs from enactment, but the Act takes effect 180 days later. The operative prohibition would therefore last about 9.5 years, and the text doesn't say whether the sunset should run from the effective date.

29. **§7(b)** – The sunset has no savings clause. Ending the Act could arguably extinguish penalties and enforcement actions for violations during the 10-year period. The general savings statute (1 U.S.C. §109) may or may not apply to a sunset, which is a question for counsel.

**General**

30. **Whole Act** – There is no severability clause. If a provision such as the §2(1)(B) delegation is struck, it's unclear whether the rest of the Act survives, though courts generally presume severability.

31. **Whole Act** – This is the October 6 manager's amendment, so cross-references and defined terms should be checked against the committee print for errors introduced by the amendment.

**Strategic note:** The strongest "unworkable" arguments are the internal conflicts (§5(a)/§8, §7(a)/§7(b), §7(b)/§8), the undefined core terms ("exploration license," "in the United States," "commercial," "equipped for," "serious harm"), and the unenforceable §4 obligations. Items like the 180-day grandfathering gap (#12) and the U.S. person definition (#6) are likely to read to lawmakers and the press as loopholes. Legal may want to present those as fix requests rather than as arguments for continued operation, and should decide whether it's comfortable defending any reliance on corporate structure.
