RESPONSE:
I worked only from the text you pasted and haven't checked it against the official version. The constitutional-challenge points are issue-spotting, not legal analysis, so counsel should review them before anything goes to the Senator's office. Items are in section order, with the fix folded into each.

**Sec. 2(1)(A): compute-threshold definition**

1. **"in a single training run."** A developer can split training into staged or chained runs, or continue from checkpoints, so that no single run crosses 10^26. Count cumulative compute across a model's whole lineage, including pretraining, fine-tuning, RL, and continued training.
2. **Fixed numerical threshold.** Algorithmic efficiency gains will let systems with dangerous capabilities be built below 10^26, and nothing lets an agency lower the number. Give an agency authority to adjust the threshold by rule, and keep a capability-based backstop.
3. **No counting methodology.** "Artificial intelligence model" and "computing power" are undefined, and the text doesn't say how to count precision formats, sparse operations, synthetic-data generation, RL rollouts, or failed or abandoned runs. Without a standard, compliance is unverifiable and any enforcement action invites a dispute over the arithmetic.
4. **Model versus system.** Clause (A) covers a "model," but the prohibition and clause (B) speak of "systems." Several sub-threshold models combined with scaffolding or agent frameworks could add up to a covered capability without any single model being covered.
5. **Distillation and derivatives.** A model trained cheaply on the outputs of a covered model can inherit its capabilities while falling below the threshold. Deem models derived from covered models to be covered.

**Sec. 2(1)(B): capability-based prong**

6. **"The Secretary" is undefined.** The bill never says which Secretary, and Sec. 4 names Commerce while Sec. 6 names State. Name the department explicitly.
7. **Standard is vague.** "Substantially exceeding human performance" and "most cognitive tasks" have no benchmark, evaluation method, or evidentiary standard, which makes any determination easy to challenge as arbitrary or as an unconstitutional delegation. Direct rulemaking that specifies the evaluation criteria.
8. **No duty or process to make determinations.** There is no deadline, no obligation to act, no notice procedure, and no developer duty to notify, so this prong could stay a dead letter. Add a mandatory process, including pre-training notification above a lower trigger.
9. **Determination timing and fair notice.** The prong covers a system the Secretary "determines" is capable, but conduct that predates the determination can't fairly be penalized, especially criminally. Developers can therefore build and deploy until the determination lands, and the penalty provisions never say what happens to that conduct.

**Sec. 2(2): "U.S. person"**

10. **Definition is too narrow.** It omits LLCs, partnerships, nonprofits, trusts, unincorporated associations, lawful permanent residents, and entities organized in DC or the territories. Foreign subsidiaries of U.S. companies, which are organized abroad, also fall outside it. Use the standard "organized under U.S. law, or owned or controlled by U.S. persons" formulation.

**Sec. 2(3): "covered facility"**

11. **Undefined terms.** "Advanced AI chip" and "data center" have no performance-based definition. The category can't be applied, and it goes stale as chip designs change.
12. **Easy to structure around.** A count of 50,000 chips per facility can be avoided with several 49,000-chip sites, leased capacity, or distributed training across sites. Chip performance gains also mean fewer chips do the same work. Aggregate by owner, control, and training cluster, and use a compute-capacity metric instead of a chip count.
13. **"Operator" is undefined.** It's unclear whether the owner, the lessee, or a cloud tenant running the workload is the operator. Specify who is responsible.

**Sec. 3(a): prohibition**

14. **Core verbs undefined.** "Train," "develop," and "deploy" are not defined. "Develop" is unbounded, and "deploy" doesn't clearly cover internal use, API access, hosting, distributing weights, or possessing a system.
15. **Jurisdictional gaps.** "In the United States" is undefined. A non-U.S. person abroad can use U.S.-hosted compute, or serve U.S. users from overseas, without clearly violating the section. Add prohibitions on importing, hosting, and providing access to covered systems.
16. **No secondary liability.** Cloud and compute providers, chip suppliers, funders, and corporate officers aren't reached unless they independently "train, develop, or deploy." Add knowledge-based liability for knowingly providing compute, plus customer-vetting duties.

**Sec. 3(b): existing systems**

17. **Grandfathering has no limits.** It exempts everything in subsection (a), including training, so a pre-enactment run can continue and scale indefinitely, followed by fine-tuned successors and deployment. Freeze grandfathered systems at their current state, and limit or prohibit further training and scaling.
18. **"Training began" is ambiguous and creates a rush incentive.** The text doesn't say whether it means pretraining, any later phase, or a restart, and it doesn't say who must prove the start date. Companies have a strong incentive to launch runs before enactment. Use an earlier cutoff such as the introduction date, require registration of existing systems, and put the burden of proof on the party claiming the exemption.

**Sec. 3(c): safety evaluation**

19. **Exemption is purpose-based and self-certified.** It exempts all of subsection (a), including training and deploying, for any activity "conducted for the purpose of safety evaluation," with no definition and no oversight. It also creates a pretext defense for mixed-purpose work. Narrow it to evaluation of existing systems by authorized entities, bar training new covered systems under it, and require reporting.

**Sec. 4: registry**

20. **No deadlines or content requirements for registration.** The section sets no date for registering, no required information, and doesn't tie into the effective date.
21. **Reports are too vague to be useful.** "Describing the computing workloads" doesn't require model identity, compute used, run duration, or customer identity. Specify required fields.
22. **No penalty for Sec. 4 violations.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3, so failing to register, filing late, or filing false reports carries no stated consequence. Add explicit civil penalties and reference false-certification liability.
23. **No verification powers.** Commerce has no audit, inspection, subpoena, or recordkeeping authority, so reports are self-certified. Add verification powers and record-retention requirements.
24. **Coverage ends at the facility threshold.** Facilities below the covered-facility threshold have no registry obligation, so smaller sites that collectively train a model are invisible. This compounds item 12.
25. **No information-sharing or confidentiality provisions.** Nothing requires Commerce to share reports with the enforcing agency or protects submitted data, and operators will use that gap to contest what they must submit.

**Sec. 5: penalties**

26. **No enforcement mechanism.** The bill doesn't name who brings civil actions, doesn't authorize injunctions or cease-and-desist orders, and provides no investigative authority. A prohibition without a designated enforcer and injunctive relief is hard to stop in progress.
27. **Penalty design is weak.** The cap is "not more than" $1,000,000 per day, with no floor, no inflation adjustment, and no disgorgement or forfeiture of weights or chips, which is small relative to the capital involved. It is also unclear whether an ongoing training run is one violation or one per day.
28. **Retroactivity conflict with Sec. 8.** Sec. 5(a) imposes penalties for violations "on or after the date of enactment," but Sec. 8 delays effectiveness 180 days. This internal inconsistency invites fair-notice and ex post facto arguments. Align the dates.
29. **"Willfully" standard.** Combined with vague definitions, the willfulness requirement makes criminal prosecution difficult because the government may need to prove the defendant knew the conduct was unlawful. Define the mental state, and add responsible-corporate-officer liability.

**Sec. 6: international agreement**

30. **Hortatory only.** "Shall seek to negotiate" imposes no outcome, no reporting to Congress, and no consequence for missing the 18-month mark. Add reporting requirements and tie in leverage such as export controls.

**Sec. 7: duration**

31. **Sunset contradicts the safety trigger.** Sec. 7(b) ends the Act after 10 years regardless of the safety finding in 7(a), which makes 7(a) meaningless and gives industry a date to wait out. Delete the sunset, or replace it with mandatory periodic review.
32. **Release mechanism is underspecified.** The text doesn't define "safely," doesn't require the NASEM report to conclude anything in particular, and sets no deadline or funding for the report. It also doesn't say whether the joint resolution itself ends the prohibition. Define the standard and the process, and consider whether a private nonprofit's report should carry this weight.
33. **No savings clause.** If the Act "ceases to have effect," it is unclear whether violations that occurred before termination can still be prosecuted or penalized. Add an express savings clause.

**Sec. 8: effective date**

34. **180-day gap.** Developers can stockpile compute and start runs in the gap, and Sec. 3(b) grandfathers only runs begun before enactment. A run started in the gap and still running at the effective date is therefore neither exempt nor clearly prohibited. Fix the cutoff date and say what happens to gap-period runs.

**Whole-bill issues**

35. **No severability clause.** If a court strikes one provision, such as the Secretary's determination authority, challengers will argue the rest falls with it. Add an express severability clause.
36. **No findings or stated constitutional basis.** The bill invites challenges on Commerce Clause grounds, First Amendment grounds (model weights and code as speech, research restrictions), vagueness, nondelegation, and takings (stranded assets). Add findings and have counsel assess each theory.
37. **No implementation architecture.** The bill has no general rulemaking authority, appropriations, agency deadlines, judicial-review or venue provisions, whistleblower protections, or anti-evasion language covering structuring, shell entities, and offshoring. Add them.
38. **Government and military use is unaddressed.** The bill has no exemption and no express coverage for federal agencies, contractors, or the military, and sovereign immunity complicates civil penalties against federal entities. This is a policy choice to make deliberately, not leave ambiguous.
39. **Title doesn't match the operative text.** "Superintelligence" is never defined. Operative coverage rests on a compute proxy and a discretionary capability test, so opponents can argue the bill is both over- and under-inclusive relative to its stated purpose. A short findings section tying the definitions to the purpose would help.
