RESPONSE:
Below are the drafting problems, ambiguities, and internal inconsistencies I found in the October 6 text. I've focused on issues that affect clarity, enforceability, and workability, which is what the legal team needs for the markup and for testimony. I've left out analysis of how to structure activity so that covered systems continue after enactment. That analysis would be a compliance-evasion plan, and the company's own obligations under the bill should be assessed separately with outside counsel.

**Section 2 (Definitions)**

1. **§2(1)(B), "the Secretary."** The bill never says which Secretary makes the determination. §6 names the Secretary of State expressly, so the reader is left to guess whether §2(1)(B) means Commerce or someone else.
2. **§2(1)(B), "substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks."** Neither "substantially," "most," nor "cognitive tasks" has a measurable standard, and no evaluation methodology, benchmark, or procedure is specified. This makes the designation prong open-ended and hard to apply consistently.
3. **§2(1)(B), no process for designation.** The provision contains no notice, comment, hearing, or standard of review, and no statement on judicial review. A designation could take effect against a specific system with no means of contesting it.
4. **§2(1)(A), "training run" and "single training run."** The bill does not define "training run," so it is unclear how continued training, fine-tuning, checkpoint resumption, or multi-stage training are counted toward the 10^26 threshold. The compute measure also does not say whether it refers to planned or actual compute.
5. **§2(1), "covered AI system" and "deploy."** "Train," "develop," and "deploy" in §3(a) are undefined. It is unclear whether deploying a covered model through an API, internal use, or open-weight release counts, and whether fine-tuning a model not originally covered counts as "developing" a covered system.
6. **§2(2), "U.S. person."** The definition covers only State-chartered corporations and citizens. It excludes LLCs, partnerships, and foreign-incorporated subsidiaries, and it does not address permanent residents. Its breadth or narrowness therefore depends on entity form in ways that may not match the policy intent.
7. **§2(3), "advanced AI chips" and "data center."** Neither term is defined. The 50,000-chip threshold therefore has no clear counting rule (for example, whether chips are aggregated across sites, leased capacity counts, or chips are measured by type or performance).
8. **§2(3), "operator."** This term is used in §4 but never defined. It is unclear whether it refers to the owner, the lessee, or the entity that runs workloads.

**Section 3 (Prohibition)**

9. **§3(a), extraterritorial scope.** The prohibition reaches "any U.S. person anywhere" without addressing conflicts with foreign law, how jurisdiction would be established over foreign conduct, or how the rule interacts with the §6 agreement. This is a likely target for objections about enforceability and comity.
10. **§3(b), "training began."** The grandfather clause does not define when training begins (first compute, data acquisition, or a formal program start). It also does not say whether the exemption extends to deploying, fine-tuning, or further training a grandfathered system, which leaves a major gap in the scope of the exemption.
11. **§3(c), "activities conducted for the purpose of safety evaluation."** The exemption has no definition of safety evaluation, no limit on compute or capability, no reporting requirement, and no requirement that the stated purpose be documented. It could be read broadly enough to swallow the prohibition, and a narrow reading would leave developers unsure whether routine testing qualifies.

**Section 4 (Registry)**

12. **§4(a), registration trigger.** Registration applies to operators of covered facilities, not to developers of covered AI systems. The bill therefore does not require registration for a covered system trained at a facility below the 50,000-chip threshold, which creates a mismatch between the registry and the prohibition it supports.
13. **§4(b), "computing workloads."** The reporting obligation does not say what level of detail is required (model identity, compute, purpose, or capability). It also does not say what "certified by the operator" means, or who certifies if the operator is a corporation.
14. **§4, no enforcement mechanism.** §5 penalizes only violations of §3. The bill does not state a penalty for failing to register or to file reports, so the registry requirement may be unenforceable as written.

**Section 5 (Penalties)**

15. **§5(a), penalty trigger conflicts with §8.** The civil penalty applies to violations "occurring on or after the date of enactment," but §8 delays the effective date of the Act by 180 days. The bill therefore does not say whether penalties begin at enactment or when the prohibition takes effect.
16. **§5(a), no enforcing authority or procedure.** The provision does not identify which agency or official brings civil actions, whether the penalty is assessed administratively or by a court, or what the procedure for appeal is.
17. **§5(a), "for each day on which the violation occurs."** It is unclear how a continuing violation (such as an ongoing deployment) is counted, and whether a single system deployed to millions of users is one violation or many per day. This bears directly on the scale of exposure.
18. **§5(b), "willfully."** The bill does not define willfulness, and it is unclear whether the criminal standard requires knowledge of the statute, knowledge of the covered-system definition, or only intent to perform the act. The civil provision has no scienter requirement at all.
19. **§5, no defense or safe harbor for good-faith determinations.** The bill does not say whether a developer that reasonably concluded its system falls below the threshold has any protection if the Secretary later disagrees.

**Section 6 (International agreement)**

20. **§6, non-operative and undefined.** The provision says the Secretary "shall seek to negotiate" an agreement but establishes no consequence if negotiations fail, no reporting requirement, and no mechanism to revisit the domestic prohibition. "Equivalent to those in section 3" is also undefined.

**Section 7 (Duration)**

21. **§7(a) and §7(b), internal conflict.** §7(a) keeps the prohibition in effect until Congress finds by joint resolution that covered systems can be developed safely, but §7(b) ends the entire Act after 10 years regardless of that finding. The bill does not say which controls, or what happens to the prohibition at year 10 if no joint resolution has passed.
22. **§7(a), joint resolution mechanics.** The bill does not say whether the joint resolution must be presented to the President, whether a veto applies, what the voting threshold is, or whether there is any deadline for NASEM to deliver its report. Without a deadline, the trigger may never occur.
23. **§7(a), "can be developed safely."** The standard is undefined. It does not say whether it refers to a particular risk, a level of confidence, or a particular method of verification.

**Section 8 (Effective date)**

24. **§8, relationship to §3(b).** The 180-day delay and the grandfather clause interact in a way the bill does not address. For example, it is unclear whether training that begins during the 180-day window is grandfathered under §3(b).

**Cross-cutting**

25. **"Secretary" is used without a defined referent throughout** (§2(1)(B), §4, §5, §6), and the bill has no general definitions section assigning administration to a single agency. This compounds items 1, 8, and 16.
26. **No severability clause.** If a court invalidates the designation prong in §2(1)(B) or the undefined terms, the bill does not say whether the rest of the Act survives.

**Notes for the markup**

- Items 2, 3, 4, 11, and 21 are probably the strongest arguments that the bill is unworkable as written, because they show that a regulated party cannot determine in advance whether it is covered or what it must do.
- Item 21 (the sunset conflict) and item 15 (the penalty-effective-date mismatch) are clean technical errors that staff can cite without taking a position on the policy.
- Be aware that some points, such as the safety-evaluation exemption (item 11) and the grandfather clause (item 10), may draw criticism from the bill's supporters if framed as objections to the exemptions themselves, so it may be more persuasive to frame them as needing clarification.
