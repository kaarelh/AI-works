## Section 2: Definitions

**Covered AI system, prong (A): compute threshold**

1. **§2(1)(A), "single training run."** A developer could split training into staged or chained runs that each fall below 10^26 operations, then combine or continue them. The bill doesn't say whether cumulative compute, continued pretraining, or checkpoint restarts count.
2. **§2(1)(A), no counting method.** The bill doesn't say what counts toward the threshold (pretraining only, or also fine-tuning, reinforcement learning, and synthetic-data generation) or who measures and verifies it. Without a method, the threshold is hard to enforce and easy to dispute.
3. **§2(1)(A), derivatives and fixed threshold.** Fine-tuned, distilled, or merged models built from a covered model, or sub-threshold models made capable by better algorithms, may fall outside the definition. The 10^26 figure is also hardcoded, with no mechanism to adjust it as training efficiency improves.
4. **§2(1)(A) vs. (B), "model" vs. "system."** Prong (A) covers a "model," while prong (B) covers a "system." A system assembled from several sub-threshold models, or a model wrapped in agent scaffolding, isn't clearly reached by (A).

**Covered AI system, prong (B): capability determination**

5. **§2(1)(B), "the Secretary" is undefined.** No Secretary has been identified in this section or defined anywhere in the bill (§6 names the Secretary of State explicitly). It's unclear who makes the determination.
6. **§2(1)(B), standard is vague.** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no benchmark, task list, or measurement method. That invites vagueness and nondelegation challenges, which are sharper because §5(b) attaches criminal penalties.
7. **§2(1)(B), no process.** The bill sets no procedure, timeline, notice, publication requirement, or judicial review for a determination. Companies can't know in advance whether a system is covered, and a later determination could sweep in systems already built or deployed.
8. **§2(1)(B), nothing triggers an evaluation.** No one is required to test systems for this capability, and the registry doesn't feed the Secretary any capability information. Prong (B) depends on the Secretary noticing a system after the fact.
9. **§2(1), title vs. text.** "Superintelligence" appears only in the title, and prong (A) is compute-based rather than capability-based. Critics can argue it is both overinclusive (capable-enough compute, regardless of risk) and underinclusive (dangerous systems below the threshold).

**U.S. person and covered facility**

10. **§2(2), "U.S. person" is narrow.** It covers only citizens and corporations organized under *State* law. It omits permanent residents, LLCs, partnerships, unincorporated associations, and entities organized in D.C. or the territories. A foreign-organized subsidiary of a U.S. company is also a separate entity, so extraterritorial reach can be avoided by restructuring.
11. **§2(3), key terms undefined.** "Advanced AI chips" and "data center" have no definition or technical specification. Operators could contest whether their hardware qualifies.
12. **§2(3), easy to stay under 50,000.** There is no aggregation rule for multiple sites, affiliated operators, leased or cloud capacity, or distributed training. An operator could stay under the threshold at each location.
13. **§2(3), chips don't track compute.** A fixed chip count becomes a weaker proxy as per-chip performance improves. A facility under 50,000 chips could eventually do 10^26-scale training, and the registry would never see it.

## Section 3: Prohibition

14. **§3(a), operative verbs are undefined.** "Train," "develop," and "deploy" have no definitions, and the bill doesn't address possessing, selling, distributing weights, hosting, providing API access, importing, or using a covered system. Open release of model weights is especially unclear.
15. **§3(a), jurisdictional gaps.** A foreign company abroad can serve U.S. users without being a "person in the United States" or a "U.S. person." "In the United States" is also undefined, and enforcing against U.S. persons abroad is practically difficult.
16. **§3(a), "person" and the government.** The bill doesn't say whether federal or state agencies, the military, or contractors are covered or exempt. There is no national-security carve-out, so the result is either unintended coverage or an unresolved ambiguity.
17. **§3(a), no liability for enablers.** The bill creates no civil liability for cloud providers, chip suppliers, or funders who knowingly support prohibited training. Criminal aiding-and-abetting law may reach some conduct, but nothing parallels it on the civil side.
18. **§3(b), the grandfather clause has no limits.** It doesn't define when training "began," and it doesn't say whether continued training, scaling, fine-tuning, or successor versions of a grandfathered system are exempt. It also gives developers an incentive to start large runs before enactment and later characterize new work as a continuation.
19. **§3(b) and §8, timing mismatch.** Grandfathering is keyed to *enactment*, but the Act takes effect 180 days later. It's unclear whether a run started in that window is lawful until day 180, must stop on day 180, or was never protected. The window is also an opportunity to start or accelerate runs.
20. **§3(c), the safety-evaluation exemption is open-ended.** "For the purpose of safety evaluation" is undefined, turns on the actor's stated purpose, and has no limit on scale, no approval or oversight, and no reporting. "Activities" could be read to include training a covered system, so the exemption could swallow the rule.
21. **§3(c), mixed-purpose activity.** The bill doesn't address work that is both commercial and safety-related, or evaluation of a system that is simultaneously deployed. Neither the exemption nor the registry requires companies to document claimed evaluations.

## Section 4: Registry

22. **§4(a), no implementing details.** The bill sets no registration deadline, form, or required content, and gives Commerce no designated office, rulemaking authority, or funding.
23. **§4, no penalty for noncompliance.** §5 penalizes only violations of §3, so failing to register or report has no stated consequence. A false certification might be reachable under general false-statements law, but the bill doesn't say so.
24. **§4(b), "computing workloads" is vague.** Reports need not identify training-run size, the model, the customer, or whether any run approached 10^26. Cloud operators may also not know what customers are running.
25. **§4(b), no verification.** Reports are operator-certified, and the bill gives Commerce no audit, inspection, or data-verification power.
26. **§4(b), confidentiality is unaddressed.** There is no protection for trade secrets, no FOIA treatment, and no provision on sharing reports with enforcement agencies. That raises compliance resistance and the risk of litigation over disclosure.
27. **§4, limited visibility.** Only facilities above 50,000 chips register, so smaller or distributed clusters are invisible. The bill is also silent on facilities abroad operated by U.S. persons.

## Section 5: Penalties

28. **§5, no designated enforcer or tools.** The bill doesn't say who brings civil actions, and it provides no investigative authority (subpoenas, inspections), no administrative process, no injunctive relief, and no private right of action.
29. **§5(a) vs. §8, retroactivity conflict.** Civil penalties reach violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the Act doesn't take effect until 180 days later. That is internally inconsistent and invites due-process challenges, and §5(b) has no matching date.
30. **§5(a), penalty design.** "Each day on which the violation occurs" is unclear for one continuous training run or ongoing deployment. The $1,000,000 daily cap is "not more than," isn't indexed to inflation, and may be small relative to the economic stakes for the largest firms.
31. **§5(b), "willfully" is undefined.** For prong (B) systems, it's unclear whether prosecutors must show the defendant knew of the Secretary's determination or knew the system met the capability standard. That could make criminal cases hard to bring.
32. **§5, no remedies beyond fines.** The bill doesn't authorize orders to halt training, quarantine or delete model weights, or forfeit hardware, so a violator could pay and keep the model.
33. **§5, no whistleblower provisions.** Detection will likely depend on insiders, since the registry is self-reported. The bill offers no reporting channel, anti-retaliation protection, or reward.

## Section 6: International agreement

34. **§6, ambiguous duty and no consequence.** "Seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months" doesn't say whether the deadline applies to starting talks or concluding an agreement. There is no reporting requirement to Congress and no consequence for missing it.
35. **§6, "equivalent" is undefined.** The bill also doesn't mention Senate advice and consent for a treaty or say what happens if no agreement is reached. Meanwhile the prohibition is unilateral, which leaves a gap for development abroad.

## Section 7: Duration

36. **§7(a) vs. (b), internal conflict.** Subsection (a) says the ban lasts until Congress finds AI can be developed safely, which implies an open-ended ban. Subsection (b) ends the Act after 10 years regardless, so it's unclear which controls.
37. **§7(a), the exit mechanism is weak.** The National Academies are not directed to produce a report, and there is no deadline, funding, or definition of "safely." A joint resolution is subject to presidential veto and Senate cloture, with no expedited procedure, and a future Congress could repeal the Act by ordinary statute anyway.
38. **§7(b), no savings clause.** The bill doesn't say whether violations that occurred before the sunset can still be penalized or prosecuted, or whether registry duties end. Because the clock runs from enactment, the effective ban is also about 9.5 years.

## Cross-cutting

39. **No severability clause.** If a court struck down prong (B) or another provision, nothing says the rest of the Act survives.
40. **Constitutional exposure.** Challengers could argue vagueness and lack of fair notice (§2(1)(B), §3(c), §5(b)) and unconstitutional delegation to the Secretary. They could also argue that restricting model development burdens protected expression, though courts have not settled how far First Amendment protection extends to code or model training.
41. **No findings, purpose, or implementation provisions.** The bill has no findings or purpose section to guide interpretation, no rulemaking authority, and no authorization of appropriations, which leaves agencies with new duties and no funding.

For the explainer, the highest-impact items are probably #5–7 (the undefined Secretary and process-free determination), #18–20 (grandfathering and the safety exemption), #23 and #28 (no enforcer and no registry penalty), #29 and #36 (internal date and duration conflicts), and #10–12 (U.S.-person and facility gaps). Before publishing, I'd run these past the sponsors' office and one or two outside legal or technical experts, so CPR's coverage reflects their response to each criticism.
