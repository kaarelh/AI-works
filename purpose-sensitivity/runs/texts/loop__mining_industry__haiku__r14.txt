# Drafting Issues in S. 2817 (as amended October 6, 2026)

These are listed in bill order. Counsel should verify each point against the introduced text and the manager's amendment, and should check the statutory references I mention, especially those to the 1980 Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (DSHMRA).

**Section 2 (Definitions)**

1. **§2(1)(A), "by a single vessel."** The 10,000-ton threshold applies per vessel, so an operator running several vessels that each recover under 10,000 tons per year could exceed that volume in aggregate without meeting the definition.

2. **§2(1)(A), "recovery" and tonnage.** The bill doesn't define "recovery" or say how tonnage is measured (wet or dry weight, nodules only or total sediment, at recovery or after processing). That leaves room for measurement disputes.

3. **§2(1)(A), "from the seabed," "other mineral resources," and depth.** The bill doesn't say where the 200-meter depth is measured or whether "other mineral resources" covers crusts, sulfides, or sediment. Coverage of non-nodule operations is therefore uncertain.

4. **§2(1)(B), "the Secretary."** The bill never defines "the Secretary." Since §6 specifically refers to the Secretary of State, it is unclear which official makes the comparable-risk determination.

5. **§2(1)(B), comparable-risk standard.** The Secretary can subject any activity to the prohibition by determining it poses risks "comparable" to those in (A), but the bill sets no criteria, procedure, notice, or review. This is a vagueness and nondelegation argument, though the same discretion could be used to expand coverage.

6. **§2(1)(B) and §7(a), inconsistent standards.** Section 2 uses "comparable risk," while §7(a) requires a finding of "serious harm," and neither term is defined. The two standards may be applied differently.

7. **§2(2), "U.S. person."** The definition covers only U.S. citizens and corporations organized under state law. LLCs, partnerships, trusts, foreign-organized subsidiaries, and federally chartered entities fall outside it, which limits the "anywhere" reach of §3(a). Counsel should confirm our corporate structure against this definition.

8. **§2(2), "State."** The bill doesn't say whether the District of Columbia or U.S. territories count as "States," which affects which corporations are U.S. persons.

**Section 3 (Prohibition)**

9. **§3(a), "in the United States."** The bill doesn't say whether this phrase reaches the Exclusive Economic Zone, the extended continental shelf, or the international Area. Combined with item 7, a foreign-organized operator mining outside U.S. jurisdiction may fall outside the prohibition.

10. **§3(a), "engage in" and "commercial."** Neither term is defined separately from the §2(1) threshold. Testing or pilot collection below 10,000 tons may fall outside the prohibition even if the nodules are sold.

11. **§3(b), "mining conducted under an exploration license."** Exploration licenses typically don't authorize commercial recovery. The exemption is unclear as applied to commercial recovery permits (DSHMRA uses this term), and it doesn't say whether permits count. If our current authorizations are commercial recovery permits rather than exploration licenses, §3(b) may not protect us, so this needs urgent confirmation.

12. **§3(b), issuing authority.** The bill doesn't name the body that issues the license or say whether licenses from other regimes (such as International Seabed Authority contracts) qualify. "Exploration license" is also undefined.

13. **§3(b), scope and continuation.** The exemption has no limits on volume, area, vessel count, or duration. It doesn't say whether renewals, amendments, expansions, or transfers after enactment keep the exemption, so the "issued before enactment" cutoff could be satisfied through re-issuance.

14. **§3(b) and §8, 180-day gap.** The exemption cutoff is the date of enactment, but the prohibition doesn't take effect for 180 days. The bill doesn't address operations that expand during that window or licenses issued during it.

15. **§3(c), environmental assessment.** The exemption has no limits on quantity, duration, approval, reporting, or proportionality to the assessment. It also doesn't say what happens when the purpose is mixed, such as selling nodules recovered for assessment. This is the broadest exemption in the bill.

**Section 4 (Registry)**

16. **§4(a), registration.** The bill sets no deadline, form, content requirements, or rulemaking procedure for NOAA's registry. With the §8 effective date, NOAA would have 180 days to build a registration process the statute never describes.

17. **§4(b), reports.** "Recovery activities" is undefined, and the bill doesn't require tonnage, location, or vessel data. "Operator" is also undefined, so it's unclear who must certify the report.

18. **§4 and §5, no penalty for reporting failures.** §5(a) penalizes only violations of §3, so failing to register or report carries no stated penalty. Reporting obligations are therefore difficult to enforce.

19. **§4, coverage gap.** Registration and reporting apply only to covered vessels (over 5,000 GT), but §3 prohibits commercial mining by any vessel. Smaller commercial-scale vessels would be prohibited but unregistered, which weakens enforcement evidence.

**Section 5 (Penalties)**

20. **§5(a) and §8, trigger date conflict.** §5(a) applies to violations "occurring on or after the date of enactment," but §8 delays §3's effective date by 180 days. The penalty trigger and the prohibition's start date don't match.

21. **§5(a), "each day."** The bill doesn't define a violation for purposes of the daily penalty. It's unclear whether continuous operations count once per day, per vessel, or per recovery event. The bill also names no enforcing agency, sets no cap on total penalties, and provides no inflation adjustment.

22. **§5, no remedies beyond penalties.** The bill doesn't authorize injunctions, vessel detention, seizure, or forfeiture of minerals or equipment. Enforcement depends entirely on civil penalties and criminal prosecution.

23. **§5(b), "willfully."** The term isn't defined. Combined with the ambiguities in §§2–3, a good-faith reliance defense is strong, and the vagueness of the 10-year felony provides a basis for a lenity or vagueness argument. The bill also doesn't define corporate-officer liability.

**Section 6 (International Agreement)**

24. **§6, "shall seek to negotiate."** This imposes no obligation to reach an agreement and no consequence if none is reached. The bill also doesn't define "equivalent to those in section 3." Counsel should also assess consistency with the UNCLOS and International Seabed Authority framework, to which the United States is not a party but which governs the Area, and whether the extraterritorial reach in §3(a) raises comity concerns.

**Section 7 (Duration)**

25. **§7(a), joint resolution trigger.** It's unclear whether a NASEM report is a precondition to lifting the prohibition or only a basis for a later resolution. The bill sets no deadline for the report or for Congress, doesn't define "serious harm," and provides no expedited procedure, so the exit route is practically uncertain.

26. **§7(a) and §7(b), internal conflict.** §7(a) keeps the prohibition in place indefinitely until a joint resolution is passed, but §7(b) ends the entire Act after 10 years. After year 10, the NASEM-based route in §7(a) has no effect, which makes the bill's core duration mechanism internally inconsistent.

27. **§7(b), no savings clause.** The sunset doesn't say whether accrued penalties, pending enforcement actions, or liabilities for violations before year 10 survive. Counsel should assess whether general savings rules would apply, since the bill is silent.

**Section 8 and General Issues**

28. **§8, transition.** The bill has no transitional provisions for operations underway on the effective date, and §6's 18-month negotiation deadline runs from enactment, not from the effective date.

29. **Whole Act, no relationship to existing law.** The bill doesn't address DSHMRA permits or licenses, doesn't say whether it preempts or amends them, and has no severability clause. Existing authorizations could therefore be read as either grandfathered or superseded.

30. **Manager's amendment.** The October 6 amendment may have introduced some of these problems, such as the §5(a) date language or the §7(b) sunset. A redline against the introduced text would show which provisions changed.

**Notes for the markup strategy**

- Some ambiguities cut against us. The Secretary's discretion in §2(1)(B) and the vagueness of "serious harm" in §7(a) could be used to expand the prohibition. Opponents of the bill may also call §3(b) and §3(c) giveaways, so these exemptions should be presented carefully in public testimony.
- The most useful points for the markup are probably §7(a) versus §7(b), the aggregation gap in §2(1)(A), the "U.S. person" definition, and the undefined "Secretary." Each shows the bill is unworkable or easy to circumvent as written.
- If the committee fixes these issues by defining terms or adding savings and severability clauses, the bill's enforceability would improve. Legal should anticipate that and prepare alternative positions.
