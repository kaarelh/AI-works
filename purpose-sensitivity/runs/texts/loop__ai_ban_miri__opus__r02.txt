# S. 2817 Issues for Senator Whitfield's Office

Based on the text as amended by the October 6 manager's amendment. The items I'd treat as most serious are **#11 (grandfather clause)**, **#13 (safety-evaluation exemption)**, **#3 (single-run evasion)**, **#33 (10-year sunset)**, and **#22 (registry has no penalties)**.

## Sec. 2: Definitions

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "artificial intelligence model."** Neither "artificial intelligence" nor "model" is defined, which invites disputes over what counts. The bill should define AI by cross-reference to an existing statutory definition, such as the one in the National AI Initiative Act at 15 U.S.C. 9401(3).

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A) vs. 2(1)(B), "model" vs. "system."** Paragraph (A) covers a "model," while (B) covers a "system." A developer could combine several sub-threshold models into one agentic system and argue that no single model crosses the compute line.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "in a single training run."** This is the easiest evasion in the bill. A developer could split training into nominally separate runs, continue training or fine-tune an existing model, or merge checkpoints, each step under 10^26. The threshold should cover cumulative compute used to produce a model, including all pretraining, continued training, fine-tuning, and reinforcement learning, plus compute used to train any model it was distilled from or built on.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A), compute accounting.** The bill doesn't say how operations are counted. Open questions include synthetic-data generation, RL rollouts, and parallel runs whose results are later combined. Without a counting methodology, or rulemaking authority to set one, companies can choose favorable accounting.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(A), fixed threshold.** Algorithmic efficiency keeps improving, so a fixed 10^26 threshold will capture less dangerous capability every year. Nothing authorizes the agency to lower it. The bill should require periodic review and allow downward adjustment only.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B), "the Secretary."** The bill never says which Secretary. Sec. 6 refers to the Secretary of State, which makes this worse.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(B), the capability standard.** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no defined tasks, evaluation method, or procedure. It is a strong candidate for a vagueness challenge, especially because it carries criminal penalties under Sec. 5(b).

8. **Sec. 2(1)(B), timing.** This prong only catches a system after it already substantially exceeds humans, which is too late to prevent the harm the bill targets. It should also reach systems trained or developed with the intent or reasonably foreseeable effect of reaching that capability. It should also let the agency designate systems based on dangerous precursor capabilities, such as autonomous AI R&D.

9. **Sec. 2(2), "U.S. person."** The definition is much narrower than the one used in export-control law. It omits:
   - lawful permanent residents and U.S. nationals;
   - non-corporate entities such as LLCs and partnerships, the structure many AI developers use;
   - entities organized in D.C. or the territories, if "State" is read literally;
   - foreign subsidiaries controlled by U.S. companies.

   A U.S. lab could simply move frontier training to a foreign affiliate.

10. **Sec. 2(3), "covered facility."** "Advanced AI chips" and "data center" are undefined. A chip-count threshold can be evaded by splitting capacity across several sites under 50,000 chips each, or by using fewer, more powerful chips. The threshold should be based on aggregate compute capacity under common ownership or control, and should cover distributed training across sites.

## Sec. 3: Prohibition

11. **Sec. 3(b), grandfather clause.** This exempts any covered system "whose training began before" enactment, with no limit on how long that training continues, how much it scales, or how long the system is deployed. Every lab will start a nominal run before enactment and claim the exemption indefinitely. At minimum, the clause should cover only training completed before a fixed date (ideally the introduction date). It should also bar further training and capability scaling of grandfathered systems.

12. **Sec. 3(b) and 2(1)(B), grandfathered superintelligence.** A pre-enactment system later found to meet the 2(1)(B) capability standard would still be exempt under 3(b). This applies to the systems of greatest concern.

13. **Sec. 3(c), safety-evaluation exemption.** "Activities conducted for the purpose of safety evaluation" is undefined, self-certified, and not limited to evaluation. On its face it would exempt training a covered system if the developer says the purpose is safety evaluation. This exemption could swallow the whole prohibition. It should be limited to evaluating already-lawful systems, should expressly exclude training or capability enhancement, and should require prior agency authorization.

14. **Sec. 3(a), "develop."** "Develop" is undefined. It is unclear when a "develop" violation begins and ends, which matters for per-day penalties under Sec. 5(a). It is also unclear whether it reaches capabilities research that isn't itself a training run.

15. **Sec. 3(a), no secondary liability.** Nothing prohibits knowingly providing compute, chips, cloud capacity, or funding for a prohibited training run, including foreign runs. Cloud providers and chipmakers have no duty to verify customers. The bill should add facilitation liability and know-your-customer obligations for large compute providers.

16. **Sec. 3(a), export of compute.** A foreign entity that isn't a U.S. person can train a covered system abroad on U.S.-made chips. Nothing in the bill links the prohibition to chip export controls.

17. **Sec. 3(a), model weights.** Possessing, transferring, or publishing the weights of a covered system isn't expressly covered. Coverage depends on how a court reads "deploy." Open-weight release and weight transfers to foreign parties should be addressed directly.

18. **Sec. 3(a), government actors.** "Person" is undefined, and under the Dictionary Act it generally excludes the federal government. Federal agencies and national laboratories could therefore be outside the prohibition. The bill should state expressly whether government entities and their contractors are covered.

19. **Sec. 3(a), attempt and conspiracy.** There is no attempt provision. General federal conspiracy law may apply, but an express attempt and conspiracy clause would remove doubt.

## Sec. 4: Registry

20. **Sec. 4(a), no deadline.** The bill sets no deadline for registration and no process for facilities that become covered later.

21. **Sec. 4(a)–(b), "operator."** "Operator" is undefined. It is unclear whether the duty falls on the facility owner, the colocation provider, or the customer running workloads, and each could point to another.

22. **Sec. 4, no penalties.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3. Failing to register, failing to report, or filing a false report carries no penalty under this Act, which leaves the registry unenforceable.

23. **Sec. 4(b), report contents.** "Describing the computing workloads" sets no required content. Reports should include, at minimum, compute used per customer and per model, customer identity, and the largest training runs.

24. **Sec. 4, no verification authority.** Commerce has no authority to inspect, audit, subpoena, or issue rules. Self-certified quarterly reports with no verification will not detect violations.

25. **Sec. 4, confidentiality.** The bill says nothing about handling confidential business information. That invites litigation and resistance from industry, and leaves unclear whether reports can be shared with enforcement agencies.

## Sec. 5: Penalties

26. **Sec. 5(a), conflict with the effective date.** Sec. 5(a) applies to violations "on or after the date of enactment." Under Sec. 8, the Act doesn't take effect until 180 days later. The inconsistency will be litigated, and penalizing conduct before the effective date raises fair-notice concerns. The two dates should be aligned.

27. **Sec. 5(a), penalty size.** A $1M-per-day cap is small next to frontier training budgets and lab valuations, so firms may treat it as a cost of doing business. Options include penalties scaled to revenue or training cost, and forfeiture of compute hardware and model weights.

28. **Sec. 5, no enforcement authority.** The bill doesn't say who enforces it: no agency is named for civil penalties, no administrative process is set out, and the Attorney General isn't authorized to bring civil actions.

29. **Sec. 5, no injunctive relief.** There is no express authority to seek an injunction halting an ongoing training run, which is the remedy that matters most here.

30. **Sec. 5(b), criminal fines.** "Fined under title 18" caps organizational fines at the general title 18 levels. These are small for this context unless the alternative gain-or-loss measure applies. The bill should also consider express liability for officers and directors.

## Sec. 6: International Agreement

31. **Sec. 6, "shall seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months."** It is unclear whether the deadline applies to starting negotiations or concluding them. "Seek to" creates no enforceable duty, and there is no reporting requirement. The bill should require progress reports to Congress and set out the elements an agreement must contain, including verification and compute monitoring.

32. **Sec. 6, "equivalent" prohibitions.** Tying the agreement to Sec. 3 imports all of Sec. 3's loopholes into the international standard.

## Sec. 7: Duration

33. **Sec. 7(b), 10-year sunset.** The sunset ends the entire Act automatically after 10 years, whether or not Sec. 7(a) has been triggered and whether or not anyone has shown that development can be done safely. Companies can simply wait it out, and the sunset contradicts the "until it can be done safely" structure of 7(a). It should be removed, or replaced with a requirement for congressional review that does not lapse the prohibition.

34. **Sec. 7(a) vs. 7(b), conflicting termination provisions.** The two provisions set conflicting termination conditions. The bill also has no savings clause, so pending prosecutions and penalty actions could abate at the sunset.

35. **Sec. 7(a), no mechanism for the NASEM report.** No one is directed to commission the report, there is no funding or schedule, and no criteria for "safely" are given. A joint resolution is constitutionally sound (unlike a concurrent resolution), but the bill should specify the report's process and standards.

## Sec. 8: Effective Date

36. **Sec. 8, 180-day delay.** Combined with the grandfather clause, the delay creates a window to race. During those 180 days, training is lawful. Any run started before enactment is exempt forever, giving labs every reason to launch runs while the bill is pending. The bill should consider immediate effect for the training prohibition, or a grandfather cutoff tied to the date of introduction.

## Missing Provisions

37. **No rulemaking authority.** No agency has general authority to issue regulations defining terms, setting compute-accounting methods, or updating thresholds.

38. **No severability clause.** If a court strikes a vulnerable provision such as Sec. 2(1)(B), a severability clause would help preserve the rest of the Act.

39. **No whistleblower protections.** Lab employees are often the people best placed to know about violations, and nothing protects them for reporting.

40. **No preemption or savings language.** The bill doesn't address how it interacts with state AI laws or existing export-control authorities.
