# Drafting Problems and Loopholes in S. 2817 (Manager's Amendment, Oct. 6, 2026)

**Definitions (§2)**

1. **§2(1)(B):** The "Secretary" is never identified. §4 refers to the Department of Commerce and §6 to the Secretary of State, so readers can't tell which official makes the capability determination.
2. **§2(1)(B):** "Substantially exceeding human performance," "most cognitive tasks," and "human" have no benchmark, test, or evidentiary standard, and the bill sets no procedure, notice, or appeal for a determination. This is the provision most exposed to vagueness and nondelegation challenges, and a developer can't know in advance whether its system is covered.
3. **§2(1)(B):** The bill doesn't say whether a determination applies only going forward or can reach systems already deployed. Combined with the willfulness requirement in §5(b), this raises a fair-notice problem.
4. **§2(1)(A):** The phrase "single training run" lets developers split a project into several runs (pretraining, post-training, fine-tuning, distillation), each below 10^26 operations, while the total exceeds it. The bill doesn't say whether compute is aggregated across a model's lineage.
5. **§2(1)(A):** The bill says "integer or floating-point operations" but doesn't specify precision, counting method, or verification. Different conventions can move a model above or below the threshold.
6. **§2(1)(A):** A compute threshold doesn't measure capability, and algorithmic efficiency can reduce the compute needed for a given capability over time. The bill has no mechanism to update the 10^26 figure, and the only capability-based trigger, (B), depends on the Secretary acting.
7. **§2(2):** "U.S. person" excludes corporations organized abroad, including foreign subsidiaries of U.S. companies, which could train outside §3(a) entirely. It also excludes permanent residents abroad and unincorporated entities such as LLCs and partnerships, and "State" is undefined (so it's unclear whether D.C. or the territories count).
8. **§2(3):** "Advanced AI chips" and "data center" are undefined. The 50,000-chip threshold is also unconnected to the 10^26 compute trigger, since a 10^26 run can be completed on fewer chips over a longer period.
9. **§2 (general):** "Person" is not defined in the Act, leaving open whether governments, nonprofits, and unincorporated groups are covered.

**Prohibition (§3)**

10. **§3(a):** "Train," "develop," and "deploy" are undefined. It's unclear whether "deploy" covers API access, open-weight release, or internal use, and the bill doesn't address possessing, transferring, or using covered systems trained abroad.
11. **§3(a):** The prohibition reaches only persons in the United States and U.S. persons. Non-U.S. persons abroad are outside it, leaving that gap to the non-binding §6. The bill also doesn't prohibit supplying compute, chips, cloud services, or financing for covered training, and doesn't address aiding, abetting, or conspiracy. Remote training (for example, a U.S. user directing a cloud job located abroad) is also unaddressed.
12. **§3(b):** The grandfather clause turns on when training "began," which is undefined. A run started before enactment could continue indefinitely, and the bill doesn't require registration of grandfathered systems or limit their deployment. It's also unclear whether post-enactment fine-tuning, continued training, or successor checkpoints of a grandfathered system are exempt.
13. **§3(c):** "Safety evaluation" is undefined, and "for the purpose of" is a subjective test. The exemption has no time limit, no bar on commercial deployment, no reporting, and no containment requirements, and it could be read to cover the training run itself, which evaluation may require.

**Registry (§4)**

14. **§4(a):** The registry applies only to facilities with more than 50,000 advanced AI chips, but a covered system can be trained on fewer. Much of the activity the prohibition targets may fall outside the registry.
15. **§4(b):** "Describing the computing workloads" is vague. The bill doesn't require operators to identify models, training runs, compute used, or whether any covered system was involved, and reports are self-certified with no audit or inspection authority.
16. **§4:** No penalty is tied to failing to register or report, since §5 penalizes only violations of §3. The bill also has no public-disclosure or confidentiality rules.

**Penalties (§5)**

17. **§5(a):** The bill doesn't name the agency or official who enforces the civil penalty, the forum, or the investigative powers available. It also gives no authority to halt ongoing training, so a per-day fine may be cheaper for a developer than stopping a multi-month run.
18. **§5(a):** The unit of violation is undefined. "Each day" could mean per model, per run, per facility, or per day of operation, which would make penalty calculations contestable.
19. **§5(a) and §8:** The phrase "for any violation occurring on or after the date of enactment" conflicts with the 180-day effective date in §8, which governs the whole Act. It's unclear whether this creates liability before §3 takes effect.
20. **§5(b):** "Willfully" is undefined, and the bill doesn't address individual versus corporate liability or attempt and conspiracy. Because imprisonment applies only to natural persons and the fine defaults to title 18, the criminal fine for a company may be small relative to its compute spending.

**International agreement (§6)**

21. **§6:** "Shall seek to negotiate" creates no obligation to reach an agreement, and the bill sets no consequence or reporting requirement if negotiations fail. "Equivalent" is undefined, and nothing in the domestic prohibition changes if an agreement is reached.

**Duration (§7)**

22. **§7(a) vs. §7(b):** The two conflict. §7(b) terminates the entire Act 10 years after enactment whether or not Congress has acted under §7(a), so the open-ended duration in (a) is effectively capped. The explainer should clarify which provision controls.
23. **§7(a):** The bill doesn't require the National Academies report to be commissioned, sets no deadline, and gives no content criteria. "Developed safely" is undefined: it's unclear whether the standard is applied to all covered systems or to specific ones, and what level of safety is required.
24. **§7(a):** It's unclear whether a joint resolution itself lifts the prohibition or only records a finding, whether it requires presidential signature, and whether it needs implementing legislation. The bill also includes no expedited procedure for such a resolution.

**Effective date (§8)**

25. **§8 and §3(b):** The grandfather clause keys to enactment, not the effective date, so a run begun during the 180-day window is neither grandfathered nor prohibited at its start. It's unclear whether a run that began during the window and continues past day 180 violates §3(a), and the gap creates an incentive to rush runs before the prohibition takes effect.

**Whole-Act issues**

26. **General:** As provided, the bill has no general rulemaking authority, no designated enforcement agency, no appropriations, no severability clause, no judicial review provision, and no preemption or savings clause for state law. Without a severability clause, it's unclear whether the rest of the Act survives if §2(1)(B) is struck.
27. **Likely litigation targets:** Based on the text, the most likely challenges are vagueness (due process) against §2(1)(B) and §5(b), nondelegation against the Secretary's determination power, and compelled-disclosure challenges against the quarterly reports in §4. These are arguments opponents could raise, not conclusions; the explainer should attribute them as such.

**Notes for the explainer**

- **Top priorities:** Items 4, 7, 11, 12, 17, and 22 are probably the most important for readers. They identify the widest gaps (split training runs, foreign subsidiaries, extraterritorial reach, grandfathering, enforcement, and the sunset conflict).
- **Separate drafting defects from policy choices.** The grandfather clause (§3(b)), the safety-evaluation carve-out (§3(c)), the 10-year sunset (§7(b)), and the compute threshold are likely deliberate tradeoffs. Framing them as "loopholes" could mislead readers and sounds advocacy-driven. Describing them as design choices with stated tradeoffs fits our nonpartisan standard better. Items 22 and 19 are clear drafting conflicts; items 2, 4, and 10 are ambiguities that can be fixed without changing the policy.
