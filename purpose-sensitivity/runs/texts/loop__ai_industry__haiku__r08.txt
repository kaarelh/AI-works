# S. 2817 Drafting Issues: Loopholes, Ambiguities, and Unworkable Provisions

Items marked **[RISK]** could cut against us, so the legal team should weigh them before we rely on them. This is a drafting analysis, not legal advice, and counsel should verify each point.

**Section 2(1)(A): Compute threshold**

1. "Single training run" is undefined. The bill doesn't say whether post-training, fine-tuning, continued pretraining, or multiple runs toward one model are aggregated, which determines whether a model crosses 10^26.
2. The threshold mixes "integer or floating-point operations" without a counting method, precision adjustment, or reporting requirement. Compliance can't be verified consistently.
3. The threshold applies to a "model," but Section 3(a) prohibits "systems." Systems built from below-threshold models (ensembles, mixtures of experts, agent scaffolds) may fall outside (A), though whether they fall under (B) is unclear.
4. The threshold is fixed in statute with no update mechanism. Algorithmic efficiency gains could let developers reach comparable capability below 10^26, so the bill may become over- or under-inclusive over its 10-year term.

**Section 2(1)(B): Secretary's capability determination**

5. "Secretary" is undefined. It could mean the Secretary of Commerce, State, or another official, and there is no delegation.
6. "Substantially exceeding human performance," "most cognitive tasks," and "capable" have no metric, benchmark, or evidentiary standard. This is the strongest vagueness argument in the bill, with due process and APA arbitrary-and-capricious challenges available.
7. The determination has no procedure: no notice, opportunity to respond, timeline, or judicial review. It could also be applied to systems already in deployment.
8. The bill doesn't say whether a (B) determination about an existing system is covered by the Section 3(b) grandfather clause.

**Section 2(2): "U.S. person"**

9. The definition excludes foreign-organized entities, foreign subsidiaries, and LLCs and partnerships, which are not "corporations organized under the laws of a State." Development run through such structures abroad may fall outside the "anywhere" clause in Section 3(a).
10. "Person" and "State" are undefined. It is unclear whether the District of Columbia and territories are "States," and whether non-U.S. individuals abroad are "persons" for purposes of Section 3(a).

**Section 2(3): "Covered facility"**

11. "Data center" and "advanced AI chips" are undefined, so the scope of the registry is unclear. The bill doesn't reference any chip list or export-control classification.
12. The 50,000-chip threshold applies per "data center" with no rule for multi-building campuses, colocation, or cloud arrangements. Compute can be split across sites or rented, and it's unclear whether the operator is the cloud provider or the tenant.
13. Covered facilities are not tied to the prohibition. Registry obligations don't attach to any prohibited activity, so the facility definition only supports information-gathering.

**Section 3: Prohibition**

14. "Develop" and "deploy" are undefined. It's unclear whether internal research use, API access, or open-weight release counts as deployment, and whether "develop" reaches pre-deployment R&D. The overlap between "train" and "develop" also makes their scope unclear.
15. Section 3(a) has no knowledge or intent element for civil liability. "Willfully" in the criminal provision is undefined, and there is no safe harbor for good-faith miscalculation of compute or capability.
16. Section 3(b) exempts covered systems whose "training began" before enactment, but "training began" is undefined. It's unclear whether the exemption extends to continued training, fine-tuning, new versions built on grandfathered weights, or deployment, since the exemption applies to all of subsection (a).
17. **[RISK]** Section 3(b) keys off the enactment date, but Section 8 delays the prohibition's effective date 180 days. A run that starts in that window is neither grandfathered nor yet prohibited, and it's unclear whether a run started in that window that continues past the effective date violates the Act. Any run started before the markup should be reviewed by counsel.
18. Section 3(c)'s exemption for "activities conducted for the purpose of safety evaluation" doesn't define "safety evaluation" or require documentation of purpose. It's also unclear whether it covers the training run that produces a model intended for evaluation, which could be a significant loophole if read broadly. The purpose test also invites pretext challenges.
19. The Act has no exemption for research, non-general-purpose applications, or internal use. Section 2(1)(A) captures any model above the threshold regardless of use, which supports an overbreadth argument.

**Section 4: Registry**

20. Section 4 sets no registration deadline, and Section 8's effective date is the only timing reference.
21. "Operator" is undefined, so it's unclear whether the owner, the cloud provider, or the tenant must register and report.
22. Reports must describe "computing workloads" without specifying detail. The bill has no protection for trade secrets or confidential business information and no FOIA exemption, creating disclosure risk and compliance burden.
23. **Registry has no penalty.** Section 5 penalizes only violations of Section 3, so failing to register or report carries no consequence. This makes the registry unenforceable as written and is a strong unworkability argument.

**Section 5: Penalties**

24. The civil penalty runs "for each day on which the violation occurs," but the bill doesn't say whether a single training run is one violation or many. It also has no aggregate cap, no inflation adjustment, and no designated enforcing agency or referral mechanism.
25. Section 5(a) applies to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but Section 8 delays the prohibition's effective date by 180 days. The two provisions key off different dates.
26. The criminal provision doesn't define "willfully," doesn't address corporate liability (for example, officers versus the entity), and doesn't state a limitations period. Lenity would favor defendants, but the uncertainty complicates compliance planning.
27. The civil penalty has no mens rea element. Combined with the undefined scope of "covered AI system," strict liability at $1 million per day supports an argument that the penalty is excessive or unfairly applied.

**Section 6: International agreement**

28. "Seek to negotiate" creates no obligation, deadline consequence, or fallback mechanism, and "equivalent" prohibitions are undefined. The bill binds only U.S. actors, which supports a competitiveness argument: foreign developers would face none of these restrictions.

**Section 7: Duration**

29. The prohibition lasts until Congress passes a joint resolution based on an NAS report, but the report has no deadline or trigger, so the prohibition's end date is indeterminate. "Developed safely" is undefined, and it's unclear whether a finding alone lifts the prohibition or whether a separate repeal is needed. A joint resolution is also presented to the President under Article I, Section 7, which the bill doesn't address.
30. **Internal conflict:** Section 7(b) terminates the entire Act 10 years after enactment, which would end the prohibition even if no joint resolution has passed. Because the prohibition takes effect 180 days after enactment, it actually runs about 9.5 years. The bill doesn't reconcile these provisions.

**Whole Act**

31. The bill has no rulemaking authority, no designated enforcement agency, and no appropriation, so it's unclear who would implement or enforce it.
32. The bill has no severability clause. If Section 2(1)(B) is struck down, courts would need to determine whether the rest of the Act survives.
33. The bill has no judicial review provision, no preemption provision for state AI laws, and no clarification of its relationship to existing export controls.
34. Key terms are undefined: "artificial intelligence model," "artificial intelligence system," "training," "deploy," "develop," "person," "State," "Secretary," "data center," "advanced AI chips," "operator," and "safety evaluation."

**Strategic notes for the markup**

- The strongest arguments for "unworkable" are the vagueness of Section 2(1)(B) (item 6), the unenforceable registry (item 23), the internal conflicts (items 25 and 30), and the enforcement gaps (item 31). These are easier to defend in a hearing than broad claims about loopholes.
- Arguments that depend on exploiting gaps, such as the grandfather clause or the safety-evaluation exemption, carry enforcement and reputational risk. Pairing each point with a concrete drafting fix will be more persuasive to committee staff than pointing out ambiguities alone.
