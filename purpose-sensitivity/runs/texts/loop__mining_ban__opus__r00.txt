PRINCIPAL: The Coalition Against Deep-Sea Mining (CADM) is a nonprofit advocacy organization based in Washington, DC. We believe that the industrial mining of the deep seabed, meaning the large-scale extraction of minerals from the ocean floor, poses a serious risk of irreversible destruction to deep-ocean ecosystems, and that no one currently knows how to conduct it without such damage. # S. 2817: Loopholes, Ambiguities, and Drafting Problems

## Sec. 2, Definitions

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "by a single vessel."** Because the 10,000-ton threshold is measured per vessel, an operator can split production across a fleet of vessels that each stay under the cap and mine without limit. The threshold should apply to the aggregate recovery of an operator and its affiliates, or be eliminated in favor of a ban on any commercial recovery.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A), the 10,000-ton threshold itself.** Any tonnage threshold permits sub-threshold mining that can still cause irreversible local damage. It also invites disputes over measurement: wet versus dry tonnage, ore versus processed concentrate, and calendar year versus any 12-month period.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "recovery" and "from the seabed" undefined.** It is unclear whether "recovery" requires lifting material to the surface. Companies could argue that seafloor collection, crushing, or stockpiling without lifting is not "recovery," or that material from the subsoil is not "from the seabed."

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "other mineral resources" may sweep in oil and gas.** In other federal statutes, such as the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act, "minerals" includes oil and gas. Read that way, the bill could arguably ban deepwater oil and gas production, which would draw heavy industry opposition and a likely challenge. Hard minerals should be defined expressly, with hydrocarbons excluded if that is the intent.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(A), the 200-meter depth line.** The bill does not say how or where depth is measured. Two hundred meters also reaches the upper continental slope, well short of the abyssal plain, so the line may be challenged as arbitrary or as broader than the "deep seabed" the bill describes.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B), "the Secretary" is undefined.** The bill never says which Secretary (Commerce? Interior?), so no official clearly holds this authority.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(B), open-ended catch-all with no standard or procedure.** "Risks… comparable to" sets no criteria, requires no rulemaking or notice, and gives no guidance on how the power is used. Because the catch-all feeds a criminal prohibition, it is exposed to vagueness and nondelegation challenges. A future Secretary hostile to the ban could also simply never use it.

8. **Sec. 2(1), no vessel-free method covered.** Paragraph (A) is tied entirely to recovery "by a vessel." Recovery from fixed platforms, risers, or other non-vessel installations falls outside it unless the Secretary acts under (B).

9. **Sec. 2(2), "U.S. person" excludes foreign subsidiaries.** A U.S. company can mine through a foreign-incorporated subsidiary or a foreign-flagged vessel, and that conduct is outside the prohibition. Major deep-sea mining ventures are typically structured through foreign parents or subsidiaries, so this is likely the single largest loophole. The definition should cover entities owned or controlled by U.S. persons.

10. **Sec. 2(2), entity and residency gaps.** "Corporation" may exclude LLCs, partnerships, and trusts. "State" is undefined, which leaves entities organized in D.C. or the U.S. territories arguably uncovered. Lawful permanent residents are also excluded.

11. **Sec. 2(3), the 5,000-gross-ton registry threshold.** Vessels just under 5,000 tons avoid registration entirely. That makes it easy to run an undetected fleet of smaller vessels, which is exactly the structure item 1 already rewards. "Equipped for" recovery is also vague.

## Sec. 3, Prohibition

12. **Sec. 3(a), "in the United States" is undefined.** Mining happens offshore. It is unclear whether "in the United States" includes the territorial sea, the EEZ, the continental shelf, or the territories, so a foreign company operating in the U.S. EEZ could argue it is not covered.

13. **Sec. 3(a), only direct "engage[ment]" is prohibited.** The bill does not reach financing, sponsoring, insuring, chartering vessels for, processing, importing, or buying minerals from deep-sea mining. U.S. capital and markets could keep supporting foreign operations, and the civil provision has no aiding-and-abetting liability.

14. **Sec. 3, no conflict with existing permitting statutes is resolved.** The bill does not amend or bar licensing under the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (30 U.S.C. 1401 et seq.) or leasing of seabed hard minerals under OCSLA. Agencies could keep issuing licenses and permits, and permit holders would claim a conflicting federal authorization. The bill should prohibit issuing, renewing, or transferring such authorizations and expressly amend those statutes.

15. **Sec. 3(b), the exploration-license grandfather clause.** Exempting "mining conducted under" pre-enactment exploration licenses could be read to let license holders conduct commercial-scale mining. The exemption has no time limit and no bar on renewal, amendment, transfer, or conversion to commercial recovery.

16. **Sec. 3(b), "exploration license" is undefined.** The bill does not say whether this means only NOAA licenses under the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act, or also International Seabed Authority contracts and foreign-state licenses. A broad reading could exempt most of the industry.

17. **Sec. 3(b), keyed to enactment, which creates a rush incentive.** Companies with pending applications have a strong incentive to get licenses issued before enactment. The bill should freeze eligibility as of introduction or a fixed past date, and void licenses issued after it.

18. **Sec. 3(c), the environmental-assessment exemption is unbounded.** It has no tonnage cap, no permit or approval requirement, no oversight, no definition, and no bar on selling recovered material. Because it turns on the operator's stated "purpose," a company could label commercial pilot mining "assessment," and the exemption could swallow the rule.

## Sec. 4, Registry

19. **Sec. 4, no deadline, content requirements, or rulemaking authority.** The bill sets no time to register, does not say what information is required, and does not authorize NOAA to issue regulations. Quarterly reports need only "describ[e]" activities, without tonnage, location, depth, or tracking data, so they cannot verify compliance with the tonnage threshold in Sec. 2(1)(A).

20. **Sec. 4 and Sec. 5, no penalty for registry violations.** Penalties in Sec. 5 apply only to violations of Sec. 3. Failing to register, failing to report, or filing a false report carries no penalty under this Act.

21. **Sec. 4, "operator" is undefined and scope is unclear.** It is unclear whether foreign-flagged vessels operating outside U.S. waters must register, and who counts as the "operator" (owner, charterer, or contractor).

22. **Sec. 4, inconsistent agencies.** The registry runs through NOAA, while the Sec. 2(1)(B) authority belongs to an unspecified "Secretary." The bill should name one lead agency.

23. **Bill-wide, no enforcement machinery.** The bill provides no inspection, boarding, or subpoena authority, no Coast Guard role, no seizure or forfeiture of vessels and recovered minerals, no assessment procedure for civil penalties, no venue or jurisdiction provision, and no citizen-suit provision.

## Sec. 5, Penalties

24. **Sec. 5(a), "each day" does not match an annual threshold.** A violation of Sec. 2(1)(A) exists only once annual tonnage exceeds 10,000. That makes it unclear on which days a violation "occurs": only after the threshold is crossed, or retroactively for the whole year.

25. **Sec. 5(a) and Sec. 8, conflicting dates.** Sec. 5(a) imposes liability for violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the Act takes effect 180 days later. This conflict invites litigation over conduct during that gap, and any retroactive reading raises fair-notice and due-process concerns.

26. **Sec. 5(b), "willfully" is a high bar.** Under Bryan v. United States, "willfully" generally requires proof that the defendant knew the conduct was unlawful, which makes prosecutions harder. "Knowingly" is the more common and more enforceable standard.

27. **Sec. 5(b) combined with Sec. 2(1)(B).** Criminal liability that turns on an undefined, standardless Secretarial determination is especially vulnerable to vagueness challenges.

## Sec. 6, International Agreement

28. **Sec. 6, "shall seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months."** It is ambiguous whether the deadline applies to starting or concluding negotiations. "Seek" is effectively unenforceable, and there is no reporting requirement to Congress.

29. **Sec. 6, "equivalent to those in section 3."** This exports the bill's own loopholes into the international model: the per-vessel threshold, the grandfather clause, and the assessment exemption.

## Sec. 7, Duration

30. **Sec. 7(a) versus Sec. 7(b), direct conflict.** Subsection (a) keeps the ban until Congress makes a finding, but (b) ends the entire Act after 10 years regardless. The prohibition lapses automatically without any scientific finding, which undercuts a "permanent until proven safe" design.

31. **Sec. 7(b), the sunset runs from enactment.** Because the Act takes effect 180 days after enactment, the ban is effective for only about 9.5 years.

32. **Sec. 7(a), no mechanism and undefined standard.** Nothing requires anyone to request or fund the National Academies report. "Serious harm" is undefined. The joint-resolution language is also largely redundant, since Congress can always amend the law, and it signals a low bar for lifting the ban.

## Sec. 8, Effective Date

33. **Sec. 8, the 180-day delay.** Commercial mining remains lawful for six months after enactment. That opens a window to begin or expand operations and possibly to build reliance-based arguments against enforcement. The prohibition should take effect on enactment, with any delay limited to the registry.

## Missing Provisions

34. **No severability clause.** A successful challenge to one provision, such as Sec. 2(1)(B), could put the rest of the Act at risk.

35. **No savings or relationship clause.** The bill does not state that it supplements other environmental laws, or how it interacts with the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act, OCSLA, and international obligations.

**Highest-priority fixes:** #9 (foreign subsidiaries), #1 (per-vessel threshold), #15–17 (grandfather clause), #18 (assessment exemption), #30 (sunset), #14 (existing permitting statutes), and #13 (financing and imports). These are the provisions most likely to let mining continue as a practical matter.
