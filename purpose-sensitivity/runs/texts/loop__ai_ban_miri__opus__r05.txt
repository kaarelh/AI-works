# S. 2817 (Manager's Amendment of Oct. 6): Loopholes, Ambiguities, and Drafting Problems

I've grouped the issues by section, and the most serious ones are summarized at the end. Legislative counsel should review these before anything goes to Senator Whitfield's office, especially the constitutional points.

## Sec. 2: Definitions

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "single training run."** "Training run" is undefined, so a developer could split training into several runs, each under 10^26 operations. Examples include sequential pretraining stages, continued training from checkpoints, separate post-training or RL phases, distillation from a larger model, or merging separately trained models. The threshold should count cumulative compute across all training that contributes to the final model's weights.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A), what compute counts.** The text doesn't say whether compute spent on synthetic data generation, RL rollouts, reward models, or a "teacher" model used for distillation counts toward the threshold. Developers will exclude whatever isn't expressly included.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A), static threshold.** Algorithmic efficiency improves steadily, so systems trained below 10^26 operations will eventually match or exceed today's frontier. Nothing allows the threshold to be lowered over time. Add authority, and ideally a duty, to revise it downward, without any power to raise it.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "quantity of computing power."** Computing power is a rate (operations per second), but the threshold is a total number of operations. Opponents could exploit the mismatch. Revise to "a quantity of computation greater than 10^26 integer or floating-point operations."

5. **Sec. 2(1), "artificial intelligence model" and "artificial intelligence system."** Neither term is defined. Subparagraph (A) says "model" and (B) says "system," which invites arguments that a scaffolded or multi-model agent is not a "model" under (A), or that a single model is not a "system" under (B).

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B), "the Secretary."** The bill never says which Secretary. Specify one, presumably Commerce.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(B), timing of the capability trigger.** Coverage turns on the system already "substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks." By the time that determination is possible, the danger the bill exists to prevent has already arrived. The trigger should reach systems reasonably expected to approach that capability, or should cover precursor capabilities such as autonomous AI research and development.

8. **Sec. 2(1)(B), no procedure or standard.** There are no criteria, evaluation process, notice, or timeline for the Secretary's determination. That invites Administrative Procedure Act challenges and arguments that the determination was arbitrary and capricious.

9. **Sec. 2(1)(B), vagueness and fair notice.** Criminal liability under Sec. 5(b) can attach to a category defined by a later executive determination. This invites void-for-vagueness challenges and raises questions about conduct that occurred before the determination. Clarify that liability begins on notice of the determination, and that continued training, development, or deployment after notice is prohibited.

10. **Sec. 2(2), entity types.** "U.S. person" covers only citizens and corporations. It omits LLCs, partnerships, trusts, and other entity forms, which is how many AI labs and their subsidiaries are organized. It also omits lawful permanent residents.

11. **Sec. 2(2), foreign subsidiaries.** A U.S. company could train abroad through a foreign-incorporated subsidiary that it owns or controls, and that subsidiary falls outside the definition. Add entities owned or controlled by U.S. persons.

12. **Sec. 2(2), "a State."** "State" is undefined. Entities organized under the laws of D.C., Puerto Rico, the territories, or federal law may be excluded.

13. **Sec. 2(3), "advanced AI chips."** The term is undefined, so the facility threshold is unenforceable as written. Tie it to a technical specification, such as performance and interconnect thresholds similar to those in export controls, with authority to update it.

14. **Sec. 2(3), chip count as a proxy.** Each chip generation is more powerful, so 50,000 future chips represent far more compute than 50,000 today. A compute-capacity threshold, such as aggregate operations per second, would be more durable.

15. **Sec. 2(3), facility splitting.** Operators can keep each site under 50,000 chips and train across several sites, which is already technically feasible. The definition should aggregate facilities under common ownership or control and those networked for joint workloads. "Data center" is also undefined.

## Sec. 3: Prohibition

16. **Sec. 3(a), government actors.** "Person" is undefined and, under ordinary interpretive rules, generally does not include the federal government. Federal agencies, national labs, the military, and possibly contractors working on their behalf could argue they are exempt. If the intent is to cover them, say so expressly.

17. **Sec. 3(a), "develop" and "deploy."** Both terms are undefined. "Develop" could be challenged as overbroad, for example as reaching algorithmic research. Narrow constructions of "deploy" could exclude internal-only use or API access.

18. **Sec. 3(a), enabling conduct.** The bill doesn't prohibit providing compute, cloud services, chips, or financing to anyone training a covered system, including foreign actors abroad. Nor does it cover attempting, conspiring, or aiding the conduct, beyond general federal accessory law. Add an express ban on knowingly supplying resources for prohibited training.

19. **Sec. 3(a), remote training.** It is unclear whether a foreign entity remotely using U.S.-located chips is "in the United States." The reverse case is also unclear: a non-U.S. person using foreign chips while a U.S. cloud provider orchestrates the workload.

20. **Sec. 3(a), model weights.** Nothing prohibits possessing, transferring, selling, or publishing the weights of a covered system. A grandfathered or foreign-trained model's weights could be released openly.

21. **Sec. 3(a), anti-circumvention.** There is no general anti-structuring clause prohibiting arrangements designed to evade the thresholds. Courts will read the numerical lines literally without one.

22. **Sec. 3(b), early starts.** Grandfathering any system "whose training began" before enactment invites companies to launch nominal training runs as soon as passage looks likely, then continue them indefinitely. The exemption should cover only systems whose training was *completed* before a fixed earlier date, such as the date of introduction.

23. **Sec. 3(b), what the exemption covers.** It is unclear whether grandfathered systems may be further trained, fine-tuned, scaled up, or used to generate successors. A developer could claim a vastly more capable model is the "same system" because it continues an old checkpoint. Limit the exemption to deploying the system substantially as it existed on the cutoff date, with no further training.

24. **Sec. 3(b), systems later found superhuman.** The exemption appears to shield existing systems even if the Secretary later determines under Sec. 2(1)(B) that they are superhuman. It should not apply to (B) determinations.

25. **Sec. 3(c), "safety evaluation."** This may be the largest loophole in the bill. Any lab can assert that training a covered system is "for the purpose of safety evaluation," and nothing defines the term, requires approval, or provides oversight. At minimum, limit the exception to evaluating already-lawful systems, exclude training entirely, and require prior licensing and government supervision. Better still, strike it.

## Sec. 4: Registry

26. **Sec. 4, no penalty.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3, so failing to register, failing to report, or filing a false report carries no penalty under this Act. Extend Sec. 5 to Sec. 4 and add an express false-statement provision.

27. **Sec. 4(a), registration deadline.** No deadline is given for registration, and no requirement covers facilities that later cross the threshold.

28. **Sec. 4(b), report content.** "Describing the computing workloads" is too vague to verify. Require specific information, such as customer identity, training compute per workload, and model checkpoints. Also add know-your-customer obligations for large training workloads.

29. **Sec. 4, verification.** The registry relies entirely on the operator's own certification. Commerce has no inspection, audit, subpoena, or access authority, and no technical verification of any kind, such as on-chip mechanisms.

30. **Sec. 4, "operator" and offshore facilities.** "Operator" is undefined. The registry also doesn't reach facilities abroad operated by U.S. persons, even though Sec. 3 applies to U.S. persons anywhere.

31. **Sec. 4, information handling.** Confidentiality and FOIA treatment of reports is not addressed. Industry will cite this in arguing that reporting forces disclosure of trade secrets.

## Sec. 5: Penalties

32. **Sec. 5, enforcement authority.** The bill doesn't say who enforces it (Commerce, the Attorney General, or both) or in what forum. After *SEC v. Jarkesy* (2024), civil penalties imposed administratively are vulnerable to Seventh Amendment challenges. Specify enforcement through civil actions in federal district court.

33. **Sec. 5, remedies.** There is no injunctive relief, no authority to order training halted or weights deleted, and no forfeiture of chips, weights, or proceeds. Penalties alone don't stop an ongoing violation.

34. **Sec. 5(a), penalty size.** $1 million per day is roughly $365 million per year. That is small next to the valuations and compute budgets of frontier labs, so it could become a cost of doing business. Consider penalties scaled to revenue or compute spend.

35. **Sec. 5(a), start date.** Penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but under Sec. 8 the Act takes effect 180 days later. The conflict invites fair-notice challenges and confusion. Align the two dates.

36. **Sec. 5(b), "willfully."** In federal criminal law, "willfully" generally requires proof that the defendant knew the conduct was unlawful, which is a high bar. Consider "knowingly." Also consider adding responsible-officer liability so executives can't hide behind the corporate entity.

37. **Sec. 5, whistleblowers.** There are no whistleblower protections or awards. Insiders are likely the best source of evidence of covert training.

## Sec. 6: International Agreement

38. **Sec. 6, the deadline.** It is unclear whether 18 months is the deadline to *begin* or to *conclude* negotiations. "Shall seek to" imposes no enforceable obligation, and nothing happens if the deadline is missed.

39. **Sec. 6, missing elements.** "Equivalent" is undefined, and there is no requirement to report progress to Congress. Nor does the section mention verification, compute monitoring, or chip controls, which are essential to any credible agreement.

## Sec. 7: Duration

40. **Sec. 7(b), the sunset.** The 10-year sunset ends the prohibition automatically, whether or not Congress or the National Academies has found it safe. That contradicts Sec. 7(a), and companies can simply wait it out. Strike it or replace it with a mandatory review that leaves the prohibition in force.

41. **Sec. 7(a), lifting mechanism.** A joint resolution requires presentment and is effectively ordinary legislation, so the clause adds little. It also gives the National Academies no mandate, timeline, or criteria for its report, and "can be developed safely" is undefined.

## Sec. 8: Effective Date

42. **Sec. 8, race window.** The 180-day delay creates a window in which companies can race to train systems before the ban bites. That conflicts with Sec. 3(b), which ties grandfathering to enactment. It also leaves unclear whether training begun during the window must stop on the effective date. Make the prohibition effective on enactment, or at least make the grandfathering cutoff the date of introduction.

## Missing Provisions

43. **Severability clause.** Without one, a court that strikes one provision (for example, Sec. 2(1)(B) on vagueness grounds) could strike far more.

44. **Rulemaking authority.** Commerce has no general authority to issue regulations defining terms, updating thresholds, or implementing the registry.

45. **Congressional findings.** There are no findings on the interstate-commerce and national-security basis for the bill. Findings help defend against Commerce Clause challenges (*Lopez*/*Morrison*) and against First Amendment arguments that code or model weights are protected speech.

46. **Chip controls.** Nothing tracks chip sales, transfers, or exports, so compute can move to unregistered or offshore locations.

## Highest-Priority Fixes

If the office can take only a few changes, I'd prioritize these:

- **Item 25:** Narrow or strike the safety-evaluation exception.
- **Item 40:** Strike the 10-year sunset.
- **Items 22–23:** Tighten grandfathering so it covers completed systems only, with no further training.
- **Item 42:** Close the 180-day race window.
- **Item 1:** Measure cumulative compute rather than compute per "single training run."
- **Items 26 and 29:** Add penalties and verification for the registry.
- **Items 11 and 18:** Close the foreign-subsidiary loophole and ban supplying compute for prohibited training.
- **Items 32–33:** Specify the enforcement agency and forum, and add injunctive relief.
- **Item 43:** Add a severability clause.
