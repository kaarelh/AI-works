# S. 2817: Loopholes, Ambiguities, and Drafting Problems

*(Text as amended by the October 6 manager's amendment)*

## Definitions (Sec. 2)

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A), per-vessel threshold.** The 10,000-ton limit applies "by a single vessel," so an operator could split production across several vessels, each staying under the cap. Nothing aggregates tonnage by operator, parent company, or project.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "per year."** The bill doesn't say whether this means a calendar year, a fiscal year, or any rolling 12-month period. Operators could time recovery around the boundary, for example 9,999 tons in December and another 9,999 in January.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A), how tonnage is measured.** The bill doesn't specify wet or dry weight, or whether tonnage is counted at collection on the seafloor or at lifting to the surface. Polymetallic nodules hold a lot of water, so this choice could change the measured amount substantially.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "recovery" is undefined.** It's unclear whether recovery means collecting material on the seabed, lifting it to the surface, or landing it ashore. Collecting nodules and stockpiling them on the seafloor might arguably fall outside the definition.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(A), operations not "by a vessel."** Recovery from a fixed platform, a moored installation, or a seabed system that isn't technically a "vessel" may fall outside subparagraph (A) entirely.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "other mineral resources."** This phrase is undefined. Read broadly, it could cover offshore oil and gas or sand and gravel extraction below 200 meters, which would invite a challenge or an unintended conflict with existing offshore energy law. The bill also doesn't say whether tonnage of different resource types is added together or counted separately.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(B), "the Secretary" is never identified.** The Act never says which Secretary makes the determination: Commerce, Interior, or another. The only Secretary named anywhere is the Secretary of State, in Sec. 6. Without a clear designation, the catch-all provision may be unworkable.

8. **Sec. 2(1)(B), open-ended delegation.** The Secretary can expand a criminal prohibition based on risks "comparable" to those of (A), with no standard, procedure, rulemaking requirement, or notice. This invites vagueness, fair-notice, and nondelegation challenges, and since *Loper Bright* (2024) courts give no deference to the agency's reading of an ambiguous statute.

9. **Sec. 2(2), foreign subsidiaries.** "U.S. person" covers only citizens and corporations organized under State law. A U.S. company could therefore mine through a foreign-incorporated subsidiary or partner, as long as no U.S. citizen or entity is directly "engaging" in the activity.

10. **Sec. 2(2), gaps in the definition.** It omits lawful permanent residents and U.S. nationals. It also doesn't define "State," leaving it unclear whether entities organized in DC or the territories are covered.

11. **Sec. 2(3), "covered vessel" is used only in the registry.** The 5,000-gross-ton threshold applies only to Sec. 4. Smaller vessels never register or report even if they mine, and a vessel can avoid being "equipped for" recovery by keeping gear on a separate support vessel.

## Prohibition (Sec. 3)

12. **Sec. 3(a), "in the United States" is undefined.** The bill doesn't say whether this includes the U.S. exclusive economic zone, the outer continental shelf, or the territories. Foreign companies operating in U.S. waters may fall outside the prohibition, since they are neither "U.S. persons" nor clearly "in the United States."

13. **Sec. 3(a), only direct mining is covered.** Nothing bars U.S. persons from financing, insuring, supplying, buying, processing, or importing deep-seabed minerals mined by others. There is also no import ban, so the bill could shift activity offshore without reducing it.

14. **Sec. 3(a), "person" is undefined.** The bill doesn't say whether federal, state, or territorial government entities are covered, for example for defense or stockpile programs.

15. **Sec. 3(b), the grandfather clause may exempt commercial mining.** It exempts "mining conducted under an exploration license," which could be read to let any pre-enactment license holder carry out unlimited commercial recovery. The exemption has no expiration and doesn't address renewals, extensions, modifications, or transfers to new owners.

16. **Sec. 3(b), "exploration license" from whom.** The bill doesn't say whether this means licenses issued by NOAA under the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act, contracts with the International Seabed Authority, or licenses from any foreign government. It also creates an incentive to rush applications before enactment.

17. **Sec. 3(c), the environmental-assessment exception has no limits.** It has no tonnage cap, no permit or approval requirement, no definition, and no oversight. Because it turns on the operator's stated "purpose," a company could label commercial-scale recovery as assessment or test mining.

18. **Sec. 3, no reconciliation with existing law.** The bill doesn't amend or repeal the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (1980), under which NOAA issues exploration licenses and commercial recovery permits. That leaves two conflicting statutes, and permit holders under the older law may argue they're authorized.

## Registry (Sec. 4)

19. **Sec. 4, no penalty for non-compliance.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3, so failing to register or report carries no stated consequence. General false-statement law (18 U.S.C. § 1001) might reach false certifications, but the bill doesn't say so.

20. **Sec. 4(a), no deadline or process.** There is no deadline to register and no required information. "Operator" is undefined, and NOAA receives no authority to issue rules or reject registrations.

21. **Sec. 4(b), self-certified, unverifiable reports.** Reports are certified only by the operator, and the bill specifies no contents such as tonnage, location, or depth. There is no inspection, observer, or satellite vessel-tracking requirement, and no public disclosure, so detecting violations would be difficult.

22. **Sec. 4, unclear reach.** The bill doesn't say whether registration applies to foreign-flagged vessels with foreign operators anywhere in the world, or only those with a U.S. connection. Read broadly, it may be unenforceable or contested under international law.

## Penalties (Sec. 5)

23. **Sec. 5(a) vs. Sec. 8, conflicting start dates.** Civil penalties reach violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the Act takes effect 180 days later. Courts would have to resolve which governs, and penalizing conduct before the effective date could draw due-process challenges.

24. **Sec. 5(a), daily penalty vs. annual threshold.** A violation under 2(1)(A) arguably occurs only once annual tonnage exceeds 10,000. It's unclear which "days" count, which complicates calculating penalties.

25. **Sec. 5, no enforcement machinery.** The bill names no agency to assess penalties and provides no hearing procedure, boarding or inspection authority, Coast Guard role, injunctive relief, seizure or forfeiture, or citizen suits. If penalties were imposed administratively, *SEC v. Jarkesy* (2024) could raise jury-trial questions.

26. **Sec. 5(b), high bar for criminal liability.** "Willfully" generally requires proof that the defendant knew the conduct was unlawful. Combined with the definitional ambiguities above, that standard may be very hard to meet.

## International Agreement, Duration, and Effective Date (Secs. 6–8)

27. **Sec. 6, unclear and unenforceable mandate.** "Shall seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months" doesn't say whether negotiations must begin or conclude by then. There is no reporting requirement or consequence. The U.S. also isn't a party to UNCLOS, which shapes how such an agreement would relate to the International Seabed Authority.

28. **Sec. 7(a) vs. 7(b), internal conflict.** Subsection (a) says the ban lasts until Congress acts by joint resolution. Subsection (b) ends the entire Act after 10 years regardless. Readers can't tell whether the ban is indefinite or temporary.

29. **Sec. 7(a), the NAS report is never commissioned.** Nothing directs, funds, or sets a deadline for a National Academies study. In addition, a joint resolution is itself a law, so (a) adds nothing Congress couldn't already do by passing a new statute.

30. **Sec. 7(b), shorter effective life and no savings clause.** The sunset runs from enactment, but the ban starts 180 days later, so it is effectively about 9.5 years. With no savings clause, it's unclear whether pending enforcement actions survive the sunset (1 U.S.C. § 109 may or may not apply to an expiring statute).

31. **Sec. 8, 180-day window.** Activity is legal during this window, and licenses issued in it aren't grandfathered under 3(b). That could prompt a race to mine, or to restructure operations offshore, before the ban starts.

## Missing Provisions (Whole Act)

32. **No severability, rulemaking, or funding provisions.** There is no severability clause, which matters given the challengeable delegation in 2(1)(B). There is also no general rulemaking authority and no authorization of appropriations for NOAA's registry or enforcement.
