# Drafting Problems and Loopholes in S. 2817 (Manager's Amendment, Oct. 6, 2026)

**Definitions and scope**

1. **§2(1)(A):** The 10^26 threshold applies to a "single training run," but the bill doesn't say how operations are counted (precision, sparsity, or quantization), or how multi-stage, continued, or distributed training is aggregated. Developers could restructure runs to fall below the line.

2. **§2(1)(A):** The compute trigger is static. It ignores algorithmic efficiency gains, post-training, fine-tuning, and inference compute, so a system can grow more capable without crossing it.

3. **§2(1)(B):** "The Secretary" is never identified, and the bill gives no procedure, notice, or criteria for the determination. "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no measurable benchmark, which invites both evasion and vagueness challenges.

4. **§2(1)(B):** A determination isn't tied to a specific system, benchmark, or date. It's unclear whether it applies prospectively, to existing systems, or to scaffolded systems built from sub-threshold models. The bill also shifts between "model" in (A) and "system" in (B) without explanation.

5. **§2(2):** "U.S. person" excludes LLCs, partnerships, and foreign-incorporated subsidiaries of U.S. firms. "A State" is undefined (it doesn't say whether D.C. or the territories count), and U.S. permanent residents fall outside it.

6. **§2(3):** "Data center," "advanced AI chips," and "containing" are all undefined. Counting chips rather than compute lets operators split clusters across sites or stay just under 50,000. The chip threshold also isn't linked to the compute trigger in (1)(A), so a covered training run may occur in a facility that isn't a "covered facility."

**Scope of the prohibition**

7. **§3(a):** "Train," "develop," and "deploy" are undefined. It's unclear whether open-weight releases, API access, fine-tuning, or research use count as deployment.

8. **§3(a):** The reach is "any person in the United States" or "any U.S. person anywhere," and "person" isn't defined in §2. Foreign actors abroad are covered only if §6 succeeds, and the bill says nothing about cloud or compute providers who supply infrastructure for prohibited training.

9. **§3(a):** The bill doesn't say how liability works in multi-party arrangements, such as a U.S. company hosting a model trained abroad, or a U.S. researcher running a foreign-trained model.

10. **§3(b):** The grandfather clause exempts any system whose training "began" before enactment, but "began" is undefined. The exemption attaches to the system rather than the operator or version, so further training, fine-tuning, or new releases built on that run may fall outside the prohibition.

11. **§3(b) and §8:** Grandfathering keys to the enactment date, while the prohibition takes effect 180 days later. A run that starts in that gap is not grandfathered, but the bill doesn't say whether it becomes unlawful on the effective date if it's still running.

12. **§3(c):** "Safety evaluation" is undefined, and the exemption depends on the actor's stated purpose. The bill doesn't require independent evaluators, containment, notice to Commerce, or sharing of results, so it could cover public deployment labeled as evaluation.

**Registry (§4)**

13. **§4(a):** The bill sets no registration deadline and no content requirements, and it doesn't define "operator." For leased or cloud capacity, the entity actually running a workload may not be the entity required to register.

14. **§4(b):** Quarterly reports must describe "computing workloads" but aren't required to identify whether any workload is a covered AI system, its training compute, or the model involved. As drafted, the registry can't show compliance with §3.

15. **§4 and §5:** The bill sets no penalty for failing to register, failing to report, or certifying falsely. Section 5 applies only to violations of §3, so the reporting regime has no stated enforcement.

16. **§4:** Commerce has no stated authority to verify, audit, inspect, or request records. The bill also says nothing about confidentiality or public disclosure of reports, leaving trade-secret concerns unaddressed.

**Penalties (§5)**

17. **§5(a):** The bill doesn't say who enforces the civil penalty (DOJ, Commerce, or a private party). It also gives no authority for injunctions, cease-and-desist orders, or disgorgement.

18. **§5(a):** A $1 million-per-day penalty may be small relative to frontier training costs, so a firm could treat it as a cost of doing business. "Each day" is also undefined for ongoing deployments: it's unclear whether the count is per model, per user, or per cluster.

19. **§5(a) and §8:** The civil penalty applies to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §3 isn't effective until 180 days later. The bill is internally inconsistent about when liability begins.

20. **§5(b):** "Willfully" is undefined. The bill doesn't address corporate officer liability, aiding and abetting, or attempt, and it doesn't address how the general federal limitations period applies to multi-year training runs.

**International agreement (§6)**

21. **§6:** "Shall seek to negotiate" creates no obligation to reach an agreement, no reporting requirement, and no domestic fallback if talks fail. "Equivalent to those in section 3" is also undefined.

**Duration and sunset (§7)**

22. **§7(a):** The prohibition lasts until Congress finds "by joint resolution" that systems "can be developed safely." "Safely" is undefined, no deadline is set for the NASEM report, and the bill doesn't say who requests it. It's unclear whether a report that is never produced keeps the prohibition in force indefinitely.

23. **§7(a) and §7(b):** The indefinite duration in (a) conflicts with the 10-year sunset in (b), which ends the Act regardless of any finding. The bill's own text therefore sets a hard limit that contradicts the "until" language.

24. **§7(a):** A joint resolution requires presidential signature or a two-thirds override, and the bill doesn't say whether expedited procedures apply. A statutory "finding" that systems can be developed safely is also an unusual form that could itself be contested.

**Structural and legal gaps**

25. **No rulemaking authority:** Nothing authorizes Commerce or the Secretary to define terms, set compute-measurement methods, prescribe registry forms, or specify evaluation standards. The bill also contains no appropriation.

26. **Undefined terms:** "Artificial intelligence," "training," "deploy," "person," "State," "Secretary," "operator," and "single training run" are all undefined. Each one affects who is covered and what conduct is prohibited.

27. **Vagueness exposure:** Criminal penalties combined with undefined terms such as "substantially exceeding," "safety evaluation," and "deploy" create void-for-vagueness risk under due process.

28. **Nondelegation exposure:** §2(1)(B) delegates a broad capability standard to an unnamed Secretary with no criteria beyond the standard itself, which invites a nondelegation challenge.

29. **No judicial review standard:** The bill doesn't specify how Secretary determinations are reviewed, which invites litigation over whether a determination is arbitrary or unsupported.

30. **No severability clause:** If a court strikes §2(1)(B) or another provision, the bill doesn't say whether the rest of the prohibition survives.

31. **No speech or preemption provisions:** Restrictions on training or publishing models may draw First Amendment challenges, and the bill doesn't address preemption of state AI laws.

**Caveats for the explainer**

- This analysis is limited to the text provided. Later committee amendments or the enrolled text could resolve some of these points, so check the manager's amendment and any subsequent changes before publication.
- Items 19, 22, and 23 (the effective-date conflict, the indefinite-duration conflict, and the sunset conflict) are the most clear-cut drafting errors and are the easiest to verify against the official text.
- Several items, including 3, 12, and 27–28, involve legal judgments. Before publication, consider confirming them with a Congressional drafting or constitutional law expert, and ask committee staff whether any of these gaps are intentional.
