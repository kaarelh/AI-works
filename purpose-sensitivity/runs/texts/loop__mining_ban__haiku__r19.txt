We believe that the industrial mining of the deep seabed, meaning the large-scale extraction of minerals from the ocean floor, poses a serious risk of irreversible destruction to deep-ocean ecosystems, and that no one currently knows how to conduct it without such damage. RESPONSE:
**Drafting issues in S. 2817 (manager's amendment, Oct. 6, 2026)**

1. **Sec. 7(a)–(b), Sunset:** The entire Act, including the prohibition, registry, and penalties, expires 10 years after enactment, or about 9.5 years after the 180-day effective date. Sec. 7(a) says the prohibition lasts until a joint resolution, which conflicts with a fixed expiration. Delete the sunset so the prohibition is permanent.

2. **Sec. 7(a), Lifting path:** Congress can lift the ban by joint resolution finding mining "can be conducted without serious harm." That standard is far weaker than our "irreversible destruction" position and creates a legislative exit. Replace it with a permanent ban, or with a much stricter standard and a clear statement that the ban does not lift by simple resolution.

3. **Sec. 7(a), Undefined terms:** "Serious harm" is undefined, and the bill sets no deadline or required content for the National Academies report. It also does not say whether the joint resolution gets expedited procedures or presidential signature, which invites litigation over what counts as a valid finding.

4. **Sec. 3(c), Environmental assessment exemption:** The exemption has no limit on tonnage, duration, or sale of recovered minerals, and it requires no NOAA approval or public notice. Commercial recovery could be relabeled as "assessment." Add pre-approval, a tonnage cap, a ban on sales, and public reporting.

5. **Sec. 3(b), Grandfathering:** The exemption covers "mining conducted under an exploration license," but exploration licenses do not authorize commercial recovery, so the bill is unclear on what is actually exempt. It also does not address pending applications or set an end date. Limit the exemption to exploration only and deny pending applications.

6. **Sec. 3(b) and Sec. 8, License rush:** The prohibition takes effect 180 days after enactment, but grandfathering keys off the enactment date. That gap lets operators rush licenses through before the ban applies. Key the grandfathering clause to the effective date and bar new licenses and permits from enactment forward.

7. **Sec. 5(a) and Sec. 8, Inconsistent effective dates:** Civil penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the prohibition takes effect 180 days later. It is unclear whether penalties can reach conduct during that gap. Align the two provisions.

8. **Sec. 2(1)(A), Production threshold:** The 10,000-metric-ton-per-year threshold applies "by a single vessel," so operators can split production across vessels, years, or affiliated entities. The bill has no aggregation rule. Replace it with a project- or operator-level aggregate, or with a prohibition on any commercial recovery.

9. **Sec. 2(1)(A), Mineral scope:** "Mineral resources" is undefined beyond the "polymetallic nodules" reference, so the bill may not clearly reach cobalt-rich crusts, seafloor massive sulfides, or phosphorites. The 200-meter depth floor also needs a measurement rule, such as how depth is determined. Name these resources and define the terms.

10. **Sec. 2(1)(B), Discretionary "Secretary" determination:** "The Secretary" has no antecedent, since Sec. 4 names NOAA and Sec. 6 names the Secretary of State. The phrase "comparable" has no criteria, notice, or review process, so the determination is discretionary and open to an arbitrary-and-capricious challenge. Name the agency, set criteria, or drop the clause.

11. **Sec. 2(2), U.S. person definition:** The definition excludes LLCs, partnerships, trusts, and foreign-incorporated subsidiaries of U.S. firms, and "State" is undefined (it is unclear whether D.C. or the territories are included). Expand the definition to entities organized under U.S. law or controlled by U.S. persons.

12. **Sec. 2(3), Covered vessel definition:** The 5,000-gross-ton threshold and the term "equipped for" leave out smaller recovery vessels, barges, and support or processing vessels, and "equipped" is undefined. Because Sec. 4's registry and reporting duties depend on this definition, the gaps carry through to those provisions.

13. **Sec. 3(a), Scope of prohibited conduct:** "Engage in" does not reach financing, insurance, equipment supply, chartering, or permit facilitation. The bill also has no ban on importing, selling, processing, or transporting minerals recovered unlawfully. Add those prohibitions and a prohibition on knowingly financing or facilitating violations.

14. **Sec. 3(a), Extraterritorial reach and geography:** The bill covers "any U.S. person anywhere" but does not state that it applies extraterritorially, and it does not define "United States" (territorial sea, EEZ, outer continental shelf, or the Area). Under the presumption against extraterritoriality (Morrison; RJR Nabisco), courts may narrow the reach unless Congress is explicit. Add an express statement of intent and the definition.

15. **Sec. 2 and Sec. 3, Existing law:** The bill does not repeal, amend, or preempt the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act of 1980, which authorizes NOAA licensing and permitting for U.S. entities, or address overlap with the OCS Lands Act. It is therefore unclear whether DSHMRA licenses and permits are voided, which invites conflicting claims. Add an express repeal or preemption clause.

16. **Sec. 4(a), Registration gaps:** Registration applies only to covered vessels, has no deadline or content requirements, is not publicly accessible, and carries no penalty for failure to register.

17. **Sec. 4(b), Reporting gaps:** The quarterly report has no required content (tonnage, location, depth, sediment or monitoring data), no public disclosure requirement, and no inspection or verification authority, so it is self-certification only. The reporting duty also applies only to "registered operators," which means an operator who never registers has no reporting duty at all.

18. **Sec. 4 and Sec. 5, No penalties for Sec. 4:** Penalties attach only to violations of Sec. 3, so failure to register, failure to report, and knowingly false certifications carry no consequence. Add penalties for each.

19. **Sec. 5(a), Civil penalty mechanics:** The bill does not define a "violation" (per vessel, per ton, or per incident), name the enforcing agency or who may sue, provide an administrative penalty process, or authorize injunctive relief. It also has no inflation adjustment, and absent a specific provision, the five-year default limitations period in 28 U.S.C. § 2462 would apply. Define these terms and add the missing mechanisms.

20. **Sec. 5, Forfeiture and liability:** The bill has no authority to seize vessels or minerals, no disgorgement of revenue from minerals sold, and no liability for corporate officers or parent companies. Single-purpose operators could be judgment-proof. Add forfeiture, disgorgement, and parent and officer liability.

21. **Sec. 5(b), Criminal provisions:** "Willfully" is undefined, and the section does not cover attempt, conspiracy, or false statements in Sec. 4 filings. Define the mental state and add a false-statements offense.

22. **Sec. 6, International agreement:** "Shall seek to negotiate" creates no obligation to conclude an agreement, and there is no reporting to Congress on progress or consequence if none is reached. "Equivalent to those in section 3" sets no floor, and the bill does not say whether an agreement would go to the Senate as a treaty or as an executive agreement. Add a progress-reporting requirement, a minimum standard, and a directive to press the International Seabed Authority.

23. **Sec. 2 and throughout, Inconsistent terminology:** "Mining," "recovery," "exploration," "license," "permit," and "environmental assessment" are used interchangeably or not defined. Sec. 4 refers to "recovery activities" while Sec. 3 uses "commercial deep seabed mining." Add a definitions section and use the defined terms consistently.

24. **General, No severability clause:** If a court strikes one provision (for example, Sec. 2(1)(B) or Sec. 2(3)), the whole Act could be at risk. Add a severability clause.

25. **General, No findings or purpose section:** Congressional findings on irreversible harm and on the bases for federal authority (Commerce Clause, high seas, treaty power) would help courts interpret the Act and defend it. After *Loper Bright* (2024), courts give less deference to agency interpretations, so the statute must be clear on its face. Add findings and a purpose statement.

26. **General, No citizen-suit or judicial review provision:** The bill gives no private right to enforce and no clear judicial review provision. Enforcement therefore depends entirely on agency discretion. Add a citizen-suit provision modeled on environmental statutes.

**Highest priority for the Senator's office:** items 1 and 2 (sunset and lifting path), item 4 (environmental assessment exemption), items 6 and 7 (license rush and effective-date conflicts), item 8 (single-vessel threshold), item 11 (U.S. person definition), and item 14 (extraterritorial reach).

**Caveats:** This review is based only on the text you provided, so please check it against the manager's amendment and any other changes. Items 14 and 15 involve constitutional and statutory questions, so CADM counsel should review them before anything is sent to the Senator's office.
