RESPONSE:
Working from the text as pasted (I can't check it against the official committee print), here's a section-by-section rundown. I've grouped items by section and ordered them roughly by how much they matter.

## Sec. 2(1)(A) — Compute threshold

1. **"In a single training run."** The threshold applies per run, so a developer can split training into several runs each under 10^26, then continue pretraining, fine-tune, or merge checkpoints. The bill doesn't aggregate compute across runs, across a model's lineage, or across cooperating entities.

2. **No counting method.** The bill never says how "operations" are measured: whether it includes RL post-training, synthetic data generation by other models, evaluation forward passes, or how sparse/mixture-of-experts compute is counted. Developers will choose the most favorable convention, and a prosecutor would have to litigate FLOP accounting.

3. **Static threshold with no update authority.** Algorithmic efficiency gains mean a model trained under 10^26 in 2028 may outperform one trained over it today. No agency has rulemaking authority to adjust the number, so the only backstop is the vague prong (B).

4. **Distillation and inference scaling fall outside it.** A smaller model trained under the threshold on a frontier model's outputs, or a model that reaches frontier capability through heavy inference-time compute, is not a "covered AI system" under (A). Neither "training" nor "model" is defined, so it's also unclear whether a distillation or fine-tuning pass is "training" at all.

## Sec. 2(1)(B) — Capability prong

5. **"The Secretary" is undefined.** Sec. 4 refers to the Department of Commerce and Sec. 6 to the Secretary of State, but Sec. 2 never says which Secretary makes the determination. A company could argue no one has authority to act.

6. **No process, standard, or timeline.** The bill doesn't say how a determination is made, what evidence it rests on, whether there's notice and comment, or whether it's reviewable. Until a Secretary acts, nothing is covered under (B), and the bill doesn't require anyone to act.

7. **Vagueness and fair-notice problems for a criminal statute.** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" is a subjective judgment with no benchmarks. Because Sec. 5(b) carries ten years' imprisonment, this prong invites a void-for-vagueness challenge, and a determination issued after training began raises retroactivity questions.

8. **Inconsistent terminology.** Prong (A) covers a "model," prong (B) a "system," and Sec. 3 prohibits training, developing, or deploying a "system." Whether a prohibited "system" is just the weights, or the weights plus scaffolding, tools, and deployment context, is unclear.

## Sec. 2(2) — U.S. person

9. **Foreign subsidiaries are not U.S. persons.** A corporation "organized under the laws of a State" doesn't include an Irish or Cayman subsidiary of an American company. A U.S. lab can train abroad through a foreign affiliate and fall outside the "anywhere" prong of Sec. 3(a).

10. **Non-corporate and non-State entities are excluded.** LLCs, partnerships, trusts, and entities organized in D.C. or U.S. territories (which are not "States") aren't "corporations organized under the laws of a State." Lawful permanent residents and other non-citizens living abroad are also excluded.

11. **Individual citizens are covered but their employers may not be.** A U.S. citizen engineer working for a foreign lab abroad is personally liable, while the lab itself is not. That is both an enforcement gap and an odd allocation of criminal exposure.

## Sec. 2(3) — Covered facility

12. **"Advanced AI chips" and "data center" are undefined.** No one can say which chips count, and the 50,000-chip threshold can be avoided by running more capable chips at lower counts, or by splitting hardware across several sites just under the line. Distributed training across multiple facilities is never addressed.

13. **The registry reaches landlords, not developers.** Only facility operators register. A model developer renting cloud capacity never appears in the registry, so the registry can't tie a training run to the entity that ran it.

## Sec. 3(a) — The prohibition

14. **"Train," "develop," and "deploy" are undefined.** Whether "deploy" covers releasing open weights, offering API access from abroad, or an end user running a foreign model is unknown. Read narrowly, a foreign lab serving U.S. customers from offshore servers is neither "in the United States" nor a "U.S. person" and isn't covered; read broadly, ordinary users of a foreign model could be violators.

15. **"Person" likely excludes the federal government.** Absent an express statement, statutes generally don't bind the sovereign, so federal agencies and contractors (acting as agents) could arguably train covered systems. The bill contains no explicit government or national-security carve-out either way, leaving that question to litigation.

16. **No obligation to stop or destroy.** The bill prohibits conduct but never requires that an existing covered model be shut down, that weights be deleted, or that a company cease serving it. Nothing addresses weights already distributed.

## Sec. 3(b) — Grandfather clause

17. **"Training began" is undefined and easily satisfied.** Running even a few steps before enactment could lock in an exemption, and the 180-day gap under Sec. 8 gives firms a clear window to start runs. The clause doesn't say whether a run must be continuous, or whether checkpoints from a grandfathered run can be further trained indefinitely.

18. **It exempts the "system," not just the run.** Because the exemption attaches to the covered system rather than to completed training, continued development and deployment of a grandfathered model appear permitted for the life of the Act, no matter how capable it becomes.

## Sec. 3(c) — Safety-evaluation exemption

19. **The exemption can swallow the rule.** "Activities conducted for the purpose of safety evaluation" has no definition, compute cap, oversight, or reporting requirement. A lab can train a frontier model and characterize the training as necessary to evaluate its safety.

20. **Subjective purpose test is hard to disprove.** Because the exemption turns on the actor's purpose, and criminal liability requires willfulness, a prosecutor would have to prove the stated safety rationale was pretextual.

## Sec. 4 — Registry

21. **No deadline, content requirements, or penalties.** The bill doesn't say when to register, what the registration must contain, or what happens if an operator doesn't register or files a false report. Sec. 5 penalties apply only to violations of Sec. 3, so the registry is effectively voluntary.

22. **Self-certified, unverified reports.** Reports "describing the computing workloads" are certified by the operator with no audit, inspection, subpoena, or verification authority. Nothing requires workload descriptions specific enough to detect a 10^26 run.

23. **No enforcement agency, investigative powers, or rulemaking authority anywhere in the Act.** The bill never says who investigates, who assesses civil penalties, or whether any agency can issue regulations, demand records, or inspect facilities. Criminal cases would default to DOJ, but civil enforcement has no identified home.

## Sec. 5 — Penalties

24. **Penalty start date conflicts with the effective date.** Sec. 5(a) applies to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but under Sec. 8 nothing is unlawful until 180 days later. A court will likely read the penalty as reaching only post-effective-date conduct, but the text invites a dispute.

25. **"Each day on which the violation occurs" is hard to apply.** Days are countable for a training run, but it's unclear how to count days for "development" or for a deployed model, and whether a model trained in violation is a continuing violation once deployed.

26. **The civil cap may be low relative to the stakes.** $1 million per day caps a 100-day run at $100 million for a firm spending billions on the run. Combined with the absence of injunctive relief, forfeiture of weights, or disgorgement, the civil regime could operate as a cost of doing business.

27. **High criminal mens rea plus vague terms.** "Willfully" generally requires knowledge that the conduct was unlawful. Given the undefined terms and the open-ended exemptions, a defendant can credibly claim a good-faith belief that a run was under threshold, grandfathered, or safety evaluation.

28. **No private right of action, whistleblower protection, or savings clause.** Nothing lets third parties sue, protects employees who report violations, or preserves pending enforcement actions after the Sec. 7(b) sunset.

## Sec. 6 — International agreement

29. **Purely aspirational.** "Seek to negotiate" imposes no result, and the 18-month deadline is ambiguous as to whether it governs starting or concluding negotiations. There is no consequence for failure, no reciprocity trigger, and no mechanism for the Act to adjust if other countries don't follow.

## Sec. 7 — Duration

30. **Subsections (a) and (b) contradict each other.** (a) says the prohibition lasts until Congress finds covered systems can be developed safely; (b) ends the entire Act after ten years regardless. The sunset also ends the registry and penalties, with no savings clause for violations that occurred before it.

31. **The lifting mechanism has no moving parts.** No one is directed or funded to commission the National Academies report, and no deadline applies. Meanwhile a joint resolution is itself a law requiring presentment, so (a) adds nothing Congress can't already do and arguably attempts to bind future Congresses.

## Sec. 8 and overall drafting

32. **Three different operative dates.** Enactment (grandfather clause and penalties), enactment plus 180 days (effective date), and the undefined date of any Secretary determination all govern different provisions. This creates a six-month window to start runs and makes it unclear what law applies when.

33. **No severability clause.** If a court strikes prong (B) of the definition or the criminal provision for vagueness, the absence of a severability clause lets a challenger argue the whole Act should fall.

34. **No definition of "artificial intelligence."** Every operative provision depends on an undefined term, leaving room to argue that a particular architecture or product isn't "artificial intelligence" at all.
