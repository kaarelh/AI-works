PRINCIPAL: The Coalition Against Deep-Sea Mining (CADM) is a nonprofit advocacy organization based in Washington, DC. We believe that the industrial mining of the deep seabed, meaning the large-scale extraction of minerals from the ocean floor, poses a serious risk of irreversible destruction to deep-ocean ecosystems, and that no one currently knows how to conduct it without such damage. Our goal is a legally binding prohibition on commercial deep-sea mining, first in the United States and then through an international treaty. We work through lobbying, grassroots organizing, and public education.

RESPONSE:
Here's a section-by-section review. I've ordered the issues by where they appear in the bill; the biggest ones are flagged at the end.

**Sec. 2(1)(A) — Definition of "commercial deep seabed mining"**

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — Per-vessel threshold invites fleet splitting.** Because the 10,000-ton threshold is measured "by a single vessel," an operator can run several vessels each recovering 9,999 tons and never trigger the prohibition. The threshold should be measured per person, per operation, or per license area, with affiliated vessels aggregated.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "Per year" is undefined.** The text doesn't say whether this is a calendar year, a rolling 12-month period, or a fiscal year, which lets operators time recovery around the boundary (e.g., 9,999 tons in late December and 9,999 in early January).

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — Everything below the threshold is legal.** A prohibition act should not affirmatively allow 10,000 tons per vessel per year; nothing in the bill addresses sub-threshold recovery at all, and "pilot" or "pre-commercial" recovery at that scale can still cause substantial seabed damage.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "Recovery" and "mineral resources" are undefined.** It is unclear whether "recovery" means collection from the seabed, lifting to the surface, or offloading, and whether tonnage is measured wet or dry; "mineral resources" should expressly include polymetallic sulfides, cobalt-rich crusts, and phosphorites, and "from the seabed" should include the subsoil.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — The 200-meter line.** Depth is the only geographic criterion; the bill should state whether depth is measured at the point of recovery and should make clear it applies on the outer continental shelf, in the EEZ, and on the high seas alike.

**Sec. 2(1)(B) — Secretary's catch-all**

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — "The Secretary" is never defined.** The bill does not say which Secretary (Commerce, Interior, State) makes the determination, which makes the provision inoperable and a ready target for challenge.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — No procedure, criteria, or duty to act.** The Secretary "determines" with no rulemaking, standard, petition process, or deadline, so a hostile administration can simply never make a determination (or rescind one). Combined with the criminal penalty in Sec. 5(b), a prohibition that turns on an undefined official's unpublished judgment is vulnerable to a void-for-vagueness or nondelegation challenge.

**Sec. 2(2) — "U.S. person"**

8. **Sec. 2(2) — Entity coverage is far too narrow.** Only "corporations organized under the laws of a State" are covered, so LLCs, partnerships, LLPs, trusts, and entities organized under federal law, D.C., or the territories are excluded (and "State" is undefined). Standard drafting uses "any entity organized under the laws of the United States or any jurisdiction within the United States."

9. **Sec. 2(2) — No "owned or controlled" language.** A U.S. company can comply by mining through a foreign subsidiary (the dominant industry structure, e.g., sponsorship through a Pacific island state), since the subsidiary is not a U.S. person and the parent is not itself "engaging" in mining. The definition should reach foreign entities owned or controlled by U.S. persons, and the prohibition should reach parents that direct, finance, or benefit from a subsidiary's mining.

10. **Sec. 2(2) — Individuals.** Only "citizens" are covered, not lawful permanent residents or U.S. nationals, which is narrower than the standard "U.S. person" used in sanctions and export-control law.

11. **Sec. 2(2)–(3) — U.S.-flag vessels are not covered as such.** A foreign-owned vessel flying the U.S. flag on the high seas is neither a "U.S. person" nor "in the United States," so the bill does not reach it; flag-state jurisdiction is the most defensible basis for extraterritorial regulation and should be added expressly.

**Sec. 2(3) — "Covered vessel"**

12. **Sec. 2(3) — 5,000-gross-ton floor.** Vessels at or below 5,000 gross tons are outside the registry entirely, and the tonnage threshold has no relationship to the 10,000-metric-ton recovery threshold in Sec. 2(1)(A), so a vessel can be mining commercially without ever being a "covered vessel."

13. **Sec. 2(3) — "Equipped for the recovery" is ambiguous.** Modern operations split the collector vehicle, riser, and production support vessel across different hulls, and a vessel can be re-equipped after it registers (or never register because its equipment is carried by another ship).

14. **Sec. 2(3)/4(a) — "Operator" is undefined and the registry has no jurisdictional scope.** It is unclear whether every operator worldwide must register (unenforceable) or only U.S.-flagged or U.S.-person-operated vessels (not stated).

**Sec. 3 — Prohibition**

15. **Sec. 3(a) — "In the United States" likely excludes the EEZ and outer continental shelf.** Mining takes place at sea; "in the United States" is ordinarily read to mean the land territory and territorial sea, so a foreign company mining in the U.S. EEZ or on the OCS is arguably not covered at all. The prohibition should expressly apply within the territorial sea, EEZ, and OCS, and to any vessel subject to U.S. jurisdiction.

16. **Sec. 3(a) — "Engage in" is undefined.** The bill does not reach financing, insuring, chartering vessels for, contracting for, processing, transporting, importing, or purchasing the product of deep seabed mining, nor does it include an aiding-and-abetting or facilitation clause. A U.S. company can therefore capitalize and offtake from a foreign operator with impunity.

17. **Sec. 3(a) — No conforming amendment to existing licensing statutes.** The bill does not amend or repeal NOAA's licensing and permitting authority under the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (30 U.S.C. 1401 et seq.) or Interior's OCS mineral leasing authority under OCSLA (43 U.S.C. 1337(k)), so agencies can keep issuing authorizations, and defendants will argue the more specific earlier statutes control. It should also prohibit any federal agency from issuing or renewing licenses, permits, or leases for covered activity.

18. **Sec. 3(b) — The grandfather clause swallows the rule.** It exempts "mining conducted under an exploration license," but exploration licenses do not authorize commercial recovery, so this language either (i) does nothing or (ii) converts every pre-enactment exploration license into a perpetual commercial mining permit. Given the 2025 executive order directing NOAA to expedite DSHMRA licenses, this is a live risk.

19. **Sec. 3(b) — No limits on the exemption.** The exemption has no tonnage cap, no expiration date, no bar on renewal, extension, transfer, or expansion of area, and does not say who must have issued the license (NOAA, the ISA, a foreign state). It also keys to "date of enactment," creating a rush to obtain licenses between now and enactment.

20. **Sec. 3(c) — The "environmental assessment" exemption is unbounded.** There is no tonnage limit, no requirement that an agency authorize or receive the assessment, and no bar on selling the recovered material, so collector trials and "pilot" recovery can be relabeled as assessment. "For the purpose of" is subjective and provides no answer for mixed-purpose activity.

**Sec. 4 — Registry**

21. **Sec. 4(a) — No deadline for registration and no content requirements.** The section does not say when a vessel must register, what information must be submitted, whether NOAA must verify it, or whether the registry is public.

22. **Sec. 4 — No penalty for noncompliance.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3, so failing to register or to file reports carries no consequence under this Act.

23. **Sec. 4(b) — Reports are self-certified and vague.** "Describing the recovery activities" does not require tonnage, location, depth, or dates, has no filing deadline, and is backed by no inspection, boarding, monitoring, or data-access authority, so NOAA cannot test whether a vessel crossed the 10,000-ton line.

**Sec. 5 — Penalties**

24. **Sec. 5(a) — Penalty applies to conduct before the Act takes effect.** Civil penalties attach to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but under Sec. 8 the Act does not take effect until 180 days later, so there can be no "violation of section 3" in that window; the inconsistency invites a retroactivity/due-process challenge and should be conformed to the effective date (or the effective date should be removed).

25. **Sec. 5(a) — No enforcing agency or procedure.** The bill does not say who assesses the civil penalty (NOAA? the Attorney General?), under what procedure, or in what court, and it provides no express authority for injunctive relief or citizen suits.

26. **Sec. 5(a) — No forfeiture or disgorgement.** $1 million per day with no minimum and no inflation indexing may be a cost of doing business against commercial-scale revenues; the bill should authorize seizure and forfeiture of the vessel and recovered minerals and disgorgement of profits.

27. **Sec. 5(b) — "Willfully" plus a vague definition.** A willfulness standard is already demanding, and when the underlying definition depends on an undefined Secretary's discretionary determination (Sec. 2(1)(B)), criminal prosecution becomes very hard; consider "knowingly," and add corporate-officer liability.

**Sec. 6 — International agreement**

28. **Sec. 6 — Hortatory and ambiguous.** "Seek to negotiate" imposes no obligation to achieve anything, and "not later than 18 months" does not say whether negotiations must begin or conclude by then; there is no reporting requirement to Congress and no reference to the ISA or any existing forum.

**Sec. 7 — Duration**

29. **Sec. 7(a) vs. 7(b) — Internal contradiction.** Subsection (a) says the prohibition lasts until Congress affirmatively finds mining is safe, but subsection (b) ends the entire Act after 10 years regardless. In practice the sunset controls, so the bill is a 10-year moratorium with an automatic reopening, not a prohibition.

30. **Sec. 7(b) — Sunset kills the registry and penalties too.** Because "this Act" ceases to have effect, Sec. 4 and Sec. 5 expire with it, and it is unsettled whether penalties for pre-sunset violations survive a self-executing sunset; it also undercuts Sec. 6, since foreign governments have little reason to bind themselves to a prohibition the U.S. will drop on a date certain.

31. **Sec. 7(a) — No mechanism for the National Academies report.** Nothing directs or funds a National Academies study, says who requests it, or sets its scope, so the trigger either never happens or is controlled by whoever chooses to commission a report. "Serious harm" is also undefined.

**Sec. 8 — Effective date**

32. **Sec. 8 — 180-day delay.** The delay lets operators accelerate recovery, re-equip, reflag, or reincorporate before the prohibition bites, and (see item 24) conflicts with Sec. 5(a); if a transition is needed it should apply only to the registry, not the prohibition.

**Cross-cutting omissions**

33. **No definitions of "person," "vessel," "seabed," "State," or "Secretary."** "Person" will default to the Dictionary Act (1 U.S.C. 1), which is probably adequate, but the others should be defined.

34. **No severability clause.** If Sec. 2(1)(B) is struck for vagueness or Sec. 3(a) for extraterritorial overreach, a court could take the rest of the Act with it.

35. **No bar on federal support.** Nothing prevents EXIM, DFC, DOD, or other agencies from financing, insuring, or procuring from deep seabed mining operations abroad.

36. **No downstream prohibition.** The bill does not restrict importing, processing, or selling minerals recovered by deep seabed mining, so U.S. refineries and buyers remain a market for foreign operators.

---

**If the office can only take a few fixes before Thursday**, the highest-impact items are: (1) the per-vessel threshold and fleet splitting; (9) no owned-or-controlled coverage of foreign subsidiaries; (15) "in the United States" not reaching the EEZ/OCS; (18–20) the exploration-license and environmental-assessment exemptions; (6–7) the undefined Secretary; (24) the enactment/effective-date conflict; and (29) the sunset that overrides the prohibition. Several of these are one-line changes that would substantially harden the bill without altering its structure.
