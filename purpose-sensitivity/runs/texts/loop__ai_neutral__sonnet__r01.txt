# S. 2817 drafting problems, by section

These are drafting and enforceability issues, not predictions about how a court or agency would rule. For an explainer, I'd attribute the legal-risk points to outside experts rather than state them as fact.

## Definitions (Sec. 2)

1. **§2(1)(A), "single training run" is undefined.** A developer could split training into staged runs, resume from checkpoints, merge models, or distill a larger model, keeping each "run" under 10^26 operations. The bill doesn't say whether compute spent on a base model counts toward a fine-tuned successor.

2. **§2(1)(A), fixed threshold and no measurement method.** The number is hard-coded with no mechanism to update it, so algorithmic efficiency gains could produce highly capable models below 10^26. The bill also doesn't say how operations are counted (precision, failed or discarded runs, who verifies).

3. **§2(1)(B), "the Secretary" is never defined.** Sec. 4 refers to the Department of Commerce and Sec. 6 to the Secretary of State, so it is unclear which official makes the capability determination.

4. **§2(1)(B), the capability standard is vague.** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no benchmarks, criteria, procedure, notice requirement, or deadline. That invites vagueness and nondelegation challenges, especially because violations carry criminal penalties.

5. **§2(1)(B), timing of the determination.** Nothing requires the Secretary's finding to be public or to come before the conduct it penalizes, so a developer may not know in advance whether a system is covered. Because §3(b) exempts systems whose training began before enactment, the determination power can only reach future systems.

6. **§2(1), "model" vs. "system" are undefined and used inconsistently.** Clause (A) covers a "model" and clause (B) a "system." An agentic or ensemble system built from several sub-threshold models might be reachable only through the discretionary clause (B).

7. **§2(1), "superintelligence" is not defined, and the coverage test doesn't match the title.** The compute threshold is a proxy that could sweep in systems nobody would call superintelligent while missing capable ones below it. That mismatch could feed both over-breadth and under-inclusion arguments.

8. **§2(2), "U.S. person" is narrow.** It omits lawful permanent residents, partnerships, LLCs (arguably not "corporations"), nonprofits, and unincorporated groups. It is also unclear whether "a State" includes D.C. or territories, and a foreign-organized subsidiary of a U.S. company falls outside it, so the "anywhere" reach in §3(a) can be sidestepped.

9. **§2(3), "advanced AI chips" and "data center" are undefined.** The 50,000 threshold applies per facility, so an operator could spread chips across sites or stay just under it. The bill doesn't say when chips are counted or how distributed training across multiple sites is treated.

## Prohibition (Sec. 3)

10. **§3(a), "train, develop, or deploy" is undefined.** "Develop" overlaps with "train" and may reach ordinary research. The bill doesn't say whether "deploy" covers API access, internal use, open-weight release, or use of a model someone else built.

11. **§3(a), "person" is undefined and there is no government or national-security treatment.** It is unclear whether federal agencies, the military, or intelligence components are covered, exempt, or immune from the penalties.

12. **§3(a), jurisdictional gaps.** A foreign company abroad that serves U.S. users may fall outside "in the United States" or "U.S. person." The bill doesn't say where cloud-based or remote training and deployment are deemed to occur.

13. **§3(b), the grandfather clause exempts the whole system.** The text doesn't limit further training, scaling, fine-tuning, or successor versions of a pre-enactment system. It also rewards starting large runs before enactment.

14. **§3(b), "training began" is undefined and unverifiable.** The bill doesn't say whether it means data preparation, the first training step, or a declared start. With no pre-enactment reporting requirement, regulators would struggle to disprove a claimed start date.

15. **§3(c), the safety-evaluation exemption is open-ended.** "Purpose of safety evaluation" is undefined, has no cap on scale, no oversight or reporting, and covers all of "train, develop, or deploy." Enforcers would have to prove a subjective purpose.

16. **§3(a), preparatory activity isn't restricted.** Stockpiling chips, assembling data, and doing sub-threshold work aren't addressed, which lets firms prepare to resume at scale after the sunset or a repeal.

## Registry (Sec. 4)

17. **§4, no penalty for violations.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of §3, so failing to register or filing a false report has no stated consequence in the bill. Existing false-statement laws might apply but aren't referenced.

18. **§4, no rulemaking, deadlines, or verification.** The bill sets no registration deadline, gives Commerce no explicit authority to write rules or forms, and provides no audit or inspection power. Reports are operator-certified with no independent check.

19. **§4(a), "operator" is undefined.** Colocation and cloud operators may not know what their tenants are running, and the bill doesn't require tenant or customer disclosure.

20. **§4(b), "computing workloads" is vague, and the reporting is too infrequent to be useful.** Reports needn't identify training-run size, model, or customer. Quarterly filing lets a run start and finish between reports, and the registry has no explicit link to the §3 thresholds.

21. **§4, confidentiality, information-sharing, and funding are unaddressed.** The bill doesn't say how Commerce protects trade secrets, shares data with enforcers, or pays for the program.

## Penalties (Sec. 5)

22. **§5(a), no designated enforcer or procedure.** The bill doesn't say who assesses or sues for civil penalties, or whether this happens in court or through agency adjudication, which could raise Seventh Amendment questions.

23. **§5(a), "each day" is ambiguous and the cap may be small.** It is unclear whether a months-long training run is one continuing violation or a daily one, or whether each system or deployment counts separately. A flat $1 million/day isn't scaled to revenue or training cost, and critics may argue it is a manageable cost for large firms.

24. **§5(a) vs. §8, conflicting dates.** Penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §8 delays the Act's effect by 180 days. That creates ambiguity about whether conduct in the gap is penalizable and raises retroactivity and fair-notice problems.

25. **§5(b), "willfully" is unclear.** The bill doesn't say what a defendant must know, such as the compute count or that a system is covered. Proof gets harder still where coverage depends on a discretionary §2(1)(B) determination.

26. **§5(b), "fined under title 18" sets no amount.** The default fine caps in title 18 could be lower than the civil exposure, and the bill is silent on officer liability versus entity liability.

27. **§5, no injunctions, stop orders, or forfeiture.** The penalties are after-the-fact, with no express power to halt a run, seize equipment, or force shutdown.

28. **§5, no whistleblower protections or private right of action.** Given the registry's weakness, insiders may be the main detection route, and the bill doesn't protect or incentivize them.

## International agreement (Sec. 6)

29. **§6, the mandate is soft and ambiguous.** "Shall seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months" could mean the effort or the result must meet the deadline. "Equivalent" is undefined, and there is no reporting requirement, consequence for missing the deadline, mention of Senate treaty consent, or link to chip export controls.

## Duration and effective date (Secs. 7–8)

30. **§7(a) vs. §7(b), indefinite vs. fixed term.** Subsection (a) implies the ban lasts until Congress acts, while (b) ends the whole Act after 10 years regardless. The "until" condition can only shorten the ban, and (a) can mislead readers about its permanence.

31. **§7(a), the off-ramp is underspecified.** The bill doesn't require the National Academies to produce a report, or set a deadline, funding, or a definition of "safely." It also doesn't say whether the joint resolution itself lifts the ban or merely records a finding, and a joint resolution is subject to presidential veto.

32. **§7(b), the sunset repeals everything, with no savings clause.** The registry and penalties lapse along with the ban, and the bill doesn't preserve liability for pre-sunset violations. The fixed end date also gives firms an incentive to wait out the clock.

33. **§8, the 180-day gap.** Developers can start or accelerate runs between enactment and the effective date. Those runs aren't grandfathered under §3(b), but the bill doesn't say whether they must stop on the effective date, and §5(a) muddies the picture.

## Cross-cutting

34. **No severability clause.** If a court struck down the vague §2(1)(B), it is unclear whether the rest of the Act would survive.

35. **Constitutional exposure (flag for outside experts).** Likely lines of attack include vagueness (§2(1)(B) with §5(b)), delegation, Excessive Fines (§5(a)), the extraterritorial reach of §3(a), and First Amendment arguments that code or model weights are protected expression.

36. **No findings, purpose statement, funding, or preemption language.** Courts and agencies would have little guidance on congressional intent, and the bill doesn't say whether it displaces state AI laws or how it will be financed.
