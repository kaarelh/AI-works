# Loopholes, Ambiguities, and Drafting Problems in S. 2817 (Oct. 6 Manager's Amendment)

This list is based only on the text you provided. Counsel should verify each item against the official text and any later amendments before the markup.

## A. Routes to continued operation

1. **§3(b) (grandfather clause):** The exemption applies to systems "whose training began" before enactment, but "began" is undefined. It is unclear whether later checkpoints, fine-tunes, successor versions, or continued deployment of that system remain protected.

2. **§2(1)(A) ("single training run"):** The 10^26 threshold applies per "single training run," with no aggregation across sequential, parallel, or phased runs. A developer could split a large training effort into runs that each fall below the threshold.

3. **§2(1)(A) (compute measurement):** The bill does not say how "computing power" is measured. It does not address whether to count precision-adjusted operations, fine-tuning, or inference, or who certifies the count.

4. **§3(c) (safety evaluation exemption):** "Activities conducted for the purpose of safety evaluation" is undefined and subjective. The exemption has no notice, approval, scope limit, or duration limit, and it does not address mixed-purpose activities.

5. **§2(1)(B) (Secretary's determination):** The second prong reaches only systems the Secretary has determined to be covered. Until a determination is made, it is unclear whether a system is covered, and the bill does not say whether determinations apply retroactively to systems already deployed.

6. **§2(2) ("U.S. person"):** The definition covers only citizens and state-law corporations. It does not clearly reach LLCs, partnerships, or foreign-incorporated affiliates operating abroad, and "State" is undefined.

7. **§3(a) ("train, develop, or deploy"):** None of these verbs is defined. It is unclear whether open-weight release, API access, internal research use, or preparatory development counts as covered conduct.

8. **§2(1)(A) vs. §2(1)(B) ("model" vs. "system"):** Subparagraph (A) refers to a "model," while (B) refers to a "system," and the prohibition uses "covered AI system." It is unclear whether a below-threshold model combined with tools or scaffolding becomes a covered system.

9. **§2(3) and §4 (covered facility):** The "covered facility" definition is used only for registration and reporting, not for the prohibition in §3. The definition also depends on undefined terms ("advanced AI chips," "contain").

## B. Ambiguous or undefined terms

10. **§2(1)(B) ("Secretary"):** The term is undefined. §6 uses "Secretary of State" expressly, so it is unclear which official is responsible for determinations.

11. **§2(1)(B) ("substantially," "most cognitive tasks," "human performance"):** None of these terms has a benchmark or standard. It is unclear which humans, which tasks, and what baseline apply.

12. **§2(1)(A) ("computing power," "integer or floating-point operations"):** The bill mixes integer and floating-point operations without specifying how they are counted or normalized, which affects whether a system crosses the threshold.

13. **§4 ("operator," "computing workloads," "advanced AI chips"):** These terms are undefined. The bill also does not say who is the "operator" in multi-tenant cloud or leased facilities.

14. **§4(b) (reporting content):** The bill does not specify what a "computing workload" report must contain or how it is certified.

15. **§5(b) ("willfully"):** The standard is undefined. Whether it requires knowledge of the law or of the conduct matters, especially given the vague definitions in §2.

16. **§5(a) ("violation"):** The unit of violation is undefined (per model, per facility, per person, or per day). The phrase "each day on which the violation occurs" also raises the question of whether continuing conduct stacks penalties.

17. **§5(a) (civil penalty mens rea):** The civil penalty contains no knowledge or intent requirement, so it appears to be strict liability. Combined with the vague definitions, this supports a fair-notice argument.

18. **§7(a) ("can be developed safely"):** This is the standard for lifting the prohibition, and it is undefined. It is also unclear whether the finding applies to all covered systems or to specific ones.

19. **§7(a) (joint resolution):** The bill does not say whether a joint resolution requires presidential signature or whether it is self-executing. It also does not say whether Congress can act without a NAS report.

20. **§2 (general):** "State" and "date of enactment" are undefined.

## C. Internal conflicts and timing gaps

21. **§5(a) vs. §8:** Civil penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the prohibition takes effect 180 days after enactment. The bill is inconsistent about when exposure begins.

22. **§3(b) vs. §8:** The grandfather clause is keyed to enactment, while the prohibition takes effect 180 days later. Training that begins in that window is neither grandfathered nor clearly prohibited, and it is unclear whether training that starts before the effective date but continues after it is covered.

23. **§7(a) vs. §7(b):** §7(a) keeps the prohibition in place indefinitely until Congress acts, while §7(b) ends the entire Act after 10 years. The bill does not say which controls. The sunset runs from enactment, so the effective period is about 9.5 years.

24. **§4 vs. §5:** Registration and reporting duties under §4 carry no penalty, and §5 penalizes only violations of §3. Failure to register or report may therefore be unenforceable as written.

## D. Enforcement and implementation gaps

25. **§5 (enforcement authority):** The bill does not name who brings civil actions (DOJ, Commerce, or private parties), and it provides no administrative process or injunctive relief.

26. **Whole Act (rulemaking and funding):** The bill gives no rulemaking authority and no appropriation for Commerce to define FLOP measurement, operate the registry, or set reporting forms. This supports an implementation-feasibility argument.

27. **§4 (deadlines):** The bill sets no deadline for initial registration or the first quarterly report.

28. **§4(b) (confidentiality):** The bill does not protect submitted workload data as confidential or trade secret, and it does not say how Commerce may use or share it.

29. **Whole Act (severability and judicial review):** The bill has no severability clause, no judicial review provision, and no safe harbor for good-faith reliance on determinations or guidance.

30. **§2(1)(A) (threshold updates):** The 10^26 threshold is fixed, with no mechanism to update it as the field changes. This supports an argument that the bill would become obsolete or arbitrary.

31. **§6 (international agreement):** The Secretary need only "seek to negotiate." There is no consequence if negotiations fail, no link between the agreement and the domestic prohibition, and "equivalent" is undefined.

32. **§3(a) (extraterritorial scope):** The bill applies to "any U.S. person anywhere" without addressing conflicts with foreign law or how compliance is expected for foreign operations.

## Caveats and strategy notes

- **Strength varies.** Items 21–24 (internal conflicts), 25–26 (enforcement and implementation gaps), and 29 (no severability clause) are the most concrete drafting defects. Courts often read ambiguous terms charitably, so the vagueness arguments in items 10–20 should be presented as risks, not certainties.
- **Likely fixes.** The grandfather clause (§3(b)) and the safety evaluation exemption (§3(c)) are the most direct routes to continued operation, but they are also the provisions sponsors are most likely to tighten. Publicly emphasizing them could prompt those fixes.
- **Strongest "unworkable" case.** The implementation gaps (items 25–28) and the undefined measurement standards (items 2, 3, and 12) are probably the most persuasive for the markup, because they show the bill cannot be administered as written.
- **Scope.** This analysis does not verify case law, legislative history, or whether later amendments have been filed. Counsel should confirm all conclusions before they are used in testimony or public statements.
