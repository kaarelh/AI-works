RESPONSE:
I read the text as amended on October 6. Items are grouped by section, and the ones I'd lead with are **#6, #11, #17, #19, #25, #33, and #36**. I'm not a lawyer, so Legislative Counsel should check the legal-doctrine points (vagueness, nondelegation, Title 18 fine caps) before anything goes to Senator Whitfield's office.

**Sec. 2(1)(A): compute threshold**

1. **Static compute threshold.** A fixed 10^26 figure with no mechanism to update it will be overtaken by algorithmic efficiency gains, so dangerous systems can be built below it, especially over a 10-year life. Add an adjustment authority or a capability-based backstop that doesn't depend on discretionary action.
2. **"Single training run" is undefined.** A developer can split training into staged runs, restart from checkpoints, continue training a base model, or merge models, with each piece staying below the threshold. Define the unit as cumulative compute across a model lineage, including prior runs and checkpoints.
3. **Counting rules are missing.** The text doesn't say whether "trained using" includes fine-tuning, reinforcement learning, synthetic-data generation, or distillation from a larger model. "Artificial intelligence model" is also undefined, and there are no measurement or attestation standards.
4. **"Model" versus "system."** Paragraph (A) covers models, but agentic or ensemble systems built from several sub-threshold models can only be reached through the discretionary paragraph (B).

**Sec. 2(1)(B): Secretary determination**

5. **"Secretary" is undefined.** The Act never says which department head is meant, which invites jurisdictional confusion and challenges to any determination.
6. **The capability standard is vague.** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no benchmark, human baseline, or task list. Attaching it to a 10-year felony invites a void-for-vagueness challenge.
7. **No determination process or powers.** There's no deadline, notice-and-comment, appeal, or judicial review. The Secretary also has no authority to compel model access, testing, or information, and developers have no duty to notify the government as they approach the capability line.
8. **Retroactivity and fair notice.** The text doesn't say whether a determination operates only prospectively. If it can apply to past conduct, there is a due process problem. If it can't, developers can proceed until named. A thin standard also invites nondelegation arguments.

**Sec. 2(2)–(3): other definitions**

9. **"U.S. person" is too narrow.** It omits LLCs, partnerships, trusts, and unincorporated associations, as well as D.C. and territorial entities and lawful permanent residents. It also doesn't reach foreign-incorporated subsidiaries of U.S. firms, which is the obvious restructuring route.
10. **Covered-facility test is easy to evade.** "Advanced AI chip" and "data center" are undefined. A raw chip count isn't tied to performance, so a smaller cluster of newer chips can exceed 10^26 while staying under 50,000. Operators can also split capacity across sites, and there is no aggregation rule for sites under common control.

**Sec. 3(a): prohibition**

11. **The operative verbs are undefined.** "Train, develop, or deploy" leave open whether internal use, fine-tuning, inference, or releasing weights counts.
12. **The prohibition attaches only after the fact.** A model becomes "covered" only once trained above the threshold, so the ban bites when the line is crossed. Nothing prohibits attempting to cross it, approaching it, or preparing for it, and nothing supports an injunction before the harm occurs.
13. **No ban on possession, transfer, or support.** The Act doesn't reach holding or publishing weights, or providing compute, funding, or services to a covered effort. Cloud providers and financiers are covered only if "develop" is read very broadly.
14. **Territorial gaps.** "United States" is undefined. A foreign entity can train abroad and serve U.S. users, and nothing restricts importing or using a foreign-built covered system. It's also unclear whether a foreign party remotely using U.S. cloud capacity is "in the United States."
15. **Government activity is unaddressed.** The Act is silent on federal agencies, national-security programs, and their contractors, and "person" may not include the government. Whether to cover them is a policy call, but the silence will be exploited either way.

**Sec. 3(b): existing systems**

16. **The grandfather clause is uncapped.** It exempts the whole system, so a run begun the day before enactment could continue indefinitely, along with scaling, fine-tuning, and successor versions. Cap the exemption in scale and duration, and exclude derivative models.
17. **Incentive to rush, and no proof of start date.** Developers have every reason to start runs before enactment, and there's no registry or documentation requirement to verify when a run began. "Training began" is also undefined, so a new model could claim a predecessor's start date.

**Sec. 3(c): safety evaluation**

18. **The carve-out can swallow the rule.** An exemption based on "the purpose of safety evaluation" is subjective and hard to disprove. There's no cap on scale, no government approval or licensing, and no reporting requirement, so any lab can characterize a frontier run as evaluation.
19. **No limits on what follows evaluation.** Nothing stops an evaluated system from being retained, deployed, or its weights leaked, and there are no security conditions. The text also doesn't say whether the exemption allows training a new covered system or only testing an existing one.

**Sec. 4: registry**

20. **No penalty for Sec. 4 violations.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of section 3, so failing to register or filing a false certification carries no specific consequence. Existing false-statement law may partly fill the gap, but the Act shouldn't rely on that.
21. **Basic registry mechanics are missing.** There's no registration deadline, no definition of "operator," and no rulemaking authority for Commerce. Commerce also has no direction on what to do with the reports or whether to share them with enforcement agencies.
22. **Reports won't reveal violations.** "Describing the computing workloads" doesn't require identifying models, run sizes, cumulative compute, or customers. There is no audit, inspection, or verification authority, and no confidentiality protections to encourage compliance and withstand trade-secret objections.
23. **The registry misses distributed and cloud activity.** Anyone running below 50,000 chips per facility, or spreading a run across sites, is never registered. There are also no know-your-customer duties for compute providers.

**Sec. 5: penalties**

24. **Effective-date conflict.** Sec. 5(a) imposes penalties for violations "on or after the date of enactment," but Sec. 8 delays the Act's effect by 180 days. That is a fair-notice problem and an easy litigation target. Pick one date.
25. **No enforcer or procedure.** The text doesn't say who brings civil actions, and there's no injunction or cease-and-desist authority, administrative process, or stated limitations period.
26. **Penalty amounts may be absorbable.** A flat $1,000,000-per-day cap is small against frontier training budgets, and "each day on which the violation occurs" is ambiguous for a continuous run. The default Title 18 fine caps are also low unless the alternative-fine provision is invoked (counsel should confirm), so consider penalties scaled to revenue or compute spend.
27. **The criminal mens rea is hard to meet.** "Willfully" generally requires knowing the conduct was unlawful, and with the vague coverage standard in 2(1)(B), prosecutors would struggle to prove it. The Act also has no responsible-corporate-officer provision.
28. **No remedies against the model or hardware.** The Act lacks authority to order deletion of weights, forfeiture or seizure of chips, or disgorgement of gains. A violator could pay fines and keep the system.
29. **No whistleblower provisions.** Insiders are the likeliest source of evidence of violations, and the Act offers no protection from retaliation and no incentives.

**Sec. 6: international agreement**

30. **"Seek to negotiate" is toothless.** It imposes only a duty of effort, and it's unclear whether the 18-month deadline applies to starting talks or concluding them. There's no reporting to Congress, no consequence for missing the deadline, and no definition of "equivalent" or any verification requirement.
31. **No supply-chain or export tools.** The Act has no chip or hardware export controls and no leverage over foreign development in the interim, which weakens the argument that the bill actually reduces global risk.

**Sec. 7: duration**

32. **The sunset undermines the permanent ban.** Sec. 7(a) says the prohibition lasts until Congress finds development can be safe, but 7(b) ends everything after 10 years regardless. That makes 7(a) largely meaningless and gives companies a wait-it-out strategy: stockpile chips and build facilities in anticipation.
33. **No savings clause.** Nothing says violations committed before the sunset can still be prosecuted or penalized afterward, which hands defendants an argument and weakens deterrence in the final years.
34. **The lifting mechanism is underspecified.** "Can be developed safely" has no standard, and the National Academies has no obligation, deadline, or funding to produce a report. "Acting on a report" doesn't say whether the report must favor lifting, and a joint resolution is all-or-nothing, with no way to lift the ban partially or conditionally.

**Sec. 8 and general**

35. **No interim measures during the 180-day delay.** Companies can accelerate training and chip acquisition before the prohibition takes effect. The grandfather date (enactment) also differs from the effective date, leaving runs begun in between in an ambiguous status.
36. **No severability clause.** If a court strikes the vague Secretary-determination clause or another provision, the whole Act could be at risk.
37. **No findings or jurisdictional grounding.** The bill has no congressional findings supporting Commerce Clause authority or the need for the restriction. That weakens its defense against Commerce Clause and First Amendment challenges, since model weights and code have been argued to be speech.
38. **Missing definitions overall.** Besides the terms noted above, the Act never defines "person," "United States," "artificial intelligence model/system," "train," "develop," "deploy," "operator," "advanced AI chip," "data center," or "safety evaluation." Each gap is an opening for litigation.
39. **No funding or staffing.** The bill authorizes no appropriations for Commerce's registry, the Secretary's determinations, State's negotiations, or the National Academies' work, so implementation depends on separate action.
