RESPONSE:
**Key caveats:** This analysis covers only the text you provided. I haven't compared it against the pre-amendment bill, and I don't know our actual licenses and authorizations, so several items below depend on facts counsel will need to confirm. Items 3, 14, 18, and 30 especially need checking against our records and the current statutory framework.

**Definitions (§2)**

1. **§2(1)(A):** Operations below 10,000 metric tons per year fall outside the definition unless they qualify under (B). Smaller commercial recovery stays outside the prohibition.

2. **§2(1)(A):** The phrase "by a single vessel" means recovery split across several vessels, each under the threshold, may not count as commercial mining. The bill doesn't say whether volumes are aggregated by operator, project, or license.

3. **§2(1)(A):** "Per year" has no measurement period (calendar year, license year, or rolling 12 months). "Recovery" is also undefined, so it's unclear whether material lifted and discarded counts toward the threshold.

4. **§2(1)(A):** "At depths greater than 200 meters" doesn't say whether depth refers to water depth or the depth at which recovery occurs.

5. **§2(1)(A):** "Other mineral resources" is undefined, so it's unclear whether crusts, sulfides, or sediment count, and whether they count toward the 10,000-ton threshold.

6. **§2(1)(B):** The Secretary's "risk comparable" determination has no standard, procedure, notice requirement, or review mechanism. This invites an arbitrary-and-capricious challenge and makes the definition hard to apply in advance.

7. **§2(1)(B):** "Comparable to those of the activities described in subparagraph (A)" compares risk to the activities rather than to a measurable threshold, so there is no objective test.

8. **§2 (generally):** "Secretary" is never defined. §6 uses "Secretary of State," and NOAA sits within Commerce, so it's unclear which Secretary makes the (B) determination.

9. **§2(2):** "U.S. person" covers only citizens and state-law corporations. It doesn't address LLCs, partnerships, or other project vehicles, and "State" is undefined (it's unclear whether it includes D.C. and the territories).

10. **§2(3):** The 5,000-gross-ton "covered vessel" threshold isn't tied to the 10,000-ton production threshold. A vessel under 5,000 GT could exceed 10,000 tons per year, and "equipped for the recovery" is undefined.

11. **§2 (generally):** "Exploration," "license," "environmental assessment," and "person" are undefined. Each of these terms matters to the exemptions below.

**Prohibition and exemptions (§3)**

12. **§3(a):** "Engage in" is undefined. The bill doesn't say whether it reaches contractors, charterers, financiers, equipment suppliers, or onshore processing.

13. **§3(a):** "In the United States" is undefined. It's unclear whether it covers the EEZ, the continental shelf, or the Area beyond national jurisdiction. Combined with "any U.S. person anywhere," the extraterritorial reach raises questions under UNCLOS and the ISA regime, and it doesn't reach foreign subsidiaries operating outside U.S. jurisdiction.

14. **§3(b):** The existing-operations exemption covers mining "under an exploration license," not commercial recovery authorizations. If our current authorizations are commercial permits, or contracts such as ISA exploration contracts rather than licenses "issued" under U.S. law, they may fall outside the exemption.

15. **§3(b):** The exemption has no time limit, volume cap, area limit, or condition for continued validity. It's unclear whether renewals, amendments, expansions, or transfers after enactment still count as licenses "issued before" enactment.

16. **§3(b) and §8:** Licenses issued between enactment and the 180-day effective date aren't grandfathered, and §3's effective date is ambiguous because §3 has no date of its own. Opponents may use this to argue the operative date is unclear.

17. **§3(c):** "For the purpose of environmental assessment" has no purpose test, volume cap, agency approval requirement, reporting duty, or time limit. Commercial-scale recovery could be labeled assessment ("test mining"). This is the broadest carve-out in the bill, and it's likely to draw the most scrutiny.

18. **§3(c):** The bill doesn't say whether material recovered during assessment can be sold or retained, or who decides whether an activity's purpose is assessment.

**Registry and reporting (§4)**

19. **§4(a):** "Operator" is undefined. The vessel owner, charterer, and operator may be different entities, so the registration duty could fall on the wrong party or on no one. No registration deadline is set.

20. **§4(b):** The reporting duty specifies no content, no certification standard, no public-disclosure rule, no confidentiality protection for commercial data, and no penalty for false certification. It also applies only to covered vessels.

**Penalties and enforcement (§5)**

21. **§4 and §5(a):** Civil penalties attach only to violations of §3. Failing to register or file reports is not a §3 violation, so this Act provides no penalty for registry non-compliance.

22. **§5(a):** The penalty applies to violations "occurring on or after the date of enactment," but §8 delays the Act's effective date by 180 days. The text doesn't resolve whether penalties can attach in the interim. "Violation" and "each day" are also undefined, so it's unclear whether continuing violations accrue daily and whether counting is per vessel or per operation.

23. **§5 (generally):** The bill designates no enforcement agency, no civil procedure (administrative or judicial), no statute of limitations, no injunctive-relief provision, and no mental-state standard for civil violations.

24. **§5(b):** "Willfully" is undefined, and the bill doesn't address the knowledge standard for corporate defendants or whether individual officers can be personally liable.

**International agreement (§6)**

25. **§6:** "Shall seek to negotiate" creates no duty to conclude an agreement and no consequence for failure. "Prohibitions equivalent to those in section 3" is undefined, and there is no reporting requirement to Congress. This undermines the bill's stated rationale even though it doesn't directly affect domestic operations.

**Duration and sunset (§7)**

26. **§7(a):** "Serious harm" is undefined, so the standard for lifting the prohibition is open-ended. The bill also sets no deadline for the National Academies report, and it's unclear what happens if no report is issued.

27. **§7(a):** A joint resolution is a bill-form measure that requires presentment and presidential signature or veto override. Describing Congress as "finding by joint resolution" is imprecise, and the drafting should say whether the resolution has the force of law.

28. **§7(a) vs. §7(b):** §7(a) says the prohibition "remains in effect until" a joint resolution is enacted, but §7(b) says the entire Act "shall cease to have effect" after 10 years. The text doesn't say which controls, so the 10-year sunset may end the prohibition regardless of any §7(a) finding, or the prohibition may survive it.

29. **§7(b):** The bill contains no savings clause for violations or enforcement actions pending when the Act sunsets. Counsel should check how general statutory rules on repeal (including 1 U.S.C. § 109) apply to a sunset provision.

**Effective date and implementation (§8 and general)**

30. **§8 (generally):** The bill sets no effective date for the registry requirements and no deadline for initial registration or first reports.

31. **General:** The bill grants no rulemaking authority, yet §2(1)(B) and §4 require agency action. It also has no appropriation, no severability clause, and no provision addressing the interaction with other federal or state regimes.

32. **General:** The bill doesn't mention the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (1980), under which NOAA issues exploration licenses and commercial recovery permits for U.S. companies. It neither amends nor reconciles with that statute. If our current authorizations derive from DSHMRA, the bill's relationship to that framework is a central question. Counsel should verify this before the markup.

**Notes for the legal team**

- Items 17 and 14 are our most direct protections under the current text, but also the most likely to be attacked as loopholes. Expect opponents to frame §3(c) as a shield for commercial mining. Present it as a legitimate assessment pathway only if we can show it has defined limits in practice.
- Items 6, 26, and 28 are the strongest points for a "bill is unworkable as drafted" argument because they create open-ended agency discretion or conflicting terms.
- Please check item 32 and the facts behind items 14 and 15 before relying on them in the markup.
