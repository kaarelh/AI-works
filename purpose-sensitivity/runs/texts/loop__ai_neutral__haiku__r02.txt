**Working notes:** This analysis is based only on the bill text you pasted, which is labeled as the October 6 manager's amendment. I couldn't check it against the official text on congress.gov, so verify the wording before publication. These are drafting and enforcement issues, not positions on the bill. Items are roughly in section order, and the final section lists cross-cutting gaps.

**Definitions (Sec. 2)**

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A):** The 10^26 threshold applies to a "single training run," so a developer could split training across several runs or checkpoints, or continue training in stages, and stay under the line. The text also doesn't say whether fine-tuning or post-training compute counts toward a model's total.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A):** There is no method for measuring compute, no rule for whether to use estimated or actual figures, and no guidance on combining "integer or floating-point operations." Whether a given model crosses the threshold would therefore be open to dispute.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A):** The threshold is fixed in statute with no mechanism to update it, and the Act gives no rulemaking authority to change it. Algorithmic efficiency gains could bring comparable capabilities below 10^26 FLOP.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(B):** "The Secretary" is never identified, so it is unclear which agency or official makes the capability determination.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(B):** The determination process has no stated criteria, notice, comment period, timing, or judicial review standard. Because violations carry criminal penalties, a determination could be challenged as arbitrary and as too vague to give fair notice.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B):** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" and "AI system" are undefined. It is unclear whether "AI system" covers only a model or also agent scaffolding and tool-using wrappers around a model.

7. **Sec. 2(2):** The "U.S. person" definition covers citizens and state-chartered corporations only. It excludes LLCs, partnerships, other unincorporated entities, foreign-incorporated subsidiaries of U.S. firms, and lawful permanent residents, even though Sec. 3(a) reaches "any U.S. person anywhere."

8. **Sec. 2(2)–(3):** "State" is not defined, so it is unclear whether D.C. and U.S. territories count, and "advanced AI chips" is not defined. Without a chip definition or performance metric, the 50,000-chip count cannot be applied consistently.

9. **Sec. 2(3):** The facility threshold is not linked to the model definition. A 10^26 FLOP training run can be completed with far fewer than 50,000 chips over a longer period, so smaller facilities fall outside the registry. "Containing" is also unclear for leased cloud capacity and for training distributed across multiple sites.

**Prohibition (Sec. 3)**

10. **Sec. 3(a):** "Train," "develop," and "deploy" are undefined. It is unclear whether these terms cover sub-threshold research, fine-tuning a non-covered model into a covered capability, releasing open weights, or customers using a covered system through an API.

11. **Sec. 3(a):** The prohibition reaches "any person in the United States" and "any U.S. person anywhere," so training or deployment abroad by non-U.S. persons, including for users worldwide, is outside the Act. "In the United States" is also undefined for compute located abroad but operated from the U.S. Sec. 6 is the only provision aimed at this gap, and it is non-binding.

12. **Sec. 3(a):** "Control" or "direction" is not defined for determining whether a U.S. parent "trains" or "develops" through a foreign subsidiary. This leaves the reach of "any U.S. person anywhere" unclear.

13. **Sec. 3(b):** The grandfather clause depends on when "training began," which is undefined. A run started before enactment that is later continued, extended, or restarted from earlier checkpoints could be argued to be covered by the exemption.

14. **Sec. 3(b):** The exemption applies to the covered system as a whole, not just the pre-enactment training run. As written, the system could be developed and deployed indefinitely, although the heading says "existing systems," a term the operative text never uses.

15. **Sec. 3(c):** "Safety evaluation" is undefined. The exemption has no limits on scale, duration, access, containment, reporting, or public deployment after an evaluation, and it does not say whether it covers training or only testing.

16. **Sec. 3(c):** The exemption turns on the activity being conducted "for the purpose of" safety evaluation. Proving intent is difficult, which makes the exemption easy to claim and hard to disprove.

**Registry (Sec. 4)**

17. **Sec. 4(a):** "Operator" is undefined. It is unclear whether this means the owner, the cloud provider, or the tenant, and who must register at multi-tenant facilities.

18. **Sec. 4:** The Act sets no deadline for initial registration and no required content. It also imposes no penalty for failing to register or to report, since Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3. The registry therefore lacks any enforcement mechanism.

19. **Sec. 4(b):** "Computing workloads" is undefined, and the report need not say whether a covered model was trained. The self-certified report could therefore fail to reveal a violation.

20. **Sec. 4(b):** The Act gives Commerce no verification, audit, or inspection authority and sets no explicit penalty for a false certification. Only general federal false-statement law would apply, and the bill doesn't cite it.

21. **Sec. 4:** The Act has no confidentiality or data-use provision for reported workload data. This is both a practical issue and a potential ground for legal challenge.

**Penalties (Sec. 5)**

22. **Sec. 5(a):** "For each day" versus "each violation" is ambiguous. It is unclear whether a continuing training run counts as one violation per day or one per model or run, and this changes the penalty dramatically.

23. **Sec. 5(a):** The civil penalty has no inflation adjustment, no designated enforcing agency, no stated procedure (administrative or court action), and no limitations period. The default five-year period under 28 U.S.C. 2462 would likely apply.

24. **Sec. 5(a):** At $1 million per day, the penalty may be small relative to the cost of frontier training runs, which could let companies treat it as a cost of doing business. The Act also does not expressly authorize injunctions or disgorgement, so courts would have to infer that authority.

25. **Sec. 5(b):** "Willfully" is undefined. The Act does not say how corporate liability or officer liability applies to the criminal penalty.

26. **Sec. 5(b):** Criminal penalties attach only to Sec. 3 violations. Sec. 4 violations and false certifications carry no criminal penalty under the Act.

27. **Sec. 5:** Nothing addresses enforcement against foreign actors with no U.S. assets, such as asset freezes, service of process, or coordination with foreign authorities.

**International, duration, and effective date (Secs. 6–8)**

28. **Sec. 6:** "Shall seek to negotiate" creates no obligation to reach an agreement, and the Act sets no consequence if talks fail and no reporting requirement. "Equivalent" is also undefined, so it is unclear whether a foreign agreement would need to match the registry and penalties or only the prohibition.

29. **Sec. 7(a):** The Act does not require the National Academies to produce a report, sets no deadline or scope, and gives no standard for when covered systems "can be developed safely." Congress could never act, leaving the prohibition in place indefinitely with no default path.

30. **Sec. 7(a):** The Act does not specify expedited procedures for the joint resolution or say what legal effect it has. A joint resolution goes to the President, so a veto and override would be possible, and the text doesn't address this.

31. **Sec. 7(a) vs. 7(b):** These provisions conflict. Sec. 7(a) makes the prohibition last until a joint resolution is enacted, while Sec. 7(b) ends the entire Act 10 years after enactment regardless of any finding. The text doesn't say which controls.

32. **Sec. 7(b):** The sunset has no savings clause. The text doesn't say whether pending enforcement actions, penalties for past violations, or reporting duties survive. The sunset is also measured from enactment, so the prohibition would run about 9.5 years, not 10, since it takes effect 180 days later.

33. **Sec. 5(a) vs. Sec. 8:** The civil penalty applies to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the prohibition takes effect 180 days after enactment. The text does not say whether conduct during that gap is penalized.

34. **Sec. 8 with Sec. 3(b):** Training that starts after enactment is not grandfathered, but the Act doesn't say whether training begun during the 180-day window must stop on the effective date. This ambiguity could also create an incentive to start runs during the window.

**Cross-cutting gaps**

35. **Whole Act:** The Act has no severability clause. If a court strikes a provision such as the Secretary's determination authority in Sec. 2(1)(B), it is unclear whether the rest survives.

36. **Whole Act:** The Act gives no rulemaking authority to define key terms, set compute-measurement methods, or update thresholds, and it provides no appropriations for Commerce's registry or enforcement work.

37. **Whole Act:** The Act does not address its relationship to existing export controls, other federal AI authorities, or state AI laws, so it is unclear whether it would preempt or coexist with them.
