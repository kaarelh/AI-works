RESPONSE:
Items are ordered roughly by severity: internal contradictions first, then major loopholes, then enforcement and drafting gaps. I worked only from the text you provided and haven't checked current statutes, pending state AI laws, or the Senate's rules, so counsel or the Senate Legislative Counsel should verify the legal points before this goes to the office.

**Internal contradictions and timing**

1. **§7(a) vs. §7(b): sunset conflicts with indefinite duration.** Section 7(a) keeps the prohibition in force until Congress passes a joint resolution, but §7(b) ends the entire Act 10 years after enactment, and nothing says which controls. Because the sunset runs from enactment rather than the §8 effective date, the prohibition could be in force for only about 9.5 years. The sunset should be removed or replaced with a periodic review requirement.

2. **§8 vs. §5(a): effective date mismatch.** Penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §8 delays the Act's effect 180 days. It is unclear whether §3 is enforceable during that window, and the bill should align the two dates.

3. **§3(b): grandfather clause is too broad.** Read literally, any system whose training "began" before enactment is exempt from all of §3 (training, development, and deployment) with no end date, no completion requirement, and no bar on deployment. "Training began" is undefined, so a trivial early checkpoint could qualify. Combined with the 180-day gap in §8, this invites a rush to start large runs before enactment. The exemption should cover only runs completed by a fixed date, prohibit deployment absent a determination, and require registration.

**Major loopholes in coverage**

4. **§2(1)(A): "single training run" allows splitting.** Developers can divide one training effort into sequential runs, continued pretraining, post-training, or fine-tuning, each below 10^26 operations. The bill needs an aggregation rule that counts all compute used to produce or materially modify a model.

5. **§2(1)(A): fixed threshold will age poorly.** Algorithmic efficiency gains could make dangerous capabilities possible below 10^26 operations, and the Secretary has no authority to adjust the threshold. The bill should give the Secretary rulemaking authority to revise it.

6. **§2(1)(A) vs. §2(1)(B): inconsistent terms.** Subparagraph (A) covers an "artificial intelligence model," while (B) covers an "artificial intelligence system," and "system" is undefined. A developer could argue that a below-threshold model combined with tools or scaffolding is outside (A), leaving only the discretionary determination in (B) as a backstop.

7. **§2(1)(B): undefined Secretary and no determination process.** "The Secretary" is never identified, and the bill sets no procedure, notice, criteria, or timeline for finding a system capable of "substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks." This invites vagueness challenges and APA arbitrary-and-capricious claims, and it leaves coverage uncertain until a determination is made. It should also say whether determinations apply to already-trained systems.

8. **§2(3): "advanced AI chips" and "data center" are undefined.** Operators could split capacity across sites or leased arrangements to stay under 50,000 chips. The bill should define these terms and address aggregation across facilities and cloud tenancy.

9. **§2(3) and §4: registry threshold misses covered runs.** A training run above 10^26 operations can be completed on well under 50,000 chips if the run is long enough, so the facility-based registry would miss many covered runs. Registration should also cover covered training runs and developers, not just facilities.

10. **§3(a): "train, develop, or deploy" omits key conduct.** The prohibition does not reach providing compute or chips to others, publishing or distributing model weights (including open-weight release), fine-tuning, or knowingly enabling another party's covered training. Each of these should be added or expressly covered.

11. **§2(2) and §3(a): extraterritorial gap.** The prohibition covers "any person in the United States" and "any U.S. person anywhere," but "U.S. person" excludes LLCs and partnerships (which are not "corporations"), foreign-incorporated subsidiaries of U.S. firms, and foreign nationals abroad. A foreign affiliate of a U.S. lab operating abroad would fall outside the Act. The definition should be broadened.

12. **§3(c): safety evaluation exemption is unlimited.** "Activities conducted for the purpose of safety evaluation" is undefined and has no registration, compute cap, deployment bar, or sole-purpose requirement. This is the most likely route for labs to continue frontier training by relabeling it. The exemption should be narrowed, require advance registration, prohibit deployment or release of outputs, and require reporting.

**Registry and reporting**

13. **§4(a): no deadline or content requirements for registration.** The bill does not say when registration is due or what it must include. "Operator" is ambiguous when a facility has several operators or a developer is only a tenant.

14. **§4(b): "computing workloads" is undefined.** Reports need not identify training runs, compute used, models trained, or safety evaluations, so they could be generic. The certification has no stated consequence for false statements.

15. **§4 is not tied to any penalty.** Section 5 penalizes only violations of §3, so failing to register or filing false reports is not punishable under the Act. The bill should add civil and criminal penalties for §4 violations.

**Enforcement**

16. **§5: no enforcing agency or remedies.** The bill does not say who brings civil actions, whether there is an administrative penalty process, whether injunctions or temporary restraining orders are available, or whether there is a private right of action. Without these, the penalties may be hard to apply in practice.

17. **§5(a): penalty may be too small.** At $1 million per day, the penalty is roughly $365 million per year, which may be a cost of doing business for large developers and hyperscalers. "Each day" is also unclear for a continuous training run. The bill should consider per-run, per-facility, or revenue-based penalties.

18. **§5(b): "willfully" is undefined and hard to prove.** The bill does not define the mental state required, does not address attempt or conspiracy, and does not address aiding and abetting or responsibility for corporate officers. These should be added.

19. **No investigation or inspection authority.** The bill gives no power to inspect facilities, compel documents, issue subpoenas, or require recordkeeping, and it has no anti-obstruction provision. As drafted, the registry depends entirely on self-reporting.

20. **No anti-evasion clause.** Nothing prevents splitting runs, shifting legal entities, relocating compute, or restructuring to fall outside the definitions. The bill should declare such evasion a violation and authorize aggregation.

21. **No whistleblower protections.** Employees are often the first to know of violations at AI labs, and the bill gives them no protection for reporting.

**International and duration provisions**

22. **§6: obligation is only to "seek to negotiate."** There is no duty to conclude an agreement, no reporting to Congress, and no consequence for failure. "Equivalent prohibitions" is undefined. The section also omits export controls on chips and model weights, which are the main tool for keeping foreign actors from building covered systems.

23. **§7(a): NASEM report and "developed safely" are undefined.** The bill does not require NASEM to produce a report or set a deadline, does not provide funding, and does not define "can be developed safely." Because joint resolutions are presented to the President, the bill should also say what happens if one is vetoed and whether expedited procedures apply.

**Boilerplate and legal robustness**

24. **Missing definitions.** The bill does not define "person," "State," "Secretary," "develop," "deploy," "training began," "training run," or "safety evaluation."

25. **"Develop" may reach research and publication.** Read broadly, "develop" could cover ordinary machine-learning research and possibly publication of code or papers. This creates First Amendment and vagueness exposure and gives opponents an easy argument. The bill should either clarify the scope or narrow the term.

26. **No severability clause.** If one provision is struck down, the whole Act could be at risk without one.

27. **No congressional findings or stated constitutional basis.** Courts look to findings when reviewing the scope of federal regulation, and the bill does not say which power, such as the Commerce Clause, supports it.

28. **No savings clause.** The bill does not preserve liability for violations that occurred before the sunset or repeal.

29. **No preemption clause.** The bill does not say whether it preempts state AI laws or leaves them in place. This creates litigation risk and gives opponents an argument. Counsel should review current state laws before this is settled.

30. **No appropriations or rulemaking authority.** Without these, implementation depends on agency discretion and may be underfunded.

**Suggested priorities for the markup:** items 1–3 (the sunset conflict and effective-date mismatch), item 3's grandfather clause, item 12 (the safety evaluation exemption), item 16 (no enforcer), and item 19 (no inspection authority) are the most important to fix. These are likely to matter most to the office and to opponents.

I can draft amendment language for any of these, starting with the top priorities, if that would help.
