**Drafting review: S. 2817 (text as amended October 6, 2026)**

These are textual issues only. Some could be read differently by sponsors and opponents, and some points about existing law (the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act and the International Seabed Authority framework) come from background knowledge I can't check here. Please verify those against current sources and confirm the amended text with committee staff before publishing.

**Section 2: Definitions**

1. **§2(1)(A), "by a single vessel":** The 10,000-ton threshold applies per vessel, so an operator could use several vessels each below the limit, or split operations among affiliated companies, and fall outside the definition.
2. **§2(1)(A), "per year":** The bill doesn't say how tonnage is measured (wet or dry weight, nodules or total sediment processed) and sets no cumulative limit, so an operator could run just under the annual cap indefinitely.
3. **§2(1)(A), 200-meter floor:** Activities at shallower depths fall outside the definition entirely, and the bill doesn't say whether mining on the U.S. continental shelf or in the EEZ, which other federal law may govern, is covered.
4. **§2(1)(A), undefined terms:** "Recovery," "seabed," and "other mineral resources" are undefined, so it's unclear whether dredging sand or gravel, or extracting from subsoil deposits such as sulfides or crusts, counts.
5. **§2(1), "commercial":** The definition never mentions sale, profit, or commercial purpose, so the word "commercial" limits nothing and it's unclear whether high-volume test or research recovery is covered.
6. **§2(1)(B), unnamed "Secretary":** The bill never says which Secretary makes the determination (Sections 4 and 6 name other agencies), and it sets no process, notice, deadline, or criteria.
7. **§2(1)(B), "comparable" risk:** There's no standard for "comparable" risk, and it's unclear whether the comparison is to the 10,000-ton threshold or to actual ecosystem effects. Such an open standard invites vagueness challenges and makes it hard for operators to know whether they're covered.
8. **§2(1)(B), reviewability:** Because coverage under (B) depends on an agency determination, the prohibition is only as strong as the agency's willingness to act, and the bill doesn't say whether determinations can be challenged or appealed.
9. **§2(2), "U.S. person":** Limiting this to citizens and state-chartered corporations excludes LLCs, partnerships, trusts, foreign-incorporated subsidiaries of U.S. firms, and permanent residents, which weakens the "anywhere" reach in §3(a) for ventures organized through those structures.
10. **§2(2), "State":** The term is undefined, so it's unclear whether D.C., territories such as Guam or Puerto Rico, or federally chartered corporations are included.
11. **§2(3), "covered vessel":** The 5,000-gross-ton vessel threshold doesn't match the 10,000-metric-ton production threshold in §2(1)(A), so a vessel can exceed the prohibition's threshold while escaping the registry. Gross tons and metric tons are also different units, and the mismatch isn't addressed.
12. **§2(3), "equipped for":** No standard defines when a vessel is "equipped for" recovery, so a vessel could be used for mining without clearly falling within the definition.

**Section 3: Prohibition**

13. **§3(a), "engage in":** The bill doesn't say whether financing, chartering vessels, supplying equipment, or processing or selling minerals counts, leaving downstream and financial activity outside the prohibition. The bill also has no import or sale ban for minerals recovered abroad.
14. **§3(a), geographic reach:** "In the United States" is undefined and doesn't say whether it includes the U.S. EEZ, the outer continental shelf, or the international seabed Area. Non-U.S. operators in international waters are outside the prohibition unless they are "U.S. persons," which the definition narrows.
15. **§3(b), exploration-license exemption:** The exemption covers "mining conducted under" an exploration license, so an exploration license could authorize commercial-scale recovery with no tonnage cap or scope limit.
16. **§3(b), undefined licensing terms:** "Exploration license" isn't defined, and the bill doesn't address commercial recovery permits, pending applications, or the interaction with the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act of 1980 and its licensing regime. Verify the current statutory framework before describing it in the explainer.
17. **§3(c), environmental assessment exemption:** This exemption has no quantity cap, no approval or reporting requirement, no time limit, and "environmental assessment" is undefined. Recovered minerals could be sold under this label, which undercuts the prohibition.

**Section 4: Registry**

18. **§4(a), "operator":** The term is undefined, so it's unclear who must register when ownership, operation, and vessel control are split among different entities.
19. **§4(a), registration terms:** The section sets no registration deadline, no required content, and no public disclosure requirement.
20. **§4(b), report content:** "Describing the recovery activities" doesn't require tonnage, depth, location, or sales data, and there are no audit rights or penalties for a false certification.
21. **§4, no penalties:** Section 5 penalizes only violations of §3, so failing to register or file a report carries no stated penalty.
22. **§4, scope:** Reporting covers only "covered vessels," so mining conducted with multiple smaller vessels, submersible systems, or shore-based equipment falls outside the registry.

**Section 5: Penalties**

23. **§5, enforcement machinery:** The bill doesn't say which agency investigates, who brings civil actions, whether penalties are administrative or judicial, or whether there is authority for injunctions, vessel or mineral seizure, or forfeiture.
24. **§5(a), "violation" and "each day":** The bill doesn't define when a continuing operation is a violation or how daily violations are counted (per vessel, per day, or per ton). It also has no inflation adjustment, which affects the penalty's deterrent value over time.
25. **§5(b), "willfully":** Criminal liability depends on willfulness, which isn't defined, and the bill has no attempt or conspiracy language and no criminal provision for false reporting under §4.
26. **§5(a) versus §8, timing conflict:** Civil penalties apply to violations of §3 "on or after the date of enactment," but §3 doesn't take effect until 180 days after enactment. Read literally, the penalty provision could reach conduct before the prohibition applies.

**Section 6: International agreement**

27. **§6, soft obligation:** The Secretary of State must only "seek to negotiate" by the 18-month deadline, so starting talks would satisfy it. The bill doesn't require an agreement, reporting to Congress, or any consequence for failure. "Equivalent to those in section 3" is also undefined, and the bill doesn't mention the International Seabed Authority or UNCLOS.

**Section 7: Duration**

28. **§7(a) versus §7(b), internal conflict:** Section 7(a) keeps the prohibition in effect until Congress passes a joint resolution, but §7(b) ends the entire Act 10 years after enactment. It's unclear whether the prohibition lapses at year 10 even without a finding.
29. **§7(a), "serious harm" and the NASEM report:** "Serious harm" is undefined, no deadline requires the National Academies report, and the bill doesn't say what happens if no report issues or it's inconclusive. It also doesn't address presidential action on the joint resolution.
30. **§7(b), no savings clause:** The sunset provision doesn't address pending enforcement actions, penalties for pre-sunset violations, or existing licenses and contracts after expiration.

**Section 8: Effective date**

31. **§8 and §7(b), timing gaps:** The prohibition, registry, and penalties take effect 180 days after enactment, while the §3(b) grandfather clause keys to the enactment date. That leaves a 180-day window in which mining is not prohibited. The 10-year sunset also runs from enactment rather than the effective date, so the operative period is about nine and a half years, and the bill doesn't say so explicitly.

**Whole Act**

32. **Missing general provisions:** The bill has no severability clause, so a court striking one provision (for example, the §2(1)(B) catch-all as unconstitutionally vague) could leave the rest in doubt. It also lacks a general definitions section, rulemaking authority, appropriations for enforcement, a judicial review provision, and a clause addressing interaction with existing law, international obligations, and contracts. Each gap makes the Act easier to challenge or harder to administer.
