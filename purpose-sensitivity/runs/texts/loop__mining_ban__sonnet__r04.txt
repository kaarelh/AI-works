RESPONSE:
I reviewed only the text you pasted, not the underlying statutes it interacts with. I'd have Legislative Counsel confirm the cross-references (especially the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act, DSHMRA, and OCSLA points). Items 1-6 are the ones I'd put first.

## Highest priority

1. **Sec. 7(b) (sunset).** The Act expires 10 years after enactment (about 9.5 years of effect, given Sec. 8), which contradicts Sec. 7(a)'s open-ended prohibition and defeats the purpose of a durable ban. Strike it, or replace it with a periodic review that cannot end the prohibition automatically.

2. **Sec. 3(b) (existing operations).** "Mining conducted under an exploration license" has no cap on volume, area, or duration, and it doesn't say whether renewals, extensions, modifications, or transfers are covered. It could be read to allow commercial-scale recovery under an exploration license. Limit it to exploration activity, bar expansion, and give it an end date.

3. **Sec. 3(b) (licensing authority).** "An exploration license" doesn't say who must have issued it. Licenses from foreign governments or the International Seabed Authority (ISA) could qualify, which would exempt U.S. persons operating abroad under foreign-sponsored contracts. Limit the exemption to licenses issued under U.S. law.

4. **Sec. 3(c) (environmental assessment).** The exemption turns on subjective "purpose," with no volume cap, no definition of "environmental assessment," no independence requirement, and no agency approval. A company could run large "test mining" campaigns under this label. Define the term narrowly, cap recovery, and require a government-approved plan.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(B) (Secretary's determination).** "The Secretary" is undefined (Sec. 6 uses "Secretary of State"), and the provision sets no standards, process, or deadline. It invites nondelegation and vagueness challenges, and it is a notice problem because Sec. 5(b) attaches criminal penalties to a discretionary finding. Name the agency, require rulemaking and published determinations, and specify how a person gets notice before liability attaches.

6. **No enforcement or rulemaking authority anywhere in the bill.** It names no agency to enforce Sec. 3 and gives no rulemaking, inspection, investigation, or seizure authority. It also has no authorization of appropriations and no role for the Coast Guard or DOJ. As drafted, the penalties have no clear mechanism for being imposed.

## Definitions (Sec. 2)

7. **Sec. 2(1)(A) (10,000-ton threshold).** The threshold lets operators run sequential or parallel operations just under it. "Per year" is undefined (calendar, rolling, or permit year). Consider a lower or activity-based test, aggregation across affiliates, and a defined measurement period.

8. **Sec. 2(1)(A) ("single vessel").** Splitting recovery across multiple vessels, or using a lift vessel plus a separate collector or barge, can keep each vessel under the line. Aggregate by operator, affiliated group, or project.

9. **Sec. 2(1)(A) (how tonnage is measured).** The text doesn't say whether tonnage is wet or dry weight or where it is measured (at collection or at landing). Nodules carry substantial water weight, so this is easy to game.

10. **Sec. 2(1)(A) ("recovery" undefined).** The definition keys on mineral recovered, not on seabed area disturbed, sediment plumes, or discharges. Much of the ecological harm occurs regardless of tonnage lifted, and test or pilot collection falls outside the definition.

11. **Sec. 2(1)(A) (200-meter depth and undefined terms).** The text doesn't say how depth is measured, and it never defines "deep seabed" or "mineral resources." Overbreadth is a risk too: oil and gas, sand and gravel, or other offshore activity could be swept in, which invites challenge.

12. **Sec. 2(2) (U.S. person).** The definition covers citizens and State-organized corporations only. It omits LLCs and other non-corporate entities, partnerships, nationals and permanent residents, entities organized in DC or the territories, and foreign subsidiaries controlled by U.S. persons. The last gap is the most significant, since it lets a company mine through a foreign shell. Add control-based and ownership-based coverage.

13. **Sec. 2(3) (covered vessel).** The 5,000 gross ton floor excludes smaller vessels, barges, and platforms. "Equipped for recovery" can be avoided by removing or relocating equipment, and "gross tons" doesn't specify the measurement convention. The vessel threshold also doesn't align with the 10,000-ton activity threshold.

14. **Missing definitions.** The bill leaves "operator," "exploration license," "environmental assessment," "United States," and "Secretary" undefined. Each is outcome-determinative somewhere in the bill.

## Prohibition (Sec. 3)

15. **Sec. 3(a) ("in the United States").** The text doesn't say whether the territorial sea, the EEZ, or the extended continental shelf count. Foreign persons mining in U.S. waters may not be clearly covered. Define "United States" to include all of these.

16. **Sec. 3(a) (scope of conduct).** The bill prohibits only the mining itself. It doesn't reach processing, importing, purchasing, transporting, financing, or insuring seabed minerals extracted elsewhere, so mineral from foreign operations could still enter U.S. supply chains.

17. **Sec. 3 (no bar on new licenses or permits).** The bill doesn't stop agencies from issuing new exploration licenses, commercial recovery permits, or leases. Because Sec. 3(b) grandfathers licenses issued before enactment, there is an incentive to rush issuance before the bill passes. Add an explicit moratorium on new issuance and a rule that later-issued authorizations are void to the extent they conflict.

18. **Sec. 3 (relationship to existing law).** The bill doesn't amend, repeal, or override DSHMRA, OCSLA minerals authorities, or any pending applications. Conflicting authorizations could remain on the books, and operators could argue the older, more specific statutes control. Add conforming amendments and an express supremacy clause.

## Registry (Sec. 4)

19. **Sec. 4(a) (registration mechanics).** There is no deadline for registering, no rulemaking authority, and no consequence for not registering. "Operator" is undefined, and foreign-flagged vessels owned or chartered by U.S. persons aren't addressed.

20. **Sec. 4(b) (reporting).** Reports are self-certified, with no verification, audit, inspection, onboard observers, or vessel tracking requirement. They also cover only registered vessels, so anything below the 5,000-ton line is invisible.

21. **Sec. 4 (transparency).** Nothing requires public release of registry data or reports. Proprietary-information claims could shield them from the public and from Congress, which undermines the monitoring role of advocacy groups.

22. **Sec. 4 and Sec. 5 (no sanctions for registry violations).** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3. Failing to register, filing late, or filing false reports carries no stated civil penalty. False statements might be reachable under 18 U.S.C. 1001, but the bill shouldn't rely on that.

## Penalties (Sec. 5)

23. **Sec. 5(a) (date conflict).** The penalty applies to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but Sec. 3 doesn't take effect until 180 days later (Sec. 8). That creates a retroactivity or ex post facto argument and real confusion about what is unlawful during the gap. Conform the dates.

24. **Sec. 5(a) (civil penalty mechanics).** There is no designated assessing authority, notice-and-hearing process, or judicial review provision. It also has no inflation adjustment, statute of limitations, injunctive relief, or procedure for recovering penalties.

25. **Sec. 5 (no remedies beyond fines).** The bill lacks forfeiture of vessels, equipment, and minerals, as well as disgorgement of proceeds and permit or registry revocation. At $1 million a day, a high-value operation may still treat fines as a cost of doing business.

26. **Sec. 5(b) ("willfully").** The text doesn't say what the defendant must know, such as the facts, the law, or the status of the activity. When liability can turn on the Secretary's discretionary finding under Sec. 2(1)(B), this makes prosecution hard and invites a vagueness defense.

27. **Sec. 5 (no corporate or officer liability provisions).** Nothing addresses parent-company liability, responsible corporate officers, or liability for knowingly financing or directing prohibited activity through subsidiaries.

28. **Sec. 5 (no citizen suit).** There is no private or citizen enforcement mechanism, so enforcement depends entirely on executive-branch willingness. A citizen suit provision with fee shifting would address this.

29. **Sec. 5(b) (extraterritorial prosecution).** Sec. 3(a) reaches U.S. persons "anywhere," but the bill has no venue or jurisdictional provisions for conduct abroad. Counsel should check whether existing law fills the gap.

## International agreement (Sec. 6)

30. **Sec. 6 (hortatory and ambiguous).** "Shall seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months" is unclear about whether the deadline applies to starting talks or concluding them. There are no reporting requirements, consequences, or link to the ISA process, and the U.S. is not a party to UNCLOS.

31. **Sec. 6 ("equivalent to those in section 3").** Any agreement modeled on Sec. 3 would import all of the loopholes above, including the exemptions and thresholds. Describe the intended international standard directly instead.

32. **Sec. 6 (form of agreement).** The text doesn't say whether the agreement is a treaty requiring advice and consent or an executive agreement. That affects durability and Senate involvement.

## Duration and effective date (Secs. 7-8)

33. **Sec. 7(a) ("serious harm").** "Serious harm" is undefined and is a weak, easily contested standard. The test is whether mining "can be conducted" without it, not whether it has been shown, with no precautionary or burden-of-proof language. It is also inconsistent with CADM's position that no one currently knows how to mine without irreversible damage. Consider a higher, more specific standard.

34. **Sec. 7(a) (National Academies report).** There is no requirement for who commissions the report, when, or on what questions, and no minimum content or independence. "Acting on a report" lets Congress rely on any report, including a narrow or industry-influenced one.

35. **Sec. 7(a) (all-or-nothing lifting).** A joint resolution would lift the whole prohibition with no accompanying licensing, environmental, or benefit-sharing regime. At minimum, require that any lifting be paired with a regulatory framework.

36. **Sec. 7(b) (no savings clause).** When the Act expires, nothing preserves liability for violations that occurred during its life. Add a savings clause so enforcement and penalties survive.

37. **Sec. 8 (180-day delay).** This gives operators six months to accelerate operations, obtain licenses, or stockpile, and it is out of sync with the enactment-date references in Secs. 3(b) and 5(a). Shorten it or add interim measures covering the gap.

## Structural and litigation-vulnerability gaps

38. **No findings or constitutional authority statement.** The bill has no findings or purpose section tying it to Commerce Clause, treaty, or Property Clause authority. That weakens its defense against challenges, especially to its extraterritorial reach.

39. **No severability clause.** If a court strikes the Sec. 2(1)(B) delegation or another provision, there is no instruction to preserve the rest. Add severability.

40. **No takings or compensation provision.** The bill doesn't address claims by license holders whose investments are affected. Setting out the legal position expressly (for example, that no vested right to commercial recovery exists under an exploration license) would reduce exposure.

41. **No rule of construction on other laws.** The bill doesn't say it supplements, rather than displaces, NEPA, the Endangered Species Act, the Marine Mammal Protection Act, and state laws. A savings clause would prevent an argument that it occupies the field or implicitly narrows other protections.
