RESPONSE:
Here are the weak spots I found, in section order. I worked only from the bill text you provided. I did not have the introduced version, legislative history, or CRS analysis, so the staff should check whether the October 6 manager's amendment changed any of these points. Some items may be deliberate policy choices, so the explainer may be better framed as open questions than as flaws.

1. **§2(1)(A), single-vessel threshold:** The 10,000-ton test applies "by a single vessel," so an operator could split production across several vessels that each stay under the line. The bill also doesn't say whether the tonnage is wet or dry, ore or nodules, or recovered or retained.

2. **§2(1)(A), "recovery" and "other mineral resources":** Neither term is defined. It is unclear whether non-nodule resources (such as crusts or sulfides) count, whether tests that recover material without retaining it count, and whether discharged sediment counts.

3. **§2(1), "commercial":** The defined term is purely quantitative and doesn't reference profit or commercial intent. A sub-threshold pilot or cost-recovery operation could argue it isn't "commercial."

4. **§2(1)(A), 200-meter depth floor:** Operations shallower than 200 meters fall outside the definition. Staff should confirm whether this is intentional, since it affects coastal and shelf operations.

5. **§2(1)(B), "Secretary":** The bill doesn't say which Secretary (Commerce, Interior, State, or another), and "Secretary" appears nowhere else in the bill except §6, where "Secretary of State" is used.

6. **§2(1)(B), "determines":** No standard, procedure, notice, or review process is provided for the Secretary's determination. This makes the catch-all vulnerable to vagueness and arbitrariness challenges and invites litigation.

7. **§2(1)(B), "comparable":** Nothing defines "comparable" or sets a metric for comparing risks to deep-sea ecosystems.

8. **§2(2), "U.S. person":** The definition covers citizens and State-chartered corporations only. It leaves out LLCs, partnerships, trusts, lawful permanent residents, foreign-incorporated subsidiaries of U.S. firms, and federally chartered entities, and "State" is undefined (so the status of D.C. and the territories is unclear).

9. **§2(3), "covered vessel":** "Gross tons" (a volume measure) is inconsistent with "metric tons" (a weight measure) in §2(1)(A). "Equipped for" is undefined, and the 5,000-gross-ton cutoff means smaller vessels can conduct large-scale recovery without registering.

10. **§3(a), "engage in":** The term is undefined and doesn't reach financing, insurance, chartering, equipment supply, or processing. The bill also has no aiding-and-abetting or conspiracy language.

11. **§3(a), "in the United States":** "United States" is undefined, so it is unclear whether the prohibition reaches the territorial sea or the Outer Continental Shelf, where the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (administered by BOEM) governs mineral leasing.

12. **§3(a), extraterritorial reach:** The "anywhere" prong covers only U.S. persons. Foreign persons and foreign-flagged vessels operating abroad are outside the prohibition unless they fall within the U.S. person definition.

13. **§3(a), "person":** "Person" is undefined, so it is unclear whether it includes individuals acting as corporate officers, governments, or foreign entities.

14. **§3(b), "exploration license" grandfather clause:** Exploration licenses don't authorize commercial recovery, but the clause could be read to protect commercial-scale mining conducted under one. The clause also sets no limits on tonnage, duration, renewal, transfer, or expansion, and it has no sunset for grandfathered operations.

15. **§3(b), relation to existing federal permitting:** The Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (1980, administered by NOAA) issues exploration licenses and commercial recovery permits as separate instruments. The bill doesn't say whether pending applications or existing commercial recovery permits are covered, and it doesn't address possible contract or takings claims. Staff should verify the current status of any applications before publishing.

16. **§3(c), environmental assessment carve-out:** The carve-out has no limits on scope, tonnage, duration, depth, or vessel type, and it requires no permit, notice, or reporting. Because "purpose" is subjective and hard to prove, this clause could swallow the prohibition.

17. **§4(a), "operator":** The term is undefined, so it is unclear whether the owner, charterer, parent company, or operator of record must register.

18. **§4(a), registration consequences:** The bill sets no registration deadline, required content, or penalty for failing to register. Section 5 penalizes only violations of §3, so failure to register is not an enforceable violation.

19. **§4(b), quarterly reports:** The bill sets no content requirements (tonnage, location, depth, disposal), no deadline, no public disclosure, no verification or audit, and no penalty for false certifications.

20. **§4(b), reporting gap:** Reports cover only "recovery activities," so operations claimed as environmental assessment under §3(c) would never be reported. This means the carve-out operates without transparency.

21. **§5(a), enforcement mechanism:** The bill designates no enforcing agency and provides no administrative penalty process, citizen suit provision, injunctive relief authority, or in rem remedy against vessels or equipment (which matters for vessels abroad).

22. **§5(a), "violation" and "each day":** The bill doesn't define a violation or explain how daily penalties are calculated for continuing operations. It also sets no limitations period, so the general five-year period under 28 U.S.C. §2462 would likely apply, which staff should verify.

23. **§5(a) vs. §8, conflicting effective dates:** Penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §8 delays the effect of the whole Act by 180 days. Conduct during that window could be penalized even though §3 isn't yet in force.

24. **§5(b), criminal standard:** "Willfully" is undefined, and the bill has no responsible-corporate-officer provision or knowledge standard. It also doesn't cover false statements in §4 reports or failures to register.

25. **§6, "shall seek to negotiate":** The clause doesn't require concluding an agreement, reporting to Congress, or any consequence for failure. It is also unclear whether the 18-month deadline applies to starting or concluding negotiations.

26. **§6, "equivalent" prohibitions:** The standard of equivalence is undefined, so a weaker agreement could be treated as satisfying the requirement. The bill also doesn't say whether an agreement would supersede domestic law or require Senate ratification.

27. **§6, International Seabed Authority:** The bill doesn't mention the ISA or address how U.S. law would interact with ISA rules. The U.S. is not a party to UNCLOS, so this gap matters for enforceability and for any negotiation.

28. **§7(a), "serious harm":** The term is undefined, and the bill sets no burden of proof or standard for the NASEM report. It also sets no deadline for the report and no funding for NASEM.

29. **§7(a), "finds by joint resolution":** A joint resolution normally requires presentment to the President under Article I, but the bill doesn't say whether this one would be binding or merely a finding. The clause also sets no expedited procedures for congressional action.

30. **§7(a) vs. §7(b), internal conflict:** Section 7(a) makes the prohibition indefinite, but §7(b) ends the entire Act after 10 years. As drafted, the operative duration is 10 years regardless of any NASEM report.

31. **§7(b), sunset mechanics:** The 10-year clock runs from enactment, but §8 delays effect by 180 days, so the prohibition would be in force for about 9.5 years. There is also no savings clause for penalties, investigations, or violations that occurred before the sunset.

32. **§8, phased effective date:** The 180-day delay applies to the whole Act, but §§3(b) and 5(a) are keyed to enactment. The bill has no transition rules for vessels under construction or in transit, and it doesn't say when registration obligations begin.

33. **General, no rulemaking authority:** The bill refers to NOAA in §4 but doesn't grant regulatory authority, designate an enforcing agency, or authorize appropriations. Implementation would depend on agencies acting under other authorities.

34. **General, missing definitions:** Section 2 doesn't define "United States," "State," "Secretary," "person," "operator," "recovery," or "engage in," which is a significant drafting gap for an enforcement statute.

35. **General, no severability or savings clause:** If a court struck §2(1)(B) as vague, the bill has no severability clause to clarify whether the 10,000-ton test would survive alone.

36. **General, no statement of relation to existing law:** The bill doesn't address the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act, OCSLA, or other federal permitting regimes, and it includes no preemption clause or conforming amendments. This invites conflict and litigation.

37. **General, no findings or purpose section:** Courts often use purpose to interpret ambiguous terms. Without findings, the "comparable" test in §2(1)(B) and the carve-out in §3(c) have little interpretive guidance.
