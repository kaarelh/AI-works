# S. 2817: Loopholes, Ambiguities, and Drafting Problems

Issues are grouped by theme and cited by section. Many are judgment calls about how a court or agency *might* read the text, so the explainer should frame them as open questions and ideally get responses from the sponsor's office and committee staff.

## Definitions (Sec. 2)

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A): "single training run" is easy to get around.** A developer could split training into several runs, each under 10^26 operations, or continue training an existing model, and argue no single run crossed the line. The bill also doesn't say whether pretraining and later stages (fine-tuning, reinforcement learning) count together.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A): The compute threshold is fixed, with no way to update it.** As training methods get more efficient, models trained below 10^26 operations may match or exceed today's larger ones. No agency can adjust the number. The bill also doesn't say how to count operations or who verifies the count.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A) vs. (B): "model" vs. "system" mismatch.** Clause (A) covers a *model* above the threshold, while (B) and Sec. 3 refer to *systems*. Several sub-threshold models combined into one system (ensembles, agent setups) may escape (A) entirely.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A): Possible overbreadth.** The bill is titled as a superintelligence ban, but (A) prohibits any model above 10^26 operations, whatever its capabilities. Challengers could argue the threshold isn't tailored to the stated purpose, and the mismatch may draw industry opposition at markup.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(B): "the Secretary" is never identified.** The Act refers to the Department of Commerce (Sec. 4) and the Secretary of State (Sec. 6), but never says which Secretary makes capability determinations. That opens a basic challenge to the authority of any determination.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B): The capability standard is vague and has no process.** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no defined benchmarks, and there is no notice, comment, or appeal process. Because Sec. 5(b) attaches criminal penalties, this invites a void-for-vagueness challenge, and possibly a nondelegation argument.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(B): A determination can only come after the fact.** A system's capabilities can usually be assessed only after it is trained. A developer could therefore complete training lawfully and argue that penalties can't apply to conduct before the Secretary's determination.

8. **Sec. 2: "Artificial intelligence" is undefined.** Every covered category depends on the term, so its scope is open to litigation.

9. **Sec. 2(2): "U.S. person" leaves out permanent residents and many entities.** The definition covers citizens and corporations "organized under the laws of a State." That appears to exclude lawful permanent residents, LLCs and partnerships (arguably), entities organized in DC or U.S. territories, and federally chartered entities.

10. **Sec. 2(2): Foreign subsidiaries offer an offshore route.** A U.S. company's foreign-incorporated subsidiary is not a "U.S. person," so it could train a covered system abroad. Liability would turn on whether the U.S. parent or its staff "develop" the system, which the bill doesn't define.

11. **Sec. 2(3): Key terms are undefined, and the threshold is easy to stay under.** "Data center," "advanced AI chips," and "containing" are undefined. An operator could keep each site under 50,000 chips, or train across several sites, to avoid registering.

## Prohibition (Sec. 3)

12. **Sec. 3(a): "Train, develop, or deploy" are undefined.** "Develop" could reach anything from research papers to building chips. "Deploy" doesn't say whether it covers giving U.S. users access to a covered system run abroad by a foreign company that isn't "in the United States."

13. **Sec. 3(a): No ban on access, import, or use.** U.S. individuals and businesses could apparently use a covered system built and hosted abroad by a non-U.S. person, which limits the ban's practical effect.

14. **Sec. 3(a): Possible First Amendment challenge.** Courts have treated software code as protected speech in some contexts. If "develop" reaches publishing model weights, code, or research, a challenge is plausible.

15. **Sec. 3(b): The grandfather clause rewards a race to enactment.** Exempting any system "whose training began" before enactment invites companies to start large runs before the bill becomes law. "Began" is undefined, so even a token start might qualify.

16. **Sec. 3(b): Grandfathered systems have no limits.** Once exempt, a system may be trained further, scaled up, and deployed for the life of the Act. The bill doesn't say whether fine-tuned or successor versions of a grandfathered system are also exempt.

17. **Sec. 3(c): The safety-evaluation exemption could swallow the rule.** Any activity "conducted for the purpose of safety evaluation" is exempt. There is no definition, approval process, oversight, size limit, or ban on later deployment, so a developer could describe nearly any training run as safety work.

## Registry (Sec. 4)

18. **Sec. 4: Nothing enforces the registry.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3, so failing to register, skipping reports, or filing false ones carries no penalty under this Act. A general federal false-statements law might apply to false certifications.

19. **Sec. 4(a): No registration deadline and no definition of "operator."** For leased or cloud facilities, the owner, the lessee, and the cloud customer could each argue that someone else is the operator.

20. **Sec. 4(b): The reports are thin and can't be verified.** "Describing the computing workloads" sets no required detail. Commerce gets no authority to inspect, subpoena, audit, or write rules. The bill also doesn't address confidentiality of trade-secret information, which may make companies resist reporting.

21. **Sec. 4: The registry doesn't connect to the ban.** Facility registration doesn't require reporting whether a covered system is being trained. Training spread across many unregistered sub-50,000-chip facilities would never show up.

## Penalties and Enforcement (Sec. 5)

22. **Sec. 5: No enforcing agency or procedure.** The Act doesn't say who brings civil actions (Commerce, DOJ, or another agency), whether penalties are assessed administratively or in court, or what investigative powers exist. There is also no funding.

23. **Sec. 5(a): "Each day on which the violation occurs" is unclear for one-time acts.** It's unclear how daily penalties apply to developing a system, as opposed to ongoing training or deployment. For large firms, $1 million per day may be small relative to the commercial stakes.

24. **Sec. 5(a) vs. Sec. 8: The effective dates conflict.** Sec. 5(a) applies penalties to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but Sec. 8 makes the Act effective 180 days later. The penalty provision could reach conduct before the ban takes effect, inviting due-process and fair-notice challenges, or a court could simply resolve the conflict in defendants' favor.

25. **Sec. 5(b): Criminal liability rests on vague terms.** Prison terms of up to 10 years attach to undefined terms ("develop," "deploy," "safety evaluation") and to the open-ended capability standard in Sec. 2(1)(B). That increases both vagueness risk and the likelihood courts read the law narrowly.

## International Agreement (Sec. 6)

26. **Sec. 6: The negotiation mandate has no teeth.** "Shall seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months" is unclear on whether negotiations must *begin* or *conclude* by that date. "Seek" creates no enforceable duty, and there are no reporting requirements, consequences, or definition of "equivalent." The bill also doesn't say whether the result would be a Senate-ratified treaty or an executive agreement.

## Duration and Effective Date (Secs. 7–8)

27. **Sec. 7(a): The lifting mechanism is largely symbolic.** A joint resolution goes to the President and has the force of law, so it adds nothing Congress couldn't already do by statute. Nothing requires or funds the National Academies to write the report, and no court could review whether Congress "acted on" one.

28. **Sec. 7(a) vs. 7(b): Unclear interaction.** If Congress lifts the Sec. 3 ban early, it's unclear whether the Sec. 4 registry and the Sec. 5 penalties for past violations continue until the 10-year sunset.

29. **Sec. 7(b): The sunset has no savings clause.** Without language preserving pending cases, defendants could argue that prosecutions or penalty actions for pre-sunset violations lapse when the Act expires. Whether the general federal savings statute (1 U.S.C. § 109) covers an expiring statute is contested. The fixed end date also lets companies plan to wait it out.

30. **Sec. 8: The 180-day gap is a training window.** Training that begins after enactment but before the effective date isn't grandfathered under Sec. 3(b), but it isn't illegal yet either. That makes it unclear whether a system trained during the window can be deployed afterward.

## Omissions Across the Act

31. **No rulemaking authority, preemption clause, or whistleblower protection.** Without rulemaking, no agency can fill the gaps above. Without a preemption clause, the relationship to state AI laws is unclear. Without whistleblower protection, violations inside companies are hard to detect.
