# S. 2817 Issues for Senator Whitfield's Office

Items are grouped by section. The ones I'd prioritize are marked **[HIGH]**: they either let development continue or make the bill easy to challenge.

## Sec. 2: Definitions

1. **Sec. 2(1)(B): "Secretary" is undefined. [HIGH]** The Act never says which Secretary makes capability determinations, and Sec. 4 refers to the Department of Commerce without designating a lead official. Add a definition, presumably the Secretary of Commerce.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A): "Artificial intelligence," "AI model," and "AI system" are undefined.** The terms are also used inconsistently ("model" in (A), "system" in (B), "system" in Sec. 3). Companies could argue that a product built from multiple models, or a model plus scaffolding, is not the "model" that crossed the threshold.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A): The "single training run" threshold is easily evaded. [HIGH]** Developers could split training into multiple runs, such as pretraining, continued pretraining, fine-tuning, and RL post-training, each under 10^26 operations. The threshold should measure cumulative compute across all training of a model and its derivatives, including compute used to generate synthetic training data.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A): The compute threshold is fixed. [HIGH]** Algorithmic efficiency improves every year, so dangerously capable systems will eventually be trainable below 10^26 operations. Give the Secretary authority to lower the threshold by rule, but not to raise it, or index it to capability benchmarks.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(A): Distillation and derivative models are not covered.** A model trained to imitate a covered or grandfathered model with under 10^26 operations is outside the definition. Add "derivative of a covered AI system" to the definition.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B): The capability standard is vague. [HIGH]** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no defined benchmarks, process, or timeline. Because criminal liability rests on this definition, it invites void-for-vagueness and nondelegation challenges and gives the Secretary nothing concrete to apply.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(B): A determination can only come after the system exists.** Capability can generally be assessed only after training. So the prohibition on "training" or "developing" such a system can't operate in advance, and a company could argue it had no notice before the determination. Require notice-and-comment rulemaking that sets capability criteria in advance and lets determinations apply to systems in development.

8. **Sec. 2(2): The "U.S. person" definition has gaps. [HIGH]** It omits lawful permanent residents, LLCs, partnerships, trusts, entities organized under D.C. or territorial law, and federally chartered entities. Most importantly, it omits foreign subsidiaries controlled by U.S. companies. A U.S. lab could train abroad through a foreign affiliate.

9. **Sec. 2(3): "Advanced AI chips," "data center," and "operator" are undefined.** Without definitions, the registry's scope is contestable from day one.

10. **Sec. 2(3): A chip-count threshold is easy to evade. [HIGH]** An operator can split capacity across facilities of 49,999 chips each, and distributed training across multiple sites makes per-site counts irrelevant. Chip counts also become less meaningful as each chip gets more powerful. Define coverage by aggregate compute capacity, aggregate across commonly controlled or networked facilities, and allow the threshold to be adjusted by rule.

## Sec. 3: Prohibition

11. **Sec. 3(a): The prohibited acts are undefined and incomplete. [HIGH]** "Train," "develop," and "deploy" are undefined. The section also doesn't reach possessing, transferring, publishing, or exporting model weights, or providing compute to someone else who trains a covered system, such as a cloud provider renting capacity. Add these acts plus explicit attempt, conspiracy, and aiding-and-abetting liability for the civil provisions.

12. **Sec. 3(a): The definition is circular in timing.** Under Sec. 2(1)(A), a model becomes "covered" only once it has been trained past 10^26 operations. Defendants can argue no violation occurs until training is complete. Prohibit conducting or initiating any training run that is reasonably expected to exceed the threshold.

13. **Sec. 3(a): Foreign-hosted systems serving U.S. users are not covered.** A foreign company with no U.S. presence could train a covered system abroad and offer it to U.S. customers over an API, apparently without violating the Act. Consider prohibiting offering or providing access to covered systems in the United States, and prohibiting U.S. persons from purchasing or facilitating such access.

14. **Sec. 3(a): Government activity may be excluded.** Under the general presumption that "person" doesn't include the sovereign, federal agencies and national labs are probably not covered. Contractors acting for them may also claim exemption. State explicitly whether the federal government is covered.

15. **Sec. 3(a): There is no anti-evasion clause.** Add a general prohibition on structuring activities to evade the Act, such as splitting runs, splitting facilities, or offshoring through affiliates.

16. **Sec. 3(b): The grandfather clause is open-ended. [HIGH]** Any covered system whose training "began" before enactment is permanently exempt, including systems meeting the capability definition in 2(1)(B). The exemption has no limit on further training, scaling, or deployment. This creates a strong incentive to start enormous runs before enactment.

17. **Sec. 3(b): "Training began" is ambiguous. [HIGH]** Companies can argue that continued training, fine-tuning, or scaling up an existing checkpoint is part of a run that "began" before enactment. Limit the exemption to deploying systems as they existed on a fixed date, set before introduction, and bar further training of grandfathered systems above the threshold.

18. **Sec. 3(c): The safety-evaluation exemption is self-certified and unlimited. [HIGH]** "Purpose of safety evaluation" is undefined. The exemption requires no approval, oversight, or reporting, and it doesn't bar later deployment of the system. As written, any lab could characterize frontier training as safety evaluation. Limit it to evaluating already-lawful systems, require prior approval and supervision by the Secretary, and prohibit training new covered systems under it.

## Sec. 4: Registry

19. **Sec. 4: There are no penalties for registry violations. [HIGH]** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3. Failing to register, filing late, or filing a false or incomplete report carries no penalty under this Act; 18 U.S.C. § 1001 may cover false statements, but that's indirect. Extend Sec. 5 to Sec. 4 and add an explicit false-certification offense.

20. **Sec. 4(a): There is no registration deadline or procedure.** Specify a deadline, such as 30 days after the effective date or after a facility first crosses the threshold, and the required contents.

21. **Sec. 4(b): "Describing the computing workloads" is too vague to enforce.** Specify required fields: customers, compute used per job, training-run identifiers, and model-weight custody.

22. **Sec. 4: Monitoring relies on self-reporting.** Quarterly self-certified reports with no audits, inspections, or know-your-customer obligations won't catch violations, especially distributed runs. Add inspection authority, independent audits, customer screening, and a duty to report suspected covered training runs.

23. **Sec. 4: There is no confidentiality provision.** Industry will object to disclosing proprietary information and may litigate over FOIA exposure. A confidentiality protection, with a FOIA exemption under 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(3), removes that argument.

## Sec. 5: Penalties

24. **Sec. 5(a): The penalty date conflicts with the effective date. [HIGH]** Penalties apply "on or after the date of enactment," but under Sec. 8 the Act takes effect 180 days later. This creates confusion and possible retroactivity challenges. Key penalties to the effective date.

25. **Sec. 5(a): The per-day penalty fits poorly with one-time acts.** It's unclear how "each day on which the violation occurs" applies to discrete acts like "develop." The cap of "not more than" $1,000,000 is also small relative to frontier-lab budgets. Consider a penalty tied to compute used or revenue, plus disgorgement.

26. **Sec. 5: There are no injunctive or forfeiture remedies. [HIGH]** The Act doesn't authorize injunctions, seizure of compute, or forfeiture or destruction of model weights. A company could pay fines and keep the system. Add these remedies explicitly.

27. **Sec. 5: No enforcement authority is designated.** The Act doesn't say who brings civil actions (for example, the Attorney General), in which court, or whether penalties are assessed administratively. It also grants no investigative or subpoena authority.

28. **Sec. 5(b): "Willfully" sets a high bar.** Under *Bryan v. United States*, "willfully" generally requires knowledge that the conduct was unlawful. Combined with the vague capability standard in 2(1)(B), this makes convictions hard and vagueness challenges likely. Consider "knowingly" for clearly defined conduct, such as threshold-based training.

29. **Sec. 5: There are no whistleblower protections.** Insider reporting is probably the most realistic way violations will surface. Add anti-retaliation protections and a reporting channel.

## Sec. 6: International Agreement

30. **Sec. 6: The 18-month deadline is ambiguous.** It's unclear whether the deadline is to begin or to conclude negotiations, and "shall seek to" imposes no enforceable obligation. Add reporting requirements to Congress and clarify the deadline. "Equivalent" prohibitions is also undefined.

## Sec. 7: Duration

31. **Sec. 7(b): The 10-year sunset ends the prohibition regardless of safety. [HIGH]** Companies only need to wait, and could prepare compute and research to resume immediately after. This conflicts with the Act's own premise in 7(a). Strike the sunset or apply it only to non-core provisions.

32. **Sec. 7(a) vs. 7(b): The two subsections contradict each other.** 7(a) says the prohibition remains until Congress finds safety, while 7(b) ends the Act at 10 years. The conflict creates interpretive ambiguity.

33. **Sec. 7(a): The National Academies are never directed to act.** Nothing requires or funds them to produce the report the section relies on. Since a joint resolution must be signed by the President, 7(a) adds little beyond ordinary repeal.

34. **Sec. 7(b): There is no savings clause.** Expiration could disrupt pending prosecutions and penalty actions. Whether the general savings statute (1 U.S.C. § 109) covers expiring statutes is not fully settled, so add an explicit savings clause.

## Sec. 8: Effective Date

35. **Sec. 8: The 180-day window invites a race. [HIGH]** Between enactment and the effective date, starting covered training runs is legal, which encourages a rush to start and complete them. Make the prohibition, or at least a freeze on new runs above the threshold, effective on enactment, and keep the delay for registry obligations.

## Missing Provisions

36. **No congressional findings or Commerce Clause basis.** Findings on interstate commerce, national security, and catastrophic risk would strengthen the bill against Commerce Clause and First Amendment challenges, such as arguments that code or model weights are speech.

37. **No severability clause.** Given likely challenges to 2(1)(B) and other provisions, a severability clause protects the rest of the Act.

38. **No general rulemaking authority.** The Secretary needs explicit authority to issue regulations that define terms, update thresholds, and implement the registry.

39. **No preemption or savings clause for state law.** State whether stricter state laws are preserved, which avoids litigation over implied preemption.

40. **"United States" is undefined for geographic scope.** Clarify whether territories are included.
