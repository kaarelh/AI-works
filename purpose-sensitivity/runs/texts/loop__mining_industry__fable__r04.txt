RESPONSE:
Below is a section-by-section review of S. 2817 as amended. Items are grouped by section and numbered continuously. I've flagged where an issue is a genuine gap (conduct that stays lawful as written) versus a drafting or workability defect, and I've noted a few places where the argument cuts both ways so your legal team isn't surprised.

## Sec. 2(1)(A) — Definition of "commercial deep seabed mining"

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "by a single vessel."** The 10,000 t/yr threshold is measured per vessel, not per operator, project, or contract area. An operator running several vessels each recovering under 10,000 t/yr (or rotating vessels mid-year) is outside the definition entirely, so aggregate production is uncapped.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "per year" is undefined.** The bill does not say calendar year, fiscal year, rolling 12 months, or year from the effective date, which makes it unclear when a vessel "crosses" the threshold and when a violation begins.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "metric tons" has no measurement basis.** Nodules are recovered wet with high water and sediment content; the bill does not say whether the threshold is wet weight, dry weight, or contained metal, nor who measures or verifies it.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "recovery" is undefined.** It is unclear whether "recovery" occurs at seabed collection, at the riser/surface, at transfer to a shuttle vessel, or at landing, and whether seabed collector units (which are not "vessels") count as the recovering vessel. Subsea collectors feeding a surface vessel, or ore offloaded to transport vessels, create arguable gaps about which vessel's tonnage counts.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "polymetallic nodules or other mineral resources" is dangerously broad.** Under ordinary usage (and OCSLA's definition), "mineral resources" of the seabed include oil and gas, and a single deepwater FPSO or production platform in >200 m recovers far more than 10,000 t/yr of hydrocarbons from the seabed. As written the bill arguably prohibits deepwater oil and gas production — a strong "unworkable as drafted" point.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — only "recovery" is regulated.** Processing, refining, transporting, importing, financing, insuring, chartering, or selling nodules is not covered. Nothing stops a U.S. refinery from processing nodules mined by a non-U.S. entity.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "depths greater than 200 meters" is ambiguous.** It does not specify water depth at the point of recovery versus depth of the resource in the seabed, and does not address resources straddling the 200 m line.

## Sec. 2(1)(B) — Secretary's "comparable risk" determination

8. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — "the Secretary" is never defined.** The Act names the Secretary of State (Sec. 6) and NOAA (Sec. 4, within Commerce), but no section identifies which Secretary makes determinations under (B). A criminal prohibition keyed to a determination by an unidentified official is a serious drafting error and a strong vagueness argument.

9. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — no procedure, standard, or review.** There is no requirement of notice-and-comment rulemaking, no definition of "risks to deep-sea ecosystems," no factors, no judicial review provision, and no requirement that the determination precede the conduct. Criminal liability (Sec. 5(b)) that turns on an after-the-fact, standardless agency determination raises void-for-vagueness, fair-notice, and nondelegation objections.

10. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — cuts both ways.** Note that (B) is also the government's tool to close the single-vessel loophole in item 1: a Secretary could declare that multi-vessel operations under the threshold pose "comparable risks." Any reliance on fleet-splitting should account for that risk.

## Sec. 2(2) — "U.S. person"

11. **Sec. 2(2) — entity forms other than corporations are excluded.** "A corporation organized under the laws of a State" omits LLCs, limited partnerships, general partnerships, trusts, and other unincorporated entities. A Delaware LLC is not a "U.S. person" under this text.

12. **Sec. 2(2) — corporations organized under federal law, D.C., or territorial law are excluded.** "Laws of a State" does not reach federally chartered corporations or entities organized in the District of Columbia, Puerto Rico, Guam, etc.

13. **Sec. 2(2) — foreign subsidiaries are not U.S. persons.** A foreign-organized subsidiary (even if wholly owned and controlled by a U.S. parent) operating foreign-flagged vessels on the high seas is neither a "U.S. person" nor a "person in the United States," so it falls outside Sec. 3(a) entirely. This is the single largest gap in the bill. (Caution: prosecutors could attempt to reach U.S. parent officers via 18 U.S.C. §§ 2 and 371 for the criminal offense, but there is no comparable secondary liability for the civil penalty.)

14. **Sec. 2(2) — individuals.** "Citizen of the United States" excludes lawful permanent residents and U.S. nationals, but conversely it means individual U.S.-citizen employees, officers, and crew of a foreign operator could be personally liable while their employer is not — an incoherent result that is worth highlighting as a workability problem.

## Sec. 2(3) — "Covered vessel"

15. **Sec. 2(3) — 5,000 GT threshold.** Vessels at or under 5,000 gross tons are not "covered vessels" and are outside Sec. 4 entirely, regardless of what they recover.

16. **Sec. 2(3) — "equipped for the recovery" is vague and static.** It does not say when the vessel must be equipped (at registration? at any time?), whether a vessel that is later outfitted must register, or whether a general-purpose support vessel qualifies.

17. **Sec. 2(3) — no jurisdictional nexus.** The definition is not limited to U.S.-flagged vessels or U.S.-person operators, so Sec. 4 literally purports to require every mining-equipped vessel in the world to register with NOAA — unenforceable as to foreign operators and a clear extraterritoriality problem.

## Sec. 3 — Prohibition

18. **Sec. 3(a) — "engage in" is undefined.** It does not reach aiding, abetting, contracting for, financing, or directing mining, so a U.S. person that contracts with or funds a non-U.S. operator is not clearly covered.

19. **Sec. 3(a) — "in the United States" does not clearly include the EEZ or outer continental shelf.** A foreign operator mining the U.S. OCS or EEZ beyond the territorial sea is arguably not "in the United States," leaving domestic seabed resources less protected than those on the high seas.

20. **Sec. 3(a) — "person" is undefined.** The bill defines "U.S. person" but not "person," so the first prong's scope (natural persons? entities? foreign governments?) depends on the Dictionary Act default (1 U.S.C. § 1), which is an avoidable ambiguity in a criminal statute.

21. **Sec. 3(b) — "exploration license" has no issuing authority.** It does not say whether the license must be a NOAA license under the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (DSHMRA), an International Seabed Authority contract (which the ISA calls a "contract," not a "license"), or a foreign-state license. Any pre-enactment exploration authorization from any issuer arguably qualifies.

22. **Sec. 3(b) — the grandfather clause is internally illogical.** Exploration licenses by definition authorize exploration, not commercial recovery, so either the exemption is empty (nothing to grandfather) or it must be read to exempt commercial mining conducted in an area covered by a pre-enactment exploration license. The latter reading would exempt most existing contract areas worldwide; the former makes the subsection surplusage. Conversely, DSHMRA *commercial recovery permits* issued before enactment are not grandfathered at all — presumably inverted from what the drafters intended.

23. **Sec. 3(b) — no durational or scope limits.** The exemption does not require the license to remain valid, does not cap tonnage, and does not address renewals, extensions, amendments, or expansions of a pre-enactment license. A license issued before enactment and later renewed or expanded arguably remains covered.

24. **Sec. 3(b) — pre-enactment window.** The exemption is keyed to licenses "issued before the date of enactment," so licenses obtained between now and enactment qualify, creating an incentive (and opportunity) to secure additional exploration licenses before the President signs.

25. **Sec. 3(c) — "environmental assessment" is unbounded.** There is no definition, tonnage cap, duration, certification, or approval requirement. Collector trials, bulk sampling, and pilot-scale recovery conducted to generate baseline or impact data for an EIS or ISA environmental plan are literally "for the purpose of environmental assessment," and the clause does not address mixed purposes or whether recovered material may be sold.

## Sec. 4 — Registry

26. **Sec. 4 — no penalty.** Sec. 5 penalizes violations of Sec. 3 only; nothing in the Act penalizes failing to register or failing to report. The registry is unenforceable.

27. **Sec. 4(a) — no deadline, no rulemaking authority, no implementation.** The bill does not say when registration is due, give NOAA authority to issue regulations or forms, or appropriate funds.

28. **Sec. 4(b) — compelled self-reporting of a crime.** Requiring operators to certify quarterly descriptions of "recovery activities" — activities that may be felonies under Secs. 3 and 5(b) — invites a Fifth Amendment self-incrimination challenge (the *Marchetti/Grosso* line of cases) and no immunity or use restriction is provided.

29. **Sec. 4(b) — "operator" and "recovery activities" are undefined**, and the report requirement is not tied to the 10,000-ton threshold, so it apparently applies to exploration, sampling, and environmental-assessment activity that is lawful under Sec. 3(c).

## Sec. 5 — Penalties

30. **Sec. 5(a) — direct conflict with Sec. 8.** Civil penalties attach to violations "occurring on or after the date of enactment," but the prohibition does not take effect until 180 days after enactment (Sec. 8). Either no violation can occur in that window (making the clause surplusage) or the drafters intended retroactive penalties for conduct that was lawful when done — a due process problem, and an ex post facto problem if the same logic is applied to Sec. 5(b).

31. **Sec. 5(a) — per-day penalty does not fit an annual threshold.** Because the offense is defined by exceeding 10,000 t in a "year," it is unclear whether the "violation" occurs only on the day the threshold is crossed, on every day of recovery thereafter, or on every day of the year retroactively. The maximum exposure is indeterminate.

32. **Sec. 5(a) — no enforcement mechanism.** The Act does not say which agency assesses the civil penalty, whether it is assessed administratively or by a court, what procedure or factors apply, whether there is judicial review, or what the limitations period is.

33. **Sec. 5(b) — "willfully" interacts badly with Sec. 2(1)(B).** Willfulness requires knowledge that conduct is unlawful; where unlawfulness depends on an unpublished or after-the-fact Secretary determination with no standard, willful violations will be nearly impossible to prove, and the vagueness objection in item 9 is strongest here.

## Sec. 6 — International agreement

34. **Sec. 6 — "seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months" is ambiguous.** It is unclear whether the Secretary of State must *begin* seeking negotiations or *conclude* an agreement within 18 months; either way there is no reporting requirement or consequence for noncompliance, so the section is hortatory.

35. **Sec. 6 — no interface with the existing international regime.** The section ignores the UNCLOS/ISA framework (to which the U.S. is not a party) and does not say what happens to the domestic prohibition if an agreement with different or weaker terms is reached, or if none is.

## Sec. 7 — Duration

36. **Sec. 7(a) — the "joint resolution" condition is legally meaningless.** A joint resolution is ordinary legislation requiring presentment, and Congress can always repeal or amend the Act; the subsection adds nothing except an implication that repeal by ordinary bill is somehow insufficient, which cannot bind a future Congress.

37. **Sec. 7(a) — the National Academies report is never commissioned.** No agency is directed to request the study, no funding is provided, and no deadline is set, so the stated pathway out of the prohibition may never be triggered. "Serious harm" is also undefined.

38. **Sec. 7(a) vs. 7(b) — internal conflict.** Subsection (a) says the prohibition "shall remain in effect until" a congressional finding, while (b) says the entire Act ceases to have effect after 10 years. If no finding is made, it is unclear whether the prohibition survives the sunset; the better reading is that (b) controls, but the ambiguity invites litigation.

39. **Sec. 7(b) — sunset of the whole Act.** The 10-year sunset terminates not just the prohibition but the registry and penalty provisions. Expiration of a temporary statute does not extinguish accrued penalties (1 U.S.C. § 109), but the bill is silent on pending proceedings, and the sunset runs from enactment while the prohibition starts 180 days later, so the effective prohibition period is 9½ years.

## Sec. 8 — Effective date and global issues

40. **Sec. 8 — inconsistent dating throughout.** Secs. 3(b), 5(a), 6, and 7(b) are all keyed to the "date of enactment," while the operative provisions take effect 180 days later; the bill never reconciles which obligations (registration, reporting, penalties) attach in the gap.

41. **No savings, repeal, or conforming amendment to DSHMRA.** The Act does not amend or repeal the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (30 U.S.C. § 1401 et seq.), under which NOAA retains authority to issue exploration licenses and commercial recovery permits. Two federal statutes would simultaneously authorize and prohibit the same activity, with no rule of priority.

42. **No severability clause.** Given the constitutional vulnerabilities in items 9, 28, and 30, the absence of a severability clause means a successful challenge to one provision could take down the whole Act — or, from the drafters' side, is something they will likely want to add at markup.

43. **No agency is given general rulemaking, enforcement, or implementation authority**, and no appropriation is made. Beyond the specific gaps above, the Act has no administrative machinery to run on.
