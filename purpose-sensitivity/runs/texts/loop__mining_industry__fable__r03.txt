RESPONSE:
Below is a section-by-section review. I've grouped items by section and flagged at the end which ones I think are strongest. Standard caveat: this is drafting analysis, not legal advice, and several of these are arguments rather than clear wins — courts read anti-evasion into statutes, and the criminal provision raises the stakes on anything that looks like deliberate structuring.

**Section 2(1)(A) — Definition of "commercial deep seabed mining"**

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — per-vessel threshold.** The 10,000 t/yr threshold applies "by a single vessel," so a fleet of vessels each recovering under 10,000 t/yr falls outside the definition entirely. There is no aggregation across vessels, operators, affiliates, or contract areas.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "per year" undefined.** The bill does not say calendar year, fiscal year, rolling 12 months, or the 12 months following the effective date. An operator could argue that volumes recovered before the effective date (Sec. 8) don't count, and the measurement window is contestable in any enforcement action.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — wet vs. dry weight.** "Metric tons" is unspecified; nodules lose a large share of their mass on drying. An operator could measure on a dry basis and recover well over 10,000 wet tons.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "recovery … by a single vessel."** If the seabed collector (crawler/ROV) rather than the surface ship is characterized as what "recovers" the nodules "from the seabed," one can argue the surface vessel is merely lifting and transporting, not recovering. This is a stretch, but the bill never defines "recovery" or "vessel."

5. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "or other mineral resources."** As written this captures any seabed mineral at >200 m, including deepwater oil and gas (an FPSO "recovers" far more than 10,000 t/yr), sand/gravel, and crusts/sulfides. This is a strong unworkability point: the bill on its face prohibits most deepwater hydrocarbon production in the Gulf of Mexico.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — depth reference point.** "At depths greater than 200 meters" does not specify whether that is the depth of the seabed, of the collector, or of the activity. Minor for nodules but another vagueness point.

**Section 2(1)(B) — Secretary's catch-all**

7. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — "the Secretary" is never defined.** The bill references the Secretary of State (Sec. 6) and NOAA, an agency of the Secretary of Commerce (Sec. 4), but no definitions section identifies which Secretary makes 2(1)(B) determinations. The catch-all may be inoperable until Congress or a court resolves this.

8. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — no process, standard, or publication requirement.** The Secretary may expand a criminal prohibition by "determination" with no rulemaking, notice, findings, judicial-review provision, or definition of "comparable" risk. This invites nondelegation and void-for-vagueness challenges, especially since Sec. 5(b) attaches up to 10 years' imprisonment to a definition the executive can rewrite.

9. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — no effective-date mechanism for determinations.** The bill does not say when a determination takes effect or whether conduct before a determination is published can be penalized, creating a due-process problem for any enforcement based on 2(1)(B).

**Section 2(2) — "U.S. person"**

10. **Sec. 2(2) — entity coverage is narrow.** "Corporation organized under the laws of a State" omits LLCs, LPs, partnerships, trusts, and other non-corporate entities, as well as corporations chartered under federal law or the laws of D.C. or a territory. A Delaware LLC operating on the high seas is arguably not a "U.S. person."

11. **Sec. 2(2) — foreign subsidiaries excluded.** There is no "owned or controlled by" language, so a subsidiary organized abroad (and its foreign-flagged vessel) is not a U.S. person even if wholly owned, managed, and financed from Houston. This is the most obvious structural gap.

12. **Sec. 2(2) — natural persons.** Only "citizens" are covered; U.S. nationals, permanent residents, and foreign nationals employed by the company are not U.S. persons (though citizens working aboard would be).

**Section 3 — Prohibition**

13. **Sec. 3(a) — "in the United States" undefined.** Mining does not occur on land, and the bill does not say whether "in the United States" includes the territorial sea, the EEZ, the outer continental shelf, or U.S.-flagged vessels. A non-U.S. person mining in the U.S. EEZ is arguably not "in the United States."

14. **Sec. 3(a) — "engage in" undefined.** The prohibition reaches the act of mining, not financing, chartering, contracting for, purchasing, importing, transporting, or processing nodules. A U.S. company can buy production from, or hold an equity stake in, a foreign operator with no apparent violation.

15. **Sec. 3(a) — "person" undefined.** Sec. 2 defines "U.S. person" but not "person," so the 1 U.S.C. § 1 default presumably applies to 3(a) and 5; the mismatch between the broad default and the narrow "U.S. person" definition is itself a drafting inconsistency, and federal and state governments are generally not "persons," leaving government-conducted recovery uncovered.

16. **Sec. 3(b) — "exploration license" undefined.** The bill does not say who must have issued the license (NOAA under DSHMRA, the ISA via a sponsoring state, or a foreign government), and it exempts "mining" — not just exploration — conducted under such a license. Read literally, any pre-enactment exploration license anywhere grandfathers full commercial recovery.

17. **Sec. 3(b) — no limits on the grandfather clause.** There is no tonnage cap, duration limit, or restriction on amendments, renewals, extensions, transfers, or area expansions of a pre-enactment license. It is also unclear whether mining under a commercial recovery permit that is later issued on the basis of a pre-enactment exploration license is still "conducted under" that license.

18. **Sec. 3(b)/Sec. 8 — timing window.** Grandfathering keys to licenses issued before "enactment," while the prohibition takes effect 180 days later, so there is an incentive to secure licenses now and a six-month period in which mining is lawful but newly issued licenses are not protected. The two dates should be aligned.

19. **Sec. 3(c) — environmental assessment exemption is open-ended.** "Environmental assessment" is undefined, there is no tonnage cap, and "for the purpose of" does not require it to be the sole or primary purpose. Collector trials and pilot-scale recovery can plausibly be characterized as assessment activity.

20. **Sec. 3 — no anti-evasion, attempt, or conspiracy provision.** The bill contains no language reaching conduct designed to circumvent it (e.g., restructuring to avoid "U.S. person" status), and 18 U.S.C. § 2 aiding-and-abetting applies only to the criminal provision.

**Section 4 — Registry**

21. **Sec. 4 — no deadline, procedure, or penalty.** There is no date by which registration or reports are due, no rulemaking authority, and Sec. 5 penalties apply only to violations of Sec. 3, so failure to register or report carries no statutory consequence.

22. **Sec. 4 — scope mismatch.** "Covered vessel" (>5,000 GT, "equipped for" recovery) has no nexus to U.S. persons, U.S. waters, or U.S. flag, so it purports to reach every such vessel in the world; meanwhile vessels under 5,000 GT, or with modular/removable gear, are not covered at all. "Operator" and "gross tons" (ITC 1969 GT vs. GRT) are undefined, and reports must cover "recovery activities," not "commercial deep seabed mining."

**Section 5 — Penalties**

23. **Sec. 5(a) — penalty reaches back before the effective date.** Civil penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but Sec. 3 does not take effect until 180 days later (Sec. 8), so no violation can occur in that window. The provision is either surplusage or an attempt at retroactive liability that a court would not enforce.

24. **Sec. 5(a) — "each day on which the violation occurs" with an annual threshold.** Because the definition is 10,000 t/yr, the violation arguably only "occurs" on days after the threshold is crossed; the first 10,000 tons per vessel per year carry no penalty, and the per-day count is ambiguous.

25. **Sec. 5 — no enforcement machinery.** No agency is designated to assess civil penalties, there is no administrative process, judicial-review provision, injunctive authority, forfeiture, seizure, or citizen-suit provision, and no limitations period (28 U.S.C. § 2462's five-year default would apply).

26. **Sec. 5(b) — "willfully."** The willfulness standard, combined with the vagueness of 2(1)(B) and the undefined terms above, makes criminal prosecution difficult where an operator has a good-faith reading that it falls outside the definition.

**Section 6 — International agreement**

27. **Sec. 6 — hortatory and ambiguous.** "Shall seek to negotiate … not later than 18 months" is unenforceable, and it is unclear whether the 18 months is a deadline to begin or to conclude negotiations. No reporting requirement or consequence attaches.

**Section 7 — Duration**

28. **Sec. 7(a) — no mandate for the National Academies report.** The prohibition lifts only on a joint resolution "acting on a report" of the National Academies, but nothing requires, funds, or sets a deadline for that report, and "serious harm" is undefined. The lifting mechanism may never be triggered.

29. **Sec. 7(a) vs. 7(b) — internal conflict.** 7(a) says the prohibition lasts until Congress finds mining safe; 7(b) says the entire Act ceases 10 years after enactment regardless. The sunset also terminates the registry, and since it runs from enactment while the prohibition runs from the effective date, the actual prohibition period is 9½ years.

**Section 8 and general drafting**

30. **Sec. 8 — 180-day window.** The delayed effective date allows continued recovery, stockpiling, corporate restructuring, and license acquisition before the prohibition bites.

31. **General — no interaction with existing law.** The bill neither amends nor repeals the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (30 U.S.C. § 1401 et seq.) or addresses OCSLA, so NOAA retains statutory authority to issue exploration licenses and commercial recovery permits that this Act purports to prohibit using. The two regimes conflict on their face.

32. **General — no severability clause.** If 2(1)(B) or the penalty provisions are struck as vague or an improper delegation, the bill is silent on whether the rest survives (courts generally presume severability, but the omission is a drafting gap worth noting).

---

**Strongest points for the markup:** the per-vessel/no-aggregation threshold (1), the foreign-subsidiary and LLC gaps in "U.S. person" (10–11), the undefined "Secretary" (7), the overbreadth capturing deepwater oil and gas (5), the unlimited exploration-license grandfather (16–17), the penalty/effective-date conflict (23), the 7(a)/7(b) contradiction (29), and the absence of any conforming change to DSHMRA (31). Items 4, 12, and 15 are weaker and I'd keep them in reserve.
