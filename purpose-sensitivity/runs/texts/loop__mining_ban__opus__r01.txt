# S. 2817 (as amended Oct. 6): Loopholes, Ambiguities, and Drafting Problems

**Top priorities for Senator Whitfield's office:**
- The Sec. 7 sunset (#33)
- The open-ended exemptions in Sec. 3(b) and 3(c) (#18–23)
- The per-vessel tonnage threshold (#1–2)
- The missing EEZ coverage (#13)
- The lack of any penalty for violating the registry (#24)
- The unrepealed licensing authorities (#16)

---

### Sec. 2: Definitions

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A): "By a single vessel."** An operator can spread recovery across several vessels, each staying under 10,000 tons a year, or rotate vessels mid-year. The threshold should apply to an operator and its affiliates in aggregate, across all vessels and equipment.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A): Threshold level.** A 10,000-ton annual allowance per vessel permits substantial pilot and test mining, which is the activity that generates seabed disturbance and sediment plumes. Consider a much lower threshold, or prohibiting all recovery outside narrowly permitted research.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A): Wet vs. dry tonnage.** The bill does not say whether tonnage is measured wet or dry, or before or after shipboard processing and discard. Nodules carry significant water content, so the choice changes how much mining fits under the cap and invites disputes over measurement.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A): "Per year."** The bill does not say whether this means a calendar year, a federal fiscal year, or any rolling 12-month period. Operators could concentrate activity across the boundary between two years.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(A): "Recovery" is undefined.** Seabed collectors can disturb, crush, or process material on the seafloor, or discharge it in the water column, without lifting it aboard. Under this wording, that activity may never count as "recovery." The prohibition should cover collection and disturbance, not only material brought to the surface.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(A): Non-vessel methods.** Tying the definition to a "vessel" leaves out recovery from fixed platforms, seabed-resident systems, or shore-based infrastructure.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(A): "Other mineral resources" and the depth measurement.** The phrase is undefined. It could be read narrowly to exclude crusts or sulfides, or broadly to reach oil, gas, or sand, which would generate opposition and litigation. It is also unclear whether "depths greater than 200 meters" is measured from the sea surface or by some other reference.

8. **Sec. 2(1)(B): "The Secretary" is undefined.** No department is named, here or anywhere in the Act. That ambiguity extends to every reference to the Secretary and to the agency split between the Secretary and NOAA in Sec. 4.

9. **Sec. 2(1)(B): Criminal liability turns on an executive determination.** Letting the Secretary define the scope of a crime ("comparable risks") without standards, procedures, or notice-and-comment invites vagueness and nondelegation challenges. The open-ended standard also lets a future administration decline to make any determinations. The bill should specify procedure, criteria, and publication requirements.

10. **Sec. 2(2): Foreign subsidiaries escape.** "U.S. person" covers only corporations organized under State law, so a U.S. company can conduct mining through a subsidiary incorporated abroad. Add entities owned or controlled by U.S. persons.

11. **Sec. 2(2): Entity types and jurisdictions left out.** The definition reaches only "corporations." It omits LLCs, partnerships, trusts, and other entities, as well as entities organized under the laws of D.C., the territories, or federal law. "State" is also undefined.

12. **Sec. 2(2): Permanent residents.** Lawful permanent residents and other U.S.-domiciled individuals are not covered when acting abroad.

### Sec. 3: Prohibition

13. **Sec. 3(a): "In the United States" is undefined.** It is unclear whether the bill reaches the territorial sea, the Exclusive Economic Zone, the continental shelf, or the territories. A foreign company mining in the U.S. EEZ (for example, off Hawaii, American Samoa, or Guam) may fall outside the prohibition entirely.

14. **Sec. 3(a): Facilitation, financing, and sponsorship are not covered.** The prohibition reaches only persons who "engage in" mining. It does not reach those who finance, insure, sponsor, charter vessels for, direct, or aid and abet mining by foreign entities. A U.S. parent company could argue that directing a foreign affiliate is not "engaging."

15. **Sec. 3(a): No import or processing ban.** Minerals mined from the deep seabed by non-U.S. persons can still be imported, processed, and sold in the United States. That preserves the commercial market the bill aims to eliminate.

16. **Sec. 3 (whole section): Existing licensing authority is not addressed.** The bill does not amend or repeal the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (NOAA licenses and permits) or the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (Interior leasing). It does not bar agencies from issuing new licenses, permits, or leases either. This leaves a statutory conflict that courts would have to resolve through implied repeal, which is a weak and litigable position.

17. **Sec. 3 (whole section): Inconsistent terms.** Sec. 3(a) prohibits "commercial deep seabed mining," while Sec. 3(b) exempts "mining." The bill should use defined terms consistently so the exemption cannot be read more broadly than the prohibition.

18. **Sec. 3(b): The exploration-license exemption swallows the rule.** The bill exempts *mining*, not just exploration, conducted under a pre-enactment exploration license. Holders could therefore conduct commercial-scale recovery. Exempt activity should be limited to exploration activities authorized by the license's original terms.

19. **Sec. 3(b): No time limit and no limit on renewals, amendments, or transfers.** The exemption is permanent. It also appears to follow the license through renewals, extensions, scope expansions, and sale to new operators.

20. **Sec. 3(b): Issuing authority is unspecified.** The bill does not say whether "exploration license" means only U.S. (NOAA) licenses or also International Seabed Authority contracts and foreign-sponsored licenses. The broader reading would exempt most companies now active.

21. **Sec. 3(b): Incentive to rush.** Because any license issued before enactment is grandfathered, companies have an incentive to obtain licenses before the bill passes. Use a fixed date tied to introduction or markup instead.

22. **Sec. 3(c): The environmental-assessment exemption is unbounded.** There is no definition, tonnage cap, permit requirement, oversight, or independent-research requirement. Test mining can simply be labeled "environmental assessment."

23. **Sec. 3(c): Purpose-based test.** Because the exemption turns on the actor's stated "purpose," the government would bear the hard burden of proving a company's motive. Replace it with objective criteria: a permit, scale limits, a non-commercial disposition of any recovered material, and public data release.

### Sec. 4: Registry

24. **Sec. 4: No penalty for violating the registry.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3. Failing to register, failing to report, or filing false reports carries no specific consequence beyond general false-statement law.

25. **Sec. 4(a): Coverage gap.** Vessels of 5,000 gross tons or less are exempt from registration, as are non-vessel systems. The bill gives no deadline to register and no jurisdictional scope, so it is unclear whether foreign-flagged vessels in the EEZ must register or whether the requirement purports to reach all vessels worldwide.

26. **Sec. 4(a)/2(3): "Equipped for the recovery" is ambiguous.** Operators could argue that a vessel is not "equipped" when its collector or riser is removable, leased, or carried on a separate vessel.

27. **Sec. 4(b): Reports are self-certified and unverifiable.** Reports need not include tonnage, location, or depth. The bill provides no vessel monitoring, observers, inspection, boarding, or record-keeping authority, so the 10,000-ton threshold cannot actually be verified.

28. **Sec. 4 / Sec. 5: No enforcement agency or authorities.** The bill does not say which agency enforces the prohibition. It provides no investigative, subpoena, or boarding powers, no Coast Guard role, and no forfeiture of vessels, equipment, or recovered minerals.

### Sec. 5: Penalties

29. **Sec. 5(a): Conflict with the effective date.** Penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but under Sec. 8 the Act takes effect 180 days later. This inconsistency will be litigated.

30. **Sec. 5(a): "Each day" does not fit an annual-tonnage offense.** A violation is defined by exceeding a yearly total, so it is unclear which days count. Is it the day the threshold is crossed, every day thereafter, or every day of the year?

31. **Sec. 5(a): No assessment procedure.** The bill does not say who assesses civil penalties, by what process, or with what hearing and judicial-review rights. That invites due-process challenges. The maximum penalty is also not adjusted for inflation and may be small relative to cargo value, and there is no disgorgement or forfeiture.

### Sec. 6: International Agreement

32. **Sec. 6: Weak and ambiguous mandate.** "Shall seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months" could mean begin or conclude, and either way it is unenforceable. The bill requires no reporting to Congress. Because the target is prohibitions "equivalent to those in section 3," the international goal would carry the same loopholes listed above.

### Sec. 7: Duration

33. **Sec. 7(a) vs. (b): The sunset defeats the permanent prohibition.** Subsection (a) keeps the ban in place until Congress makes a scientific finding, but subsection (b) ends the entire Act after 10 years regardless. This is the single biggest problem in the bill. Strike (b), or limit it to non-core provisions.

34. **Sec. 7(a): Undefined standard and unclear effect.** "Serious harm" is undefined. The bill does not say what the joint resolution does: whether it ends only Sec. 3 or the whole Act, or how the National Academies report is commissioned.

35. **Sec. 7(b): No savings clause.** When the Act ceases to have effect, pending enforcement actions and penalties for past violations may be extinguished.

### Sec. 8: Effective Date

36. **Sec. 8: 180-day open window.** Mining is lawful until the effective date. The bill also does not say whether the registry obligation and agency authority begin at enactment or at the effective date.

### Missing Provisions (whole Act)

37. **No severability clause.** If one provision falls, for example Sec. 2(1)(B) on delegation grounds, a court could strike more of the Act.

38. **No rulemaking authority.** The bill grants no regulatory authority to fill gaps such as measurement methods or the meaning of "equipped," and it says nothing about its relationship to State law.

39. **No citizen-suit or judicial-review provision.** Nothing allows outside parties to compel enforcement, and nothing channels challenges by industry, such as takings claims by license holders if Sec. 3(b) is narrowed, into a defined forum and timeline.
