RESPONSE:
# S. 2817 (as amended Oct. 6, 2026): Loopholes, Ambiguities, and Drafting Problems

Some of these items are strong arguments. Others are weaker than they look, or courts and DOJ would likely close them. I've noted which is which so the team doesn't rely on readings that won't hold up. Several "loopholes," such as splitting training runs or invoking the safety-evaluation exception, would likely be treated as evasion. Courts read prohibitions purposively, and the willfulness standard in §5(b) makes structured workarounds a criminal-exposure risk, not a safe harbor. Those items are better used as unworkability arguments than as operating plans.

## Sec. 2: Definitions

1. **§2(1)(A), "single training run" is undefined.** The bill doesn't say whether compute from pretraining, continued pretraining, RL/post-training, and fine-tuning is aggregated, or whether separate runs combined through merging or distillation count. A model could arguably be assembled from multiple sub-threshold runs. Expect a court to aggregate where the runs are functionally one effort, especially if they were structured to avoid the threshold.

2. **§2(1)(A), no compute-counting methodology.** The bill gives no rules for estimating operations, for example whether to count synthetic-data generation, reward-model training, failed or restarted runs, or theoretical versus actual utilization. Without agency rulemaking authority, there's no mechanism to supply these rules, which makes compliance uncertain.

3. **§2(1)(A), "quantity of computing power" is technically incoherent.** "Power" is a rate, while the threshold is a total count of operations. This is a minor drafting error, but it's citable as evidence of imprecision.

4. **§2(1)(A), the threshold is static.** A fixed 10^26 figure doesn't adjust for algorithmic efficiency gains. Over the bill's 10-year life, it will both under-capture capable systems and over-capture routine ones. This is a workability argument, not a loophole.

5. **§2(1)(B), "the Secretary" is undefined.** No Secretary is designated anywhere in §2. §4 refers to the Department of Commerce and §6 to the Secretary of State, so it is unclear who makes capability determinations. This is one of the strongest drafting defects.

6. **§2(1)(B), the capability standard is vague.** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" doesn't specify which humans (median or expert), which tasks, what "substantially" or "most" means, or how performance is measured. Because the standard carries criminal penalties under §5(b), it invites a void-for-vagueness challenge.

7. **§2(1)(B), no procedure and no fair notice.** There is no notice, hearing, or review process for a determination. Because a determination can only follow a system's existence, a developer cannot know in advance whether training a sub-threshold model is unlawful. That raises due process and retroactivity problems for liability under §5.

8. **§2(1), "model" versus "system" inconsistency.** Prong (A) covers an "artificial intelligence model" and prong (B) an "artificial intelligence system," and neither term (nor "artificial intelligence") is defined. It's unclear whether a scaffolded agent built on a sub-threshold model is covered.

9. **§2(2), "U.S. person" is narrow.** It covers only citizens and "corporations," so it arguably excludes lawful permanent residents, LLCs, partnerships, and other non-corporate entities acting abroad. Note that a US parent directing a foreign affiliate faces aiding-and-abetting (18 U.S.C. §2) and conspiracy (18 U.S.C. §371) exposure for criminal violations regardless.

10. **§2(2), "State" is undefined.** Without a definition, entities organized under the laws of D.C. or the territories may fall outside "U.S. person." Foreign-organized subsidiaries of US companies are clearly outside it when they operate abroad.

11. **§2(3), key terms are undefined.** "Advanced AI chips," "data center," and "containing" (at any moment? on average?) have no definitions. Operators could split capacity across sites to stay at or below 50,000 chips. The chip count also says nothing about chip generation or performance.

## Sec. 3: Prohibition

12. **§3(a), core verbs are undefined.** "Train," "develop," and "deploy" are not defined. "Develop" could reach research and component work far upstream of any training run. "Deploy" could reach downstream businesses and end users, not just the developer.

13. **§3(a), "deploy" sweeps in foreign models.** As written, any person in the US who deploys any covered system is liable, including companies using foreign-trained frontier models. Foreign models whose training begins after enactment can never be used in the US. This is a strong overbreadth and workability argument.

14. **§3(a), the territorial scope is ambiguous.** It is unclear whether a person in the US remotely directing a training run on a foreign cluster is training "in the United States." It's also unclear whether a non-U.S.-person employee abroad is covered. A US-located person directing the run is likely reachable.

15. **§3(a), no prohibition on enabling conduct.** The bill doesn't address providing compute, cloud capacity, chips, or model weights to a foreign developer. Cloud providers serving foreign customers are arguably outside §3 except through accomplice liability.

16. **§3(b), the grandfather clause is keyed to the wrong date.** Grandfathering applies to training that "began before the date of enactment," but the Act takes effect 180 days later (§8). Training begun during that window is lawful when it starts but becomes unlawful to continue or deploy on the effective date. This creates a confusing cliff and possible takings or reliance arguments.

17. **§3(b), the grandfather's scope is unclear.** The bill doesn't say whether continued training, fine-tuning, new checkpoints, or successor versions of a grandfathered system remain exempt. A run that "began" pre-enactment might be extended indefinitely. Courts would likely read this narrowly, but the text is silent.

18. **§3(b), it incentivizes a pre-enactment race.** Developers have a strong reason to start runs before enactment and keep them nominally "ongoing." This undercuts the bill's purpose and is a policy argument against the drafting.

19. **§3(b), no proof standard.** The bill doesn't say what evidence establishes when training "began." That creates evidentiary uncertainty for both enforcement and compliance.

20. **§3(c), "safety evaluation" is undefined and unconditioned.** The bill provides no definition, no approval or notice requirement, no limit on scale, and no rule for mixed-purpose activity. On its face the exception is very broad. In practice, a purpose-based exception invites pretext findings, and invoking it for commercial development would be high-risk under §5(b) and potentially 18 U.S.C. §1001 for any related certifications.

21. **§3(c), unclear whether it covers training.** It's ambiguous whether the exception allows training a covered system in order to evaluate it, or only evaluating systems that already exist.

## Sec. 4: Registry

22. **§4, no penalty for non-compliance.** §5 penalizes only violations of §3, so failing to register or report carries no penalty under the Act. False certifications could still trigger 18 U.S.C. §1001.

23. **§4, the registry is disconnected from the prohibition.** Covered facilities are defined by chip count, while the prohibition is defined by compute and capability. Registration has no operative link to §3 enforcement.

24. **§4(a), core terms and deadlines are missing.** "Operator" is undefined, there is no registration deadline, and no Commerce component is designated. It's unclear whether facilities abroad operated by US companies must register.

25. **§4(b), the reporting requirements are vague.** The required level of detail for "describing the computing workloads" is unspecified. Given the vagueness, a minimal description is arguably compliant.

26. **§4(b), no confidentiality protection.** The bill provides no trade-secret or FOIA protection for workload reports. That is a legitimate objection, and a fair amendment request.

## Sec. 5: Penalties

27. **§5(a), the penalty date conflicts with the effective date.** Civil penalties attach to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §8 delays effectiveness by 180 days. Penalizing conduct during the window when §3 isn't yet in force is internally inconsistent and raises fair-notice problems.

28. **§5(a), no enforcing authority or procedure.** The bill doesn't say who assesses civil penalties (DOJ or Commerce), whether they are imposed administratively or through a court action, or what review is available. It also provides no injunctive-relief authority.

29. **§5(a), the unit of violation is unclear.** It's ambiguous whether the per-day penalty runs per system, per training run, or per deployment instance. It's also unclear whether deployment by many customers multiplies the penalty.

30. **§5(b), criminal liability tied to a vague standard.** Criminal penalties attach to violations involving §2(1)(B) systems that may only be identified after the fact. This compounds the vagueness and due-process problems in items 6–7. "Willfully" mitigates the problem somewhat, but it also cuts against anyone who proceeds knowingly under an aggressive reading.

## Sec. 6: International Agreement

31. **§6, the timing is ambiguous.** "Seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months" could mean the Secretary must begin negotiations within 18 months or conclude them. Either way, the obligation is hortatory and unenforceable.

32. **§6, the prohibition doesn't depend on reciprocity.** The prohibition takes effect regardless of whether any agreement is reached, and "equivalent" is undefined. This is a strong unilateral-disadvantage argument, though it's a policy point rather than a drafting defect.

## Sec. 7: Duration

33. **§7(a), the off-ramp is illusory.** A joint resolution requires presentment and is functionally just a new statute, which Congress could pass anyway. The provision doesn't direct or fund NASEM to produce a report, sets no timeline, and leaves "developed safely" undefined.

34. **§7(a) and §7(b), relationship and scope.** The two subsections are best read as "whichever comes first." But §7(a) ends only "the prohibition in section 3," while §7(b) ends the whole Act, so it's unclear whether the §4 registry survives a §7(a) resolution.

35. **§7(b), the sunset is measured from enactment.** The 10-year clock runs from enactment, not the effective date, so the operative period is about 9.5 years. This is minor.

36. **§7(b), the sunset is not a liability escape.** 1 U.S.C. §109 provides that expiration of a temporary statute does not extinguish penalties incurred while it was in force. The team should not rely on the sunset to moot liability.

## Sec. 8 and Structural Omissions

37. **§8, the effective date is inconsistent with other sections.** The 180-day delay conflicts with §3(b) and §5(a), as described in items 16 and 27.

38. **No rulemaking authority.** No agency is authorized to issue regulations defining terms, compute-counting rules, or determination procedures. That limits the government's ability to cure the ambiguities above administratively, which strengthens the vagueness arguments.

39. **No definitions of "person" or "Secretary."** "Person" defaults to the Dictionary Act (1 U.S.C. §1), which includes corporations, so this is not a real gap. "Secretary" is a real gap (see item 5).

40. **No severability clause.** This has limited value: courts presume severability absent contrary intent. Even if §2(1)(B) were struck, §2(1)(A) would likely survive.

41. **No preemption clause.** The bill is silent on state AI laws, which could produce overlapping or conflicting regimes.

## Strongest Arguments for Markup

These are the most persuasive points to present as genuine drafting defects:
- The undefined "Secretary" (item 5)
- Vagueness and lack of notice under §2(1)(B), combined with criminal penalties (items 6, 7, 30)
- The date conflicts among §§3(b), 5(a), and 8 (items 16, 27, 37)
- The absence of an enforcement mechanism or rulemaking authority (items 28, 38)
- The undefined safety-evaluation exception (item 20)
- Overbreadth of "deploy," including foreign models (item 13)

Raising these points may prompt the committee to fix them in markup instead of leaving them as gaps. That's worth factoring into whether the team flags each one publicly or holds it for later litigation.
