# Weak Spots in S. 2817 (as amended October 6, 2026)

1. **§2(1)(A), "by a single vessel":** The 10,000-ton threshold applies only to one vessel. An operator could divide recovery across several vessels that each stay under the cap, and the bill doesn't say whether fleet totals count.

2. **§2(1)(A), tonnage measurement:** The bill doesn't say how "metric tons" are measured. It doesn't specify wet or dry weight, or whether sediment and discharged material count toward the total, so the cap could be contested or gamed.

3. **§2(1)(A), undefined terms:** "Recovery," "seabed," and "other mineral resources" are undefined. The open-ended phrase could cover or exclude sulfides, crusts, or sediment extracted for testing or processing.

4. **§2(1)(A), depth:** The 200-meter threshold doesn't say whether depth refers to water depth or seabed depth at the recovery site. Activity in shallower water falls outside the definition entirely.

5. **§2(1)(B), "the Secretary":** The bill never identifies which Secretary is meant. Section 6 names the Secretary of State, which suggests others are not meant, but §§2 and 7 leave the question open.

6. **§2(1)(B), discretionary expansion:** The Secretary's determination that an activity is "comparable" has no stated standard, procedure, notice requirement, or judicial review. That makes the designation easy to challenge in either direction.

7. **§2(2), "U.S. person":** The definition covers only citizens and corporations "organized under the laws of a State." It excludes LLCs, partnerships, and trusts, and "State" is undefined (it's unclear whether DC or territories count).

8. **§§2(2), 3(a), foreign-incorporated firms:** A foreign-incorporated company operating abroad appears to fall outside both prongs of §3(a), even if U.S. persons control it. Only its U.S. persons would be liable, and only if they personally engage in mining.

9. **§2(3), "equipped for":** "Equipped" could mean designed, fitted, or actually used for recovery, and it's unclear which applies. Converted or smaller vessels could fall outside the definition.

10. **§§2(3), 3(a), 4, scope mismatch:** Section 3 never refers to covered vessels, so the registry and reporting duties in §4 reach only the vessels above 5,000 gross tons. Smaller mining vessels are outside the paperwork regime even though they're still subject to the ban.

11. **§3(a), "engage in":** The phrase is undefined. It doesn't clearly reach financing, insurance, equipment supply, or purchase and processing of minerals recovered abroad.

12. **§3(a), jurisdiction:** The bill doesn't state its jurisdictional basis or address the high seas, the Area, or the International Seabed Authority. The United States isn't a party to UNCLOS, so enforcement against foreign-flagged or foreign-based activity would depend on other countries' cooperation.

13. **§3, overlap with DSHMRA (1980):** The bill doesn't mention or amend the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act, under which NOAA already issues permits to U.S. citizens. It's unclear whether existing permits are overridden, and the bill's "U.S. person" definition differs from DSHMRA's "citizen" standard.

14. **§3(b), "exploration license":** Exploration licenses typically don't authorize commercial recovery, so it's unclear whether the grandfather clause allows commercial mining under one. The clause also sets no limit on volume, area, or duration, so "mining conducted under" could allow indefinite operation.

15. **§3(b), scope of grandfathering:** The clause covers licenses "issued before" enactment but doesn't address renewals, extensions, amendments, transfers, or new vessels operating under existing licenses.

16. **§§3(b), 8, timing gap:** Section 3(b) is keyed to the date of enactment, but §8 delays the prohibition's effect by 180 days. Operators could rush to obtain licenses in that window, and the bill doesn't say whether licenses issued during it are grandfathered.

17. **§3(c), "environmental assessment":** The term is undefined, and the exemption has no cap on tonnage, duration, or scale and no approval, notice, or reporting requirement. Commercial-scale recovery could be labeled "assessment," making this the bill's largest loophole.

18. **§3(c) and §4, unreported assessment:** Assessment activity is fully exempt from §3, and §4 reporting applies only to vessels above 5,000 gross tons. Smaller assessment vessels could operate with no registration and no reports.

19. **§4(a), no penalty for non-registration:** The section sets no registration deadline. Section 5 penalizes only violations of §3, so failing to register under §4 carries no stated consequence.

20. **§4(b), report content:** The reports must "describe" recovery activities but specify no content, such as tonnage, location, depth, or discharge. There's no verification or audit provision, and the penalty for a false certification is unstated.

21. **§4(b), circular and incomplete reporting:** The duty applies only to "registered operators," so a non-registrant has no reporting duty. The bill also lacks NOAA authority to request further information or inspect vessels, and it doesn't require public release of reports.

22. **§4, "operator":** The term is undefined. An owner, charterer, or contractor could argue it isn't the "operator" and avoid the duties.

23. **§5, enforcement mechanism:** The bill doesn't name an enforcing agency or procedure. It's unclear whether penalties are imposed administratively, sought by DOJ, or enforceable by citizen suit.

24. **§5(a), per-day counting:** "Each day on which the violation occurs" doesn't define a "violation" or how a continuing operation or vessel in transit is counted. The bill also has no minimum penalty and no inflation adjustment.

25. **§5, missing remedies:** The bill has no injunctive relief, vessel seizure or forfeiture, restoration or remediation remedy, or liability for responsible corporate officers. Without these, the penalties may be hard to collect from thinly capitalized entities.

26. **§§5(a), 8, effective-date conflict:** Section 5(a) applies penalties to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §8 delays the prohibition 180 days. Penalties could thus be read to apply to conduct the prohibition doesn't yet cover.

27. **§5(b), "willfully" and scope:** "Willfully" is undefined, and the criminal provision doesn't mention attempt, conspiracy, aiding, or financing. It also has no tier for repeat violators.

28. **§6, "shall seek to negotiate":** This creates no duty to reach an agreement, no reporting to Congress, and no consequence if talks fail. The bill also doesn't say whether an agreement would override domestic law or require Senate advice and consent.

29. **§7(a) vs. §7(b), internal conflict:** Section 7(a) keeps the prohibition in force until Congress finds it safe, but §7(b) ends the entire Act after 10 years. It's unclear whether the ban lapses at year 10 regardless of any finding. This is probably the most important drafting problem.

30. **§7(a), "serious harm" and the NAS report:** "Serious harm" is undefined. The bill sets no deadline for the National Academies report and doesn't direct anyone to request it, so the review may never happen.

31. **§7(a), joint resolution mechanics:** The bill doesn't say whether the joint resolution follows expedited procedures or whether it goes to the President. A joint resolution ordinarily requires presidential signature or a veto override, and the bill doesn't address that.

32. **§7(b), no savings clause:** The sunset doesn't address violations committed before it takes effect, pending penalty actions, or grandfathered licenses under §3(b).

33. **§8, no implementing authority:** The bill doesn't authorize or require implementing regulations, and it has no transition rules for vessels already at sea.

34. **General, missing boilerplate:** The bill has no severability clause and no savings or rule-of-construction clause addressing other laws, such as NEPA, the Marine Mammal Protection Act, or the Clean Water Act.
