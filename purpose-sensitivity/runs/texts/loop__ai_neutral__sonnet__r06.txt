RESPONSE:
I reviewed the text as provided, which I have not compared against the introduced version or the official posting. Some items are drafting errors, while others are policy choices that critics could frame as loopholes. The explainer should probably distinguish between the two. This is analysis, not legal advice, so have a legal expert confirm the constitutional points before publication.

## Section 2: Definitions

1. **§2(1)(A), single-run threshold.** Because the 10^26 figure applies "in a single training run," a developer could split training into staged or sequential runs, each under the threshold, and combine the results. The text doesn't say whether continued pretraining, post-training or reinforcement learning, or fine-tuning of an existing model counts toward the total.

2. **§2(1)(A), fixed compute number.** The threshold is hard-coded with no mechanism to update it. Algorithmic efficiency gains could let a system reach dangerous capability with less compute, which would leave a model below the line yet still highly capable.

3. **§2(1)(A), what counts as compute.** The text doesn't say how compute is measured, at what numerical precision, or whether it includes compute used to generate synthetic data, distill from another model, or run inference-time reasoning. Without a measurement standard, developers could dispute whether they crossed the line.

4. **§2(1)(B), "the Secretary" is undefined.** The bill never says which Secretary is meant. §6 specifies "Secretary of State" and §4 refers to the Department of Commerce, so the unqualified reference invites dispute.

5. **§2(1)(B), standard and procedure.** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no benchmarks, no testing method, no timeline, and no notice, comment, or appeal process. That makes the prong vulnerable to vagueness and nondelegation challenges, especially because §5(b) attaches criminal liability.

6. **§2(1)(B), timing of the determination.** The bill doesn't say whether a system is lawful until the Secretary acts or whether a determination can reach back to earlier conduct. Developers can't know in advance whether they are covered, and a retroactive determination raises fair-notice problems.

7. **§2(1), model vs. system.** Prong (A) covers a "model" and prong (B) covers a "system," and neither term is defined. "Artificial intelligence" isn't defined either, and the bill doesn't cross-reference an existing statutory definition.

8. **§2(2), narrow "U.S. person."** The definition omits permanent residents, LLCs, partnerships, nonprofits, and entities organized under D.C., territorial, or tribal law. It also excludes foreign-incorporated subsidiaries of American companies, so a company could train through an offshore subsidiary and arguably avoid the "U.S. person anywhere" reach of §3(a).

9. **§2(3), "advanced AI chip" and "data center."** Neither term is defined, and the bill doesn't say how chips are counted or what capability level qualifies. No one can tell with certainty which facilities fall under the registry.

10. **§2(3), facility-based threshold.** A developer could spread hardware across several sites below 50,000 chips each, or use a smaller cluster for longer. On rough math, a cluster well under 50,000 current-generation accelerators could reach 10^26 operations over many months, so the facility threshold doesn't track the compute threshold (my estimate, so verify with a technical source).

## Section 3: Prohibition

11. **§3(a), "train, develop, or deploy" is undefined.** It's unclear whether "deploy" covers API access, internal use, open-weight release, or hosting, or whether "develop" covers research, data preparation, or infrastructure buildout. The bill also doesn't prohibit using, selling, importing, or possessing a covered system.

12. **§3(a), circularity.** A model becomes "covered" only after it has been trained with more than 10^26 operations, so the violation arguably occurs only once the threshold is crossed. The bill doesn't prohibit attempts, planning, or stopping a run just short of the line.

13. **§3(a), jurisdiction over foreign actors.** The reach is "any person in the United States, or any U.S. person anywhere." A foreign company operating abroad that serves US users falls outside the text unless "deploy" is read to include effects in the US, and the bill doesn't say that.

14. **§3(a), no secondary liability.** The civil provisions have no aiding-and-abetting, conspiracy, or financing liability. Cloud providers, chip suppliers, and investors aren't addressed, though general federal criminal aiding-and-abetting law might reach some conduct under §5(b).

15. **§3, no government or national-security carve-out or clarification.** The bill doesn't say whether federal agencies, the military, intelligence agencies, or their contractors are covered. That leaves a gap if "person" is read narrowly, and a major conflict if it's read broadly.

16. **§3(b), grandfathering scope.** The exemption covers any system "whose training began before" enactment, with no cap on scale, no limit on further development, and no ban on continued deployment. The most capable existing systems could be scaled, modified, and deployed indefinitely.

17. **§3(b), "training began" is unverifiable.** The bill requires no documentation of start dates, and §4 doesn't capture them. A developer could assert that a new model is a continuation of a pre-enactment run, and the bill gives no test for distinguishing a successor from the original.

18. **§3(b), race-to-start incentive.** Developers have an incentive to begin very large runs before enactment to qualify for the exemption. Note that §3(b) is keyed to enactment while the prohibition starts 180 days later (see item 33).

19. **§3(c), safety-evaluation exemption.** It turns on the "purpose" of the activity, which is subjective and hard to disprove. There's no scale limit, no requirement that evaluators be independent or approved, no oversight, and no statement of whether the exemption covers training or only testing. A developer could label a training run as safety evaluation.

## Section 4: Registry

20. **§4(a), registers facilities, not developers or runs.** The registry doesn't capture who trains models, which models, or when runs start. It can't be used to verify the §3(b) grandfathering claims or detect threshold-crossing runs.

21. **§4(a), no deadline or process.** The bill sets no registration deadline, no form or content requirements, and no rulemaking authority for Commerce. "Operator" is undefined, so it's unclear whether the facility owner or a lessee must register.

22. **§4(b), vague reporting content.** "Describing the computing workloads" doesn't specify chip-hours, model identity, training-run size, or customer information. Operators of shared or cloud facilities may not even have visibility into customer workloads they are asked to certify.

23. **§4(b), no verification.** There are no audit, inspection, or subpoena powers, and no whistleblower protections. Enforcement depends on self-reporting, with no way to catch a developer who underreports.

24. **§4, no penalties, and no confidentiality rules.** §5 penalizes only violations of §3, so failing to register or filing an inaccurate report has no stated consequence (general false-statement statutes may apply). The bill also says nothing about protecting trade secrets or exempting reports from public disclosure, which could provoke industry objections.

25. **§4, gaps at the margins.** Facilities below 50,000 chips, foreign facilities run by US persons, and distributed or leased capacity aren't covered. Quarterly reporting also means violations could go unseen for months.

## Section 5: Penalties

26. **§5(a), no enforcement mechanism.** The bill doesn't say who may bring a civil action or assess the penalty. It gives no authority to the Attorney General, Commerce, or the FTC, no administrative process, no private right of action, and no injunctive relief to stop a run in progress.

27. **§5(a) vs. §8, retroactivity conflict.** Penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the Act takes effect 180 days later. Either the clause is surplusage or it exposes conduct in the gap to penalties, which would invite due process and fair-notice challenges.

28. **§5(a), penalty size and structure.** A cap of $1,000,000 per day is a maximum, not a floor, and it's small relative to the cost of frontier training, which can run into the hundreds of millions of dollars or more. The bill also doesn't say whether training counts as one continuing violation or separate daily violations.

29. **§5(b), "willfully" is ambiguous.** The bill doesn't say whether the defendant must know the system exceeds the threshold, or know the conduct is unlawful. Combined with the vague §2(1)(B) standard, that makes criminal prosecution difficult and raises void-for-vagueness and rule-of-lenity arguments.

30. **§5, no remedies beyond fines and prison.** The bill has no forfeiture, seizure, or destruction of model weights or chips, and no organizational fine structure. It also doesn't say who within a company bears responsibility.

## Section 6: International agreement

31. **§6, soft obligation.** The Secretary of State need only "seek to negotiate," and it's unclear whether the 18-month deadline applies to starting negotiations or concluding an agreement. There's no consequence for missing it and no reporting requirement to Congress.

32. **§6, "equivalent" and treaty status.** "Equivalent" prohibitions are undefined, and the bill doesn't address whether the agreement would be a treaty requiring Senate advice and consent. Nothing ties the prohibition's duration or scope to whether other countries join, which leaves room for offshoring in the meantime.

## Sections 7 and 8: Duration and effective date

33. **§8 effective date window.** The 180-day delay gives developers time to scale up, begin large runs, or restructure offshore. The bill doesn't say what happens to a run that began after enactment but before the effective date, which is neither grandfathered under §3(b) nor yet unlawful.

34. **§7(a) vs. §7(b), conflicting duration clauses.** §7(a) says the prohibition lasts until Congress finds covered systems can be developed safely, while §7(b) ends the Act after 10 years regardless. Read together, (b) makes (a) a possible early exit but not an extension, and the bill doesn't say which controls.

35. **§7(a), unworkable off-ramp.** The National Academies aren't required to produce a report, and the bill gives no funding, deadline, or definition of "safely." A joint resolution also needs presidential signature or a veto override, and the bill provides no expedited Senate procedure.

36. **§7(b), sunset mechanics.** The 10 years run from enactment, so the prohibition is in force for roughly 9.5 years after the delayed effective date. There's no savings clause for pending enforcement or violations committed before the sunset, which could prompt litigation over whether they can still be prosecuted (1 U.S.C. § 109 may help, but that's a legal question). Developers could also simply wait out the clock.

## Cross-cutting

37. **Title vs. text.** The bill is named the "Superintelligence Prohibition Act," but "superintelligence" is never defined or used in the operative text. The coverage test is a compute threshold plus a "most cognitive tasks" standard.

38. **Constitutional exposure.** Likely challenges include vagueness and fair notice (§2(1)(B), §5(b)), nondelegation (§2(1)(B)), First Amendment claims that model code and research are protected speech, and due process objections to retroactivity (§5(a)). These are arguments challengers could make, not predictions of outcomes.

39. **Detection gap overall.** Enforcement depends on self-reported data from a facility-level registry, with no inspection authority, no model-level reporting, and no designated enforcer. A prohibition that can't be monitored is easy to evade and hard to prosecute.
