RESPONSE:
Here's a section-by-section review. I've grouped items by section and flagged the ones I'd put at the top of the list for Whitfield's office with ★.

## Section 2 — Definitions

1. ★ **Sec. 2(1)(A), "in a single training run."** This is the biggest evasion route in the bill. A lab can split training into multiple "runs" (checkpoint and resume, pre-train then continue pre-training, train several sub-threshold models and merge or distill them), each under 10^26, and never trigger the definition. The threshold should be cumulative across all compute used to produce the model, including continued pre-training, fine-tuning, and any parent/teacher models.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A), no counting method.** The bill doesn't say how operations are to be counted (e.g., whether sparsity, mixed precision, or MACs vs. FLOPs count differently), so two labs could report the same run as above or below the line. Commerce needs rulemaking authority to specify the methodology.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A), static threshold.** 10^26 is fixed in statute with no authority to lower it as algorithmic efficiency improves. Within a few years, systems at the capability level the bill targets may be trainable well below 10^26, leaving only the vague prong (B) to catch them. Add authority (and arguably a duty) to revise the threshold downward by regulation.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "trained using" covers only training compute.** Inference-time compute, test-time scaling, and agentic scaffolding are not counted, so capability gains achieved by running a sub-threshold model harder are outside prong (A).

5. ★ **Sec. 2(1)(B), "the Secretary" is undefined.** The bill references the Secretary of State in Sec. 6 and the Department of Commerce in Sec. 4 but never says which Secretary makes capability determinations. That's a straightforward drafting error that opponents will point to as evidence of sloppiness and that a court would have to resolve.

6. ★ **Sec. 2(1)(B), determination-based coverage has no process and no timing.** A system is only "covered" under (B) once the Secretary determines it is, which means training and deployment are lawful until that determination is made, likely after the fact. There are no criteria, deadlines, information-gathering powers, or rulemaking procedures for making the determination, and nothing prevents the Secretary from simply never making one.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(B), vagueness.** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no measurable content, and because Sec. 5(b) attaches a 10-year criminal penalty, this prong invites a void-for-vagueness challenge. At minimum, criminal liability should be limited to prong (A) or to conduct occurring after a published (B) determination.

8. **Sec. 2, no definition of "artificial intelligence model/system," "train," "develop," or "deploy."** Every operative term in Sec. 3 is undefined. "Develop" in particular could be read to cover publishing research or writing code, which both overreaches (First Amendment exposure) and is uncertain enough to chill enforcement.

9. ★ **Sec. 2(2), "U.S. person" is far too narrow.** It covers only citizens and "a corporation organized under the laws of a State." It omits lawful permanent residents; LLCs, partnerships, trusts, and nonprofits (most AI labs are not plain corporations); entities organized in DC or the territories; and foreign subsidiaries owned or controlled by U.S. persons. A lab could spin up a Cayman or Irish subsidiary and train abroad with no U.S.-person nexus.

10. **Sec. 2(2), no control test.** Even with a broader entity list, the definition should reach entities "owned or controlled by" a U.S. person, and arguably entities that are substantially funded by or provide services to U.S. persons, or the extraterritorial prong of Sec. 3(a) is meaningless.

11. **Sec. 2(3), "advanced AI chips" is undefined.** There's no reference to the export-control definitions (e.g., ECCN 3A090) or performance thresholds, so nobody knows which chips count. Cross-reference the Commerce export-control definitions and give authority to update.

12. ★ **Sec. 2(3), 50,000-chip threshold per "data center."** Operators can divide a campus into multiple "data centers" of 49,999 chips, and a 10^26 run can be distributed across several sub-threshold sites. The registry should aggregate chips under common ownership or control within a region, or be keyed to compute capacity rather than chip count, and "data center" needs a definition.

## Section 3 — Prohibition

13. **Sec. 3(a), "train, develop, or deploy" omits the supply chain.** The bill doesn't reach providing compute, chips, cloud services, or capital for a covered run; possessing or hosting a covered model; or aiding, abetting, or conspiring. Cloud providers are the easiest chokepoint for enforcement and are currently untouched.

14. **Sec. 3(a), ambiguity for foreign-trained systems.** A foreign company that trains abroad and then offers a covered system to U.S. users via API is arguably not "a person in the United States" and may or may not be "deploying" in the U.S. The bill should explicitly cover providing, offering, or importing a covered system for use in the United States.

15. **Sec. 3(a), government and military.** "Any person" ordinarily does not include the federal government, so federal agencies and contractors acting for them may be outside the prohibition. The bill should say whether the prohibition applies to the government (we'd want it to), or at least address it expressly, since silence will be read as an exemption.

16. ★ **Sec. 3(b), grandfather clause.** Exempting any system "whose training began" before enactment creates a race to start runs before the bill is signed, and the 180-day effective date in Sec. 8 widens that window. "Began" is also undefined: a lab could claim that any continued-training run is part of a "training" that began years ago on an earlier checkpoint. If any grandfathering is kept, it should apply only to training completed before enactment and should not cover deployment.

17. ★ **Sec. 3(c), safety-evaluation exemption swallows the rule.** Any training run can be characterized as being "for the purpose of safety evaluation." The exemption has no definition, no compute cap, no licensing or approval, no reporting, and no restriction on deployment of the resulting system. It should be deleted or replaced with a narrow, licensed exemption for evaluating already-existing systems, with no training above the threshold.

## Section 4 — Registry

18. ★ **Sec. 4, no penalty for non-compliance.** Sec. 5 penalizes violations of Sec. 3 only. An operator that never registers or files false reports faces nothing under this Act (18 U.S.C. § 1001 may apply to certifications, but there is no penalty for simply not filing).

19. **Sec. 4(a), "operator" is undefined.** In a cloud setting, the facility owner, the cloud provider, and the tenant running the workload are often different entities, and it's unclear who must register or who knows what workloads are running.

20. **Sec. 4(b), reporting is retrospective and vague.** A quarterly report "describing the computing workloads" arrives after a 10^26 run could already be finished. Require advance notice before any training run expected to exceed a lower threshold (e.g., 10^25), specify the content of reports (compute per run, customer, purpose), and require record retention.

21. **Sec. 4, no verification powers.** There is no inspection, audit, subpoena, or technical-verification authority and no funding for Commerce to do any of this. A registry without verification is self-attestation.

## Section 5 — Penalties

22. ★ **Sec. 5(a), penalty is too low to deter.** $1M per day is about $365M per year, less than the cost of a single frontier training run and a fraction of the expected value of a successful one. Tie the penalty to a percentage of global revenue or a multiple of the compute cost, and add injunctive relief and forfeiture of the model weights and hardware used.

23. ★ **Sec. 5(a), retroactivity conflict with Sec. 8.** Civil penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the Act doesn't take effect until 180 days later. This is internally inconsistent and, if read to penalize pre-effective-date conduct, invites a due process/retroactivity challenge.

24. **Sec. 5, no enforcement mechanism.** The bill doesn't say who brings civil actions (Attorney General? Commerce?), doesn't authorize injunctions or seizure, provides no whistleblower protection or reward, and gives no statute of limitations. Also absent: individual liability for officers and directors, which matters because a corporate fine alone can be priced in.

25. **Sec. 5(b), "willfully" plus vague definition.** Willfulness generally requires knowledge that the conduct is unlawful; combined with the undefined prong (B) and the ambiguous "single training run" language, a defendant can credibly claim they believed their run was under the threshold or exempt. Consider a "knowingly" standard for prong (A) violations.

## Section 6 — International Agreement

26. **Sec. 6, no teeth.** "Seek to negotiate" imposes no obligation to conclude anything, has no reporting requirement to Congress, and the 18-month clause is ambiguous (must the Secretary begin seeking within 18 months, or finish?). Add a reporting requirement and authority to restrict imports/compute exports to non-party countries.

## Section 7 — Duration

27. ★ **Sec. 7(a) and 7(b) conflict.** Subsection (a) says the prohibition stays in effect until Congress affirmatively finds superintelligence can be built safely; subsection (b) sunsets the entire Act in 10 years regardless. The sunset controls as a practical matter and defeats the purpose of (a). Strike (b), or at minimum convert it to a reauthorization vote with the prohibition continuing absent action.

28. **Sec. 7(a), no mechanism for the NAS report.** Nothing commissions, funds, or sets a timeline for the National Academies report, and nothing prevents Congress from acting without it (one Congress can't bind another through this language anyway). The report process should be set out in the Act so the off-ramp is real and not a pretext.

## Section 8 — Effective Date

29. **Sec. 8, 180-day delay.** Combined with Sec. 3(b), the delay gives labs a six-month window to launch grandfathered runs. The prohibition on starting new covered training should take effect on enactment, with the delay applied only to registry obligations.

## Missing provisions (bill-wide)

30. **No rulemaking authority.** Commerce has no express authority to issue regulations defining terms, updating thresholds, or setting reporting formats, which hampers implementation and invites APA challenges to any guidance it does issue.

31. **No severability clause.** If prong (B) or the criminal penalty is struck as vague, there is no clause preserving the rest of the Act.

32. **No funding or appropriations.** Registry administration, verification, and enforcement are unfunded.

33. **No treatment of model weights.** The bill never addresses transfer, export, open release, or possession of covered model weights, so a grandfathered or foreign model's weights could be freely distributed.

34. **No preemption language.** The bill is silent on state law, which is fine from our perspective if the intent is to leave stricter state laws in place, but opponents may seek to insert broad preemption; worth getting express "no preemption of more protective state law" text in now.
