# S. 2817 Drafting Issues: Loopholes, Ambiguities, and Unworkability Arguments

Each item is tagged **[Operate]** if it may preserve a path to continued operations, or **[Unworkable]** if it supports arguments that the bill is unworkable, unconstitutional, or poorly drafted. Some items are both.

## Section 2: Definitions

1. **§2(1)(A), "by a single vessel."** [Operate] The 10,000-ton threshold applies per vessel, not per operator, project, or contract area. Spreading recovery across several vessels, each under 10,000 metric tons per year, would appear to avoid the prohibition entirely.

2. **§2(1)(A), tonnage measurement.** [Operate/Unworkable] The bill doesn't say whether tonnage is wet or dry. Nodules carry significant water and are lifted with sediment, so the same operation could fall on either side of the line. It also doesn't say whether co-recovered sediment, or only nodules and "mineral resources," counts.

3. **§2(1)(A), "per year."** [Operate/Unworkable] The bill doesn't specify a calendar year, fiscal year, or rolling 12-month period. This creates timing flexibility and uncertainty about when a violation begins.

4. **§2(1)(A), "recovery" undefined.** [Operate/Unworkable] The bill doesn't say whether recovery happens when material is collected on the seabed, lifted through the riser, or landed on deck. It also doesn't say whether a seabed collector vehicle or a non-self-propelled platform counts as a "vessel." Under 1 U.S.C. §3, a vessel is a watercraft "used, or capable of being used, as a means of transportation on water," so a crawler or a non-transport platform may not qualify.

5. **§2(1)(A), "from the seabed."** [Operate] The text covers resources "from the seabed" but not the subsoil or subsurface. This arguably excludes deposits requiring excavation below the seabed surface, such as massive sulfides. Nodules themselves sit on the surface, so this helps Meridian less directly.

6. **§2(1)(A), "other mineral resources" undefined.** [Unworkable] The phrase is broad enough to arguably reach sand, gravel, and other aggregate extraction at depth, or even hydrocarbons. That supports an overbreadth argument and an "unintended consequences" pitch to members with those industries in their states.

7. **§2(1), recovery-only scope.** [Operate] Only recovery is defined as mining. Prospecting, exploration, test collection under the threshold, processing, transport, financing, sponsorship, and the purchase or import of nodules recovered by others are all outside the definition.

8. **§2(1)(B), Secretary's catch-all.** [Unworkable] "The Secretary" is never defined, and §6 refers separately to the Secretary of State. It's unclear whether this means Commerce/NOAA, Interior, or State. The provision also has no procedure (no rulemaking, notice, or comment) and no standard beyond "comparable" risks.

9. **§2(1)(B), criminal vagueness and delegation.** [Unworkable] Because §5(b) attaches up to 10 years' imprisonment to §3, the catch-all lets an undesignated official define criminal conduct through an unspecified process. That supports void-for-vagueness and nondelegation arguments. After *Loper Bright*, the Secretary's determinations would also get no judicial deference.

10. **§2(2), "U.S. person" excludes foreign subsidiaries.** [Operate] Only citizens and corporations "organized under the laws of a State" are covered. A foreign-incorporated subsidiary of a U.S. company is not a U.S. person, so it could conduct recovery outside the United States.

11. **§2(2), "U.S. person" excludes non-corporate entities.** [Operate] LLCs, partnerships, and trusts are not "corporations," and permanent residents are not "citizens." Entities in these forms operating abroad appear uncovered. Note that some courts treat LLCs as corporations for certain purposes, so legal should verify.

12. **§2(2), "State" undefined.** [Operate] The bill doesn't say whether "State" includes the District of Columbia, Puerto Rico, Guam, or other territories. Entities organized there may fall outside the definition.

13. **§2(3), "covered vessel" is disconnected from the prohibition.** [Unworkable] The term is used only in §4. The prohibition in §2(1)(A) refers to "a single vessel" of any size. The 5,000-gross-ton line therefore governs only registration, so vessels at or below 5,000 GT can recover up to the limit without registering at all.

14. **§2(3), "equipped for the recovery" ambiguous.** [Operate] The bill doesn't say whether this means permanently fitted, temporarily fitted, or merely capable of carrying recovery equipment. Equipment installed only between voyages or on a separate support ship may avoid "covered vessel" status.

## Section 3: Prohibition

15. **§3(a), "United States" undefined geographically.** [Operate/Unworkable] The bill doesn't say whether "in the United States" includes the territorial sea, the EEZ, or the outer continental shelf. A foreign person operating in the U.S. EEZ may not be "in the United States," and foreign persons in areas beyond national jurisdiction are clearly not covered. This creates a competitiveness argument: the bill restricts U.S. companies while leaving foreign competitors untouched.

16. **§3(a), "engage in."** [Operate] The bill doesn't say whether contracting, chartering, financing, sponsoring, or holding equity in a recovery operation is "engaging in" mining. Civil liability for indirect participants is unclear. Criminal aiding-and-abetting may still reach them under 18 U.S.C. §2.

17. **§3, no interaction with DSHMRA or OCSLA.** [Operate/Unworkable] The bill doesn't amend, repeal, or reference the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (30 U.S.C. §1401 et seq.), and doesn't address OCSLA minerals leasing. It's unclear whether NOAA must still process DSHMRA applications or whether existing permits are revoked. The resulting conflicting statutory obligations support an "unworkable" argument.

18. **§3(b), exploration license exemption covers "mining."** [Operate] *This is likely the most significant issue.* The exemption covers "mining," not just exploration, conducted under a pre-enactment exploration license. Read literally, it allows commercial-scale recovery by any pre-enactment exploration licensee. There is no tonnage cap, time limit, or bar on renewal, extension, modification, or transfer.

19. **§3(b), issuing authority unspecified.** [Operate] "An exploration license" isn't limited to a DSHMRA license issued by NOAA. It could arguably cover licenses from foreign sponsoring states. ISA instruments are formally "contracts," not licenses, so their coverage is debatable either way.

20. **§3(b), commercial recovery permits not grandfathered.** [Unworkable] The exemption covers exploration licenses but not DSHMRA commercial recovery permits, which represent a further stage of investment. That is an anomaly in the text and creates potential takings or reliance claims for permit holders.

21. **§3(c), environmental assessment exemption.** [Operate] *Also highly significant.* "Environmental assessment" is undefined, and the exemption has no volume cap, no agency approval or permit requirement, no reporting requirement, and no requirement that assessment be the sole or primary purpose. Large-scale "test" or "assessment" collection, including sale of recovered material, appears exempt.

## Section 4: Registry

22. **§4, no enforcement mechanism.** [Operate/Unworkable] §5 penalizes only violations of §3. Failing to register or report under §4 carries no penalty, although knowingly false certifications might still reach 18 U.S.C. §1001.

23. **§4(a), no deadline or procedure.** [Unworkable] The bill sets no registration deadline, no required contents, and gives NOAA no rulemaking authority. "Operator" is also undefined: it could mean the owner, charterer, or contractor.

24. **§4(b), reports needn't include tonnage.** [Unworkable] Reports must only "describe recovery activities." They aren't required to state tonnage, so the registry can't verify compliance with the per-vessel threshold that defines the prohibition.

25. **§4, extraterritorial scope unclear.** [Unworkable] The bill doesn't say whether foreign operators of foreign-flagged covered vessels must register. If so, it's unenforceable. If not, it's unstated.

## Section 5: Penalties

26. **§5(a) vs. §8, effective date conflict.** [Unworkable] §5(a) imposes civil penalties for violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §8 makes the Act effective 180 days after enactment. Conduct in the 180-day window is either lawful or penalized, and the text contradicts itself. This is a strong drafting-error point.

27. **§5(a), per-day penalty vs. annual threshold.** [Unworkable] A violation depends on crossing 10,000 tons "per year," but penalties accrue "for each day." It's unclear whether days before the threshold is crossed count, or only days after.

28. **§5, no enforcing agency or procedures.** [Unworkable] No agency is assigned to assess civil penalties. There are no administrative procedures, hearing rights, investigative powers, seizure or forfeiture authority for vessels or recovered minerals, or citizen-suit provision.

29. **§5(b), criminal penalties tied to vague definitions.** [Unworkable] Up to 10 years' imprisonment attaches to conduct defined partly by the §2(1)(B) catch-all and by unresolved tonnage questions. This compounds the vagueness argument in item 9.

## Section 6: International Agreement

30. **§6, hortatory and ambiguous deadline.** [Unworkable] "Shall seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months" doesn't say whether the deadline applies to starting or concluding negotiations, and nothing happens if the effort fails. The provision also doesn't address that the U.S. is not a party to UNCLOS or the ISA regime. This supports a unilateral-disarmament argument: U.S. firms are barred now, and foreign competitors face only a possible future agreement.

## Section 7: Duration

31. **§7(a) vs. §7(b), internal conflict.** [Unworkable/Operate] §7(a) says the prohibition continues until Congress acts by joint resolution, while §7(b) ends the entire Act after 10 years regardless. The relationship is unstated. Under §7(b), the ban expires automatically, and because §8 delays effectiveness, it effectively lasts only about 9.5 years.

32. **§7(a), no study is actually commissioned.** [Unworkable] The bill doesn't direct, fund, or set a timeline for a National Academies report. "Serious harm" is undefined. A joint resolution is ordinary legislation requiring presentment, so §7(a) adds nothing to Congress's existing power to repeal.

## Section 8 and Structural Issues

33. **§8, 180-day window.** [Operate] Read with §3(a), operations remain lawful until the effective date, subject to the conflict in item 26. The exemption in §3(b) is fixed at enactment, though, so the window doesn't allow new licenses to be obtained.

34. **Act-wide, no rulemaking authority.** [Unworkable] No agency receives general rulemaking authority to resolve any of the ambiguities above.

35. **Act-wide, no import or trade provisions.** [Operate/Unworkable] Importing, purchasing, or processing seabed minerals recovered by non-covered persons is not addressed. Foreign recovery could continue and feed U.S. supply chains, which undercuts the bill's stated purpose.
