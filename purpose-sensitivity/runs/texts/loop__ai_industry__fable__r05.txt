RESPONSE:
Here's a section-by-section catalog. I've grouped items by section and flagged (★) the ones that look most significant. One practical note at the end about which of these are safe to rely on operationally versus which are better used as "unworkable as drafted" arguments.

## Sec. 2(1) — "Covered AI system"

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "single training run" is undefined. ★** Nothing stops a developer from splitting training into multiple runs, each below 10^26 (pretraining phase, continued pretraining, RL, fine-tuning, checkpoint restarts), and the text gives no aggregation rule. As written, a model can be trained with far more than 10^26 total operations without any single "run" exceeding the threshold.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — no counting methodology.** The bill doesn't say how to count operations (dense vs. sparse/MoE, precision, whether synthetic-data generation, evaluation, or distillation compute counts, whether ops performed on a teacher model count toward the student). Any good-faith accounting that lands under 10^26 is defensible, and the government has no rulemaking authority to fix the methodology (see item 32).

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "quantity of computing power" is technically wrong.** Computing power is a rate; operations are a quantity. Minor, but it's the kind of error that lets a litigant argue the threshold is incoherent.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "artificial intelligence model" and "artificial intelligence" are undefined anywhere in the Act.** Everything hinges on these terms and the bill never says what they mean.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(A) vs. (B) — "model" vs. "system" mismatch. ★** Prong (A) covers a *model*; prong (B) and Sec. 3 cover a *system*. A system built from multiple sub-10^26 models (agent scaffolds, ensembles, routers) contains no covered "model" and is not covered under (A); it is covered under (B) only if the Secretary affirmatively determines it is.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — fixed threshold with no indexing.** The threshold never adjusts for algorithmic efficiency gains, so the bill's own metric will become decoupled from the capability it aims at. Useful as an unworkability argument.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — "the Secretary" is never defined. ★** The Act mentions the Department of Commerce (Sec. 4) and the Secretary of State (Sec. 6) but never says which Secretary makes the determination under (B). A determination by either one is open to challenge as ultra vires.

8. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — no procedure, standard, notice, or judicial review for the determination.** The Secretary can apparently declare a system covered unilaterally with no process; a system becomes unlawful the moment the determination issues, including one already deployed. That is a serious due process problem and a strong litigation hook.

9. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — the determination can only be made after the system exists. ★** You cannot determine that a system "is capable" of exceeding human performance until it has been built, so training and development under prong (B) are never prohibited in advance; only continued deployment after a determination is. Prong (B) therefore cannot stop a sub-10^26 training run.

10. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — "substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" is undefined on every term.** Which humans (median, expert, best)? Which tasks? What counts as "most"? "Substantially"? Combined with criminal penalties, this invites a void-for-vagueness challenge.

## Sec. 2(2) — "U.S. person"

11. **Sec. 2(2) — only "citizens" and "corporations" are U.S. persons. ★** LLCs, LPs, partnerships, trusts, nonprofit corporations (arguably), and lawful permanent residents are excluded. A Delaware LLC is not a "U.S. person" and is reached only by the "any person in the United States" clause, which is tied to location.

12. **Sec. 2(2) — "organized under the laws of a State."** Entities organized under D.C., territorial, or tribal law are not covered. Foreign-organized subsidiaries of a U.S. corporation are not U.S. persons at all.

13. **Sec. 2(2)/3(a) — offshore structure falls outside the Act. ★** A foreign-organized subsidiary, training on foreign compute, with non-citizen staff, is neither a U.S. person nor a person "in the United States." The Act has no affiliate, control, beneficial-ownership, or "on behalf of" language to pierce that structure. (Citizen employees directing the work from abroad would still be covered individually; see item 16.)

## Sec. 2(3) — "Covered facility"

14. **Sec. 2(3) — "advanced AI chips" and "data center" are undefined.** No performance threshold, no reference to export-control classifications, no counting rule (packages vs. dies vs. accelerators). Whether a facility is "covered" is unknowable.

15. **Sec. 2(3) — 50,000-chip threshold is per "data center."** Adjacent buildings or separately-metered halls each under 50,000 chips, networked together, are not a covered facility. There is no aggregation across a campus, operator, or network fabric.

## Sec. 3 — Prohibition

16. **Sec. 3(a) — "train, develop, or deploy" are undefined. ★** "Develop" has no settled meaning and could mean anything from writing code to data curation. "Deploy" doesn't say whether internal use, research use, hosting a third party's weights, or serving a foreign-hosted model to U.S. users counts.

17. **Sec. 3(a) — "person in the United States" has no locational rule for distributed activity.** If staff are in the U.S. but compute is abroad (or vice versa), or if a training job is sharded across jurisdictions, the text doesn't say where the training "occurs" or who is "in the United States."

18. **Sec. 3(a) — no prohibition on use, possession, import, or inference.** Using a covered system, downloading foreign open weights, running inference on a covered model, licensing one in, or providing compute to a foreign customer who trains a covered model are all outside the operative prohibition.

19. **Sec. 3(a) — no prohibition on foreign developers serving U.S. users.** A foreign person deploying from abroad is not "in the United States" and is not a U.S. person, so the Act does nothing to prevent covered systems from being available in the U.S. market; it only prevents Americans from building them. Core "unilateral disarmament" argument.

20. **Sec. 3(b) — grandfather clause keyed to "training began" before enactment. ★** Any run that starts before the President signs is exempt in full, including its later phases, continued training, and deployment. There's no cap on how long a grandfathered run can continue, and no rule against treating continual training as a single ongoing run.

21. **Sec. 3(b) — grandfather is tied to enactment, not the Sec. 8 effective date.** Training that begins in the 180-day window after enactment is not grandfathered, but Sec. 3(a) is not yet in effect. Whether such a run becomes a continuing violation on day 181 or is simply untouched is unanswered, and penalizing it raises retroactivity problems (see item 27).

22. **Sec. 3(b) — status of derivatives of grandfathered systems is unclear.** Fine-tunes, distillations, merged checkpoints, and continued-pretraining descendants of a grandfathered model have no clear status; the text doesn't say whether a derivative's "training began" when the parent's did.

23. **Sec. 3(c) — "safety evaluation" exemption is unbounded. ★** The term is undefined, the exemption covers "activities" generally (not just testing), it doesn't require the purpose to be exclusive or primary, no one certifies or reviews the purpose, and no results need be reported. Because you cannot evaluate the safety of a system that hasn't been trained, training a covered system for the purpose of evaluating its safety is, on the text, exempt from the entire Act.

24. **Sec. 3 — no enforcement agency, no investigative authority, no injunctive relief.** The Act never says who enforces Sec. 3 (DOJ? Commerce?), gives no subpoena or inspection power, and does not authorize courts to enjoin a training run. The only remedies are after-the-fact fines and prosecution.

## Sec. 4 — Registry

25. **Sec. 4 — no penalty for noncompliance. ★** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3. Failing to register, failing to report, or filing an incomplete report carries no consequence under the Act (a knowingly false certification could still implicate 18 U.S.C. § 1001, but that is outside this bill).

26. **Sec. 4(a)/(b) — no deadlines, no "operator" definition, no content standard.** No date to register, no due date for quarterly reports, no rule for who the "operator" is in colocation or leased arrangements, and "describing the computing workloads" could be satisfied by one sentence. There's also no confidentiality or FOIA protection for reported workloads, which is a trade-secret objection worth raising.

## Sec. 5 — Penalties

27. **Sec. 5(a) — penalty clock starts at enactment, but the Act isn't effective for 180 days. ★** Civil penalties apply to "any violation occurring on or after the date of enactment," yet under Sec. 8 no conduct can violate Sec. 3 until 180 days later. The provisions are internally inconsistent; read literally, Sec. 5(a) either does nothing for 180 days or purports to penalize pre-effective-date conduct, which is a retroactivity defect.

28. **Sec. 5(a) — $1M/day is small relative to frontier training costs.** Measured against the budget of a 10^26-scale run, the maximum civil penalty is a modest line item; there is no disgorgement, no multiplier, and no per-violation stacking rule. (Useful for the argument that the bill's design is incoherent; see the caveat below before treating it as an operating plan.)

29. **Sec. 5(b) — "willfully" plus pervasive vagueness.** Criminal liability requires willfulness, and with "develop," "safety evaluation," "single training run," and prong (B) all undefined, a defendant has strong arguments that it could not have known its conduct was prohibited. The vagueness also supports a facial void-for-vagueness challenge given the 10-year prison term.

30. **Sec. 5 — no statute of limitations, no savings clause, no clarity on corporate vs. individual liability.** The Act doesn't say whether a corporation's violation exposes officers or employees, and once the Act sunsets (Sec. 7(b)) there is no savings clause preserving liability for pre-sunset violations.

## Sec. 6 — International agreement

31. **Sec. 6 — "seek to negotiate" imposes no obligation and has no consequence.** The 18-month clause is ambiguous (must the agreement be concluded, or merely sought, within 18 months?), and nothing happens either way. The Act's prohibition is not conditioned on reciprocity from any other country, which is the central competitiveness objection.

## Sec. 7 — Duration

32. **Sec. 7(a) and 7(b) conflict. ★** Subsection (a) says the prohibition lasts "until Congress ... finds by joint resolution"; subsection (b) says the whole Act expires in 10 years. If no joint resolution ever passes, (b) ends the prohibition anyway, contradicting (a). The bill doesn't say which controls.

33. **Sec. 7(a) — the joint-resolution mechanism is surplusage.** A joint resolution is just a statute, so (a) says the prohibition lasts until Congress passes a law ending it, which is always true. The clause also conditions that action on a National Academies report that no one is directed or funded to commission, with no timeline, and applies an undefined "can be developed safely" standard.

34. **Sec. 7(b) — sunset applies to "this Act," including the registry and penalties.** With no savings clause, obligations and liabilities arguably evaporate at sunset, including for conduct before it.

## Sec. 8 and general drafting

35. **Sec. 8 — effective date conflicts with Secs. 3(b) and 5(a).** See items 21 and 27; three different trigger dates (enactment for the grandfather clause and penalties, enactment + 180 days for the prohibition) with no reconciling language.

36. **General — no rulemaking authority.** No agency is authorized to issue regulations defining any of the undefined terms (chips, training run, counting methodology, safety evaluation, Secretary). Every gap above is permanent unless Congress amends.

37. **General — no appropriation, no judicial review provision, no severability clause, no preemption clause, no whistleblower provision.** The absence of judicial review language leaves prong (B) determinations in APA limbo; the absence of a severability clause gives litigants an argument (though courts generally presume severability) that striking the vague criminal provision takes the rest with it.

38. **General — "person" is undefined in the Act.** The Dictionary Act (1 U.S.C. § 1) will fill it for "any person in the United States," which actually makes that clause broader than the "U.S. person" definition. The two-tier structure is backwards: location-based coverage is broad, nationality-based coverage is narrow.

---

**A practical caveat for the legal team.** These items fall into two buckets. Items like 7, 8, 10, 24–27, 29, 31–37 are straightforward drafting defects that make a strong "unworkable as written" case for the markup. Items like 1, 13, 20, 23, and 28 describe conduct that is arguably lawful on the text but transparently contrary to the bill's purpose; a court or prosecutor applying purposive interpretation, and particularly the "willfully" standard in Sec. 5(b), may not read them as generously as a literal parse does, and a pretextual "safety evaluation" or a run deliberately split to dodge the threshold is exactly the kind of fact pattern that reads badly in front of a jury. Worth deciding internally which bucket each argument belongs in before anyone outside legal starts treating the second group as an operating plan. For the hearing itself, the first bucket is also the stronger advocacy: it lets Meridian argue the bill fails on its own terms without appearing to be shopping for ways around it.
