# S. 2817 (as amended Oct. 6): Loopholes, Ambiguities, and Drafting Problems

Issues are listed in section order. The six I'd lead with are at the end. Points about constitutional or litigation risk are my best read and should be checked by counsel before they go to the office.

## Sec. 2: Definitions

1. **§2(1)(A), "single training run."** A developer can split training into several runs below 10^26, or continue, fine-tune, or merge models, and no single run crosses the threshold. The definition should count cumulative compute across all training stages of a model and its derivatives, including pre-training, continued training, fine-tuning, RL, and model merging.

2. **§2(1)(A), fixed threshold.** As algorithms improve, models trained on less than 10^26 operations will reach the capabilities the threshold is meant to capture. The bill gives no authority to lower it. Add rulemaking authority for the agency to revise the threshold downward, without the power to raise it.

3. **§2(1)(A), no counting method.** The bill doesn't say how operations are measured or estimated, or who certifies the count. Developers can use favorable accounting, and enforcement becomes a contest between dueling experts. Direct the agency to issue a binding methodology.

4. **§2(1)(A), "computing power."** Computing power is a rate, not a quantity. The phrase should be "a quantity of computation" or "a number of operations." This is minor, but it is the kind of imprecision litigants exploit.

5. **§2(1)(A)–(B), undefined and inconsistent terms.** "Artificial intelligence," "model," and "system" are undefined, and (A) says "model" while (B) says "system." A company could argue that a scaffolded or multi-model product is not a "model," or that an individual model is not a "system."

6. **§2(1)(B) and throughout, "the Secretary" is never identified.** The bill never says which Secretary. The only Secretary it names is the Secretary of State, in §6, so a court could read §2(1)(B) as referring to State. This is a basic drafting error that undermines the whole capability prong. Name the Secretary of Commerce, or whichever official is intended, in the definitions.

7. **§2(1)(B), the capability trigger comes too late.** A system "substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" is essentially the outcome the bill exists to prevent. By the time that determination is possible, the danger has already arrived. The capability prong should be keyed to precursor capabilities, such as autonomous AI R&D, self-replication, or broad expert-level performance, and to training intended to produce such capabilities.

8. **§2(1)(B), vague standard with no process.** The bill sets no criteria, procedure, timing, or notice for a determination. It is also unclear whether liability attaches before the determination is made. This invites void-for-vagueness challenges, especially to criminal charges under §5(b), and gives regulated parties grounds to argue they lacked fair notice. Require published criteria, a determination process, and prospective effect.

9. **§2(1), no anti-evasion or distillation coverage.** A company can distill a sub-threshold model from a grandfathered frontier model, or train one on its outputs, and capture most of its capabilities outside the definition. Add coverage for derivatives of covered systems and a general anti-evasion clause.

10. **§2(2), "U.S. person" is too narrow.** The definition omits lawful permanent residents, LLCs, partnerships, and other non-corporate entities. It may also omit entities organized under the laws of D.C. and the territories, since "State" is undefined. A lab structured as an LLC working abroad would escape the extraterritorial reach entirely.

11. **§2(2), the foreign-subsidiary loophole.** A U.S. company can create a subsidiary organized abroad, and that subsidiary is not a "U.S. person." It can then train covered systems overseas, possibly using U.S.-designed chips and U.S. staff working remotely. The definition should cover entities owned or controlled by U.S. persons, and the prohibition should bar U.S. persons from directing, funding, or facilitating covered activity abroad.

12. **§2(3), "advanced AI chips" and "data center" are undefined.** Without a performance-based chip definition, the registry threshold can't be applied. Operators can argue that particular chips don't count.

13. **§2(3), the 50,000-chip threshold can be evaded by splitting.** An operator can keep each site at 49,999 chips and train across several sites. Aggregate commonly owned or controlled facilities, cover facilities connected for distributed training, and add a compute-capacity threshold in addition to the chip count. The threshold should also be adjustable.

## Sec. 3: Prohibition

14. **§3(a), the remote-access gap.** The prohibition covers "any person in the United States" and "any U.S. person anywhere." It does not cover a foreign person abroad who rents U.S. data-center compute. The prohibition should apply to any covered activity conducted using facilities or hardware located in the United States, and cloud providers should be barred from knowingly supplying compute for it.

15. **§3(a), "person" is undefined.** It is unclear whether federal, state, or local government entities, or contractors working for them, are covered. Either answer should be stated expressly so it can't be litigated.

16. **§3(a), the list of prohibited conduct is incomplete.** "Train, develop, or deploy" does not reach knowingly providing compute or chips for covered training, funding it, possessing or transferring the weights of an unlawful system, or publishing those weights. "Develop" is also undefined.

17. **§3(b), the grandfather clause is the biggest loophole in the bill.** Any training run that "began" before enactment is exempt. That exemption appears to cover training, further development, and deployment, indefinitely. A lab can start a very large run the day before enactment, or argue that a much larger successor is a continuation of an earlier run. At most, the exemption should cover only deployment of systems whose training was completed before a fixed date. Further training or scaling of those systems should be expressly prohibited.

18. **§3(b), "began" is undefined.** It is unclear what counts as the start of training. A company could claim that a small preliminary run or a data-preparation step began the training of a later frontier model. Add a definition and require disclosure of grandfathered systems within a short deadline.

19. **§3(c), the safety-evaluation exemption swallows the rule.** "Activities conducted for the purpose of safety evaluation" has no definition, no approval process, and no limits. On its face it would let a company train a new covered system as long as it says the purpose is to evaluate safety. Limit the exemption to evaluating existing lawful or grandfathered systems, exclude any new training or scaling, and require prior agency authorization and reporting.

## Sec. 4: Registry

20. **§4, no penalty for violating the registry.** §5 penalizes only violations of §3. Failing to register, failing to report, or filing a false report carries no civil or criminal consequence under the Act. At most, false statements might fall under 18 U.S.C. §1001. Extend §5 to cover §4.

21. **§4(a), no registration deadline and "operator" undefined.** The bill doesn't say when facilities must register, and it is unclear whether the owner, the lessee, or a cloud tenant is the "operator."

22. **§4(b), self-certified reports with no way to check them.** The bill provides no inspection authority, audit rights, subpoena power, or on-site verification. Enforcement therefore depends entirely on what operators choose to say. Add inspection and audit authority, along with recordkeeping requirements.

23. **§4(b), the reporting content is too vague and too infrequent.** "Describing the computing workloads" doesn't require operators to report compute in operations, the identity of customers, or the size of individual jobs. A large training run can start and finish within a single quarter. Require specific data fields, customer identification (know-your-customer), and prompt notice of large training jobs.

24. **§4, no chip tracking.** The registry covers facilities but not the chips themselves. Chips held outside registered facilities, or moved between facilities, go unseen. Consider requiring reports on chip ownership and transfers.

25. **§4, no confidentiality provision.** Without trade-secret protections and a FOIA exemption, operators have both a policy argument and a litigation argument against the reporting requirement. A confidentiality clause makes the registry more defensible.

## Sec. 5: Penalties

26. **§5(a), the bill contradicts itself on timing.** Civil liability attaches to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §8 says the Act takes effect 180 days after enactment. Conduct during that window would be penalized before the prohibition is in force, which invites due-process and retroactivity challenges. Conform the two to the effective date.

27. **§5, no enforcement authority is designated.** The bill doesn't say who brings civil actions (the Attorney General or the agency), whether penalties are assessed administratively or in court, or what procedures apply. There is also no express authority for courts to issue injunctions halting a training run in progress.

28. **§5(a), the penalty is too small to deter frontier labs.** A cap of $1M per day is small relative to the value of frontier AI. Add disgorgement, forfeiture of the hardware and model weights involved, injunctive relief, and penalties scaled to revenue or compute.

29. **§5(b), criminal liability tied to a vague definition.** Applying criminal penalties to a §2(1)(B) violation, given the problems in items 6–8, is the most likely target for a vagueness challenge. One fix is to limit criminal liability to the compute-threshold prong, or to conduct after a published determination.

30. **§5, no whistleblower protections.** Employees are the most likely to discover violations, and the bill gives them no anti-retaliation protection or reporting channel. Add both.

## Sec. 6: International Agreement

31. **§6, "seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months" is ambiguous.** It could mean begin within 18 months or conclude within 18 months. Either way, "seek to" creates no real obligation. Add progress reports to Congress, a defined timeline, and verification provisions modeled on arms-control agreements.

32. **§6, no measures toward non-parties.** The bill has nothing on export controls or compute restrictions for countries that don't join an agreement. Without them, development can simply move offshore.

## Sec. 7: Duration

33. **§7(b), the sunset defeats §7(a).** The Act expires after 10 years whether or not anyone has found that superintelligence can be built safely. Companies can simply wait it out, which contradicts §7(a)'s premise. Strike §7(b), or at minimum apply it only to the registry and not to the prohibition.

34. **§7(a), the NAS finding has no mechanism.** The bill doesn't direct the National Academies to produce a report, fund one, or set a timeline. "Can be developed safely" is also undefined. Note too that a joint resolution requires presentment, so it is functionally an ordinary law. The provision adds no protection, and its vague standard could be used to pressure Congress.

## Sec. 8: Effective Date

35. **§8, the 180-day window invites a race.** Training that begins after enactment but before the effective date is not grandfathered under §3(b), but it is also not prohibited until day 180. That creates an incentive to run as much training as possible in the window. The prohibition on new training should take effect immediately on enactment, with the delay applying only to the registry.

## Missing Provisions

36. **No severability clause.** If a court strikes one provision, for example §2(1)(B), the whole Act becomes vulnerable. Add a standard severability clause.

37. **No general rulemaking authority.** Many of the fixes above, including definitions, counting methods, and adjustable thresholds, depend on the agency having rulemaking power. The bill grants none.

38. **No statement on state law.** The bill doesn't say whether stronger state laws are preserved. Adding a savings clause would prevent industry from arguing that the federal Act preempts them.

## Recommended Top Priorities for the Whitfield Office

1. The grandfather clause and the 180-day window (items 17, 18, 35).
2. The safety-evaluation exemption (19).
3. The undefined "Secretary" (6).
4. The sunset (33).
5. The single-run, facility-splitting, and foreign-subsidiary evasion routes (1, 11, 13).
6. The unenforceable registry and the lack of a named enforcement authority (20, 22, 27).
