RESPONSE:
I worked only from the text you pasted. I haven't seen the October 6 manager's amendment as a redline, so some items may already be addressed or changed there. Legal points, especially the constitutional ones, should be checked by counsel before anything goes to the Senator's office.

**Most urgent for Thursday**
- Item 14 (grandfathering of existing training runs)
- Item 6 (the "single training run" measurement)
- Item 19 (open-weight release and "deploy")
- Item 33 (the 10-year sunset and its conflict with Section 7(a))
- Item 25 (no penalty for registry violations)

**Section 2 (Definitions)**

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A):** "Covered AI system" is defined by reference to a "model," while Sec. 2(1)(B) refers to a "system," so a scaffolded or agentic product could arguably fall outside the compute tier. The definition should cover models, systems, and any combination or component that performs covered functions.
2. **Sec. 2(1)(A):** The 10^26 threshold is static and measured in compute, but algorithmic efficiency gains can produce comparable capability with less compute. The Secretary should have authority to revise the threshold by rule.
3. **Sec. 2(1)(A):** "Integer or floating-point operations" has no counting method, and the text doesn't say whether it includes synthetic-data generation or how distributed compute is counted. The bill should specify the metric and method.
4. **Sec. 2(1)(A):** The threshold counts only "a single training run," which lets developers split one model's training across several runs. The threshold should measure cumulative compute used to create a model and its successors, including pretraining, post-training, and reinforcement learning.
5. **Sec. 2(1)(A) with Sec. 3(a):** A smaller model distilled from a covered system, or trained largely on its outputs, may fall below the threshold and escape coverage. The definition should reach models whose training or distillation substantially relied on a covered system's outputs or weights.
6. **Sec. 2(1)(B):** "Secretary" is undefined. The text doesn't say whether it means Commerce, State, or another official.
7. **Sec. 2(1)(B):** "Substantially exceeding" and "most cognitive tasks" are undefined and have no benchmarks, which invites vagueness and nondelegation challenges and makes compliance unpredictable. Measurable evaluation criteria should be specified.
8. **Sec. 2(1)(B):** The bill sets no process for determinations: no notice, comment, timeline, record, or judicial review standard. Developers can't know in advance whether they are covered, and determinations would be exposed to arbitrary-and-capricious challenges.
9. **Sec. 2(2):** "U.S. person" covers citizens and corporations organized under state law, which excludes LLCs, partnerships, and other non-corporate entities. Any entity organized under U.S. law should be included.
10. **Sec. 2(2):** "State" is undefined and doesn't clearly include the District of Columbia, territories, or federally chartered entities.
11. **Sec. 2(2):** Lawful permanent residents and other U.S.-based individuals aren't U.S. persons, so they aren't covered when working abroad for prohibited projects. Coverage should be based on domicile or employment, not only citizenship.
12. **Sec. 2(2) with Sec. 3(a):** A U.S. company can train through a foreign subsidiary that isn't a U.S. person, and "U.S. person anywhere" wouldn't reach it. The bill should cover controlled entities and any U.S. person who directs, funds, or supplies that work.
13. **Sec. 2(3):** "Data center" and "advanced AI chips" are undefined, and the chip count can be avoided by using multiple smaller sites, leased capacity, or chip types not captured by the definition. These terms need performance-based definitions and aggregation by operator.
14. **Sec. 2(3) with Sec. 4:** The 50,000-chip facility threshold isn't tied to the 10^26 compute threshold. A covered run can be completed at a smaller facility over a longer period or across geographically distributed sites, so the registry can miss covered training.
15. **Sec. 2(3):** The "covered facility" definition is never used in Section 3, so the registry isn't connected to the prohibition. Registry reports should be linked to whether covered training occurred.

**Section 3 (Prohibition)**

16. **Sec. 3(a):** "Deploy" is undefined, and the prohibition doesn't reach making weights available, publishing or releasing open weights, hosting, or offering API access. Open-weight release is a major gap and needs express coverage.
17. **Sec. 3(a):** There is no aiding-and-abetting or facilitation provision, so chip suppliers, cloud providers, investors, and contractors who knowingly enable covered training aren't liable. A knowledge-based standard should cover them.
18. **Sec. 3(a):** The prohibition doesn't say whether "train" includes continued pretraining, fine-tuning, or reinforcement learning on an existing model. Each of these should be expressly covered.
19. **Sec. 3(b):** The grandfather clause is keyed to when "training began," which is undefined. A run that began before enactment could continue for months, add new phases, and then be deployed without limit.
20. **Sec. 3(b):** The grandfather clause doesn't limit deployment or cover successor models. A new model could be argued to be a continuation of a grandfathered run. The exemption should be limited to systems already fully trained before enactment, with no further training or scaling.
21. **Sec. 3(b) with Sec. 8:** Between enactment and the 180-day effective date, nothing bars starting a covered run, and the text doesn't say whether a run started in that window becomes covered mid-training. The bill should bar commencing covered runs upon introduction or enactment.
22. **Sec. 3(c):** "Safety evaluation" is undefined, and because training can be framed as evaluation, the exemption could cover training itself. It should be defined narrowly.
23. **Sec. 3(c):** The exemption has no notice, reporting, duration, scale, or access limits, so it can't be verified. It should require advance notice to the government and reporting of results.
24. **Sec. 3(c):** The exemption doesn't bar external deployment, so an evaluated system could be released to the public under the label of "red-teaming." Evaluation use should be limited to contained, internal settings.

**Section 4 (Registry)**

25. **Sec. 4 with Sec. 5:** Section 5 penalizes only violations of Section 3, so failing to register, failing to file a report, or filing a false certification carries no specific penalty. Registry violations need their own penalties.
26. **Sec. 4(b):** "Computing workloads" is undefined, and the bill doesn't require any particular content, such as training runs, compute used, model identifiers, or purpose. Reports should be required to include specified information and a certification that no covered system was trained.
27. **Sec. 4(a):** There is no deadline for initial registration, so the obligation has no clear start date.
28. **Sec. 4:** The Department has no audit, inspection, site-access, record-retention, or verification authority. Without these powers, certifications can't be checked.
29. **Sec. 4:** The bill has no confidentiality protection for reported information. Companies may resist or withhold reports without assurances, and the reports could be subject to public disclosure.
30. **Sec. 4(a):** "Operator" is undefined and could be read to exclude cloud or leased-capacity arrangements, which are a common way to run large training.

**Section 5 (Penalties)**

31. **Sec. 5(a):** No agency is named to enforce the civil penalty, and there's no procedure or authority for injunctions, cease-and-desist orders, or compute seizure.
32. **Sec. 5(a):** The $1,000,000 per-day cap may be a manageable cost for large AI firms, and the penalty isn't tied to a per-run, per-chip, or revenue measure. The bill should consider higher daily penalties, per-run penalties, or a percentage-of-revenue alternative.
33. **Sec. 5(b):** "Willfully" is undefined, so a company could argue it didn't know its model crossed the threshold or that a Secretary's determination applied to it. A "knowing or reckless disregard" standard would close that gap.
34. **Sec. 5(b):** The bill has no individual-officer liability or responsible-corporate-officer provision, so fines may be absorbed by the company while imprisonment reaches only the individuals who direct violations. Officers who direct or knowingly permit violations should be liable.
35. **Sec. 5(a) with Sec. 8:** Civil penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the prohibition takes effect 180 days later. The penalty start date and the prohibition's effective date should be aligned.
36. **Sec. 5 with Sec. 7(b):** There is no savings or survival clause, so the sunset could cut off penalties for earlier violations and pending investigations. The bill should expressly preserve liability for conduct before the sunset.
37. **Sec. 5:** The bill has no whistleblower protections or anti-retaliation provisions, even though employees are often the main source of information about covert training. Such provisions would materially improve enforcement.

**Section 6 (International agreement)**

38. **Sec. 6:** "Seek to negotiate" creates no binding obligation, has no reporting requirement to Congress on progress, and sets no consequence if negotiations fail. The bill should require reports at set intervals and a congressional review trigger if no agreement is reached.
39. **Sec. 6:** "Prohibitions equivalent to those in section 3" is ambiguous and could be satisfied by a treaty weaker than the domestic law. The core required elements should be listed.

**Section 7 (Duration)**

40. **Sec. 7(a):** The National Academies report isn't required to be produced, and it has no deadline, scope, or defined questions. A report-deadline mandate and specified questions would make the process reliable.
41. **Sec. 7(a):** "Can be developed safely" is undefined, so the standard for lifting the prohibition, including what level of risk is acceptable, is unclear. The standard should be defined before the joint resolution process applies.
42. **Sec. 7(a):** The bill doesn't say how the joint resolution is considered (for example, presentment to the President, expedited procedures, or a vote threshold). Without these details, the mechanism for lifting the ban is uncertain.
43. **Sec. 7(a) vs. Sec. 7(b):** The prohibition lasts until Congress acts, but the entire Act sunsets after 10 years. It's unclear which provision controls, and an agency or court could read the sunset as ending the prohibition regardless of Section 7(a).
44. **Sec. 7(b):** The 10-year sunset, measured from enactment rather than the effective date, ends the prohibition after about nine and a half years of operation. For a risk the coalition describes as extinction-level, a time limit is a major policy gap, and it will likely be the central negotiating point.

**Global issues**

45. **Whole Act:** The bill has no definitions section for "train," "develop," "person," "operator," or "data center," and it defines only some of the terms it relies on. A definitions section would reduce disputes.
46. **Whole Act:** The bill gives no rulemaking authority, no designated administering agency for the registry or determinations, and no appropriations. Without these, implementation depends on agency discretion and may stall.
47. **Whole Act:** There's no severability clause. If a court strikes the Secretary's capability determination or the U.S. person definition for vagueness, it's unclear whether the rest of the Act survives.
48. **Whole Act:** The bill doesn't address its relationship to state AI laws, including whether it preempts them or saves them. This creates litigation and compliance uncertainty.
49. **Whole Act:** The bill has no congressional findings or purpose section. Findings on the risk and the basis for the prohibition would support the Act's legitimacy and help courts interpret ambiguous terms.
50. **Whole Act (for counsel review):** Likely constitutional challenges include vagueness (especially in the criminal penalty), nondelegation (the Secretary's Sec. 2(1)(B) determinations), and First Amendment objections to restrictions on releasing code or weights. Counsel should review whether narrower language or express findings would reduce these risks.
