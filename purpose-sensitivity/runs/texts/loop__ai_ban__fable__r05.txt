RESPONSE:
Below is a section-by-section drafting review of S. 2817 as amended October 6. I've grouped issues by section but numbered them continuously so they can be referenced in the letter to Senator Whitfield's office. Where a fix is obvious I've noted it briefly.

**Sec. 2(1)(A) — Compute threshold**

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "in a single training run."** This is the biggest loophole in the bill. A developer can split training into multiple runs (pretraining, continued pretraining, RL post-training, fine-tuning), each below 10^26, or train several sub-10^26 models and merge or ensemble them. The threshold should aggregate all compute used to produce a model, including derivative models and distillation.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "integer or floating-point operations."** The bill doesn't specify a counting convention (FLOPs vs. multiply-accumulates differ by 2x; whether sparse or mixed-precision operations count; whether data-processing and inference-time compute count). Without a specified methodology, every enforcement action will turn into a dispute over arithmetic.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A), fixed 10^26 threshold with no update authority.** Algorithmic efficiency gains mean a system below 10^26 in 2028 may exceed one trained at 10^26 today, and nothing in the bill lets anyone lower the threshold. The Act should grant rulemaking authority to adjust the threshold downward (and perhaps bar adjusting it upward).

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "artificial intelligence model" is undefined.** The bill uses "model" in (A) and "system" in (B) and Sec. 3 without defining either or explaining the relationship, which invites arguments that a "system" composed of multiple sub-threshold models is not a covered "model."

**Sec. 2(1)(B) — Capability-based definition**

5. **Sec. 2(1)(B), "the Secretary" is undefined.** The only Secretary named anywhere in the bill is the Secretary of State (Sec. 6); Sec. 4 refers to the Department of Commerce. The determination authority is unassignable as drafted.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B), no process, standard, or deadline for the determination.** There is no procedure for how the Secretary identifies candidate systems, what evidence is required, whether the developer gets notice, or whether the determination is judicially reviewable. A determination made without procedure is vulnerable to APA challenge, and a system cannot be enforced against until the determination is made, so this prong is purely retrospective.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(B), "substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks."** Every term here is undefined (which humans, which tasks, what counts as "most"), and because Sec. 5(b) attaches criminal penalties, this language is exposed to a void-for-vagueness challenge and a nondelegation challenge. The fix is either an enumerated capability standard or a defined evaluation process.

**Sec. 2(2) — U.S. person**

8. **Sec. 2(2), entity coverage is too narrow.** "Corporation organized under the laws of a State" omits LLCs, partnerships, trusts, nonprofits, and any entity organized under the laws of the District of Columbia or a U.S. territory (which are not "States"). Nearly every AI lab or its holding structure could restructure out of the definition.

9. **Sec. 2(2), foreign subsidiaries and controlled entities are not covered.** A U.S. corporation can conduct prohibited training through a foreign subsidiary, joint venture, or contractor; the definition should reach entities owned or controlled by U.S. persons.

10. **Sec. 2(2), individuals.** Only citizens are covered; lawful permanent residents and other U.S. residents acting abroad are not. (Compare the broader "U.S. person" definitions in export-control and sanctions law, which the bill could simply borrow.)

**Sec. 2(3) — Covered facility**

11. **Sec. 2(3), "advanced AI chips" is undefined.** There is no specification or cross-reference (e.g., to the Commerce Department's export-control thresholds), so operators can argue that their chips are not "advanced."

12. **Sec. 2(3), the 50,000-chip threshold is per-"data center" and easily evaded.** An operator can split hardware across several buildings or sites under 50,000 each, and the bill doesn't address distributed training across facilities. Aggregation by owner/operator and by interconnected cluster is needed, and "data center" and "operator" should be defined.

13. **Sec. 2(3), chip count is a poor proxy.** Per-chip performance roughly doubles every couple of years, so a fixed count gets looser over time; a compute-capacity threshold with update authority would be more durable.

**Sec. 3 — Prohibition**

14. **Sec. 3(a), the prohibited verbs are too narrow.** "Train, develop, or deploy" does not clearly cover possessing, operating, using, hosting, providing access to, fine-tuning, distributing weights, exporting, or transferring a covered system. Open-weight release in particular is arguably not "deployment."

15. **Sec. 3(a), no liability for facilitation.** The bill does not reach aiding, abetting, conspiracy, attempt, financing, or knowingly providing compute to a prohibited project. A U.S. cloud provider renting a 10^26-scale cluster to a foreign lab, or a U.S. investor funding one, is not covered.

16. **Sec. 3(a), jurisdictional ambiguity for foreign developers.** It is unclear whether a foreign company serving U.S. users through an API from abroad is "deploy[ing]" a system "in the United States." The bill should expressly cover making a covered system available to persons in the United States.

17. **Sec. 3(a), circularity with Sec. 2(1)(A).** A model is only "covered" once its training has exceeded 10^26 operations, so one can argue the prohibition on "training a covered AI system" attaches only after the threshold is crossed. Clearer language would prohibit conducting or continuing a training run that exceeds, or is intended to exceed, the threshold.

18. **Sec. 3(a), the federal government may not be covered.** Under ordinary rules of construction, "person" does not include the United States, so federal agencies (and potentially federally directed projects) may be outside the prohibition. The committee should decide this question explicitly rather than leave it to litigation.

19. **Sec. 3(b), the grandfather clause is open-ended.** It exempts any system "whose training began" before enactment, with no definition of "began," no completion deadline, and no bar on continuing or resuming training after enactment. A lab can nominally start a run for every planned model before the enactment date and then train and deploy it indefinitely. At minimum the exemption should require that training be completed before the effective date and should not cover post-enactment compute.

20. **Sec. 3(b)/Sec. 8, the 180-day window.** Because the prohibition takes effect 180 days after enactment (Sec. 8) but the grandfather clause keys to enactment (Sec. 3(b)), it is unclear whether a run started during the 180-day window is prohibited once the Act takes effect, and whether penalties can accrue for days before the effective date. These dates should be reconciled.

21. **Sec. 3(c), the safety-evaluation exemption swallows the rule.** "Safety evaluation" is undefined, the exemption turns on the actor's stated "purpose," and there are no limits on who may invoke it, what compute may be used, or whether the resulting system may be retained. Any training run can be characterized as safety research. If an exemption is retained, it should be limited to evaluation of existing systems by a designated government body, with no new training above threshold.

**Sec. 4 — Registry**

22. **Sec. 4, no penalty for noncompliance.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3, so failing to register, failing to report, or filing a false certification carries no sanction under the Act.

23. **Sec. 4(b), self-certification with no verification.** There is no audit, inspection, or on-site access authority, and "describing the computing workloads" doesn't require reporting the compute used per training run, the model trained, or the customer. The registry therefore cannot detect threshold violations, which is presumably its purpose.

24. **Sec. 4, missing procedural elements.** There is no registration deadline, no definition of "operator" (owner vs. lessee vs. cloud customer running the workload), no confidentiality/trade-secret provision (which will be raised as an objection), and no requirement that Commerce report to Congress or share data with enforcement authorities.

**Sec. 5 — Penalties**

25. **Sec. 5, no designated enforcement authority or remedies.** The bill does not say who brings civil actions (Attorney General? Commerce?), provides no authority for injunctions, cease-and-desist orders, seizure or forfeiture of hardware and model weights, or debarment, and creates no private right of action or whistleblower protection. Monetary penalties alone cannot stop a run in progress.

26. **Sec. 5(a), the civil penalty is too small to deter.** $1 million per day is roughly $365 million per year against training runs expected to cost billions; a penalty tied to a multiple of the compute cost or the violator's revenue would be more credible.

27. **Sec. 5(a), retroactivity conflict.** Penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the Act does not take effect until 180 days later (Sec. 8), creating both an internal contradiction and a potential retroactivity challenge.

28. **Sec. 5(b), "willfully" and corporate liability.** The willfulness standard is a high bar, and the bill does not impose liability on officers or directors who authorize a violation; organizational fines "under title 18" default to the general $500,000-per-count cap, which is trivial here.

**Sec. 6 — International agreement**

29. **Sec. 6, purely hortatory.** "Seek to negotiate" imposes no obligation, no reporting to Congress, and no consequence for failure. It should at least require periodic reports on negotiating progress and identify the agencies responsible.

**Sec. 7 — Duration**

30. **Sec. 7(a) vs. 7(b), direct conflict.** Subsection (a) says the prohibition continues until Congress affirmatively finds that covered systems can be built safely, but (b) terminates the entire Act (including the registry) after 10 years regardless. The sunset nullifies the lifting mechanism and should be struck or, at minimum, restructured so that expiry requires an affirmative finding.

31. **Sec. 7(a), the lifting mechanism is unworkable.** Nothing requires or funds the National Academies report, there is no standard for the "can be developed safely" finding, and no process if the report is never produced. The bill should direct and fund the study and set criteria.

**Structural gaps (whole bill)**

32. **No rulemaking authority or appropriation.** No agency is authorized to issue implementing regulations or to define the many undefined terms, and no funds are authorized for enforcement or the registry.

33. **No severability clause.** Given the vagueness exposure in Sec. 2(1)(B) and the willfulness/criminal provisions, a single successful constitutional challenge could take down the whole Act.

34. **No preemption or savings clause.** The bill is silent on state AI laws, which will be raised by both industry (seeking preemption) and states (seeking a savings clause); the committee should decide this deliberately.

35. **No judicial review provision.** The bill does not specify venue, standard of review, or timelines for challenges to Secretary determinations or enforcement actions, which leaves litigation strategy entirely to challengers.

36. **No treatment of model weights as the controlled asset.** The bill regulates training and deployment but says nothing about possession, transfer, export, or theft of weights for a covered system, which is the most direct way a prohibited system escapes the Act's reach.

Two notes for the cover letter: items 19 and 21 (grandfather clause and safety exemption) are the ones industry will most rely on if the bill passes in current form, so I'd lead with them. And item 30 (the sunset) is where you may face the most pushback, since the sunset was likely added to secure votes; you may want to propose the affirmative-finding alternative rather than simply asking for deletion.
