Everything below comes from the text you pasted. I haven't checked it against the official version of the manager's amendment. I've grouped items by section in bill order, with cross-cutting problems at the end.

## Section 2: Definitions

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "single training run" is undefined.** A developer could split a project into staged runs that each fall under 10^26 operations, or keep training a saved checkpoint in new runs. The bill doesn't say whether compute from earlier runs, post-training (fine-tuning, reinforcement learning), or synthetic-data generation counts.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A), no measurement or verification method.** The bill doesn't say how operations are counted (theoretical vs. actual, which operations) or who verifies the figure. Compute totals would be self-reported and easy to dispute.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A), the fixed threshold is a poor proxy for capability.** Algorithmic efficiency gains could produce highly capable models below 10^26, while some models above it may not be dangerous. That mismatch invites an arbitrary-and-capricious or due process challenge. The bill's title says "superintelligence," but the operative text never uses or defines the term.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A), only training compute counts.** Capability gained through inference-time compute, scaffolding, or an ensemble of several sub-threshold models isn't reached by (A), which covers a single "model." Only the discretionary (B) trigger could catch those.

5. **Sec. 2(1), "artificial intelligence" is not defined.** Neither "model" nor "system" is defined, and the bill doesn't borrow an existing statutory definition. That leaves room for disputes over what counts, and over how a "model" differs from a "system."

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B), "the Secretary" is not identified.** Sec. 6 specifies the Secretary of State, but nothing says which Secretary makes the capability determination. Sec. 4 involves Commerce, so the omission matters.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(B), the capability standard is vague and the process is missing.** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" doesn't say which humans (median or expert), which tasks, or how "most" is counted. There is no required procedure, notice, comment period, deadline, or judicial review.

8. **Sec. 2(1)(B), timing and fair notice.** A determination is necessarily made after a system exists, and the bill doesn't say whether earlier conduct becomes unlawful. Making civil and criminal liability depend on an unpredictable agency finding invites vagueness and nondelegation challenges.

9. **Sec. 2(2), "U.S. person" omits many entity types.** It covers only citizens and corporations "organized under the laws of a State." It leaves out lawful permanent residents, partnerships, trusts, nonprofits, and universities, and it's unclear whether LLCs count. "State" is also undefined, so DC and territorial entities are uncertain.

10. **Sec. 2(2), foreign subsidiaries.** A foreign subsidiary of a U.S. company is likely a foreign corporation. A parent could therefore arguably train a covered system abroad through a subsidiary and sidestep the "U.S. person anywhere" reach in Sec. 3(a).

11. **Sec. 2(3), "advanced AI chips" and "data center" are undefined.** There's no performance standard for what counts as an advanced chip. Operators could spread chips across sites, lease capacity, or stay just under 50,000.

12. **Sec. 2(3), the facility threshold doesn't match the compute threshold.** A facility with far fewer than 50,000 chips could plausibly complete a 10^26-operation run within a year. As chips improve, a fixed chip count captures less and less.

## Section 3: Prohibition

13. **Sec. 3(a), "train, develop, or deploy" are undefined.** It's unclear whether internal use, API access, open-weight release, or a research prototype is "deployment," and "develop" overlaps with "train." The bill doesn't reach owning, selling, financing, hosting, or exporting a covered system, and it has no civil aiding-and-abetting provision.

14. **Sec. 3(a), when the violation begins.** A model is "covered" under (A) only once it has been trained with more than 10^26 operations. A run in progress isn't clearly unlawful until it crosses the line, and nothing authorizes stopping it or reaching preparatory steps.

15. **Sec. 3(a), foreign actors.** The ban reaches persons "in the United States" and U.S. persons, so a foreign company abroad could train a system and serve U.S. users. Whether remote deployment into the U.S. counts is unclear.

16. **Sec. 3(b), "training began" is undefined and the grandfathering is exploitable.** The bill doesn't say whether the start is data preparation, initialization, or the first training step, and there's no registry of existing systems to prove it. It also rewards firms that start large runs before enactment.

17. **Sec. 3(b), the grandfather clause is open-ended.** It exempts the system itself, so deployment, scaling, continued training, and fine-tuning of an existing system are apparently all permitted with no compute cap. It's unclear whether a successor model built from an existing checkpoint is a "new" system. The exemption also seems to apply even if the Secretary later designates the system under (B).

18. **Sec. 3(c), the safety-evaluation exemption turns on self-declared purpose.** "Safety evaluation" is undefined, and there's no compute cap, approval requirement, oversight, or reporting. Training or deploying a covered system could be framed as evaluation, and mixed-purpose activity is hard to police.

19. **Sec. 3, government and national-security activity.** There's no express exemption or coverage for federal agencies, the military, or intelligence work, and "person" is undefined. The federal government generally isn't treated as a "person," so whether government programs are covered is ambiguous, though contractors likely are.

## Section 4: Registry

20. **Sec. 4(a), the registry covers facilities, not systems or runs.** Training at sites below 50,000 chips, or across several sites, never appears. The bill sets no registration deadline and doesn't define "operator," so owner vs. cloud tenant is unclear.

21. **Sec. 4(b), the reporting duty is thin.** "Describing the computing workloads" sets no level of detail. A multi-tenant operator may not know what customers run, especially with encrypted workloads, and nothing requires customer vetting. Reports are self-certified, quarterly, and come with no audit or inspection authority.

22. **Secs. 4 and 5, no penalty for registry violations.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3, so failing to register or to file reports has no stated consequence. False statements might be reachable under general law (e.g., 18 U.S.C. § 1001), but a failure to register wouldn't be.

23. **Sec. 4(b), no confidentiality or information-sharing rules.** The bill doesn't address trade-secret protection, FOIA treatment, or sharing with enforcement agencies. That could prompt industry objections and challenges.

## Section 5: Penalties

24. **Sec. 5(a), no enforcement mechanism.** The bill doesn't say who may bring a civil action, in what forum, or under what procedure. It also provides no injunctive relief, subpoena or inspection power, whistleblower protection, or funding, so the penalty may be unusable in practice.

25. **Secs. 5(a) and 8, an effective-date conflict.** Penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but Sec. 3 doesn't take effect until 180 days later. Courts could read the clause as meaningless or as retroactive, and either reading creates a fair-notice problem.

26. **Sec. 5(a), the penalty cap and day-counting.** A maximum of $1 million per day is small next to frontier training costs, which are often hundreds of millions of dollars or more, so it could be treated as a cost of doing business. It's also unclear how "each day" applies to a one-time training run vs. ongoing deployment.

27. **Sec. 5(b), "willfully" raises the proof burden.** Prosecutors may need to show the defendant knew the conduct was unlawful and knew the compute or capability threshold was met, which is hard given unclear measurement and the (B) designation. There's also no provision for officer or executive liability.

## Sections 6–8: Diplomacy, duration, effective date

28. **Sec. 6, the international provision is soft and ambiguous.** "Shall seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months" doesn't say whether the deadline applies to starting talks or concluding an agreement. "Equivalent" prohibitions are undefined, and there's no reporting requirement, consequence for failure, or tie to export controls. Offshore development by non-U.S. firms remains outside the bill.

29. **Sec. 7, subsections (a) and (b) conflict.** (a) says the prohibition lasts until Congress finds covered systems can be developed safely, while (b) imposes a hard 10-year sunset regardless. That makes the (a) off-ramp largely redundant and leaves it unclear which controls.

30. **Sec. 7(a), the standard and process are undefined.** "Developed safely" has no definition, and the National Academies report isn't mandated, funded, or deadlined. "Acting on a report" doesn't say whether Congress must follow its conclusions, and a joint resolution is subject to a presidential veto.

31. **Sec. 7(b), the sunset has no savings clause.** The bill doesn't say what happens to pending or past violations, or to registry data. Because Sec. 8 delays the effective date by 180 days, the ban runs about 9.5 years. Firms can plan around a known end date.

32. **Sec. 8, the 180-day gap invites a rush.** Combined with the grandfather clause in Sec. 3(b), firms have a window to start large training runs before the prohibition applies. Runs begun after enactment but before the effective date sit in a gray zone.

## Cross-cutting issues

33. **No severability clause.** If a provision such as the (B) designation were struck down, challengers could argue the rest should fall too. Courts presume severability, but the bill doesn't say so.

34. **No findings or stated constitutional basis.** Nothing grounds the bill in the Commerce Clause, which matters for purely in-state training. Opponents could also raise First Amendment claims (code, model weights, and research as speech) and due process vagueness claims. These are plausible but untested arguments.

35. **No implementing authority or funding.** The bill gives no agency rulemaking power to define terms, update thresholds, or issue guidance, and no appropriation for registry or enforcement work. Fixed thresholds in statute can only be changed by new legislation.

A few caveats. Items 8, 19, 25, 27, and 34 involve legal judgments you may want to run past a constitutional or criminal-law expert before publishing. Given CPR's nonpartisan approach, you may also want to ask the sponsor's office or committee staff whether they plan to address any of these in the markup. Several, such as the Sec. 5/Sec. 8 date conflict and the undefined "Secretary," look like fixable drafting errors rather than policy choices.
