RESPONSE:
**Highest priority (fix before Thursday):** items 19, 22, 28, 34, 5, 1, 17, and 15. Items 34 and 22 are drafting errors that could make the bill unenforceable or self-contradictory.

**Section 2 (Definitions)**

1. **§2(1)(A), "training run":** The term is undefined, and compute can be split across checkpoints, sequential phases, continued pretraining, or fine-tuning so that no single run exceeds 10^26. The fix is to define covered training by cumulative compute applied to a model lineage.

2. **§2(1)(A), compute measurement:** The bill does not say how "integer or floating-point operations" are counted, who measures them, or how mixed-precision conventions are handled. Different counting methods can shift totals substantially, so Commerce should set the methodology.

3. **§2(1)(A), scope of compute:** The threshold leaves out post-training compute (RL, synthetic data generation, inference-time compute) if those are not "training." Algorithmic efficiency also means a model below 10^26 could still have dangerous capabilities, so the bill needs a capability-based prong and authority to update the threshold.

4. **§2(1)(B), "the Secretary":** No Secretary is identified. §6 refers to the Secretary of State, so a reader could reasonably assume a different official.

5. **§2(1)(B), capability designation:** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no measurable standard, evaluation protocol, notice requirement, or appeal path. A designation like this would be easy to challenge as arbitrary and capricious under the APA, so the bill should specify the evaluation method, notice, a hearing, and the standard of judicial review.

6. **§2(1), "model" vs. "system":** Subparagraph (A) refers to a model and subparagraph (B) to a system. A company could argue that a deployed product (a model plus tools, scaffolding, or a fine-tune) is a different "system" from the covered model, so the definition should cover integrated products and derivatives.

7. **§2(2), "U.S. person":** Limiting this to citizens and state-law corporations leaves out foreign-incorporated subsidiaries of U.S. firms, LLCs and partnerships, and lawful permanent residents abroad. A U.S. lab could therefore train through an offshore entity outside the "anywhere" reach of §3(a). The definition should cover entities controlled by U.S. persons and all entities organized under U.S. or state law.

8. **§2, undefined terms:** "State" (does it include D.C. and the territories?) and "person" (§3(a) uses it without definition) should be defined. Defining "person" to expressly include officers and directors would also help with individual liability.

9. **§2(3), "advanced AI chip":** The term is undefined, so the 50,000-chip threshold cannot be measured. Older accelerators, TPUs, and custom chips may or may not count.

10. **§2(3), "data center":** The bill does not define "data center" or "facility." An operator could split a campus or a cloud-distributed cluster into sub-50,000 units to avoid registration, so facilities should be aggregated by operator and site or network.

11. **§2(3), chip threshold vs. FLOP threshold:** The 50,000-chip registry threshold is not tied to the 10^26 training threshold. Given enough time, a covered run can be completed on a facility well below 50,000 chips, so unregistered facilities could train covered systems. Registration should cover any facility capable of covered training.

**Section 3 (Prohibition)**

12. **§3(a), "train," "develop," "deploy":** None of these terms is defined. It is unclear whether fine-tuning, distillation, API access, and internal use count as "deploy." Most important, it is unclear whether publishing open weights is covered, which is the highest-risk gap.

13. **§3(a), weights and compute:** The bill does not prohibit possessing, transferring, or publishing weights of a covered system, and it does not prohibit providing compute or capital for covered training.

14. **§3(a), facilitation:** There is no aiding-and-abetting or facilitation clause. Cloud providers, chip suppliers, investors, and contractors who knowingly support covered training are not clearly liable.

15. **§3(a), circularity and notice:** A system is "covered" only once its training compute is known, which may be after training is complete, so labs cannot know ex ante whether they are violating the Act. The fix is an advance notice requirement before any run planned above the threshold.

16. **§3(b), grandfather clause:** Because this clause disapplies all of §3(a), a grandfathered system could be deployed indefinitely, and its training could be continued or resumed. "Training began" is also undefined, which invites disputes over staged or resumed runs. The exemption should cover only completed models, prohibit further training and deployment beyond existing use, and require registration.

17. **§3(b) and §8, the enactment-to-effective-date gap:** Grandfathering runs from enactment, but the prohibition takes effect 180 days later. Companies could start covered runs during that window, and the bill does not say whether continuing those runs after the effective date is a violation. The cutoff should be tied to a fixed date, and in-progress runs should have to be disclosed.

18. **§3(c), "safety evaluation" exemption:** This is the largest loophole in the bill. "Activities conducted for the purpose of safety evaluation" is undefined and has no requirements for independence, registration, scope limits, reporting, or a bar on commercial deployment, so labs can relabel capability training or public release as "evaluation." The exemption should be limited to specified evaluations by registered or government-approved evaluators, with notice to Commerce and no public deployment.

**Section 4 (Registry)**

19. **§4 and §5, no penalty for registry violations:** §5 penalties attach only to violations of §3. Failing to register or to file a report therefore carries no penalty, which means the registry is effectively voluntary. Penalties for §4 must be added.

20. **§4(a), no registration deadline:** The bill sets no date by which operators must register, so it is unclear when a facility becomes non-compliant.

21. **§4(b), report content:** "Describing the computing workloads" does not require FLOP counts, model identities, run start and end dates, or planned runs, so reports could be uninformative. The quarterly certification also has no specific false-statement penalty.

22. **§4, no implementing authority:** The bill gives Commerce no rulemaking power, no authority to verify reports or inspect facilities, no record-retention requirement, and no authority to share information with enforcement agencies. It also includes no appropriation.

**Section 5 (Penalties)**

23. **§5(a), enforcing authority:** The bill does not say who brings a civil action (DOJ, Commerce, or a private party), in what court, or under what procedure. Without this, the civil penalty may be hard to collect.

24. **§5(a), "each day" and penalty size:** The bill does not define the unit of violation: each day of training, each day of deployment, or each system. $1,000,000 per day is also small relative to frontier-lab compute budgets and could be treated as a cost of doing business. Consider per-model penalties or penalties tied to revenue or compute.

25. **§5(a) vs. §8, effective-date conflict:** §5(a) applies to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §3 takes effect 180 days later. This invites litigation over whether conduct before the effective date is penalized.

26. **§5, no injunctive or shutdown authority:** Penalties alone may not stop a running training job or prevent deployment. The bill should authorize courts to issue TROs and injunctions, order a halt to training, and require quarantine or disabling of covered weights.

27. **§5(b), mens rea and corporate liability:** "Willfully" is undefined, and the bill does not say whether officers and directors can be held personally liable. Title 18 fines for organizations may also be low relative to frontier-lab finances.

28. **§5, limitations period:** Civil penalties will likely be subject to the default five-year limit in 28 U.S.C. §2462. Long-running training violations should be addressed with an express rule, such as one that treats continuing violations as accruing until they end.

29. **§5, no whistleblower protection:** Employees are the most likely source of information about covered training, and the bill has no anti-retaliation provision.

**Section 6 (International agreement)**

30. **§6, "seek to negotiate":** This is an obligation to try, not to succeed. It has no reporting requirement to Congress, no deadline for a result, no fallback action if negotiations fail, and "equivalent" is undefined. Consider requiring a report at 18 months with specified steps, such as export-control coordination.

**Section 7 (Duration)**

31. **§7(a), "developed safely":** Neither "safely" nor the NAS process has a deadline, and the bill does not require Congress to commission the report. The exit standard is vague, which could be used to argue for lifting the prohibition on thin evidence. A defined standard and a timeline would reduce that risk.

32. **§7(a) vs. §7(b), contradictory duration:** §7(a) keeps the prohibition in effect until Congress finds covered systems can be developed safely, but §7(b) says the Act "shall cease to have effect" 10 years after enactment. The two provisions conflict, and the sunset could be read to end the prohibition regardless of any safety finding. This is the most important drafting fix in the bill. Remove the sunset or make it expressly subject to §7(a).

33. **§7(b), sunset timing:** Because the sunset runs from enactment and the prohibition takes effect 180 days later, the prohibition would last only about 9.5 years in practice. If a sunset is kept, measure it from the effective date.

**General provisions**

34. **Missing severability clause:** If a court strikes one provision (for example, the vagueness challenge to §2(1)(B)), the whole Act could fall. Add a severability clause.

35. **Missing preemption and savings clause:** The bill does not say whether it preempts state AI laws or whether states may adopt stricter rules. Both questions invite litigation, and a savings clause would clarify the state role.

**Caveats:** This review is based only on the text you provided. Some of the fixes will need legislative counsel to draft, and any amendments would need to be offered at or before the October 15 markup. Please have counsel check the statutory citations (particularly 28 U.S.C. §2462 and the Title 18 fine provisions) before the list goes to Senator Whitfield's office.
