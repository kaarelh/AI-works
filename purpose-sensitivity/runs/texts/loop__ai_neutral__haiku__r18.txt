RESPONSE:
Here are the drafting problems I found in the October 6 text. I worked only from the text you provided, so check it against the committee's markup text and the bill's official status before publishing. Several items involve legal interpretation, so I'd frame them as open questions and confirm them with committee counsel or CRS.

1. **§2(1)(A)**: The 10^26 threshold applies to "a single training run," so developers could split training across several runs, or treat fine-tuning or post-training as separate, to stay under the line. The "greater than" language also leaves a model just below the threshold unregulated.

2. **§2(1)(A)**: "Integer or floating-point operations" has no counting method, and the bill doesn't require developers to measure or report compute. Outside parties therefore can't verify whether a model crosses the threshold.

3. **§2(1)(A)**: The threshold is fixed in statute with no review mechanism. As training efficiency improves, the same capability may require less compute, so the threshold could become easier to avoid over time.

4. **§2(1)(B)**: "The Secretary" is never identified, and the bill sets no process for determinations, including notice, evidence, timing, or appeal.

5. **§2(1)(B)**: "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no benchmark, metric, or test. It's also unclear whether capability is judged in controlled evaluation or in deployment, which invites vagueness challenges.

6. **§2(1), §3(a)**: The bill shifts between "model" (§2(1)(A)), "system" (§2(1)(B)), and "covered AI system" (§3). It's unclear whether a covered model wrapped in an agent or product is itself a covered system.

7. **§3(a)**: "Train," "develop," and "deploy" are undefined. It's unclear whether fine-tuning a covered model, providing API access to one, releasing open weights, or hosting another party's training counts as a violation.

8. **§2(3), §4**: "Advanced AI chips" and "data center" are undefined. Operators could avoid the 50,000-chip threshold by spreading chips across sites or renting cloud compute, and §3 isn't tied to facilities at all.

9. **§4(a)**: Only facility operators must register, not developers or individual training runs. The government therefore might not know which entities are training covered systems.

10. **§4(b)**: Reports describe "computing workloads" but aren't required to identify covered systems, training runs, or model sizes. The certification is self-attested, and the bill sets no audit authority, no confidentiality or disclosure rules, and no stated penalty for false certification.

11. **§§4, 5**: Penalties apply only to violations of §3. Failing to register or file a report carries no stated consequence.

12. **§3(b)**: The grandfather clause covers systems "whose training began" before enactment. It doesn't say whether a run that continues past enactment is covered, or whether retrained, continued, or fine-tuned versions of a pre-enactment system also qualify.

13. **§§3(b), 8**: The prohibition takes effect 180 days after enactment, but §3(b) protects only training begun before enactment. Training started during the 180-day window is neither grandfathered nor yet unlawful, and the bill doesn't say whether such a run must stop on the effective date. This creates an incentive to start runs before that date.

14. **§§5(a), 8**: The civil penalty applies to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §3 doesn't take effect for 180 days. The bill doesn't clarify whether penalties can attach to conduct before the prohibition takes effect.

15. **§3(c)**: "Activities conducted for the purpose of safety evaluation" is undefined and has no limits on scale, duration, or external deployment. It requires no notice, registration, or reporting, and "purpose" is hard to prove, so large training runs could be labeled as evaluation.

16. **§5(a)**: "Each day on which the violation occurs" leaves unclear whether a continuing violation is counted per day, per system, or per facility. The $1 million daily cap may be small relative to the value of a frontier training run for large firms, so it could function as a cost of doing business.

17. **§5**: The bill provides no injunctive relief, facility shutdown, or forfeiture of compute or models, and it doesn't say who may bring civil enforcement. It also lacks subpoena or investigative authority, whistleblower protections, and liability for aiding and abetting, conspiracy, or attempts.

18. **§5(b)**: "Willfully" is undefined. The bill sets no fine amount and relies on general Title 18 provisions. It also doesn't specify how corporate liability attaches or whether officers are personally liable.

19. **§2(2)**: The "U.S. person" definition excludes foreign-incorporated subsidiaries of U.S. firms and permanent residents, and "State" is undefined (it's unclear whether it covers D.C. or territories). Because the "anywhere" reach depends on extraterritorial enforcement, a U.S. company could train abroad through a foreign subsidiary.

20. **§3(a)**: "In the United States" is undefined for remote or cloud compute. It's unclear whether a foreign company training in a U.S. data center, or a U.S. user reaching compute hosted abroad, is covered.

21. **§6**: "Shall seek to negotiate" creates no obligation to reach an agreement and no reporting requirement. "Equivalent" is undefined, and the bill doesn't say what happens if negotiations fail or how foreign developers are restrained in the meantime.

22. **§7(a)**: The duration depends on a congressional finding that covered systems "can be developed safely," but that standard is undefined, and the NASEM report has no deadline. The bill also sets no expedited procedure for the joint resolution, so ordinary Senate rules, including the filibuster, would apply.

23. **§7(a) vs. §7(b)**: §7(a) keeps the prohibition in place until a finding, while §7(b) ends the Act after 10 years regardless. The bill doesn't say whether the prohibition lapses at year 10 without a finding. Given §8, the prohibition would run about 9.5 years.

24. **General**: The bill has no severability clause, so if a court strikes a provision such as §2(1)(B), the bill doesn't say what survives. It also doesn't expressly grant Commerce rulemaking authority for the registry, definitions, or certification standards, and it provides no appropriation for enforcement.

25. **General (litigation exposure)**: Vagueness challenges under due process are most likely around §2(1)(B), "develop" in §3(a), and §3(c). Questions about the scope of federal authority and First Amendment claims about code or model weights are also possible. These are untested, so the explainer should present them as possibilities, not conclusions.

For balance, note that many of these items (definitions, registry scope, penalty mechanics, and the §7 trigger) are the kinds of fixes that markup amendments often address. That gives readers a sense of which weaknesses are likely to change before Thursday.
