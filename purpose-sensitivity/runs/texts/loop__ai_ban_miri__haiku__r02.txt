RESPONSE:
**Top priorities for the October 15 markup** (roughly in order of impact):

1. The Sec. 3(c) safety-evaluation exemption is open-ended and self-certified.
2. The Sec. 3(b) grandfather clause has no end date and no definition of "training began."
3. Sec. 7(b)'s 10-year sunset cuts against the prohibition in Sec. 7(a).
4. Sec. 4 has no penalty and does not identify covered training runs.
5. The compute and chip thresholds are easy to evade.

**Definitions and scope**

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "single training run."** Developers could split training into multiple runs or treat continued pretraining, post-training, RL, and fine-tuning as separate activities, so none of them crosses the threshold. The bill should aggregate cumulative compute across a model lineage, including post-training and fine-tuning.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A), compute measurement.** "Integer or floating-point operations" does not specify precision, counting methodology, or whether inference and synthetic-data generation count. Without a standard method, the threshold can be contested in every case.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A), fixed threshold.** Algorithmic efficiency gains will let future systems reach a given capability with far less than 10^26 operations, so the threshold will erode over time. Commerce should have authority to adjust it by rule, including downward.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A), distillation.** A student model trained on outputs from a covered system may fall below the threshold while approximating its capabilities. The definition should reach models derived from covered systems.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(B), "the Secretary."** The term has no antecedent in the bill, and Sec. 6 refers to the Secretary of State, so the agency responsible for the capability determination is unclear. The bill should name the agency.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B), capability standard.** "Capable," "substantially exceeding," and "most cognitive tasks" have no evaluation method, criteria, procedure, deadline, or notice requirement. This invites vagueness and nondelegation challenges and gives the agency no clear basis for acting.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(B), system scope.** It is unclear whether a determination applies to a base model or to an agentic or scaffolded system built from several models. Also, because the capability route requires an affirmative Secretarial determination, a highly capable system below 10^26 is lawful until someone acts.

8. **Sec. 2(2), "U.S. person."** The definition excludes LLCs, partnerships, and foreign subsidiaries of U.S. companies. A U.S. developer could therefore train through a foreign subsidiary and fall outside Sec. 3(a)'s "anywhere" clause. The bill should use a control-based test, and "State" should be defined to include D.C. and the territories.

9. **Sec. 2(3), "covered facility."** "Advanced AI chip" and "data center" are undefined. The 50,000-chip threshold is arbitrary, since a 10^26-operation run can be completed on fewer chips over a longer period. Distributing training across several smaller sites also evades the registry. The bill should define chips by performance, aggregate across operators and campuses, and consider a threshold based on compute capacity.

10. **Sec. 2, "operator" and other undefined terms.** "Operator," "train," "develop," "deploy," "artificial intelligence," "computing power," and "safety evaluation" are all undefined. Each is a likely point of dispute.

**Prohibition (Sec. 3)**

11. **Sec. 3(a), "deploy."** The bill does not say whether releasing model weights, offering API access, or using a model internally counts as deployment. An open-weight release of a covered system is the most obvious gap and should be addressed expressly.

12. **Sec. 3(a), enabling conduct.** The bill does not prohibit possessing or distributing covered weights, providing compute, financing, or aiding and abetting prohibited activity. Compute providers, including U.S. cloud providers renting capacity to foreign actors, are not addressed.

13. **Sec. 3(b), grandfather clause.** "Training began before" is undefined, so developers could argue that continued training of an existing run is covered. There is also no end date, no registration requirement, and no limit on further scaling or deployment, so grandfathered systems could operate indefinitely. The clause should cover only systems fully trained and disclosed before a fixed date, with no further scaling.

14. **Sec. 3(c), safety-evaluation exemption.** "Safety evaluation" is undefined, and the "purpose" test is subjective and hard to disprove. The exemption has no notice requirement, no cap on scale, and no limit on deployment after evaluation. It effectively lets developers self-certify. Fixes include a definition, notice to Commerce, independent oversight, a compute cap, and a bar on deployment beyond the evaluation itself.

**Registry (Sec. 4)**

15. **Sec. 4(a), what is registered.** The registry covers facilities, not covered training runs or systems. Commerce would not learn which runs exceed the threshold, and grandfathered systems have no registration obligation. Developers should register covered runs before they begin.

16. **Sec. 4, no penalty.** The Sec. 5 penalties attach only to violations of Sec. 3, so failing to register or report carries no consequence. The bill should add penalties for registry violations.

17. **Sec. 4(b), reporting content.** "Describing the computing workloads" does not specify the required data, such as FLOP per run, model identifiers, or start dates, so Commerce cannot verify compliance. The certification also carries no stated consequence for a false statement, and the bill gives Commerce no inspection or audit authority.

**Penalties (Sec. 5)**

18. **Sec. 5(a), effective-date conflict.** Penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but Sec. 3 takes effect 180 days later under Sec. 8. This is internally inconsistent and invites challenge. The two dates should be aligned.

19. **Sec. 5(a), penalty structure.** It is unclear whether a continuing training run counts as one violation per day or many. No enforcing agency or procedure is named, and the bill gives no express injunctive authority, no power to halt training or seize compute, and no disgorgement. At $1 million per day, the penalty may be treated as a cost of doing business for frontier-scale spending. Consider per-violation penalties tied to revenue or compute.

20. **Sec. 5(b), criminal liability.** The "willfully" standard is difficult to prove against a corporation. The bill has no attempt, conspiracy, or aiding-and-abetting language and no responsible-officer provision for those who direct a violation.

**International agreement (Sec. 6)**

21. **Sec. 6, "shall seek to negotiate."** This imposes only an obligation to try, with no reporting requirement and no consequence for failure. It is also unclear whether "not later than 18 months" modifies the start or the conclusion of negotiations. The bill should require periodic reports to Congress and authorize trade or export measures against non-participating jurisdictions.

**Duration (Sec. 7)**

22. **Sec. 7(a), lifting the prohibition.** "Developed safely" is undefined, so a narrow showing could be argued to satisfy it. The bill also does not say whether a joint resolution may lift the prohibition in part, such as for a capability class, a use case, or a developer. It should also state expressly that the resolution must follow bicameral passage and presentment.

23. **Sec. 7(b), sunset conflicts with Sec. 7(a).** After 10 years the Act ceases to have effect even if Congress has never passed a joint resolution, which contradicts the open-ended duration in Sec. 7(a). The sunset also gives developers a 10-year window to build and be grandfathered. The bill should delete the sunset or make it conditional, and specify that penalties for pre-sunset violations survive.

**Effective date (Sec. 8)**

24. **Sec. 8 versus Sec. 3(b), race window.** Sec. 3(b) is keyed to enactment, but the prohibition takes effect 180 days later. Developers can therefore start new runs during that window, and whether those runs are covered after the effective date is unclear. The ban on new covered training should take effect on enactment, and the grandfather clause should be keyed to a fixed date.

25. **Sec. 8, registry timing.** Because Sec. 4 takes effect with the rest of the Act, Commerce would have no information about covered facilities for 180 days. Sec. 4 should take effect on enactment.

**Enforcement and structure (whole bill)**

26. **Missing rulemaking and enforcement authority.** The bill gives Commerce no authority to issue regulations, define terms, set reporting forms, adjust thresholds, or create an enforcement unit. It also has no appropriation. Without these, the Act is difficult to enforce.

27. **No investigative authority or whistleblower protection.** Compliance depends largely on self-reporting. The bill should include subpoena and inspection powers and whistleblower protections.

28. **No severability clause.** If a court strikes Sec. 2(1)(B) on vagueness or nondelegation grounds, it is unclear whether the rest of the Act falls with it. A severability clause should be added.

29. **Hardware and compute access (outside the current text).** Enforcement depends on facility registration, which foreign training can bypass. Coordination with export controls and know-your-customer requirements for cloud compute would close that gap. This may be better handled as a separate amendment or companion provision.
