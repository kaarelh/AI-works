# S. 2817: Loopholes, Ambiguities, and Drafting Problems

Before the itemized list, these five problems do the most damage and should lead any memo to Senator Whitfield's office:

- **The safety-evaluation exemption (#14).** It could swallow the entire prohibition.
- **The 10-year sunset (#28).** It lets companies simply wait the ban out.
- **The grandfather clause (#11–13).** It invites a race to start runs and permits indefinite continuation of existing ones.
- **No penalties for the registry (#19).** The reporting system has no teeth.
- **Inconsistent effective dates (#25, #31).** They create an easy opening for litigation.

## Sec. 2: Definitions

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A), single training run.** The "single training run" qualifier invites splitting. A developer could pretrain at 9×10^25 operations, then label continued pretraining, RL post-training, or fine-tuning as "separate" runs. The threshold should count cumulative compute across all training of a model and its derivatives, including compute used to generate synthetic training data.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A), fixed threshold.** The 10^26 threshold is static and cannot be updated. Algorithmic efficiency gains will let more capable systems be trained below it over time. The Secretary should be authorized, and required, to lower the threshold by rule, but not to raise it.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A), distillation.** A sub-threshold model distilled from a covered or grandfathered model is not covered, even if it inherits most of that model's capability. Derivative models should be covered expressly.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A) vs. (B), "model" vs. "system."** Prong (A) covers a "model," while prong (B) and Sec. 3 refer to a "system." This invites arguments that wrapping a model in scaffolding or tools, or combining several sub-threshold models into an ensemble, produces something outside the definition.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(B), standard and procedure.** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no measurement standard and no procedure (notice, evidence, review). Because Sec. 5(b) attaches criminal penalties, this prong is the most exposed to a void-for-vagueness challenge.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B), timing.** The prong only applies once the Secretary "determines" a system is capable, so it catches systems after they are built. It does nothing to stop development aimed at such capabilities. Consider covering systems "intended or reasonably expected to" reach the capability, plus a pre-training notification requirement.

7. **Sec. 2, undefined terms.** "Secretary," "artificial intelligence," "training run," "develop," "deploy," "data center," "advanced AI chip," "operator," and "person" are all undefined. "Secretary" is presumably Commerce, but the bill never says so, and every one of these gaps will be litigated.

8. **Sec. 2(2), entity types.** "Corporation organized under the laws of a State" omits LLCs, partnerships, and nonprofits (OpenAI's original structure, for example). It also omits entities organized under D.C., territorial, or federal law, and lawful permanent residents.

9. **Sec. 2(2), foreign subsidiaries.** A U.S. company's foreign-incorporated subsidiary is not a "U.S. person." The extraterritorial prohibition can therefore be avoided by moving training to a foreign affiliate. The definition should cover entities owned or controlled by U.S. persons.

10. **Sec. 2(3), facility definition.** The 50,000-chip threshold can be evaded by splitting compute across multiple sub-threshold sites under common control, or by distributed training across networked facilities. A fixed chip count also weakens as chips improve. The definition should use aggregate computing capacity under common ownership or control, with anti-structuring language.

## Sec. 3: Prohibition

11. **Sec. 3(b), race to start runs.** Keying the grandfather clause to when training "began" rewards starting the largest possible runs before enactment, or a token run that is later scaled up. The pre-enactment period becomes a race.

12. **Sec. 3(b), indefinite continuation.** A run "begun" before enactment can arguably continue indefinitely, and grandfathered systems can apparently be further trained, fine-tuned, scaled, and deployed without limit. The exemption should cover only deployment of systems as they existed on a fixed date, with no further training.

13. **Sec. 3(b), derivatives.** It is unclear whether a fine-tuned, distilled, or merged derivative of a grandfathered model inherits its exemption. Labs will argue that it does.

14. **Sec. 3(c), safety-evaluation exemption.** Exempting any activity "conducted for the purpose of safety evaluation" turns on the developer's own stated purpose. Any lab can describe frontier training as safety research. The exemption should be limited to evaluating existing or grandfathered systems, should never authorize new training above the threshold, and should require prior approval, licensing, and oversight.

15. **Sec. 3(a), facilitation.** Supplying compute, chips, or cloud services for a covered training run, knowing what it is for, is not prohibited unless it is itself "training." Civil liability for aiding, abetting, or conspiring is also absent (18 U.S.C. § 2 reaches only crimes).

16. **Sec. 3(a), deployment by foreign developers.** It is unclear whether a foreign developer offering a covered model to U.S. users through an API "deploys" it "in the United States." Making the system available to persons in the U.S. should count expressly.

17. **Sec. 3(a), weights.** Nothing addresses exporting, publishing, or transferring model weights, so open release of a grandfathered or foreign-trained covered model is unaddressed.

18. **Sec. 3(a), government activity.** Whether federal agencies, national labs, or defense contractors acting under contract are "persons" is unaddressed. A silent government carve-out is likely to be argued.

## Sec. 4: Registry

19. **Sec. 4, no penalties.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3. Failing to register, failing to report, and filing false reports carry no penalty under this Act.

20. **Sec. 4(a), no deadline.** There is no registration deadline, no requirement to register before operations begin, and no ongoing duty to update.

21. **Sec. 4(b), self-certification.** Reports are self-certified and only quarterly, so a prohibited run could finish between reports. Commerce gets no inspection, audit, subpoena, or on-site verification authority. Reports should be required before large runs, and verification powers should be granted.

22. **Sec. 4(b), content.** "Describing the computing workloads" has no specified content. Reports should require, at minimum, the compute used per run, customer identity, and model purpose.

23. **Sec. 4(a), cloud ambiguity.** "Operator" is ambiguous in cloud settings: is it the facility owner, the cloud provider, or the tenant running the training? Reporting duties need to reach the customers actually training models (know-your-customer requirements).

## Sec. 5: Penalties

24. **Sec. 5(a), penalty size.** A maximum of $1M per day, with no minimum, is small relative to frontier lab budgets and could be treated as a cost of doing business. Consider penalties tied to revenue or compute spent, plus forfeiture of chips and model weights.

25. **Sec. 5(a) vs. Sec. 8, effective-date conflict.** Sec. 5(a) imposes penalties for violations "on or after the date of enactment," but Sec. 8 says the Act takes effect 180 days later. This contradiction will be litigated and should be reconciled.

26. **Sec. 5, missing enforcement tools.** There is no injunctive relief or emergency stop authority, and no designated enforcing agency (DOJ? Commerce?). There is also no administrative penalty process, no whistleblower protection, and no officer or director liability. Imprisonment cannot apply to a corporation, so individual responsibility should be explicit.

27. **Sec. 5(b), willfulness.** "Willfully" requires proof that the defendant knew the conduct was unlawful. Combined with the vague definitions in Sec. 2, this makes criminal convictions very hard to obtain. Consider a "knowingly" standard for at least some offenses.

## Sec. 7: Duration

28. **Sec. 7(b), sunset.** The 10-year sunset ends the entire Act automatically, regardless of whether anyone has shown safe development is possible. It directly contradicts Sec. 7(a) and lets companies wait out the ban. It should be struck.

29. **Sec. 7(a), legal effect.** A joint resolution requires presentment and is ordinary legislation, so this subsection adds nothing Congress couldn't already do by repeal. If it is read as something less than a law, it raises a legislative-veto problem under *INS v. Chadha*. It should say expressly that the prohibition ends only upon enactment of a law.

## Sec. 6: International Agreement

30. **Sec. 6, timing and enforceability.** "Shall seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months" is ambiguous: must talks begin by then, or conclude? There are no reporting requirements or consequences. Directing the executive's treaty negotiations also invites separation-of-powers objections (*Zivotofsky v. Kerry*). Recasting this as a reporting requirement plus a sense-of-Congress provision is safer.

## Sec. 8 and Missing Provisions

31. **Sec. 8, 180-day window.** During the 180 days after enactment, training is lawful but not grandfathered under Sec. 3(b). It is unclear whether a run started in that window becomes illegal mid-run or whether its output can be deployed. The window also gives labs six months to accelerate.

32. **Missing: severability clause.** Given the likely vagueness, extraterritoriality, and First Amendment (code-as-speech) challenges, one adverse ruling could threaten the whole Act without a severability clause.

33. **Missing: rulemaking authority.** No agency is authorized to issue implementing regulations, define technical terms, or update thresholds. This is especially risky after *Loper Bright* ended *Chevron* deference.

34. **Missing: anti-evasion provision.** A general clause prohibiting arrangements structured to avoid the thresholds would backstop items 1, 3, 9, and 10.

35. **Missing: Commerce Clause findings.** Congressional findings and an interstate-commerce jurisdictional element would strengthen the Act against a challenge to federal power over purely intrastate training.

36. **Missing: state law provision.** The bill is silent on preemption. A savings clause preserving stronger state laws would prevent industry from arguing that the Act preempts them.
