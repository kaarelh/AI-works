RESPONSE:
Here's a section-by-section review. I've grouped issues by section and flagged, where relevant, whether an item is more useful as an "operating room" argument or an "unworkability" argument. A caveat at the end on a few of the loopholes.

**Section 2(1)(A) — Compute threshold**

1. **§2(1)(A), "in a single training run."** The threshold is per-run, not per-model. A model could be built through multiple sequential runs (pretraining in stages, continued pretraining, large-scale fine-tuning or RL), each under 10^26, with no aggregation rule. This is the single most exploitable term in the bill.

2. **§2(1)(A), "trained using."** No rule for what compute counts toward training. Synthetic-data generation, reward-model inference, RL environment compute, distillation from a teacher, and test-time training are all arguably not "training" the model, even though they can dominate total compute.

3. **§2(1)(A), "integer or floating-point operations."** No counting convention is specified (FLOPs vs. MACs, whether sparse or skipped operations count, precision). A factor-of-two difference in convention is a factor of two in effective threshold, and there is no agency authorized to issue a counting standard.

4. **§2(1)(A), "artificial intelligence model."** Neither "artificial intelligence" nor "model" is defined. The threshold is applied to a "model," but §3 prohibits a "system"; a system composed of several sub-threshold models (ensembles, mixture-of-models, agent scaffolds, routers) is not itself "a model trained using" >10^26 operations.

5. **§2(1)(A), fixed numeric threshold.** 10^26 is hard-coded with no adjustment mechanism. As algorithmic efficiency improves, it becomes either meaningless (capabilities reachable far below it) or overbroad relative to its intent, with no rulemaking authority anywhere in the Act to update it.

6. **§2(1)(A), no verification mechanism.** Nothing in the Act requires reporting training-run compute. The §4 registry covers facilities, not runs, so compliance with the threshold is effectively unverifiable except by self-report.

**Section 2(1)(B) — Secretary's determination**

7. **§2(1)(B), "the Secretary."** Undefined. §4 refers to the Department of Commerce and §6 to the Secretary of State; the bill never says which Secretary makes capability determinations. On its face, prong (B) has no operative decision-maker.

8. **§2(1)(B), no procedure.** No notice, hearing, evidentiary standard, rulemaking, or judicial review for a determination. A criminal prohibition keyed to an unreviewable administrative finding invites due-process, nondelegation, and void-for-vagueness challenges.

9. **§2(1)(B), "substantially exceeding," "most cognitive tasks."** Undefined terms with no benchmark, baseline population, or measurement method. A defendant cannot know in advance whether a system is covered, which undercuts "willfully" in §5(b).

10. **§2(1)(B), timing.** A system is covered under (B) only once "the Secretary determines" it is. Training and deploying a system before any determination exists is arguably lawful, and the Act is silent on whether a determination is retroactive.

11. **§2(1)(B), applies to "system" only.** Prong (B) reaches a "system" the Secretary evaluates, but there is no obligation to submit systems for evaluation and no information-gathering authority, so the Secretary has no basis to make a determination about an unreleased system.

**Section 2(2) — U.S. person**

12. **§2(2), "corporation organized under the laws of a State."** Excludes LLCs, partnerships, trusts, and other non-corporate entities; excludes entities organized under the laws of DC, territories, or tribal law; and excludes foreign-organized subsidiaries. A Delaware LLC or a foreign subsidiary training abroad is not a "U.S. person."

13. **§2(2), individuals.** Only citizens are covered. Lawful permanent residents and visa holders working abroad for a foreign entity are outside the extraterritorial prong entirely.

14. **§2(2) / §3(a), "any person in the United States."** "Person" is not defined for the domestic prong (the default in 1 U.S.C. §1 would apply). Under standard canons, the federal government is not a "person," so there is no clear bar on government or military development, yet contractors are persons, so there is also no national-security exemption. Both sides of that are an unworkability argument.

**Section 2(3) — Covered facility**

15. **§2(3), "advanced AI chips."** Undefined. No performance threshold, no reference to existing export-control classifications, no way to count what qualifies.

16. **§2(3), "data center" / 50,000-chip threshold.** No aggregation across co-located buildings, campuses, or commonly controlled sites, and no rule for distributed training across multiple sub-threshold facilities. "Data center" itself is undefined.

**Section 3 — Prohibition**

17. **§3(a), "train, develop, or deploy."** None of the three verbs is defined. "Develop" could mean anything from architecture research to weight-editing; "deploy" is unclear as to internal use, research use, open-weight release, or API access. There is no "possess," "use," "import," "sell," or "operate," so holding or running a foreign-trained covered system's weights in the U.S. is arguably not prohibited.

18. **§3(a), foreign developers.** A foreign company training abroad is neither "in the United States" nor a "U.S. person." It can serve U.S. customers via API; the U.S. customer who queries it is not obviously "deploying" it. The Act would bind domestic developers while leaving foreign systems freely available domestically.

19. **§3(b), grandfather clause.** A system whose training "began" before enactment is exempt from all three verbs permanently, including continued training, further scaling, fine-tuning, and deployment. "Began" is undefined; a single long-running or continual-learning run started before enactment never falls under the prohibition.

20. **§3(b), derivative systems.** Nothing addresses systems derived from an exempt system (distillation, synthetic data, initialization from exempt weights, merges). Whether a derivative "began training" before enactment is unanswered.

21. **§3(b) vs. §8, the 180-day gap.** Grandfathering keys to enactment, but the prohibition doesn't take effect for 180 days. Runs begun in that window are neither exempt nor (yet) unlawful; whether continuing such a run after the effective date is a violation depends on whether "train" is a continuing act, which the bill does not say.

22. **§3(c), safety-evaluation exemption.** "Activities conducted for the purpose of safety evaluation" is undefined, unlimited in scale, and subjective (purpose, not conduct). Since a system must exist to be evaluated, training a covered system in order to evaluate its safety is on the text exempt. No certification, cap, oversight, or mixed-purpose rule.

23. **§3(c), scope of "activities."** The exemption is not limited to "training"; it covers all §3(a) verbs, so "deploying" a system to evaluate its safety in real conditions is also arguably exempt.

24. **§3 generally, no licensing or safe harbor.** There is no pathway (license, waiver, certification) by which any covered development could lawfully proceed, and no agency with authority to grant one. This is the core unworkability point.

**Section 4 — Registry**

25. **§4(a), "operator."** Undefined. For cloud and colocation facilities, the owner, lessor, and compute customer are different parties; the bill doesn't say who registers, and the party who registers may not know the workloads it must certify under (b).

26. **§4(b), reports.** "Describing the computing workloads" sets no content standard, no format, no confidentiality protection, and no verification or audit authority.

27. **§4, no enforcement.** §5 penalties apply only to violations of §3. There is no penalty, deadline, or consequence for failing to register or report, so §4 is unenforceable except through generic false-statement law.

28. **§4, disconnection from §3.** The registry is not linked to the prohibition in any way: registering a facility creates no obligation regarding covered systems, and the compute-threshold definition isn't tied to registered facilities.

**Section 5 — Penalties**

29. **§5(a), retroactivity conflict.** Civil penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but under §8 nothing is unlawful until 180 days later. The penalty clause purports to reach a period in which no violation can occur, and if read literally raises ex post facto concerns.

30. **§5(a), no enforcing agency.** The Act does not say who assesses or collects the civil penalty, under what procedure, or with what statute of limitations. No agency is granted enforcement, investigative, or subpoena authority anywhere in the bill.

31. **§5(b), "willfully."** Given the undefined terms in §§2 and 3, willfulness (knowledge that conduct is unlawful) will be nearly impossible to prove, particularly where a good-faith "safety evaluation" purpose or compute-counting position is asserted.

32. **§5, no corporate/individual allocation.** The bill does not address officer or employee liability, successor liability, or whether the per-day civil penalty runs per system, per entity, or per act.

**Section 6 — International agreement**

33. **§6, aspirational only.** "Seek to negotiate" imposes no result and no consequence; the 18-month date is a target for seeking, not concluding. Nothing conditions the §3 prohibition on any foreign reciprocity, so the ban is unilateral by design. "Equivalent" prohibitions is undefined.

**Section 7 — Duration**

34. **§7(a), joint resolution.** A joint resolution requires bicameral passage and presentment, i.e., it is just a new statute. §7(a) adds nothing Congress couldn't do anyway and does not create a legislative-veto or fast-track mechanism.

35. **§7(a), NASEM report.** No one is directed or funded to commission the report, no deadline or scope is set, and Congress's finding must be "acting on" a report that may never exist. The lifting mechanism has no trigger.

36. **§7(a) vs. §7(b), internal conflict.** (a) says the prohibition continues until a congressional finding; (b) says the whole Act terminates at 10 years regardless. (b) controls as a matter of ordinary reading, making (a)'s "shall remain in effect until" language inaccurate, and the sunset also terminates the §4 registry.

**Section 8 and general drafting**

37. **§8, three inconsistent dates.** Enactment governs grandfathering (§3(b)), penalties (§5(a)), and the §6 clock; enactment plus 180 days governs the prohibition. No transition rules for conduct, runs, or deployments straddling the dates.

38. **No rulemaking authority.** No agency is authorized to define terms, update the threshold, issue counting standards, or administer the registry. Every undefined term above stays undefined.

39. **No severability clause.** If any of the vagueness or delegation problems above succeeds in court, the absence of severability invites an argument that the whole prohibition falls.

40. **No judicial review, preemption, or appropriations provisions.** The Act is silent on review of determinations, on relation to state AI laws, and on funding for any of the functions it assigns to Commerce, State, or NASEM.

**Two notes for the legal team**

- The items most useful for the "unworkable as written" argument at markup are 7, 8, 14, 17, 18, 24, 27, 29, 30, 35, 36, and 38: they show the bill cannot be administered, not just that it's evadable. Those are also the points most likely to resonate with committee staff who are not sympathetic to the industry.
- Some of the operating-room items (especially 1, 19, 22, 23) are real on the text, but relying on them as a compliance strategy carries its own risk. A court or prosecutor could read "single training run" or "purpose of safety evaluation" purposively, and structuring conduct around a pretextual reading is the fact pattern that supports willfulness under §5(b). Better to use them to push for definitional fixes (or to show the bill fails at its own aims) than to plan on them.
