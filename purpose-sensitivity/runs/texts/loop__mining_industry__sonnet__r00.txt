# S. 2817 (as amended Oct. 6, 2026): Drafting Problems, Ambiguities, and Potential Gaps

Each item is tagged **(Gap)** if it could support continued operations, **(Unworkable)** if it supports an argument that the bill can't function as written, or **(Both)**.

## Section 2: Definitions

1. **§2(1)(A), single-vessel test.** The 10,000 t/yr threshold applies per vessel and is not aggregated across an operator's fleet, affiliates, or chartered vessels. Operations split across several vessels, or rotated among vessels during a year, may fall below the threshold. *(Gap)*

2. **§2(1)(A), multi-vessel systems.** The text doesn't say which vessel "recovers" when a collector, lift system, and shuttle or transport vessels are involved. This makes the single-vessel test hard to apply and easy to contest. *(Both)*

3. **§2(1)(A), measurement.** "Per year" is not defined (calendar, fiscal, or rolling). The text doesn't specify wet versus dry tons, whether sediment or entrained water counts, or what "recovery" means (seafloor pickup, lift to surface, onboard receipt, or offload). There is no measurement or verification protocol. *(Both)*

4. **§2(1)(A), depth.** The 200 m depth is not tied to a reference point, and 200 m is not "deep" seabed in the ordinary sense, so it mismatches the Act's title. It sweeps in continental shelf and slope activity, including within the U.S. EEZ. *(Unworkable)*

5. **§2(1)(A), "other mineral resources."** The term is undefined and could reach sand and gravel, phosphates, or arguably offshore oil and gas (an FPSO is a "vessel" recovering resources "from the seabed"). That overbreadth would conflict with OCSLA-based regimes and could widen the opposition coalition. *(Unworkable)*

6. **§2(1), "commercial" is purely volumetric.** The definition has no connection to sale, profit, or commercial intent. A non-commercial pilot or research campaign over 10,000 t counts, while a commercial operation under the threshold doesn't. *(Both)*

7. **§2(1)(A)/§2(3)/§3(a), three unrelated thresholds.** The bill uses 10,000 t/yr, 200 m, and 5,000 GT with no findings, studies, or rationale for any of them. They are not harmonized, which invites arbitrary-line arguments. *(Unworkable)*

8. **§2(1)(B), undefined "Secretary."** The Act never says which Secretary makes the determination (Commerce, Interior, or another). This is a basic drafting defect in the provision that expands the prohibition. *(Unworkable)*

9. **§2(1)(B), standard and procedure.** "Risks … comparable to" is not tied to any metric, and the benchmark is volume-based while ecological risk depends on other factors such as plume behavior and area disturbed. There is no notice, comment, timing, prospectivity, or judicial review requirement. Combined with the criminal penalty in §5(b), this supports vagueness, fair-notice, and nondelegation arguments. *(Unworkable)*

10. **§2(2), "U.S. person."** The definition covers only citizens and corporations organized under State law. It omits LLCs, partnerships, and trusts unless they count as "corporations," as well as permanent residents, federally chartered entities, and foreign-incorporated subsidiaries of U.S. companies. "State" is undefined, so D.C. and the territories are unclear. *(Gap)*

11. **§2(3), "covered vessel."** The 5,000 GT floor doesn't match §2(1)(A), which applies to any vessel, so smaller vessels escape the registry while still being subject to the prohibition. Gross tonnage is ambiguous for non-conventional platforms, "equipped for the recovery" is vague, and support or transport vessels appear to be excluded. *(Both)*

12. **Missing definitions generally.** "Operator," "person," "United States," "State," "vessel," "mining," "recovery," "engage in," and "exploration license" are all undefined, though the operative provisions depend on them. *(Both)*

## Section 3: Prohibition

13. **§3(a), territorial and extraterritorial reach.** "In the United States" is undefined (territorial sea, EEZ, vessels?), and "any U.S. person anywhere" reaches conduct on the high seas while the Act is silent on international-law constraints. Foreign-flag vessels, foreign affiliates, and non-U.S. persons outside U.S. territory appear to be outside practical enforcement. *(Both)*

14. **§3(a), "engage in."** The text doesn't say whether financing, chartering, processing, transporting, purchasing nodules, or providing services counts. Aiding-and-abetting and conspiracy exposure for non-mining participants is unclear. *(Both)*

15. **§3(a), no reconciliation with existing law.** The bill doesn't amend or repeal the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (under which NOAA issues exploration licenses and commercial recovery permits) or address OCSLA. There is no "notwithstanding" clause, so the Act leaves two statutes issuing conflicting commands over pending applications and any permits. Counsel should verify the current DSHMRA text. *(Both)*

16. **§3(b), what counts as an "exploration license."** The term isn't limited to licenses issued under U.S. law, so it's unclear whether foreign-issued licenses or ISA-sponsored contracts qualify. *(Gap)*

17. **§3(b), "mining conducted under an exploration license."** Exploration licenses generally don't authorize commercial recovery, and "mining" is undefined (the defined term is "commercial deep seabed mining"). One reading exempts all recovery under a pre-enactment license, while the other makes the exemption nearly meaningless. Expect both readings to be argued. *(Both)*

18. **§3(b), scope of grandfathering.** The exemption has no volume cap, area limit, or duration limit. It doesn't address renewals, extensions, modifications, expansions beyond the original work plan, assignment or transfer to affiliates, or good-standing requirements. *(Gap)*

19. **§3(b), enactment date vs. effective date, and pending applications.** Grandfathering turns on licenses issued before the date of enactment, but the Act doesn't take effect until 180 days later. Licenses issued in that window, and pending applications, get no protection, which raises reliance, due process, and takings arguments for applicants who invested under the existing framework. *(Unworkable)*

20. **§3(c), environmental assessment exemption.** "Activities conducted for the purpose of environmental assessment" is undefined, depends on subjective purpose, has no volume or duration cap, and requires no agency approval or license. It could be read broadly (test mining) or challenged as unenforceably vague. *(Both)*

21. **§3(c) vs. §7(a), catch-22.** The only route to lifting the prohibition requires a finding that mining "can be conducted without serious harm." Generating the evidence for that finding may require activity at a scale the vague §3(c) exemption doesn't clearly permit. *(Unworkable)*

## Section 4: Registry

22. **§4(a), no implementing structure.** The bill gives NOAA no rulemaking authority and sets no deadline, form, content requirements, or fee for registration. "Operator" is undefined, and there's no authority to inspect, audit, or deny registration. *(Unworkable)*

23. **§4(a), reach and overlap.** Registration applies to every covered vessel regardless of flag, ownership, location, or exemption status, with no U.S. nexus. It is unenforceable against foreign operators and may duplicate DSHMRA reporting. *(Unworkable)*

24. **§4(b), reports.** There is no required content, due date, verification method, or definition of who must certify. There is no confidentiality protection for proprietary data, and the reports aren't tied to the 10,000 t/yr metric, so they can't reliably show whether a violation occurred. *(Both)*

25. **§4, no penalty.** §5 penalizes only violations of §3, so failing to register or filing a false report carries no penalty under this Act. Counsel should consider whether general false-statement statutes (e.g., 18 U.S.C. §1001) would fill the gap. *(Gap)*

## Section 5: Penalties

26. **§5(a) vs. §8, retroactivity conflict.** §5(a) reaches violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §8 delays the entire Act (including §3) until 180 days after enactment. The text is internally contradictory and raises fair-notice and retroactivity problems. *(Both)*

27. **§5(a), "each day" vs. annual threshold.** A violation exists only once a vessel exceeds 10,000 t in a year, so it's unclear which days count as violation days (all days in the year, or only those after the threshold is crossed). The text also doesn't say whether the penalty runs per vessel or per person. *(Both)*

28. **§5, no enforcement architecture.** The bill designates no enforcing agency and creates no administrative assessment process, hearing rights, penalty factors, injunctive relief, forfeiture, investigative powers, or limitations period. Whether penalties are assessed administratively or sued for by DOJ is unspecified. *(Unworkable)*

29. **§5(a), magnitude.** Up to $1,000,000 per day, with no mitigation factors, invites Excessive Fines Clause and proportionality arguments. *(Unworkable)*

30. **§5(b), "willfully."** The mental state is undefined (knowledge of the facts, or of the law?). It's especially unclear how willfulness applies where coverage depends on an unpublished Secretary determination under §2(1)(B). A 10-year felony for a volumetric regulatory offense, with no guidance on entity or officer liability, adds to the problem. *(Unworkable)*

## Section 6: International Agreement

31. **§6, ambiguous deadline.** "Shall seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months" could mean the Secretary must begin negotiating within 18 months or must conclude an agreement within 18 months. There is no consequence for missing either. *(Unworkable)*

32. **§6, no linkage or mechanism.** "Equivalent" prohibitions are undefined, and the bill says nothing about reporting to Congress, treaty versus executive agreement, Senate advice and consent, or the ISA framework. Nothing connects the outcome of negotiations to §3 or §7, and the section implicitly concedes that a unilateral U.S. prohibition leaves other countries' operators unaffected. *(Unworkable)*

## Sections 7 and 8: Duration and Effective Date

33. **§7(a) vs. §7(b), contradiction.** §7(a) says the prohibition lasts until Congress makes a finding by joint resolution, while §7(b) ends the whole Act after 10 years. This makes §7(a) either superfluous or contradictory, and reasonable readers will disagree about which controls. *(Both)*

34. **§7(a), practically unattainable trigger.** A joint resolution needs both chambers and the President's signature, and the bill provides no expedited procedure, deadline, sponsor, or funding for the NAS report. "Serious harm" is undefined, the standard requires proving a negative, and it applies industry-wide rather than to particular sites or technologies. It also hands a statutory condition to a private nonprofit's report. *(Unworkable)*

35. **§7(b), sunset mechanics.** "Cease to have effect" has no savings clause for accrued violations, pending enforcement, or penalties, which could allow arguments that expired-statute conduct can't be prosecuted. The period runs from enactment, not the effective date, so the prohibition would operate for roughly 9.5 years. *(Gap)*

36. **§8, no transition.** There is no wind-down for non-grandfathered operations, vessels at sea, or in-progress campaigns. The 180-day gap (with §3(b) keyed to enactment) also leaves unclear what is lawful in the interim. *(Both)*

## Cross-Cutting

37. **Unfunded mandates.** The bill authorizes no appropriations for NOAA's registry, State's negotiations, enforcement, or the NAS study. *(Unworkable)*

38. **No severability or review provisions.** The bill has no severability clause and no judicial review or administrative procedure provisions, which matters most for §2(1)(B). Courts usually presume severability, so the practical effect is limited. *(Unworkable)*
