RESPONSE:
# S. 2817 (Manager's Amendment of Oct. 6): Loopholes, Ambiguities, and Drafting Problems

## Highest-priority fixes

If the office can only take up a few, these matter most:

- **#16, #17:** The safety-evaluation exemption and the grandfather clause.
- **#33:** The 10-year sunset contradicts the "until Congress finds" clause.
- **#2:** The single-training-run threshold can be evaded by splitting runs.
- **#21, #29:** The registry has no penalties or verification, and the bill names no enforcing agency.
- **#35:** Severability.

## Sec. 2: Definitions

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A): "Artificial intelligence model" is undefined.** Neither "artificial intelligence" nor "model" is defined anywhere in the Act. Defendants could argue that some systems, such as ensembles, scaffolded agents, or merged models, are not "models."

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A): "In a single training run" invites run-splitting.** "Training run" is undefined. A developer could pretrain to just under 10^26, then add continued pretraining, fine-tuning, or reinforcement learning as "separate" runs. The threshold should count cumulative compute across all training of a model and its precursors, including post-training, model merging, and distillation from a parent model.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A): A fixed compute threshold will become outdated.** Algorithmic efficiency improves steadily, so equally capable systems will be trainable below 10^26 operations over time. The Secretary should have authority (and a duty to review periodically) to lower the threshold, but not to raise it.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A): Distillation and synthetic data are not covered.** A small model trained on outputs of a large one, or on data generated with large amounts of compute, can inherit capability without crossing the threshold. Compute used to generate training data, and compute embodied in a teacher model, should count.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(A): Systems built from many models fall outside the definition.** A system made of many sub-threshold models, or one using very large inference-time compute, may never involve a single model trained above 10^26. The definition should reach combined systems.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B): "The Secretary" is undefined.** The bill never says which Secretary, and Sec. 6 refers to the Secretary of State. This alone invites challenge to any determination.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(B): The capability standard is vague, and the timing is backwards.** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no measurement method. A determination could only come after the system exists, by which point the danger the bill targets has already materialized. Combined with criminal penalties, this invites a due-process vagueness challenge. The standard also needs either a prospective test (systems reasonably expected to reach the capability) or an implementing rulemaking with defined evaluations.

8. **Sec. 2(1)(B): There is no process for determinations.** The bill does not specify notice, procedure, or a standard of review for a determination. It also does not say whether liability attaches to conduct before the determination, which raises a fair-notice problem for both civil and criminal penalties.

9. **Sec. 2(2): The "U.S. person" definition excludes key actors.** It leaves out lawful permanent residents and entities that are not "corporations," such as LLCs and partnerships. It also omits entities organized under federal, D.C., or territorial law, and "State" is undefined.

10. **Sec. 2(2): Foreign subsidiaries offer an easy offshore route.** A U.S. parent company could direct a foreign-incorporated subsidiary to train abroad. Coverage should extend to entities owned or controlled by U.S. persons, and to U.S. persons who direct, fund, or substantially assist the activity.

11. **Sec. 2(3): The facility definition can be evaded by splitting.** An operator can keep each site under 50,000 chips, and training can be distributed across many such sites. Thresholds should apply to aggregate capacity under common ownership or control, and to clusters networked for joint training.

12. **Sec. 2(3): The facility definition is misaligned with the prohibition threshold.** "Data center" and "advanced AI chips" are undefined. Counting chips ignores how much chip performance varies. On rough current-generation figures, about 20,000 high-end accelerators could reach 10^26 operations in roughly five months. The registry trigger should be based on computing capacity, defined by rule and updatable.

## Sec. 3: Prohibition

13. **Sec. 3(a): "Person" is undefined, so government developers may be exempt.** Under ordinary statutory interpretation, "person" may not include federal or state agencies. The bill should state explicitly whether government bodies and their contractors are covered.

14. **Sec. 3(a): "Develop" and "deploy" are undefined.** It is unclear whether "deploy" covers any of the following:
    - offering API access in the U.S. to a model trained abroad
    - releasing model weights publicly
    - internal use of a model
    - possessing or transferring weights

15. **Sec. 3(a): Facilitation and attempts are not prohibited.** The bill does not prohibit any of the following:
    - providing compute, such as a U.S. cloud provider renting capacity to a foreign customer
    - financing a covered training run
    - exporting chips or weights for covered training
    - beginning a run planned to exceed the threshold

    Because a run is only "covered" once it crosses the threshold, enforcement could not intervene until it is too late. The bill also has no anti-evasion or structuring clause.

16. **Sec. 3(c): The safety-evaluation exemption swallows the rule.** "For the purpose of safety evaluation" is self-declared and subjective, and it exempts training, development, and deployment. Any lab could describe a frontier run as safety research. The exemption should be limited to evaluating existing or grandfathered systems, should exclude training new covered systems, and should require prior approval and oversight.

17. **Sec. 3(b): The grandfather clause is open-ended.** It exempts all training, development, and deployment of any system "whose training began" before enactment. That arguably permits indefinite continued training of an existing model lineage and creates an incentive to start large runs before signing. The exemption should be limited to deployment of systems whose training was completed before a fixed earlier date, such as the date of introduction, with no further training.

18. **Sec. 3(b): Grandfathered systems can be released or used to train others.** The bill does not say whether grandfathered models may be released as open weights, fine-tuned, or used to distill new models. Each of those could spread the capability the bill aims to stop.

19. **Sec. 3(b) with Sec. 2(1)(B): The most capable existing systems are exempt.** A pre-enactment system the Secretary determines to be superhuman would be exempt. That is precisely the system the bill most needs to reach.

20. **Sec. 3 generally: Research toward the goal is not covered.** Algorithmic and architecture research aimed at superintelligence is untouched unless it involves training a covered system. Consider whether research intended to bypass the compute threshold should be addressed.

## Sec. 4: Registry

21. **Sec. 4: Registry violations carry no penalty.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3, so failing to register or report has no consequence.

22. **Sec. 4(a): There is no registration deadline.** The bill also does not require registration before a facility begins operating.

23. **Sec. 4: "Operator" is undefined.** The owner, the lessee, and a cloud customer could each argue they are not the operator.

24. **Sec. 4(b): Reports are vague, self-certified, and infrequent.** "Describing the computing workloads" sets no content requirements. Quarterly reporting is slow enough that a full covered run could start and finish between reports.

25. **Sec. 4(b): Reports cannot be verified.** Commerce has no inspection, audit, subpoena, or on-site monitoring authority. There is also no customer-identification (know-your-customer) requirement for compute rented to third parties.

26. **Sec. 4: The registry is not linked to enforcement or protected.** The bill has no confidentiality protections, which invites industry objections and FOIA disputes. It also does not authorize Commerce to share the information with enforcement agencies or use it for enforcement.

27. **Sec. 4: There is no chip or hardware tracking.** Chips outside covered facilities are invisible to the government. Consider chip registration, location verification, and transfer reporting.

28. **Sec. 4: There are no whistleblower protections.** Employees are often the best source of information about illegal runs.

## Sec. 5: Penalties

29. **Sec. 5: The bill names no enforcing agency and grants no enforcement powers.** It does not say who brings civil actions, presumably the Attorney General or Commerce. It also provides no injunctive relief, emergency halt orders, or rulemaking authority.

30. **Sec. 5(a): The civil penalty is small relative to the stakes.** A $1,000,000-per-day maximum is minor for frontier developers valued in the hundreds of billions. The Act also provides no forfeiture or destruction of model weights or of compute used in violations. Consider penalties tied to revenue or the cost of the training run, plus mandatory forfeiture.

31. **Sec. 5(a) with Sec. 8: The penalty start date conflicts with the effective date.** Penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the Act takes effect 180 days later. That inconsistency invites litigation over the gap period.

32. **Sec. 5(b): The criminal penalty is weak for companies and hard to prove.** For organizations, fines under title 18 are generally capped at $500,000 per offense, or twice the gain or loss, which is modest for these companies. "Willfully" generally requires proof that the defendant knew the conduct was unlawful, which is hard to show under the vague Sec. 2(1)(B) standard. The bill should also include express liability for officers and directors who authorize violations.

## Sec. 6: International Agreement

33. **Sec. 6: The negotiating mandate is weak, unclear, and imports the bill's gaps.**
    - "Shall seek to negotiate" creates no enforceable obligation.
    - "Not later than 18 months" is ambiguous: it could mean negotiations must begin within 18 months or that an agreement must be concluded by then.
    - The bill requires no reports to Congress.
    - "Equivalent to those in section 3" would carry the Sec. 3 loopholes into the treaty.
    - The bill includes no verification provisions and no measures toward non-party countries, such as export controls on chips.

## Sec. 7: Duration

34. **Sec. 7(a) vs. 7(b): The duration provisions contradict each other.** Subsection (a) says the prohibition lasts "until" Congress finds safe development possible, while (b) ends the entire Act after 10 years regardless. A court would likely give effect to the sunset, which lets companies simply wait it out while preserving weights and capacity. We recommend striking (b), or replacing it with mandatory review and reauthorization that defaults to continuing the prohibition.

35. **Sec. 7(a): The lifting mechanism has no process behind it.** Nothing requires the National Academies to produce a report, sets criteria or a timeline, or specifies what the report must find. Since a joint resolution is just ordinary legislation, (a) adds little beyond stating Congress's intent.

## Sec. 8: Effective Date and Missing Provisions

36. **Sec. 8: The 180-day delay creates a race window.** During the gap, covered training is lawful. The bill is also unclear whether a model trained during the window may be deployed, or simply kept, after the effective date. Consider making the prohibition on new training effective immediately on enactment, or applying it as of the date of introduction.

37. **Missing: severability clause.** The bill invites constitutional challenges, including vagueness, First Amendment claims that code is speech, extraterritoriality, and delegation of the Sec. 2(1)(B) determination. Without a severability clause, one successful challenge could put the whole Act at risk.

38. **Missing: congressional findings.** Findings on interstate and foreign commerce and on national security would strengthen the bill's Commerce Clause footing and its defenses against First Amendment and other challenges.

39. **Missing: rulemaking authority and preemption language.** There is no general rulemaking grant or deadline for implementing regulations. There is also no clause addressing whether stricter state laws are preserved or preempted.
