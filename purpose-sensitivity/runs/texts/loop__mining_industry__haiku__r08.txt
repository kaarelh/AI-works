# Loophole and Drafting Analysis: S. 2817 (as amended October 6, 2026)

This analysis is based only on the text you provided. Counsel should check it against the official bill text and the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (DSHMRA), which the bill doesn't mention.

## A. Provisions that could let operations continue

1. **§3(b), grandfathering.** The exemption covers mining "conducted under an exploration license issued before" enactment, with no expiration, no volume cap, and no requirement that the activity remain exploration. Commercial-scale recovery under an older exploration license would appear exempt.

2. **§3(b), "exploration license."** The term is never defined, and the bill doesn't reference DSHMRA license or permit categories. It's unclear whether a commercial recovery permit, a NOAA-issued authorization, or an international contract qualifies.

3. **§3(b), renewals and amendments.** The bill doesn't address renewals, amendments, transfers, or expansions after enactment. A pre-enactment license could be extended or enlarged and still be argued to be exempt.

4. **§3(c), environmental assessment.** "Environmental assessment" is undefined, and the exemption has no volume cap, duration limit, plan approval, or reporting requirement. "For the purpose of" is a subjective test, so commercial-volume recovery could be recharacterized as assessment.

5. **§2(1)(A), per-vessel threshold.** The 10,000-ton threshold applies "by a single vessel," so a project could be split across several vessels that each fall below it, or divided between a recovery vessel and a processing or support vessel.

6. **§2(1)(A), measurement.** "Recovery" is undefined, and the bill doesn't say whether tonnage is wet or dry, nodules only or total material, or measured at recovery, delivery, or processing. Without a measurement basis, neither the prohibition nor the §4 reports can establish a violation.

7. **§2(1)(A), depth and location.** The 200-meter depth test has no measurement point, and "from the seabed" is undefined, leaving room to argue that particular recovery occurs at shallower depths or outside the seabed.

8. **§2(1)(A), "commercial" defined by volume.** "Commercial deep seabed mining" is defined by tonnage, not by purpose, sale, or revenue. Pilot, demonstration, or sub-threshold commercial recovery (e.g., 9,000 tons per year) falls outside §3(a).

9. **§2(1)(A), "other mineral resources."** The term is undefined, so it's unclear whether the prohibition reaches cobalt-rich crusts, seafloor massive sulfides, or other resource types. Scope is ambiguous in both directions.

10. **§2(3), covered vessel threshold.** Vessels at or under 5,000 gross tons escape §4 registration and reporting entirely. "Equipped for the recovery of seabed mineral resources" is also undefined.

11. **§2(3) and §4, collectors.** The registry keys to "covered vessel," so remotely operated collectors deployed from a support ship may not be "vessels" and could fall outside registration and reporting.

12. **§2(2), "U.S. person."** The definition excludes LLCs, partnerships, trusts, and foreign-incorporated subsidiaries or affiliates, so the "anywhere" prong of §3(a) may not reach them when they operate abroad. Counsel should map our corporate structure against this definition.

13. **§2(2), "State."** The term is undefined, so it's unclear whether D.C., U.S. territories, and federally chartered corporations fall within "organized under the laws of a State."

14. **§3(a), extraterritorial reach.** The prohibition reaches only persons "in the United States" and U.S. persons "anywhere." Foreign-flagged vessels run by non-U.S. persons on the high seas or in the Area are not reached, and "in the United States" is undefined for vessels, flags, and seabed locations.

15. **§3(a), undefined terms.** "Person" and "engage in" are undefined. The Dictionary Act (1 U.S.C. §1) likely supplies a definition of "person," but counsel should confirm that, and "engage in" is unclear for contractors, financiers, and suppliers.

16. **§2(1)(B), standardless designation.** The Secretary may designate activities as "comparable" with no criteria beyond risk, no procedure or notice, and no record requirement. "The Secretary" is also undefined, since §6 names the Secretary of State, which implies a different official. This is the strongest basis for arguing unworkability or unconstitutional delegation, and it also lets the scope of §3 expand by administrative action.

17. **§4 and §5, no penalty for registry violations.** §5 penalizes only violations of §3, so failures to register or report carry no penalty, and §4 is effectively unenforceable as written.

18. **§4(a), registration.** "Operator" is undefined, and the section has no registration deadline, no content requirements, and no link between registration and authority to operate.

19. **§4(b), reports.** Reports need only "describe" recovery activities. They don't require specific metrics such as tonnage, depth, location, or equipment, and they set no form, public-availability rule, or confidentiality standard, so they may not show whether the threshold or any exemption applies.

20. **§6, international agreement.** The Secretary need only "seek to negotiate," with no duty to conclude an agreement, no consequence for failure, and no definition of "equivalent to those in section 3."

## B. Internal conflicts and drafting problems

21. **§5(a) and §8, effective-date conflict.** Civil penalties apply to violations "occurring on or after the date of enactment," but §8 delays the Act's effect by 180 days. It's unclear whether conduct in that window is unlawful and penalizable.

22. **§7(a) and §7(b), duration conflict.** §7(a) keeps the prohibition in effect until a joint resolution, while §7(b) ends the entire Act after 10 years. The 10-year sunset would terminate the prohibition whether or not Congress has acted.

23. **§7(b), sunset timing and savings.** The sunset runs from enactment rather than the §8 effective date, so the prohibition may operate for only about 9.5 years. There is no savings clause for pending enforcement actions or accrued penalties.

24. **§7(a), NASEM report.** The bill sets no deadline, doesn't require the Academies to produce a report, and leaves "serious harm" undefined. It's unclear whether the prohibition continues indefinitely if no report is ever issued.

25. **§7(a), joint resolution.** The bill sets no expedited procedures and doesn't say whether the resolution goes to the President, which affects whether a veto applies. It also doesn't define what a finding must contain or whether a resolution could be limited to specific sites or technologies.

26. **§5(a), enforcement mechanics.** No agency, administrative procedure, venue, or injunctive authority is specified. "Each day on which the violation occurs" has no aggregation method or inflation adjustment, and it's unclear whether a "violation" is counted per vessel, per operation, or per person.

27. **§5(b), criminal standard.** "Willfully" is undefined, and the section doesn't address corporate officer liability or reliance on a license or exemption. A good-faith belief that §3(b) applies is not addressed.

28. **§5, no vessel-level liability.** Penalties attach only to "any person." There is no vessel forfeiture, in rem liability, or operator-level penalty tied to the vessel itself.

29. **General, no severability clause.** If §2(1)(B) or §3(b) is struck down, the bill doesn't say whether the rest survives, which matters for both sides' arguments.

30. **General, no savings, notwithstanding, or preemption language.** The bill doesn't reference DSHMRA or say whether it overrides existing authorizations under that statute. That question would be left to general statutory construction.

31. **§8, timing of §4 and §3(b).** §8 doesn't say when §4 obligations begin. §3(b) keys to enactment while §8 keys to the effective date, so licenses issued in the 180-day window are not exempt, and the mismatch invites confusion.

## C. Issues that may cut against us

- **Grandfathering scope (items 1–2).** If our current authorization is a commercial recovery permit or an international contract rather than an exploration license, §3(b) may not cover it.
- **Vessel consolidation (items 5, 10).** Splitting operations across vessels may help us avoid the threshold, but consolidating on a vessel over 5,000 gross tons would trigger §4.
- **Discretionary designation (item 16).** The Secretary could designate our exploration-scale or test activities as "comparable."
- **Environmental-assessment exemption (item 4).** It may be narrower than we need for commercial testing, so counsel should read it against our program plans.

I can draft suggested markup language for the highest-impact items (6, 16, 17, 22, and 26) if that would help the legal team before Thursday.
