# S. 2817 (Oct. 6 manager's amendment): Loopholes, Ambiguities, and Drafting Problems

This analysis is based only on the bill text you provided. It is not legal advice, and counsel should verify each point against the official text and any later amendments.

## A. Gaps that could let covered activity continue

1. **§2(1)(A), §3(a): "single training run."** The 10^26 threshold applies to a single run, so the text does not clearly count cumulative compute across multiple runs, continued pretraining, post-training, RL, or fine-tuning. A development program split into sub-threshold runs may fall outside the definition.

2. **§2(1)(A): Compute is not capability.** The threshold measures training compute, not what a model can do, and algorithmic gains can produce capable models below 10^26. The text also does not say whether compute used for synthetic data generation, distillation, or ensembling counts toward a model's total.

3. **§2(1)(A) vs. §2(1)(B): "model" vs. "system."** Subparagraph (A) covers a "model," while (B) covers a "system." A below-threshold model embedded in a larger scaffolded system may be argued outside (A), and the text does not say whether a smaller student model distilled from a covered model is itself covered.

4. **§2(1)(A): No counting method.** The bill does not say how to count "operations," whether to normalize across precisions (FP8 vs. FP32), or who measures and reports. A 10^26 figure is measurement-dependent, so whether a given run crossed the line can be contested.

5. **§2(1)(B): No Secretarial determination yet.** Subparagraph (B) reaches only systems the Secretary "determines" are capable of substantially exceeding human performance. The bill sets no deadline, standard, notice, or procedure for that determination, so until one issues, (B) may not reach any system.

6. **§2(1)(B): Which Secretary?** "The Secretary" is undefined. §6 specifically names the Secretary of State and §4 names the Department of Commerce, so it is unclear which official makes the determination.

7. **§3(b): Grandfathering is broad.** The exemption removes a system whose training began before enactment from subsection (a) entirely, which covers training, development, and deployment. The text does not limit the exemption to completing the original run, so it could be read to protect continued development and deployment indefinitely.

8. **§3(b) and §8: Enactment vs. effective date.** The grandfather clause is keyed to enactment, but §8 delays the prohibition 180 days. A run that began in that window is not grandfathered, and the text does not say how to treat a run that began before the effective date and continues after it.

9. **§2 and §3(b): "Training began" is undefined.** It is unclear whether training begins at the first gradient step, data acquisition, or cluster provisioning. An earlier start date would expand the grandfather exemption.

10. **§3(c): Safety evaluation exemption.** The exemption covers "activities conducted for the purpose of safety evaluation," which is purpose-based, undefined, and requires no registration, reporting, or oversight. Any training or deployment could be labeled as evaluation, and the text does not address mixed-purpose activities. This is the largest gap in the bill.

11. **§3(c): No limit on scope.** The exemption covers "activities," not just the evaluation phase. The text does not say whether releasing a model to outside users, giving API access, or sharing it with third parties during evaluation is also exempt.

12. **§3(a): "Develop" and "deploy" are undefined.** The text does not say whether internal research use, API access, or limited releases count as deployment. The ambiguity gives room to argue that internal development falls outside the prohibition.

13. **§2(2), §3(a): Extraterritorial reach is limited.** §3(a) covers "any person in the United States," but reaches activity abroad only for "U.S. persons," which excludes foreign-organized entities. Training abroad through a non-U.S.-organized affiliate is outside the text. (Relying on this structure would likely prompt a fix in markup and draw enforcement and reputational scrutiny, so counsel should weigh it carefully.)

14. **§2(2): Other gaps in "U.S. person."** The definition excludes lawful permanent residents and entities that are not "corporations," such as LLCs and partnerships. "State" is undefined, so it is unclear whether D.C. and the territories are included.

15. **§2(3), §4: Facility threshold is easy to avoid.** "Advanced AI chips" and "data center" are undefined, "containing" is ambiguous, and the bill does not address distributed training across several facilities that each fall under 50,000 chips.

16. **§4 and §2(3): The registry does not restrict workloads.** Registration applies to facilities, not models, and a facility under the threshold is not covered at all. The main prohibition therefore depends on the model definition, which is the hardest part to enforce.

## B. Ambiguities and drafting defects

17. **§5(a): No enforcement mechanism.** The civil penalty does not name an enforcing agency, an assessment process, or an administrative hearing, and it creates no private right of action. The mechanism is incomplete and would depend on implementing rules that the bill does not authorize.

18. **§5(a): "Each day" is undefined.** The text does not say how to count days for a continuing violation, such as a training run spanning weeks, or whether one run is one violation or many. The penalty has no cap, so exposure is hard to predict and contestable.

19. **§5(a) vs. §8: Penalty start date conflicts with effective date.** The civil penalty applies to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §3 takes effect 180 days later. It is unclear whether penalties can accrue before the prohibition applies.

20. **§5(a): No knowledge requirement for civil liability.** The civil penalty does not require intent or knowledge, which makes it effectively strict liability. §5(b) uses "willfully" for criminal liability, but the term is undefined.

21. **§4 and §5: Reporting duties have no penalty.** §5 penalizes only violations of §3, so failure to register or to file a report is not expressly penalized. The certified reports also carry no false-statement penalty.

22. **§4(b): Reports are underspecified and expose trade secrets.** "Computing workloads" is undefined, and the bill does not specify the report's form, content, or first due date. It also provides no confidentiality protection for trade secrets or any FOIA exemption, which creates disclosure risk.

23. **§4(a): "Operator" is undefined.** The text does not say whether the cloud provider or the tenant is the operator, or who registers when several parties share a facility.

24. **General: No rulemaking or appropriations.** The bill does not authorize Commerce to issue rules, forms, or definitions, and it appropriates no funds for registration, reporting, determinations, or the NASEM study.

25. **§6: Non-binding negotiation duty.** The Secretary of State must only "seek to negotiate" an agreement, with no obligation to conclude one and no consequence if it fails. The domestic prohibition is not conditioned on the agreement, so U.S. actors bear the cost regardless.

26. **§7(a): The trigger may never occur.** The NASEM report has no deadline, funding, or procedure, and "can be developed safely" has no standard. As written, the prohibition may be effectively permanent.

27. **§7(a): Joint resolution mechanics are unclear.** The text does not say whether the NASEM report must exist before Congress acts, or whether the joint resolution requires presidential signature. Joint resolutions generally do, but counsel should confirm how the bill's language interacts with that default.

28. **§7(a) vs. §7(b): Conflicting duration.** §7(a) keeps the prohibition in effect until a safety finding, but §7(b) ends the entire Act after 10 years whether or not that finding has been made. The text does not say which controls.

29. **§7(b): No savings clause.** The bill does not say whether pending investigations, penalties, or obligations survive the sunset or any later repeal.

30. **No severability clause.** If a provision is struck down, for example §2(1)(B) or §3(c), the bill says nothing about whether the rest survives.

31. **Undefined core terms.** "Artificial intelligence model," "artificial intelligence system," and "advanced AI chips" are undefined. "Person" is also undefined, although 1 U.S.C. §1 provides a general definition that counsel should confirm applies.

## C. Points for an "unworkable as written" argument

- **Compliance cannot be determined in advance.** Items 2–5, 10, and 12 mean a company cannot know whether a given activity is covered until enforcement, which is a basis for arguing the bill fails to give fair notice.
- **Enforcement is incomplete.** Items 17, 21, and 24 show that the penalty and reporting provisions cannot operate without rules, funding, or an enforcing agency that the bill does not provide.
- **The exit path depends on an undefined trigger.** Items 26–28 show that the prohibition could last indefinitely or end on a date unrelated to safety, so neither outcome is coherent.
- **Penalties are unbounded.** Item 18 leaves daily exposure of $1,000,000 with no cap and no definition of a violation.

## D. Possible constitutional questions for counsel

These are questions for legal review, not conclusions.

- **Vagueness and fair notice:** §2(1)(B), §3(c), and §5(b), given criminal penalties.
- **Delegation:** an open-ended Secretarial determination under §2(1)(B) with criminal consequences.
- **First Amendment:** whether restrictions on training, publishing, or releasing models or code raise speech concerns. The law here is unsettled.
- **Retroactivity and due process:** whether §3(b) and §5(a) treat activity differently based on timing in ways that raise fairness questions.

## E. Strategy notes

- **Some gaps are likely to be fixed.** Items 10 and 13 are obvious targets for sponsors. If the goal is to keep the bill from covering our work, pressing those points in public may prompt tighter language.
- **Counsel should separate public and internal positions.** Pointing out drafting defects in a markup is standard advocacy. Structuring operations to rely on a gap that drafters plainly meant to cover, such as splitting one program into sub-threshold runs, carries enforcement exposure, including willfulness under §5(b) and reputational risk. Those questions should be reviewed before any internal planning relies on them.
