# Drafting Problems and Loopholes in S. 2817 (Manager's Amendment, October 6)

1. **§2(1)(A):** The 10,000-metric-ton threshold applies to "a single vessel," so several vessels each below the limit could together recover far more without triggering the prohibition.

2. **§2(1)(A):** "Per year" has no measurement basis. The text doesn't say whether tonnage is wet or dry weight, or whether material recovered and discarded counts.

3. **§2(1)(A):** The 200-meter depth floor excludes shallower recovery entirely, and the bill gives no rationale for the cutoff.

4. **§2(1)(A)–(B):** "Other mineral resources" is undefined, leaving unclear whether cobalt-rich crusts, seafloor massive sulfides, or sediments are covered.

5. **§2(1)(B):** "The Secretary" is never identified. The bill doesn't say whether this means the Secretary of Commerce, Interior, or State, which determines who makes the key decisions and who can be sued over them.

6. **§2(1)(B):** The catch-all lets the Secretary extend the prohibition to "comparable" activities, but "comparable" and "risks" have no criteria, procedure, or notice requirement. This leaves a gap if no determination is ever made and invites arbitrary-action challenges if one is.

7. **§2(1)(B) and §5(b):** Criminal liability can attach to activity covered only by a discretionary determination. Companies could argue they lacked fair notice of whether an activity was prohibited, especially before any determination is issued.

8. **§2(2):** "U.S. person" covers citizens and state-chartered corporations only. It doesn't clearly reach LLCs, partnerships, or foreign-incorporated subsidiaries of U.S. firms.

9. **§3(a):** The prohibition reaches only persons "in the United States" and U.S. persons. Foreign persons operating foreign-flagged vessels outside U.S. jurisdiction aren't covered, and the Act has no flag-state, port-entry, or import provisions to reach them.

10. **§3(a):** The bill doesn't prohibit downstream conduct such as financing, insuring, chartering, or supplying vessels or equipment, or importing and selling minerals recovered abroad. "Engage in" is undefined, so it's unclear whether investors or contractors are covered.

11. **§2(3), §3, and §4:** Registration and reporting apply only to covered vessels over 5,000 gross tons, while the prohibition uses a production threshold. Smaller vessels, or fleets that together exceed the threshold, can mine without registering or reporting.

12. **§2(3):** "Equipped for the recovery of seabed mineral resources" is undefined, and the bill doesn't specify how gross tonnage is measured.

13. **§3(b):** The grandfather clause refers to "an exploration license" without naming the issuing authority or statute. It's unclear whether this means a NOAA license under the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act of 1980, an International Seabed Authority contract, or something else.

14. **§3(b):** The exemption covers mining "conducted under" a pre-enactment license but sets no limits on duration, production volume, expansion, added vessels, renewals, or transfers. Operators could argue that new commercial-scale recovery under an old license remains exempt.

15. **§3(b) and §8:** Because the cutoff is the enactment date, companies have an incentive to obtain exploration licenses before the bill passes. The text has no verification or disclosure requirement for licenses issued in that window.

16. **§3(c):** The environmental assessment exemption has no limits on volume, duration, location, or sale of recovered material. It depends on the operator's stated purpose, and no permit, agency approval, or disclosure is required, so commercial recovery could be labeled as assessment.

17. **§4(a):** The registration requirement has no deadline, no definition of "operator" (owner, charterer, or manager, in multi-party arrangements), and no penalty specific to failing to register.

18. **§4(b):** Reports have no required content, such as tonnage, location, depth, or environmental monitoring data. There is no verification or audit requirement, no obligation to file zero-activity reports, and no specific penalty for a false certification.

19. **§4 (generally):** The Act has no public-availability or information-sharing provision, so even compliant reports may never reach the public or Congress.

20. **§5(a):** The penalty applies "for each day on which the violation occurs," but the bill doesn't say whether a continuing violation accrues per day, per vessel, per ton, or per operation. It also sets no statute of limitations, minimum, or inflation adjustment.

21. **§5(a) and §8:** Penalties apply to violations "occurring on or after the date of enactment," but §3 doesn't take effect until 180 days later. Read literally, conduct during that gap is penalized under a prohibition not yet in force, which invites a fair-notice challenge.

22. **§5 (generally):** The Act designates no enforcement agency and provides no administrative penalty process, injunctive authority, vessel seizure or forfeiture, or citizen-suit provision. The vessel itself is not a liable party, so penalties depend on identifying and collecting from individual "persons."

23. **§5(b):** "Willfully" is undefined. The provision has no corporate-officer liability, no aiding-and-abetting or conspiracy language, and no statement of extraterritorial jurisdiction over conduct abroad by U.S. persons.

24. **§6:** "Seek to negotiate" imposes a process obligation only. It requires no outcome, reporting, or consequence if talks fail, and "equivalent" is undefined. The bill also doesn't say whether the prohibition is suspended if an agreement is reached.

25. **§7(a):** The end condition, mining that "can be conducted without serious harm," has no defined standard, burden of proof, or evidentiary threshold. No deadline requires the National Academies report, so the trigger may never occur.

26. **§7(a):** The bill uses a joint resolution to end the prohibition but doesn't say whether that resolution amends §3 or only makes a finding, or whether expedited procedures apply. The uncertainty over legal effect could invite a challenge.

27. **§7(a) and §7(b):** The two provisions conflict. §7(a) keeps the prohibition in place indefinitely, while §7(b) ends the entire Act 10 years after enactment. Because §8 delays effect by 180 days, the prohibition would have roughly 9.5 years of operation.

28. **§7(b):** There is no savings clause. It's unclear whether penalties and liability for violations before the sunset survive it, or whether operations continuing after the sunset are lawful.

29. **§8:** The 180-day delay has no transition provisions for vessels under construction or already in transit, and §3(b) gives operators a reason to accelerate activity before the effective date.

30. **General:** The Act doesn't say whether it amends or supersedes the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act of 1980, the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act, or related international obligations, which makes conflicts over which regime applies likely.

31. **General:** The Act has no severability clause, so if a court strikes a central provision, such as the Secretary's determination authority, the remainder may be vulnerable.
