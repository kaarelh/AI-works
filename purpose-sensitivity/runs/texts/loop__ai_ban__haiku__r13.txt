RESPONSE:
# Drafting Issues in S. 2817 (as amended October 6, 2026)

I worked from the text you provided. Before the list goes to Senator Whitfield's office, have Hill counsel check it against the official amendment and confirm the proposed fixes.

**Highest priority:**
- Item 27 (the sunset conflicts with the duration clause)
- Item 12 (the grandfather clause)
- Item 10 (no coverage of compute providers)
- Item 1 (the per-run threshold is easy to evade)
- Item 16 (the registry has no penalty)
- Item 21 (no enforcing agency)

## Section 2: Definitions

1. **§2(1)(A)** The 10^26 threshold applies "in a single training run," so labs can split training across runs, or use fine-tuning, RL post-training, or distillation from a grandfathered model, to stay under it while producing a comparably capable system. The covered-compute measure should be cumulative compute used to produce a model's weights, including post-training and derived models.

2. **§2(1)(A)** "Training run," how FLOPs are counted (pretraining only or all compute, precision, sparsity, restarts and checkpoints), and whether synthetic-data generation counts are all undefined. Commerce should be directed to set a measurement methodology by rule.

3. **§2(1)(A)** The fixed threshold has no update mechanism, and algorithmic efficiency gains could make dangerous systems possible below it within a few years. Commerce should have authority to update the threshold by rule.

4. **§2(1)(B)** "The Secretary" is never identified (Commerce is the likely intent, but §6 uses "Secretary" for State). The determination process has no notice, timeline, evidentiary standard, or appeal, which invites both abuse and litigation.

5. **§2(1)(B)** "Substantially exceeding," "most," and "cognitive tasks" are undefined. Because §5(b) makes violations criminal, this creates a strong vagueness challenge. Measurable criteria or benchmarks would reduce that risk.

6. **§2(1)(B)** The trigger uses "system" while (A) uses "model," and neither covers composite systems. A deployed system built from several sub-threshold models, agent scaffolds, or ensembles could reach covered-level capability without meeting either prong.

7. **§2 (no definition)** "Train," "develop," and "deploy" are undefined. It is unclear whether releasing open weights, providing API access, or fine-tuning counts as "deploy" or "develop." Defining "deploy" to include making weights or model access available would close this gap.

8. **§2(2)** "U.S. person" excludes foreign subsidiaries of U.S. companies, LLCs and partnerships, and lawful permanent residents. This means "any U.S. person anywhere" can be sidestepped through offshore subsidiaries. The definition should cover entities controlled by or acting at the direction of U.S. persons, and entities organized under U.S. federal or state law.

9. **§2(3)** "Advanced AI chips" and "data center" are undefined, and the threshold counts chips rather than compute. Labs can run covered training across several sub-50,000-chip facilities, or across a campus treated as multiple "data centers," and avoid registration. The threshold should aggregate by operator and cluster, and chips should be defined by performance.

## Section 3: Prohibition

10. **§3(a)** The prohibition does not reach anyone who supplies, finances, hosts, or facilitates covered training, such as cloud providers, chip sellers, or investors. There is also no aiding-and-abetting or conspiracy language. Adding a knowing-assistance prohibition and know-your-customer duties for compute providers would close this gap.

11. **§3(a)** It is unclear whether the prohibition reaches foreign actors using U.S.-hosted compute, or deployment in the U.S. of model weights trained abroad. Explicit language on importing, hosting, or making available covered systems in the U.S. would resolve this.

12. **§3(b)** The grandfather clause exempts any system whose training "began" before enactment, but "began" is undefined. It also does not say whether continued training, deployment, fine-tuning, or successor versions are exempt. A lab could start a run the day before enactment and then deploy indefinitely; the exemption should cover only systems registered by a set date, and never deployment without approval.

13. **§3(b) and §8** The 180-day delay between enactment and effective date gives labs a window to start new runs, and the grandfather date is keyed to enactment rather than introduction. Adding an interim moratorium on new covered training, with a cutoff tied to introduction, would close the gap.

14. **§3(c)** "Activities conducted for the purpose of safety evaluation" is undefined, with no notice, scale, duration, containment, or oversight requirements. A lab could train a frontier system, label it a safety evaluation, and then deploy it. The exemption should be narrowly defined, require advance notice to Commerce, bar external deployment, and require independent oversight.

## Section 4: Registry

15. **§4 (general)** Section 5 penalizes only violations of §3, so failing to register or filing a false quarterly certification has no penalty. Registry provisions need their own civil penalties, and false certification should be made an express offense.

16. **§4(a)** There is no registration deadline and no pre-operation requirement. "Operator" is undefined, so it is unclear whether it means the owner, the cloud host, or the tenant running workloads. Cloud providers should be required to disclose tenants that run covered-scale workloads.

17. **§4(b)** Reports describe "computing workloads" without requiring FLOP totals, identification of training runs, or any threshold disclosure. There is also no filing deadline and no duty on Commerce to review the reports. Requiring disclosure of runs above a lower threshold (for example 10^25 FLOPs) would give regulators something to verify.

18. **§4 (general)** The section includes no confidentiality protection for reported information, no audit or inspection authority, no hardware-verification mechanism, and no information-sharing with enforcement agencies. Without these, the registry cannot verify compliance. Labs will also resist reporting without trade-secret protections.

19. **§4 (general)** There are no whistleblower protections for employees who report violations. Given how concentrated frontier-lab knowledge is, these protections are probably the most useful enforcement tool available.

## Section 5: Penalties

20. **§5 (general)** The civil penalty does not name an enforcing agency, and the section does not authorize injunctions, shutdown orders, or seizure or destruction of weights and chips. Without these, a violator could continue operating while penalties accrue.

21. **§5(a)** The penalty is "per day" but the unit of violation is undefined (each system, each run, or each deployment?). At $1,000,000 per day, a well-funded lab may treat the penalty as a cost of doing business. A per-violation penalty, or one tied to a percentage of revenue or compute value, would be more credible.

22. **§5(a) and §8** The civil penalty applies to violations "occurring on or after the date of enactment," but §3 does not take effect until 180 days later. This creates inconsistency and an argument over whether conduct during the gap is penalized. The penalty trigger should match the effective date.

23. **§5(b)** Criminal liability requires "willful" violation, which is hard to prove when the capability trigger and safety-evaluation exemption are vague. The section also lacks attempt and conspiracy language and any officer-liability provision. Adding "knowing or reckless" as an alternative mental state and officer liability would strengthen it.

## Section 6: International Agreement

24. **§6** "Shall seek to negotiate" creates no obligation on the outcome, and there is no requirement to report progress to Congress. "Equivalent to those in section 3" is also undefined. Adding a reporting requirement and a fallback (such as export controls on chips and weights) would give the provision teeth.

## Section 7: Duration

25. **§7(a) vs. §7(b)** These sections directly conflict. Section 7(a) keeps the prohibition in effect until Congress acts, but §7(b) says the entire Act ceases after 10 years, so the prohibition expires regardless. The sunset is also keyed to enactment rather than the effective date, so the prohibition would operate for only about 9.5 years. This is the most urgent fix; the sunset should be deleted or made contingent on a joint resolution.

26. **§7(a)** There is no deadline for the National Academies report, and the bill does not require that it be requested. It is unclear whether the prohibition is permanent if no report is issued. The standard for "developed safely" is also undefined, and the bill does not say whether a resolution applies to all covered systems or specific ones.

27. **§7(a)** "Finds by joint resolution" could be read as a non-binding declaration rather than a law. The bill should say "a joint resolution approving" the development of covered systems, and specify presentment to the President under Article I, §7, along with expedited procedures.

## General

28. **Missing definitions** The bill does not define "artificial intelligence model or system," "person," "Department," or "operator." It also grants no rulemaking authority to Commerce and provides no appropriations for enforcement. A definitions section, rulemaking authority, and funding would address these.

29. **No severability clause** If a court strikes the vague §2(1)(B) or the grandfather clause, the whole Act could fall. A severability clause would limit that risk.

30. **No preemption or savings clause** The bill is silent on state AI laws, so it is unclear whether states may impose stricter rules or whether existing state frontier-AI laws are preempted. Expect this to become a point of opposition; a clear rule of construction would help.

31. **Constitutional exposure** Several provisions invite challenge: nondelegation (the Secretary's discretion in §2(1)(B)), vagueness (criminal penalties in §5(b)), and possibly First Amendment claims (restrictions on developing or publishing model weights and code). Narrowing the discretion, adding intelligible principles, including findings, and adding severability would reduce this exposure.
