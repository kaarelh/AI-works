# Loopholes, Ambiguities, and Drafting Problems in S. 2817 (Oct. 6 Manager's Amendment)

I worked from the text you provided. I couldn't check it against the official Congress.gov version or confirm the cited statutes, so counsel should verify both. Items are grouped by section, but numbered continuously.

## Section 2 (Definitions)

1. **§2(1)(A), tonnage measurement:** The 10,000-metric-ton threshold doesn't say whether it refers to wet or dry weight, or to total material lifted versus nodules retained. Nodules carry substantial water, so the choice can move an operation across the line, and compliance can't be determined in advance.

2. **§2(1)(A), "by a single vessel":** Limiting the threshold to one vessel lets an operator split a project across several vessels, each under 10,000 tons per year, without aggregation across vessels, affiliates, contractors, or license areas.

3. **§2(1)(A), undefined terms:** "Recovery," "other mineral resources," and "seabed" are undefined, and "per year" has no averaging period, so it's unclear whether a ramp-up year or multi-year average counts.

4. **§2(1), "commercial":** The term appears in the title, but the definition is purely volume- and depth-based and doesn't require sale, profit, or marketing. It's unclear whether pilot or test recovery above the threshold is "commercial."

5. **§2(1)(B), open-ended Secretary determination:** It reaches "any activity" the Secretary determines is "comparable" in risk, with no standard, procedure, notice, or hearing. The clause is vulnerable to vagueness and nondelegation challenges, and because it applies regardless of volume, it could reach small or research activities.

6. **§2(2), "U.S. person":** "Corporation" excludes LLCs and partnerships, and foreign-incorporated entities are excluded entirely. That limits the extraterritorial reach of §3(a) ("any U.S. person anywhere"), and "State" is undefined (it's unclear whether D.C. or the territories count). Our own corporate form needs to be checked against this definition.

7. **§2(3), "covered vessel":** Vessels of 5,000 gross tons or less fall outside §4 entirely, and "equipped for the recovery" is undefined, so a vessel can be fitted for recovery without triggering the definition. "Gross tons" isn't tied to a measurement standard, and the 5,000-ton vessel threshold doesn't match the 10,000-ton production threshold in §2(1)(A).

## Section 3 (Prohibition)

8. **§3(a), scope of "engage in":** "Engage in" is undefined, and the prohibition covers only recovery. It doesn't reach processing, transporting, selling, importing, financing, or purchasing nodules or minerals recovered by others, including abroad.

9. **§3(a), territorial reach:** The clause applies to "any person in the United States" but doesn't require the activity to occur in U.S. waters or the U.S. jurisdictional zone. Whether management in Houston directing a foreign-flagged vessel in international waters is "in the United States" is unclear, which cuts both ways.

10. **§3(b), "exploration license":** The term is undefined and isn't linked to the existing NOAA licensing regime under the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act of 1980. The bill doesn't say which licenses qualify. (Counsel should confirm this statutory connection.)

11. **§3(b), scope of the exemption:** The exemption covers "mining conducted under" an exploration license rather than "commercial deep seabed mining." Commercial-scale recovery could be argued to fall within it, which could swallow the prohibition. The argument is weak if exploration licenses don't authorize commercial recovery, but the text doesn't rule it out.

12. **§3(b), renewals and amendments:** The bill doesn't address renewals, amendments, expansions into new areas, or transfers of pre-enactment licenses. The exemption also has no time limit, even though the Act sunsets after 10 years.

13. **§3(c), "environmental assessment":** The term is undefined and has no cap on volume, duration, or sales, so commercial-scale operations could be labeled as assessment. "For the purpose of" is subjective, with no documentation, permitting, or burden-of-proof requirement.

## Section 4 (Registry)

14. **§4(a), "operator":** "Operator" is undefined. It's unclear whether this means the owner, charterer, manager, or master, and contract structures can shift responsibility among them.

15. **§4(a), no consequence or deadline:** Registration has no stated effect, no deadline, and no link to the §3 prohibition. Because §5(a) penalizes only violations of §3, a failure to register has no stated penalty.

16. **§4(b), reporting depends on registration:** Only a "registered operator" must file reports, so an operator who doesn't register is outside the reporting duty entirely. The requirement is circular.

17. **§4(b), report contents:** Reports must describe "recovery activities" but don't specify tonnage, location, volume, deadlines, format, or public availability. The certification has no consequence stated in the Act itself.

18. **§4, no implementing authority:** NOAA is named, but the Act grants no rulemaking authority and sets no data standards, so implementation is left unspecified.

## Section 5 (Penalties)

19. **§5(a), no enforcement mechanism:** The civil penalty doesn't say who brings enforcement actions, whether through administrative or judicial process, or what notice and hearing rights apply. "Each day on which the violation occurs" doesn't define when a continuing violation begins or ends.

20. **§5(a) versus §8, effective-date conflict:** §5(a) reaches violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §8 makes the entire Act effective 180 days later. Counsel can argue that conduct during the 180-day window is penalized, or that it isn't prohibited at all. The §3(b) cutoff is also keyed to enactment rather than the effective date.

21. **§5(b), "willfully":** The term is undefined, which affects the criminal exposure standard and creates room to argue about knowledge and intent.

## Section 6 (International Agreement)

22. **§6, obligation to "seek to negotiate":** The Secretary must try to negotiate an agreement but isn't required to conclude one, and no report or consequence follows if talks fail. "Equivalent" is undefined, and the section doesn't address coordination with the International Seabed Authority.

## Section 7 (Duration)

23. **§7(a), "serious harm" standard:** The term is undefined, and a finding that mining proceeds "without serious harm" may be effectively impossible to make. This supports an argument that the ban is in practice indefinite. The standard of proof is also unspecified.

24. **§7(a), NAS report:** Nothing requires the National Academies report to be commissioned, funded, scoped, or completed on any timeline. The only path to lifting the ban depends on a study no one is obligated to produce.

25. **§7(a), joint resolution:** The Act doesn't say whether the joint resolution must be presented to the President or whether expedited procedures apply. If presentment isn't required, the mechanism may raise a legislative-veto problem under *INS v. Chadha* (1983). Counsel should evaluate this.

26. **§7(a) versus §7(b), conflict:** §7(a) keeps the prohibition in effect until a joint resolution is adopted, but §7(b) says the entire Act ceases after 10 years. It's unclear whether the prohibition lapses at year 10 regardless of any finding.

## Section 8 (Effective Date)

27. **§8, no transition provisions:** The 180-day effective date applies uniformly, with no phased compliance for vessels already under contract or construction, and no transition for registration or reporting.

## General Drafting Gaps

28. **Undefined "Secretary":** The Act uses "Secretary" in §2(1)(B) without naming the department. It's unclear whether Commerce, Interior, or another agency is responsible, and that affects both the §2(1)(B) determination and any enforcement authority.

29. **No severability, savings, or preemption clauses:** The Act doesn't say how it interacts with the 1980 Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act or existing international obligations. Without a savings clause, two licensing regimes could apply at once. Without a severability clause, a court's choice of which provisions to strike is unpredictable.

## Points That Could Hurt Us

- **§3(a) and Houston management:** If the "in the United States" language reaches decisions made in Houston, our operations could be covered even when the vessels are abroad.
- **§2(1)(B) discretion:** The Secretary could reach our activities below the 10,000-ton threshold.
- **§3(b) protection is narrow:** The exemption helps only if we hold an exploration license issued before enactment. Licenses issued afterward aren't covered, and renewals or expansions may not be either.
- **§5 exposure:** Penalties of $1 million per day and up to 10 years' imprisonment for willful violations are significant.
- **§4 disclosure:** Vessels over 5,000 gross tons must register and report quarterly, which creates public-facing data.
- **§7(a) standard:** The "serious harm" test could make the ban effectively permanent. The 10-year sunset in §7(b) is the main counterweight.
