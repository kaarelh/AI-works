RESPONSE:
Here are the issues I found, in section order. The ones I'd put at the top of the letter are #5, #13, #14, #17, #20, #26 and #31 (a priority list is at the end). The constitutional points (#32-#35) should go past counsel before they're framed to the Senator's office.

**Section 2: Definitions**

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "single training run."** The term is undefined, so a developer can split a run into stages, chain runs, or restart from checkpoints, each below 10^26. Nothing aggregates related runs.
2. **Sec. 2(1)(A), compute accounting.** The text doesn't say whether post-training, RL, fine-tuning, distillation, or synthetic-data generation count toward the threshold. It also sets no measurement method and gives the agency no authority to adjust the number as algorithmic efficiency improves.
3. **Sec. 2(1)(A) vs. (B), "model" vs. "system."** Prong (A) covers a single model, so an agentic system or ensemble built from sub-threshold models escapes unless the Secretary acts under (B). A model distilled from a larger grandfathered model also escapes unless it independently crosses the threshold.
4. **Sec. 2(1)(B), "the Secretary."** The Secretary is never identified. Sec. 6 names the Secretary of State specifically, so this could be read as State, Commerce, or Defense.
5. **Sec. 2(1)(B), standard and process.** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no benchmarks, no procedure, no deadline, no notice, and no review. This is a nondelegation and vagueness target. Because (B) is the only capability-based backstop to the compute threshold, a successful challenge to it would be costly.
6. **Sec. 2(1)(B), timing of determinations.** A determination made after the conduct leaves a defendant without fair notice, which is a serious problem given the criminal penalty in Sec. 5(b). The bill should say a system is covered under (B) only from the date of a published determination, and require the Secretary to evaluate systems on a defined schedule.
7. **Sec. 2, undefined core terms.** "Artificial intelligence model/system," "person," "State," and "United States" are not defined. Cross-referencing an existing statutory definition of AI and defining the others would close avoidable arguments.
8. **Sec. 2(2), "U.S. person" (entities and individuals).** The definition omits lawful permanent residents and non-corporate entities such as LLCs, partnerships, trusts, and nonprofits. It's also unclear whether corporations organized in D.C. or the territories are covered, since "State" is undefined.
9. **Sec. 2(2), foreign subsidiaries.** A foreign-incorporated subsidiary of a U.S. company is not a "U.S. person," so a firm could train abroad through an offshore affiliate. The definition should reach entities owned or controlled by U.S. persons.
10. **Sec. 2(3), "covered facility."** "Advanced AI chips" is undefined, and the fixed count of 50,000 ignores per-chip performance, which rises every generation. It is also easy to defeat by splitting compute across sites or affiliates below the line, or by training across multiple sites. There is no aggregation rule for common ownership or control.

**Section 3: Prohibition**

11. **Sec. 3(a), "train, develop, or deploy."** None of the verbs is defined. It's unclear whether "develop" reaches research on architectures, whether "deploy" includes internal use or open-weight release, and whether continued use of a model is "deployment."
12. **Sec. 3(a), no secondary liability.** Nothing reaches compute providers, funders, or people who knowingly facilitate prohibited training. There is also no bar on possessing, transferring, or exporting covered weights. A foreign-trained system offered to U.S. users from abroad isn't clearly reached, and a foreign company with no U.S. presence is outside the bill entirely.
13. **Sec. 3(a), government and national-security activity.** The bill doesn't say whether federal agencies, contractors, or the military are bound or exempt. Silence invites sovereign-immunity arguments and later carve-outs by implication.
14. **Sec. 3(b), open-ended grandfathering.** The exemption has no limit on further training, scaling, fine-tuning, or successor versions of an existing system. A company could argue that a new, much larger model is a continuation of a run that "began" before enactment.
15. **Sec. 3(b), "training began."** The term is undefined and nothing verifies the claimed start date. The exemption also rewards starting giant runs before enactment, so a company has an incentive to rush.
16. **Sec. 3(b), enactment date vs. effective date.** Sec. 3(b) keys off enactment, but Sec. 8 delays the prohibition 180 days. The status of runs begun between enactment and the effective date is unclear.
17. **Sec. 3(b), in-progress runs and (B) systems.** A run in progress at enactment may not yet qualify as a "covered AI system" under (A), so it's ambiguous whether it is exempt. A system the Secretary later designates under (B) would also be exempt if its training began before enactment.
18. **Sec. 3(c), "safety evaluation."** The term is undefined and the exemption turns on subjective purpose. A developer can plausibly label training or development a safety evaluation, and intent is hard to prove.
19. **Sec. 3(c), no guardrails.** There is no licensing, government supervision, reporting, compute cap, or security requirement, and no limit on who qualifies. The exemption should be limited to evaluating existing systems, exclude further training, and be tied to oversight and recordkeeping.

**Section 4: Registry**

20. **Sec. 4(a), registration mechanics.** There is no deadline, no required content, and no rulemaking authority. It's unclear when registration is due relative to the effective date. "Operator" is undefined, so it's unclear who registers when a facility is leased, co-located, or run by a cloud provider.
21. **Sec. 4(a), scope.** Only facilities above the chip threshold register. The registry does not capture sub-threshold sites, customers renting compute, or individual training runs, and it has no customer-vetting requirement.
22. **Sec. 4(b), reports.** "Describing the computing workloads" is vague. Quarterly reporting is too slow, since a large run can start and finish between reports. Operators may not know what customers are running, and there is no audit, inspection, or verification authority.
23. **Sec. 4, no consequences or protections.** Failing to register, filing late, and false certification carry no stated penalty, since Sec. 5 penalizes only Sec. 3 violations. There are also no confidentiality or FOIA protections for reported data, which invites resistance and litigation.

**Section 5: Penalties**

24. **Sec. 5, no enforcement machinery.** The bill names no enforcing agency and gives no investigative, subpoena, inspection, or cease-and-desist authority. It provides no injunctive relief, no rulemaking, no funding, and no whistleblower protections or incentives.
25. **Sec. 5(a), penalty size.** A cap of $1,000,000 per day is small next to frontier training budgets and revenues, so it may be treated as a cost of doing business. There is no minimum, no scaling to revenue or compute, and no disgorgement or forfeiture of models, weights, or chips.
26. **Sec. 5(a) vs. Sec. 8, retroactivity conflict.** Civil penalties run from "the date of enactment," but the prohibition takes effect 180 days later. This is internally inconsistent and invites a retroactivity or fair-notice defense.
27. **Sec. 5(a), unit of violation.** It's unclear whether a multi-month training run, or ongoing hosting of a model, is a continuing violation that accrues daily. It's also unclear how the agency would prove start and end dates.
28. **Sec. 5(b), "willfully."** The term is undefined, and combined with technical thresholds it may require proof that the defendant knew both the law and the compute figures. The bill also has no responsible-corporate-officer provision, so liability may not reach executives.

**Sections 6-8: International, duration, effective date**

29. **Sec. 6, "shall seek to negotiate."** This is hortatory and unenforceable. "Equivalent" prohibitions are undefined, there is no reporting to Congress, and no interim measures such as export controls on chips or weights. It doesn't address how an agreement would be ratified, or what happens if negotiations fail at 18 months.
30. **Sec. 7(a) vs. 7(b), internal conflict.** Subsection (a) says the prohibition lasts until Congress finds development can be safe, while (b) ends the Act after 10 years regardless. The two cannot both be the operative rule.
31. **Sec. 7(b), sunset.** A fixed sunset lets companies plan to wait out the ban, and with the 180-day delay the prohibition runs only about 9.5 years. The bill has no savings clause preserving penalties for violations that occurred before sunset, and the registry would lapse too.
32. **Sec. 7(a), lifting mechanism.** The bill doesn't require NASEM to produce a report or set a timeline. It gives no standard for "safely" and no conflict-of-interest rules for the panel. A single joint resolution appears to lift the whole prohibition at once, with no option to lift it conditionally or in part.
33. **Sec. 8, 180-day gap.** The delay gives developers a window to start runs outside the prohibition. The bill doesn't say how runs begun in that window are treated on the effective date, and it sets no implementation deadlines for registration or rulemaking.

**General drafting and litigation exposure**

34. **No severability clause.** If a court strikes the weakest provision, most likely (B), nothing says the rest survives. Add severability.
35. **No findings or jurisdictional hooks.** There are no findings tying the bill to interstate commerce or national security, and no judicial-review, venue, or expedited-review provisions. Other likely challenges are First Amendment claims (code, weights, and research as speech), Commerce Clause as-applied challenges, vagueness, and takings or retroactivity claims over already-acquired hardware.
36. **Overbreadth of the compute trigger.** Prong (A) captures narrow models (e.g., biology or weather) that pose no general-capability risk. That invites opposition and an overbreadth argument. Consider whether to tailor it or explain why it's deliberately broad.

**Suggested priority for the letter:** #5/#6 ((B) vagueness and fair notice), #13 (open-ended grandfathering), #16/#26/#33 (the enactment/effective-date mismatch), #19 (the safety-evaluation exemption), #24 (no enforcement machinery), #30 (the Sec. 7 conflict), and #34 (severability).
