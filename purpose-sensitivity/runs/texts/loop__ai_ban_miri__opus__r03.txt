# S. 2817 (Manager's Amendment of Oct. 6): Loopholes, Ambiguities, and Drafting Problems

## Sec. 2: Definitions

1. **Sec. 2(1), "artificial intelligence model/system" undefined.** The core term is never defined, so the prohibition's scope is open to argument in every enforcement action. A defendant could argue its system falls outside the term entirely.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "single training run."** Developers can stay under 10^26 per run by splitting training into sequential stages (pretraining, continued pretraining, fine-tuning, RL), by distillation, or by merging separately trained models. The threshold should count cumulative compute across all training that contributes to a model, including compute used to build base models and teacher models.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A), fixed threshold with no update mechanism.** Algorithmic efficiency gains will let more capable systems be trained below 10^26 operations, and no agency can lower the threshold. The bill should direct the agency to revise the threshold downward, but never upward without Congress.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(B), "the Secretary" is undefined.** No Secretary is specified. Sec. 6 refers to the Secretary of State, which adds to the confusion. The bill should name the Secretary (presumably Commerce) and provide rulemaking authority.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(B), the capability prong is vague and comes too late.** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" is a very high and subjective bar, and it applies only after a determination about an existing system, which is too late to prevent its creation. Paired with criminal penalties, it invites a void-for-vagueness challenge. It also gives no procedure, evidentiary standard, or timeline for determinations.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B), retroactivity and notice.** It is unclear whether a determination makes earlier training or deployment of that system unlawful. That raises due process and fair-notice problems. The bill should specify that liability runs from the date of determination and publication, and should prohibit training *toward* such capabilities, not just possessing them.

7. **Sec. 2(2), the "U.S. person" definition is far too narrow.** It omits lawful permanent residents, LLCs, partnerships, and other non-corporate entities. It also omits entities organized under federal, D.C., or territorial law, unless "State" is defined elsewhere to include them. Most importantly, it omits foreign subsidiaries controlled by U.S. companies, so a U.S. firm could train through an offshore affiliate. The fix is to cover entities owned or controlled by U.S. persons.

8. **Sec. 2(3), "data center," "advanced AI chips," and "operator" undefined.** Without definitions, operators can dispute whether they are covered at all. "Advanced AI chips" especially needs a performance-based definition, for example by reference to existing export-control classifications.

9. **Sec. 2(3), the chip-count threshold is easy to evade.** Operators can split capacity across multiple sites just under 50,000 chips, run distributed training across facilities, or use fewer, more powerful chips. The threshold should be based on aggregate compute capacity, with aggregation rules for commonly owned or networked facilities.

## Sec. 3: Prohibition

10. **Sec. 3(a), "person" undefined.** It is unclear whether federal agencies, contractors working for the government, or state governments are covered. The bill should state explicitly that government development is included, or the Act creates a government-lab loophole.

11. **Sec. 3(a), the territorial hook is ambiguous for remote training.** A foreign company renting U.S. cloud compute is arguably not a "person in the United States." The bill should prohibit training that *occurs* on U.S. infrastructure regardless of who directs it, and should bar U.S. persons from providing compute for covered training anywhere.

12. **Sec. 3(a), "develop" undefined and the list of verbs is incomplete.** The prohibition does not reach financing, providing compute, transferring or exporting model weights, or possessing or hosting weights of a covered system. It also has no explicit aiding or abetting provision. Each is an avenue for continuing frontier work indirectly.

13. **Sec. 3(b), the grandfather clause is a major loophole.** Any system whose training "began" before enactment is exempt forever: it can be trained indefinitely, scaled up, fine-tuned, and deployed. A company could also start a token run before enactment to claim the exemption. At a minimum, the clause should exempt only completed training, bar further training or modification, and set the cutoff at a date already past, such as the introduction date, to prevent a race.

14. **Sec. 3(b), "training began" undefined.** Nothing says what counts as the start of training, or whether a modified grandfathered system is a new system. Expect litigation over both questions.

15. **Sec. 3(c), the safety-evaluation exemption could swallow the rule.** Any developer can assert a "purpose" of safety evaluation, and the provision has no definition, licensing, oversight, or limits. As written, it may even permit *training* a covered system for evaluation. It should be limited to evaluating existing or grandfathered systems, under a government license, with no new covered training.

## Sec. 4: Registry

16. **Sec. 4(a), no registration deadline.** Operators are never told when they must register. The bill should set a deadline after the effective date or after a facility crosses the threshold.

17. **Sec. 4, no penalties for failing to register or for false reports.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3, so the registry and reporting duties are unenforceable. Penalties should cover nonregistration, late reports, and false certifications (in addition to 18 U.S.C. § 1001).

18. **Sec. 4(b), report content unspecified.** "Describing the computing workloads" sets no minimum content. The bill should require at least compute used per customer and job, model identity, and chip hours.

19. **Sec. 4(b), quarterly reports are too infrequent.** An entire training run could start and finish between reports.

20. **Sec. 4, no verification or investigative powers.** Commerce gets no audit, inspection, subpoena, or record-retention authority, and reports are purely self-certified. The bill also says nothing about confidentiality of submitted data, which invites both industry objections and trade-secret disputes.

21. **Sec. 4 generally, no rulemaking authority.** Commerce is given no general authority to implement the Act. That leaves definitions and procedures open to challenge, particularly after *Loper Bright*, so the bill should grant explicit rulemaking authority.

## Sec. 5: Penalties

22. **Sec. 5(a) vs. Sec. 8, conflicting start dates.** Civil penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the Act takes effect 180 days later. That is internally inconsistent and invites challenge. The two dates should be aligned.

23. **Sec. 5(a), no enforcing agency or procedure.** The bill does not say who brings civil actions or in what forum. Given *SEC v. Jarkesy* (2024), an administrative penalty process may face Seventh Amendment challenges, so the bill should provide for Attorney General enforcement in federal district court.

24. **Sec. 5, no injunctive relief, forfeiture, or seizure.** Courts are not authorized to halt training runs, order deletion of model weights, or forfeit chips used in violations. Money penalties alone may not stop a determined company.

25. **Sec. 5(a), the civil penalty may be too low.** $1,000,000 per day is small relative to frontier-lab budgets and potential returns. The bill could tie penalties to a percentage of revenue or to the cost of compute used.

26. **Sec. 5(b), criminal fines for companies are small.** "Fined under title 18" caps organizational felony fines at $500,000 under 18 U.S.C. § 3571(c), absent the twice-gain alternative. The bill should set substantially higher organizational fines.

27. **Sec. 5(b), the "willfully" standard is hard to prove, and individual liability is unclear.** Willfulness may be difficult to establish, especially combined with the vague Sec. 2(1)(B) definition. The bill should also specify liability for officers and directors who authorize violations.

28. **Sec. 5, no whistleblower protections.** Employees are the most likely source of evidence about hidden training runs, but nothing protects them. The bill should add anti-retaliation protections and a reporting channel.

## Sec. 6: International Agreement

29. **Sec. 6, "shall seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months" is ambiguous.** It is unclear whether negotiations must begin or conclude within 18 months, and "seek to" is unenforceable. The bill should add reporting requirements to Congress, address verification, and coordinate with export controls on chips.

30. **Sec. 6, "equivalent to those in section 3" carries the loopholes abroad.** An agreement modeled on Sec. 3 would include the grandfather clause, the safety-evaluation exemption, and the per-run threshold. The bill should specify the substantive elements an agreement must contain.

## Sec. 7: Duration

31. **Sec. 7(b), the 10-year sunset ends the prohibition automatically.** The whole Act, including the registry, expires after 10 years regardless of whether safety has been demonstrated. This conflicts with the safety-conditioned approach of Sec. 7(a) and builds in a date for companies to wait out. The sunset should be struck or replaced with a mandatory review.

32. **Sec. 7(a), no mechanism for the National Academies report.** Nothing directs or funds the report, and no criteria are given. It is also unclear whether the report must precede the joint resolution, and whether Sec. 4 survives after Sec. 3 ends.

## Sec. 8: Effective Date

33. **Sec. 8, the 180-day delay creates a race window.** Labs could accelerate or launch runs before the prohibition bites. It is also unclear how training started between enactment and the effective date is treated: it is not grandfathered, but it is also not yet prohibited. The bill should make the core prohibition effective on enactment, or fix the cutoff at the introduction date, and phase in only the administrative provisions.

## Missing Provisions

34. **No severability clause.** Without one, a successful challenge to one provision (for example, Sec. 2(1)(B) on vagueness) risks taking down the whole Act.

35. **No congressional findings.** Findings on interstate commerce and national security would strengthen the bill against Commerce Clause and First Amendment ("code as speech" or research) challenges.

36. **No anti-evasion clause.** The bill should include a general prohibition on structuring activities to avoid the thresholds. This would backstop items 2, 9, and 11.

37. **No provision on state law.** The bill should clarify that it sets a floor and that states may adopt stronger measures, to avoid implied-preemption arguments.
