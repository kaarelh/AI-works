RESPONSE:
**Drafting and enforceability issues in S. 2817 (text as amended October 6, 2026)**

1. **§2(1)(A), threshold:** The 10,000-metric-ton limit applies to a "single vessel," so an operator could split activity across several vessels that each stay under the line. The bill doesn't say whether affiliated companies' vessels are aggregated.

2. **§2(1)(A), "per year":** The bill doesn't say whether the threshold is measured by calendar year, rolling 12 months, or an average. Operators could time or front-load recovery to stay under it.

3. **§2(1)(A), undefined terms:** "Recovery" and "mineral resources" are undefined. It's unclear whether lifting material to the surface counts, whether collector testing or discarded material counts, and whether at-sea processing is covered.

4. **§2(1)(A), 200-meter floor:** Activity in shallower water falls outside the definition entirely, and the bill gives no scientific basis for the 200-meter line.

5. **§2(1)(B), "Secretary":** The bill never says which Secretary is meant. §6 refers to the Secretary of State, so the default reading is unclear.

6. **§2(1)(B), catch-all:** The "comparable risks" standard has no defined test, procedure, notice requirement, or review standard. A determination could be challenged as arbitrary or as an improper delegation, and the Secretary could decline to make any determination.

7. **§§2(3), 4(a), registry mismatch:** The prohibition is keyed to tonnage recovered by any vessel, but registration and reporting apply only to "covered vessels" over 5,000 gross tons. A smaller vessel that exceeds 10,000 tons a year would violate §3 without any registration or reporting duty.

8. **§2(3), "equipped for the recovery":** This is ambiguous. It's unclear whether a vessel fitted with mining gear but not operating counts, and survey or research vessels could fall into a gray zone.

9. **§2(2), "U.S. person":** The definition covers citizens and state-chartered corporations but not LLCs, partnerships, or trusts. It also doesn't address foreign-flagged vessels controlled by U.S. persons, and "State" is undefined as to whether it includes D.C. and the territories.

10. **§3(a), "engage in":** The term is undefined. The prohibition may not reach financing, insurance, chartering, equipment supply, or servicing of mining operations abroad.

11. **§3(a), no import or sale provision:** The bill bans recovery but not the import, sale, or processing of minerals mined elsewhere. Minerals recovered by foreign companies could still enter U.S. supply chains.

12. **§3(a), geographic reach:** "In the United States" is undefined. It's unclear whether it includes the territorial sea, the exclusive economic zone, or the outer continental shelf. The "U.S. person anywhere" clause may also conflict with the international regime for the deep seabed, which is a likely point of legal challenge.

13. **§3(b), exploration license exemption:** The clause exempts "mining conducted under" an exploration license, which lets mining continue under an exploration label. There is no volume cap, end date, list of covered licenses, or treatment of renewals and amendments.

14. **§3(b), existing law:** The bill doesn't say how it interacts with existing licensing under the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act of 1980, which NOAA administers, or whether International Seabed Authority contracts count as "licenses." *(Verify against current statute before publication.)*

15. **§3(c), environmental assessment exemption:** This is probably the largest loophole. "Purpose of environmental assessment" is undefined, and there is no cap on volume, no approval or reporting requirement, no time limit, and no stated decision-maker. Full-scale recovery could be labeled as assessment.

16. **§4(a), registration:** There is no registration deadline and no required content, and "operator" is undefined (owner, charterer, or master?). §5 penalizes only violations of §3, so failure to register carries no penalty.

17. **§4(b), reporting:** The bill doesn't specify what a report must contain beyond "describing recovery activities." It has no public-availability requirement, verification or audit provision, or penalty for a false certification.

18. **§5, enforcement machinery:** No agency is designated to enforce the Act. The bill also provides no investigative authority, inspection or subpoena power, injunctive relief, or citizen-suit provision, and it authorizes no appropriations.

19. **§5(a), effective-date conflict:** The civil penalty covers violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §3 doesn't take effect until 180 days later under §8. It's unclear whether conduct in that window is penalizable. The unit of violation (per day, per vessel, or per ton) is also undefined.

20. **§5(b), criminal provision:** "Willfully" is undefined. The section doesn't cover attempt, conspiracy, or aiding and abetting, and it doesn't address corporate officer liability.

21. **§5, no forfeiture:** The bill doesn't provide for seizure or forfeiture of vessels, equipment, or recovered minerals, which weakens deterrence.

22. **§6, international agreement:** "Shall seek to negotiate" is a non-binding duty with no reporting requirement and no consequence if negotiations fail. "Equivalent" is undefined, and the Act doesn't restrict imports from non-agreement countries.

23. **§7(a), termination standard:** "Serious harm" is undefined, and no evaluation criteria are given. The bill sets no deadline for the National Academies report, and no expedited procedure for the joint resolution, which would also be subject to presidential veto.

24. **§7(a) and §7(b), interaction:** §7(a) suggests the prohibition lasts until Congress acts, but §7(b) ends the entire Act after 10 years. Read together, the bill is a 10-year moratorium unless Congress extends it, not a permanent ban. The explainer should state this clearly.

25. **§7(b), sunset timing and savings:** The 10 years run from enactment, not from the §8 effective date, so the prohibition operates for roughly 9.5 years. The bill has no savings clause preserving penalties or pending enforcement actions after the sunset.

26. **§8, mismatched triggers:** §3(b) and §5(a) key off the date of enactment, while §3 takes effect 180 days later. The bill has no transition rule for operations already underway at enactment.

27. **General drafting gaps:** The bill has no severability clause, so a court striking one provision could affect the whole Act. "Person" is not defined in the bill itself.
