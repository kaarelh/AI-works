RESPONSE:
Here's a section-by-section pass. I've ordered it by section, with a few cross-cutting items at the end. Numbering is continuous so you can reference items in the explainer.

**Definitions (Sec. 2)**

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — per-vessel threshold invites fleet-splitting.** The 10,000-ton threshold is measured "by a single vessel," with no aggregation across vessels under common ownership or control. An operator could run ten vessels at 9,900 tons each (99,000 tons/year) without ever triggering the prohibition.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "per year" is undefined.** The text doesn't say calendar year, fiscal year, rolling 12 months, or year from first recovery. An operator could front-load 9,999 tons in December and another 9,999 in January, and prosecutors would have to litigate which "year" applies.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — only recovery "by a single vessel" is covered.** Seafloor crawlers, fixed or moored platforms, and autonomous collector systems that aren't "vessels" (undefined) arguably fall outside subparagraph (A). The bill's own nodule-collector scenario — a seabed crawler feeding a surface support ship via riser — raises the question of which unit does the "recovery."

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — 200-meter depth cutoff excludes shallower deposits and is ambiguous at slopes.** Cobalt crusts on seamount crests and placer or phosphorite deposits on the outer shelf can lie at or above 200 m. The text also doesn't say where depth is measured (point of extraction, average site depth, charted depth), which matters for mining along slopes.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "other mineral resources" is both overbroad and vague.** Read literally, it could sweep in deepwater oil and gas, sand/gravel extraction, dredging, or pipeline trenching, all of which can move more than 10,000 tons of seabed material a year. That overbreadth is an obvious target for a challenge and would need a carve-out or a definition tied to hard minerals.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — only "recovery" is prohibited.** Transport, processing, refining, financing, chartering, insurance, offtake contracts, and sale of seabed minerals are not covered. A U.S. company can finance a foreign operator and buy the entire output without "recovering" anything.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — "the Secretary" is undefined.** The only Secretary named anywhere in the Act is the Secretary of State (Sec. 6), while NOAA (Commerce) runs the registry. Whoever is intended, the bill never gives that official authority, procedures, or a duty to publish determinations.

8. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — a catch-all defined by future agency determination can't reliably support a 10-year felony.** Criminal liability for activity that becomes "commercial deep seabed mining" only when an unnamed official decides its risks are "comparable" raises fair-notice and vagueness problems, and in the current judicial climate a nondelegation challenge. There's no standard, no rulemaking requirement, no notice, and no judicial review provision.

9. **Sec. 2(2) — "U.S. person" excludes most of the entities that actually mine.** It covers only citizens and "corporations" organized under State law. Omitted: LLCs, partnerships, trusts, and other unincorporated entities; lawful permanent residents; entities organized under federal, DC, or territorial law ("State" is undefined); and, most importantly, foreign subsidiaries or affiliates of U.S. companies. A Delaware corporation can mine through a Marshall Islands subsidiary without being a "U.S. person."

10. **Sec. 2(3) — the 5,000-gross-ton registry threshold doesn't match the mining threshold.** A vessel under 5,000 gt can recover well over 10,000 tons per year but never has to register or report, so NOAA would have no visibility into exactly the operators most likely to be splitting fleets (item 1).

11. **Sec. 2(3) — "equipped for the recovery" is undefined.** Operators can remove or disable equipment while transiting, dispute whether support vessels or riser-and-lift ships are "equipped for recovery," or argue the standard applies only at the moment of inspection.

**Prohibition (Sec. 3)**

12. **Sec. 3(a) — "in the United States" and "United States" are undefined.** It's unclear whether the U.S. EEZ, outer continental shelf, territories, or possessions count. A foreign company mining on the U.S. OCS is probably not a "person in the United States," and a foreign company directing operations from a New York office is in the U.S. but arguably not "engaging in" mining there.

13. **Sec. 3(a) — "engage in" is undefined.** Does a U.S. person engage in mining by owning a minority stake, chartering a vessel to a foreign operator, providing technology or crew, or serving on the board of the mining company? The bill gives no answer, and the omission of aiding-and-abetting or "cause to be conducted" language leaves indirect participation untouched.

14. **Sec. 3(b) — the grandfather clause lets exploration licenses authorize commercial recovery.** Under the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (DSHMRA) and the ISA system, an exploration license is distinct from a commercial recovery permit, yet 3(b) exempts "mining conducted under an exploration license." It converts every pre-enactment exploration license into a license to mine commercially, with no volume limit.

15. **Sec. 3(b) — the exemption isn't limited to U.S. or any particular licenses.** It doesn't say who must have issued the license (NOAA, the ISA, a foreign sponsoring state), so an ISA exploration contract held by a foreign subsidiary or a license from any coastal state qualifies.

16. **Sec. 3(b) — no limit on renewals, extensions, amendments, or transfers.** The exemption attaches to the license, not the holder or its original terms, so a license could be renewed indefinitely, expanded in area, or sold to a new company, carrying the exemption with it.

17. **Sec. 3(b) — the cutoff creates a race to the courthouse.** Keying the exemption to "the date of enactment" (not introduction or markup) gives every prospective operator a window to secure exploration licenses, and pending NOAA or foreign applications could be expedited to beat the clock.

18. **Sec. 3(c) — the environmental assessment exemption is self-declared and uncapped.** There's no tonnage limit, no approval, no requirement that data be submitted or published, and no bar on selling recovered minerals. Pilot-scale "test mining" characterized as an assessment of environmental effects would fit comfortably within the text.

19. **Sec. 3(c) — mixed-purpose activity is unaddressed.** Nearly all commercial mining generates environmental data, and the exemption applies to activities conducted "for the purpose of" assessment without saying "sole" or "primary" purpose, so an operator can argue any monitored operation qualifies.

20. **Sec. 3 generally — no coordination with DSHMRA or OCSLA.** The bill neither amends nor repeals the 1980 Act, under which NOAA remains authorized to issue commercial recovery permits, nor does it touch Interior's authority over OCS minerals. NOAA could lawfully issue a recovery permit for activity the same statute makes a felony, and courts would have to reconcile the two.

**Registry (Sec. 4)**

21. **Sec. 4 — no penalty for failing to register or report.** Section 5 penalizes only violations of Section 3, so the registry and reporting duties have no enforcement mechanism at all.

22. **Sec. 4(a) — no deadline, no procedure, no rulemaking authority.** The bill doesn't say when registration is due, what information is required, whether NOAA can deny or revoke a registration, or whether it may issue implementing regulations. It also doesn't define "operator."

23. **Sec. 4(b) — self-certified reports with no verification.** Reports are "certified by the operator," with no inspection, audit, observer, monitoring, or records-access authority for NOAA, and no false-statement penalty beyond the general 18 U.S.C. § 1001. Reports also aren't made public, limiting outside accountability.

24. **Sec. 4(b) — only "registered" operators must report.** An operator who simply never registers has no reporting duty, and under item 21, no consequence for either omission.

25. **Sec. 4(a) — no jurisdictional nexus.** Unlike Section 3, the registry applies to "the operator of each covered vessel," full stop, which on its face reaches every foreign-flag mining vessel in the world. Courts will either read that as unenforceable extraterritorial overreach or narrow it to an unstated U.S. nexus, leaving the actual scope uncertain.

**Penalties (Sec. 5)**

26. **Sec. 5(a) — penalty dates conflict with the effective date.** Civil penalties apply to violations "occurring on or after the date of enactment," but under Sec. 8 the prohibition isn't effective until 180 days after enactment. Either no violation can occur in that window (making the clause surplus) or the government will argue it can, inviting a retroactivity fight.

27. **Sec. 5(a) — "each day on which the violation occurs" doesn't fit an annual-tonnage offense.** Because the offense is defined by crossing 10,000 tons in a year, a defendant can argue the violation "occurs" only on the single day the threshold is crossed, capping exposure at $1 million rather than per-day accrual.

28. **Sec. 5 — no enforcing agency and no procedure.** The bill doesn't say who assesses civil penalties (NOAA? Coast Guard? Justice?), whether assessment is administrative or judicial, what the statute of limitations is, or how penalties are collected. There's also no injunctive relief, vessel seizure or forfeiture, cargo detention, or citizen-suit provision, so the only tool against an ongoing operation is after-the-fact fines.

29. **Sec. 5(b) — the criminal provision inherits the Section 2 vagueness problems.** "Willfully" helps, but a felony keyed to an undefined "year," an undefined "Secretary's" comparability determination, and an undefined "engage in" is vulnerable on fair-notice grounds, especially for conduct on the high seas by non-citizens.

**International agreement (Sec. 6)**

30. **Sec. 6 — "shall seek to negotiate" is hortatory.** There's no reporting requirement, no consequence for failure, and it's ambiguous whether negotiations must begin or conclude within 18 months. The provision also ignores that the U.S. is not a party to UNCLOS and has no seat at the ISA, which is where this negotiation would realistically occur.

31. **Sec. 6 — "equivalent to those in section 3" exports the bill's loopholes.** Any agreement modeled on Section 3 would carry the per-vessel threshold, the grandfather clause, and the assessment exemption into international law.

**Duration (Sec. 7)**

32. **Sec. 7(a) vs. 7(b) — internal contradiction.** Subsection (a) says the prohibition lasts until Congress affirmatively lifts it; subsection (b) says the whole Act expires in 10 years regardless. Opponents can cite (b) to argue the "indefinite" moratorium is really a decade-long cap; supporters can cite (a); a court has to pick.

33. **Sec. 7(a) — the National Academies report is never commissioned.** No one is directed or funded to request the report, so the only path to lifting the ban "on a report" may never open. "Serious harm" is also undefined.

34. **Sec. 7(a) — the joint-resolution mechanism is redundant.** A joint resolution requires presidential signature and is simply a statute, so this subsection adds nothing Congress couldn't do anyway. If drafters meant a faster expedited procedure, none is provided.

35. **Sec. 7(b) — the sunset kills the registry too.** Because the entire Act expires, NOAA loses even its reporting and registration authority in year 10, and operators can line up permits and financing to begin at the sunset date.

36. **Sec. 7(b) + Sec. 8 — the ban is really 9.5 years.** The sunset runs from enactment while the prohibition starts 180 days later.

**Cross-cutting**

37. **Effective date (Sec. 8) — a six-month ramp-up window.** Operators can begin recovery, stockpile, and position vessels during the delay, and because the grandfather clause (3(b)) is keyed to enactment rather than the effective date, there's a period where new licenses aren't grandfathered but the mining they authorize isn't yet illegal either.

38. **No severability clause.** If a court strikes 2(1)(B) or the extraterritorial reach of Section 4, there is no instruction to preserve the remainder, giving challengers an argument that the whole scheme falls.

39. **No appropriations, rulemaking, or implementing authority.** No agency is authorized to issue regulations, no funds are authorized, and no agency is clearly tasked with enforcement, so even the enforceable provisions depend on agencies volunteering resources.

40. **Extraterritorial reach without enforcement tools.** Section 3 reaches U.S. persons "anywhere," including other nations' EEZs and ISA contract areas, but the bill provides no mechanism (flag-state cooperation, port-state controls, import bans) to detect or stop high-seas activity, and application in foreign EEZs could conflict with those states' sovereign rights.

41. **Key terms are never defined.** Beyond those above: "person," "vessel," "seabed," "mining," "recovery," "operator," "State," "United States," and "year" are all undefined. Each gap is a litigable question, and the first prosecution would likely turn on several of them.
