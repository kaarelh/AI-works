RESPONSE:
I worked only from the text you provided. Counsel should verify it against the official bill text and confirm the legal points, especially the constitutional and DSHMRA issues. Items are grouped by theme. The first group is where the bill may let current operations continue. The second is where the bill is ambiguous or unworkable, which is useful for the markup record.

**A. Gaps that could let operations continue**

1. **§2(1)(A), tonnage threshold:** The 10,000 metric ton test applies "by a single vessel." Multiple vessels each recovering under 10,000 tons per year appear to fall outside the definition, and the bill doesn't aggregate fleet or affiliate output.

2. **§2(1)(A), measurement:** The bill doesn't say whether the 10,000-ton figure refers to wet nodule weight, dry weight, or contained metal, or how it treats mixed or non-nodule resources. Each reading changes whether a given operation crosses the line.

3. **§2(2), "U.S. person":** The definition covers only citizens and corporations "organized under the laws of a State." It appears to exclude LLCs, partnerships, federally chartered corporations, and foreign-incorporated affiliates. Confirm how Meridian and its subsidiaries are organized before relying on this.

4. **§3(a), no import, sale, or processing ban:** The prohibition applies only to "engaging in" mining. It doesn't restrict importing, selling, processing, or financing minerals recovered elsewhere.

5. **§3(a), extraterritorial reach:** The bill reaches conduct abroad only by "U.S. persons." Foreign-incorporated subsidiaries or foreign-flagged vessels operating outside U.S. territory may fall outside it.

6. **§3(a), "engage in" undefined:** The bill doesn't say whether the term covers financing, vessel chartering, equipment supply, or management services. Narrow readings could leave significant support activity outside the ban.

7. **§3(b), grandfather clause:** The exemption covers "mining conducted under an exploration license issued before" enactment. It has no tonnage cap, no time limit, and no language excluding commercial-scale operations, so it could be read to cover commercial mining under a pre-existing exploration license. Opponents will likely argue for a narrower reading.

8. **§3(b), "exploration license" undefined:** The bill doesn't say whether this includes NOAA exploration licenses under DSHMRA, ISA exploration contracts sponsored by another state, or both. It also doesn't address renewals, amendments, or transfers after enactment.

9. **§3(c), environmental-assessment exemption:** The exemption turns on the activity being conducted "for the purpose of" assessment. It has no volume, duration, or sales cap and no approval or documentation requirement, so it may be hard to disprove.

10. **§8 with §3, 180-day gap:** The prohibition takes effect 180 days after enactment, so no §3 violation can occur before then, even though §5(a) refers to violations "on or after the date of enactment." Commercial operations could continue lawfully during that window.

11. **§7(a) and §7(b), sunset conflict:** Section 7(a) keeps the prohibition in place until Congress makes a joint-resolution finding. Section 7(b) ends the entire Act 10 years after enactment, so the prohibition lapses at year 10 even if no finding has been made. This is the most significant internal inconsistency.

12. **§7(b), effective period:** Measured from enactment, the sunset leaves roughly 9.5 years of effective prohibition, since the Act doesn't take effect until day 180.

13. **§7(b), no savings clause:** The bill doesn't say whether penalties for violations committed before the sunset can still be enforced afterward. The general savings statute (1 U.S.C. §109) addresses repeals, and counsel should confirm whether it reaches a sunset.

14. **Relationship to DSHMRA (1980):** The bill doesn't say whether it amends, repeals, or displaces DSHMRA's licensing and permit regime. Existing NOAA exploration licenses and commercial recovery permits may be left in legal limbo.

15. **§4, no penalty for registry or reporting failures:** Section 5 penalizes only violations of §3. Failing to register a covered vessel or file quarterly reports carries no stated penalty.

**B. Ambiguities and drafting problems**

16. **"Secretary" never identified:** The bill uses "the Secretary" in §2(1)(B) and elsewhere without naming the department. Section 4 refers to NOAA, but nothing assigns enforcement of §3 or §5 to a specific agency.

17. **§2(1)(B), open-ended Secretarial determination:** The Secretary can treat any activity as "commercial deep seabed mining" if it poses risks "comparable" to the tonnage-based activity. The bill sets no standard, procedure, notice requirement, or review mechanism, and "comparable" is undefined.

18. **§2(1)(B) and §5(b), constitutional exposure:** Because criminal liability (§5(b)) depends on this standard, a vagueness challenge is available. A nondelegation challenge is also possible, though courts have generally been lenient under the intelligible-principle test.

19. **§5(a), penalty unit undefined:** The bill imposes "$1,000,000 for each day on which the violation occurs" without saying whether a continuing violation, a single vessel, or a single mining event is the unit. Aggregate caps and any procedural protections are also absent.

20. **§5(b), "willfully" undefined:** The bill doesn't define willfulness or address corporate officer liability. Its penalty provisions also don't specify a limitations period.

21. **§4(b), no content requirements or confidentiality protection:** The quarterly report must describe "recovery activities" but doesn't specify what that includes. It also provides no protection for trade secrets or commercially sensitive data, which creates disclosure exposure.

22. **§7(a), "serious harm" undefined:** The standard for lifting the prohibition is not defined, so the threshold for a congressional finding is unclear.

23. **§7(a), NASEM report has no deadline or scope:** The bill doesn't require the National Academies to report by any date or specify what the report must address. If the report never arrives, the finding mechanism stalls.

24. **§7(a), "finds by joint resolution":** Joint resolutions normally go to the President for signature or veto. The bill doesn't say whether that applies here, whether the finding is a one-time vote, or how it interacts with the sunset.

25. **§6, weak international obligation:** The Secretary of State must only "seek to negotiate" an agreement with "equivalent" prohibitions within 18 months. "Equivalent" is undefined, and the bill attaches no consequence if negotiations fail or succeed.

26. **§2, "seabed," "mineral resources," and "recovery" undefined:** The bill doesn't say whether "seabed" includes the water column or whether "recovery" covers lifting material to the surface versus processing at sea. These terms determine what the prohibition actually covers.
