# S. 2817 (as amended Oct. 6): Loopholes, Ambiguities, and Drafting Problems

## Sec. 2: Definitions

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "single training run."** A developer could split training into several runs that each stay under 10^26 operations, or build on an existing model through continued pretraining, fine-tuning, or distillation. The bill doesn't say how compute is counted or whether it adds up across runs or derived models.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A), fixed compute threshold.** Training efficiency keeps improving, so the same compute buys more capability over time. Highly capable models could soon be trained below 10^26 operations, and the bill gives no authority to lower the threshold.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A), capability-blind threshold.** Any model above 10^26 operations is covered, whatever it does. That could include large scientific models, such as for weather or biology, which could support overbreadth arguments.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A) vs. (B), "model" vs. "system."** Prong (A) applies to a single "model." A system that combines several models, each under the threshold, would escape prong (A) and would be covered only if the Secretary makes a prong (B) determination.

5. **Sec. 2(1), key terms undefined.** "Artificial intelligence," "artificial intelligence model," and "artificial intelligence system" are never defined. That makes the edges of coverage contestable.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B), "the Secretary."** The bill never says which Secretary. Context suggests Commerce, but the Act only names the "Department of Commerce" in Sec. 4 and the "Secretary of State" in Sec. 6.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(B), vague capability standard.** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no benchmarks, and none of "substantially," "most," or "cognitive tasks" is defined. Because Sec. 5(b) attaches criminal penalties, defendants could argue the standard is unconstitutionally vague.

8. **Sec. 2(1)(B), no process or timing.** Nothing provides for notice, rulemaking, evidence standards, or appeal of a determination. Capability is usually known only after a system is built, so it's unclear how anyone could know in advance that they are "training" or "developing" a prong (B) system. That raises fair-notice problems.

9. **Sec. 2(2), "U.S. person" excludes many entities.** The definition covers only citizens and "corporation[s] organized under the laws of a State." It leaves out:
   - lawful permanent residents;
   - LLCs, partnerships, and other non-corporate entities;
   - entities organized in D.C. or the territories, unless "State" is defined elsewhere to include them.

10. **Sec. 2(2), foreign subsidiaries.** A foreign-incorporated subsidiary of a U.S. company is not a "U.S. person." A U.S. firm could do covered training abroad through such a subsidiary, as long as no U.S. citizens or U.S.-incorporated entities take part.

11. **Sec. 2(3), "advanced AI chips" and "data center" undefined.** With no chip specification, operators could argue their hardware isn't "advanced." The term "data center" is also open: it could mean one building, one campus, or one operator.

12. **Sec. 2(3), chip-count threshold is easy to avoid.** Operators can stay just under 50,000 chips per site, or train across several smaller facilities. As individual chips get more powerful, fewer of them are needed for the same compute.

## Sec. 3: Prohibition

13. **Sec. 3(a), "develop" and "deploy" undefined.** It's unclear whether "deploy" covers internal use, API access, open-weight release, or use by downstream customers. It's also unclear whether "develop" reaches work short of training, such as research or design.

14. **Sec. 3(a), territorial reach.** The bill doesn't say whether a foreign company serving U.S. users from abroad is "deploying" in the United States. It's also unclear whether a non-U.S. person in the U.S. who remotely runs training on foreign compute is covered.

15. **Sec. 3(b), grandfather tied to "began."** A training run that started before enactment is exempt no matter how long it continues or how much compute it eventually uses. The bill also doesn't say whether later fine-tuning or successor versions of a grandfathered system inherit the exemption.

16. **Sec. 3(b), incentive to start runs before enactment.** Because the exemption depends only on when training "began," developers have a reason to start (even nominally) large runs before the bill is signed. Grandfathered systems also appear to be deployable indefinitely.

17. **Sec. 3(c), safety-evaluation exemption is open-ended.** "Activities conducted for the purpose of safety evaluation" has no definition, no approval or oversight process, and no limits. Almost any training or deployment could be described as serving a safety purpose. The bill also doesn't say whether safety must be the sole or primary purpose.

## Sec. 4: Registry

18. **Sec. 4, no penalty for non-compliance.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3. Failing to register, failing to report, or filing a false report under Sec. 4 carries no penalty in the Act, although general false-statement law could apply.

19. **Sec. 4(a), no deadline or required contents.** The bill sets no deadline for registration and doesn't say what information registration requires.

20. **Sec. 4(b), self-certified, vague reports.** Reports are certified only by the operator. There is no audit, inspection, or verification authority. "Describing the computing workloads" doesn't require reporting compute per run, customer identities, or model details.

21. **Sec. 4, registry doesn't match the prohibition.** The registry covers only domestic facilities above the chip threshold. It doesn't reach foreign facilities used by U.S. persons or training spread across smaller sites, which are the scenarios Sec. 3 is meant to cover.

## Sec. 5: Penalties

22. **Sec. 5(a), conflict with the effective date.** Civil penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but Sec. 8 says the Act takes effect 180 days later. It's unclear whether conduct during that window is penalized. Applying penalties to it would invite challenge.

23. **Sec. 5, no enforcing agency or procedure.** The bill doesn't name who assesses or sues for civil penalties (Commerce, DOJ, or someone else). It also provides no investigative or subpoena authority, no injunctive relief, and no power to seize or destroy model weights.

24. **Sec. 5(a), penalty cap vs. developer scale.** A maximum of $1 million per day may be small relative to the resources of the largest developers. Critics may argue it works more like a cost of doing business than a deterrent. The bill also doesn't say what counts as a "day" of violation for ongoing activities like "develop."

25. **Sec. 5(b), criminal liability plus vague definition.** Combining up to 10 years' imprisonment with the open-ended prong (B) definition (item 7) strengthens a vagueness challenge. "Willfully" may reduce that risk but probably doesn't remove it.

## Sec. 6: International Agreement

26. **Sec. 6, no binding obligation.** "Shall seek to negotiate" requires only effort, not a result. It's ambiguous whether the 18-month deadline applies to starting or finishing negotiations. There is no reporting requirement and no consequence if no agreement is reached.

## Sec. 7: Duration

27. **Sec. 7(a) vs. 7(b), conflicting end dates.** Sec. 7(a) says the prohibition "shall remain in effect until" Congress acts on a National Academies report. Sec. 7(b) ends the whole Act after 10 years regardless. The bill doesn't say which controls.

28. **Sec. 7(a), mechanism adds nothing and the report is never commissioned.** A joint resolution must be presented to the President, so it's effectively a new law that Congress could pass anyway. The bill also never directs or funds the National Academies to produce the report it relies on.

29. **Sec. 7(b), no savings clause.** The bill doesn't say whether penalties for violations before the sunset can still be enforced afterward. The general federal savings statute (1 U.S.C. § 109) likely helps, but the point is open to argument.

## Sec. 8 and Structural Gaps

30. **Sec. 8, 180-day window.** Systems that start training after enactment aren't grandfathered but are legal until the effective date. That leaves unclear what happens to training or deployment that straddles the effective date, and it interacts with the Sec. 5(a) conflict (item 22).

31. **Whole Act, no rulemaking authority or appropriations.** No agency is authorized to issue regulations clarifying terms, adjusting thresholds, or setting procedures. No funding is provided for enforcement or the registry.

32. **Whole Act, no preemption clause.** The bill doesn't say whether it displaces or coexists with state AI laws.

33. **Whole Act, possible constitutional challenges beyond vagueness.** Challengers might argue that limits on training or releasing models restrict speech under the First Amendment, citing cases treating code as expression. They might also raise nondelegation concerns about the Secretary's open-ended prong (B) power. Courts would likely be skeptical of both arguments, but a challenge is plausible.
