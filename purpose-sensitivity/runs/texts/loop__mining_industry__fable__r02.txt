RESPONSE:
Below is a section-by-section catalogue of the problems I found in the October 6 text. I've flagged each item as either **[L]** (a loophole or gap that would allow continued operation if the bill passes as written) or **[U]** (a drafting defect that makes the bill unworkable, overbroad, or legally vulnerable). Some are both. Legal should verify the points that turn on outside law (DSHMRA, Dictionary Act, 28 U.S.C. § 2462, etc.).

## Definitions (Sec. 2)

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — per-vessel threshold invites fleet-splitting. [L]** The 10,000-ton trigger is measured "by a single vessel," not by operator, project, license area, or affiliated group. An operator can stay below the line indefinitely by running several vessels, rotating hulls, or transshipping, and nothing in the bill aggregates across vessels or affiliates.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "per year" is undefined. [L/U]** The bill doesn't say calendar year, fiscal year, 12-month rolling period, or license year, and doesn't address partial first years. A vessel that recovers 9,999 tons in late December and 9,999 tons in early January arguably never crosses the threshold under a calendar-year reading.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "recovery" is undefined and the production system has multiple components. [L]** In a nodule system the seabed collector (an unmanned vehicle, arguably not a "vessel") does the collecting, the riser lifts material to a production support vessel, and bulk carriers take it to shore. The text doesn't say which unit "recovers" the material, so an operator could plausibly argue the surface vessel merely receives material already recovered by a non-vessel.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — the depth criterion is ambiguous. [L/U]** "At depths greater than 200 meters" could refer to seabed depth at the point of collection, the depth of the equipment, or the water depth where the vessel sits; it also doesn't say whether a transect that crosses the 200 m contour is covered. For criminal provisions, this ambiguity will be resolved under the rule of lenity.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — the tonnage metric is unspecified. [L]** Nothing says whether 10,000 metric tons is wet weight, dry weight, nodules only, or total recovered mass including entrained sediment and water. Dry-weight nodule mass is substantially lower than wet collected mass, so the choice of metric can move an operation across the threshold.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "other mineral resources" sweeps in offshore oil and gas and other industries. [U]** Hydrocarbons are "mineral resources" under OCSLA and ordinary usage, and FPSOs and drillships are vessels recovering mineral resources from the seabed at depths well over 200 m and well over 10,000 tons per year. As drafted the prohibition would criminalize most deepwater Gulf of Mexico production, deepwater drilling, and deep sand/gravel dredging — a point that undermines the bill's basic workability.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — "the Secretary" is never defined. [U]** The bill refers to "the Secretary" in 2(1)(B) but defines no Secretary; the only cabinet officer named is the Secretary of State in Sec. 6, while NOAA (Commerce) runs the registry and Interior administers the OCS. There is no identifiable official with authority to make the determination that triggers the catch-all prong.

8. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — the "comparable risk" prong has no standard, procedure, or notice mechanism. [U]** The prong contains no criteria, no rulemaking or notice-and-comment requirement, no publication requirement, no judicial review provision, and no statement of whether a determination applies prospectively or retroactively. Tying a 10-year felony (Sec. 5(b)) to an unpublished, standardless administrative determination raises serious void-for-vagueness, fair-notice, and nondelegation objections.

9. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — the catch-all makes the (A) threshold meaningless in both directions. [U]** If the Secretary can designate any activity as "comparable," the carefully drafted 10,000-ton/200 m/single-vessel test in (A) provides no actual safe harbor; conversely, if no Secretary is ever identified, (B) is a nullity. Either reading creates an internal inconsistency.

10. **Sec. 2(2) — "U.S. person" omits most business forms. [L]** The definition covers only corporations "organized under the laws of a State." It excludes LLCs, LPs, LLPs, trusts, joint ventures, and partnerships; entities organized under the laws of the District of Columbia, Puerto Rico, Guam, or other territories; and entities chartered under federal or tribal law. A Delaware LLC is not a "U.S. person" under this text.

11. **Sec. 2(2) — no ownership or control test for foreign entities. [L]** A foreign-organized subsidiary wholly owned, financed, and directed from Houston is not a U.S. person, and there is no attribution, affiliate, or "owned or controlled by" language (contrast OFAC and export-control definitions). Operating through a foreign-flagged vessel owned by a foreign-organized affiliate takes the activity outside Sec. 3(a) entirely.

12. **Sec. 2(2) — "citizen" excludes lawful permanent residents and U.S. nationals. [L/U]** Green-card holders and non-citizen nationals resident in the United States and directing operations from here are not "U.S. persons" and are only reached if "in the United States" at the time of the conduct (see item 17).

13. **Sec. 2(3) — the "covered vessel" threshold and the prohibition threshold don't match. [L]** The registry covers vessels over 5,000 gross tons "equipped for" recovery, but the prohibition keys to tonnage recovered. A vessel under 5,000 GT is never registered and never reports, even if it is the one recovering above the threshold, and a large vessel that is "equipped" but not mining must register and report on nothing.

14. **Sec. 2(3) — "equipped for the recovery of seabed mineral resources" is overbroad. [U]** Read with item 6, this captures drillships, FPSOs, trailing-suction dredgers, and research vessels with grab or core samplers — all over 5,000 GT and all "equipped" to recover seabed minerals. NOAA would be registering a large part of the world's offshore fleet.

15. **Sec. 2 generally — key terms left undefined. [U]** "Person," "operator," "seabed," "mining," "engage in," "exploration license," "serious harm," and "environmental assessment" are all undefined. The Dictionary Act will supply a broad "person," but the remainder are load-bearing terms with no statutory content.

## Prohibition (Sec. 3)

16. **Sec. 3(a) — "engage in" is undefined and does not reach ancillary activity. [L]** The bill prohibits engaging in mining but says nothing about financing, investing in, chartering vessels to, supplying technology or crew to, contracting with, insuring, processing for, or purchasing from an entity that mines. A U.S. company can finance and offtake from a foreign operator, and U.S. refineries can process imported nodules, without "engaging in" mining under any natural reading.

17. **Sec. 3(a) — "any person in the United States" does not reach the high seas or, likely, the EEZ. [L]** Deep seabed mining occurs on the high seas or in the EEZ, where no one is "in the United States" in the ordinary territorial sense. The first prong therefore reaches essentially no mining activity; everything depends on the narrow "U.S. person" definition (items 10–12), and the bill nowhere states that it applies in the EEZ, on the OCS, or in the Area.

18. **Sec. 3(a) — no anti-evasion or anti-circumvention clause. [L]** The bill lacks the standard "or causes, attempts, conspires, or structures transactions to evade" language. Structuring through foreign affiliates, fleet-splitting (item 1), and purpose-labeling (item 22) are therefore all facially compliant.

19. **Sec. 3(a) — extraterritorial reach over individual citizens is overbroad and creates international-law friction. [U]** "Any U.S. person anywhere" makes every U.S.-citizen engineer, officer, or crew member serving on a foreign-flagged vessel under a foreign ISA sponsorship a felon, even where the flag state and the sponsoring state have authorized the activity. Expect objections from allies and from the maritime labor community.

20. **Sec. 3(b) — "exploration license" is undefined and not limited to any issuing authority. [L]** The exemption applies to any exploration license from anyone — NOAA under DSHMRA, the ISA (via sponsoring state), or any coastal state's domestic permit. Any license in hand before enactment appears to qualify regardless of area, scale, or issuer.

21. **Sec. 3(b) — the exemption covers "mining," not merely exploration, and is unlimited in scope and time. [L]** It exempts "mining conducted under" a pre-enactment exploration license without any tonnage cap, geographic limit, expiry, or restriction to pre-existing scale. It is silent on renewals, extensions, amendments, assignments, transfers to affiliates, and conversion of an exploration license into a commercial recovery permit, so the strongest reading grandfathers the full lifecycle of any licensed project. (Legal should also consider whether the clause could be read to make the DSHMRA exploration-to-commercial-recovery pathway itself the "license" under which mining is "conducted.")

22. **Sec. 3(c) — the environmental-assessment exemption has no limit. [L]** There is no tonnage cap, no duration, no requirement that assessment be the sole or primary purpose, no agency approval, and no definition of "environmental assessment." Large-scale collector trials, monitoring programs, and pilot mining conducted "for the purpose of" assessing environmental effects fall within it; the bill does not say who decides what the purpose was.

23. **Sec. 3 — no relationship clause with DSHMRA, OCSLA, or existing executive action. [L/U]** The bill neither amends nor repeals the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (30 U.S.C. 1401 et seq.), so NOAA retains statutory authority to issue exploration licenses and commercial recovery permits; nor does it address Interior's OCS leasing authority or the existing seabed-minerals executive order. Implied repeal is disfavored, so courts will try to harmonize — likely by reading Sec. 3(b) broadly.

24. **Sec. 3 — the prohibition applies to persons while the registry applies to vessels, with no in rem hook. [L/U]** There is no vessel forfeiture, detention, or denial-of-port-entry provision, so a foreign-flagged vessel operated by a non-U.S. person faces no consequence in U.S. waters or ports even when carrying above-threshold cargo.

## Registry (Sec. 4)

25. **Sec. 4(a) — no deadline, no rulemaking authority, no appropriation. [U]** Operators must "register" but no date, form, content, fee, or process is specified, and NOAA is given no authority to issue implementing regulations or any funding to run the registry.

26. **Sec. 4 — no penalty for failing to register or report. [L]** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3. Non-registration and non-reporting carry no consequence whatsoever under this Act.

27. **Sec. 4(a) — "operator" is undefined and jurisdiction over foreign operators is doubtful. [U]** The bill purports to require operators of foreign-flagged vessels with no U.S. nexus to register with NOAA, which the United States cannot enforce and which exceeds the jurisdictional basis of Sec. 3.

28. **Sec. 4(b) — certified reports of "recovery activities" raise a Fifth Amendment problem. [U]** Compelling a sworn, certified description of recovery activities that may themselves be felonies under Sec. 5(b) is a compelled self-incrimination issue; expect operators to assert the privilege or litigate the requirement.

29. **Sec. 4(b) — the reporting regime presupposes lawful recovery. [L]** The registry only makes sense if some recovery continues (below-threshold, grandfathered, assessment, or foreign-operator activity). Point to it as Congress's own acknowledgment that the "prohibition" is partial.

## Penalties (Sec. 5)

30. **Sec. 5(a) vs. Sec. 8 — penalties attach from enactment but the Act isn't effective for 180 days. [U]** Civil liability runs for violations "occurring on or after the date of enactment," while Sec. 8 says the Act (including Sec. 3) takes effect 180 days later. Either the clause is a nullity for the first 180 days or it is an impermissibly retroactive penalty; it is unworkable as written.

31. **Sec. 5(a) — "each day on which the violation occurs" cannot be squared with an annual threshold. [U]** Because the prohibited conduct is recovery of "more than 10,000 metric tons per year," the "violation" is complete only when the annual total crosses the line. It is unclear whether penalties run only for the day the threshold is crossed, every day thereafter in the year, or every day of the year retroactively — a difference of up to $365 million per vessel.

32. **Sec. 5(a) — no enforcement authority, procedure, limitations period, or judicial-review provision. [U]** The bill doesn't say who assesses the penalty (NOAA? DOJ? Coast Guard?), whether it's administrative or judicial, what procedures apply, or how it's appealed, and it provides no injunctive-relief authority. The default five-year limitations period in 28 U.S.C. § 2462 will apply by default.

33. **Sec. 5(b) — "willfully" sets a high mens rea that the vague definitions make hard to meet. [L]** A criminal defendant need only show a good-faith belief that activity fell under (b), (c), the per-vessel threshold, or an undefined term. Given the ambiguities above, willfulness will be very difficult to prove.

34. **Sec. 5 — no penalty tiering, no corporate/individual distinction, and no minimum. [U]** A $1,000,000-per-day cap with no floor and no factors (gravity, history, ability to pay) invites arbitrary enforcement challenges.

## International Agreement (Sec. 6)

35. **Sec. 6 — the 18-month deadline is grammatically ambiguous. [U]** It could mean the Secretary must *begin* seeking negotiation within 18 months or must *conclude* an agreement within 18 months; neither reading has any consequence for non-compliance.

36. **Sec. 6 — no reporting requirement, no consequence, and no interaction with the prohibition. [U]** Failure to reach an agreement does not alter, suspend, or extend anything; the section is hortatory. It also ignores that the United States is not party to UNCLOS and that the ISA framework already governs the Area for other states.

## Duration (Sec. 7)

37. **Sec. 7(a) — the triggering NAS report is never commissioned. [U]** No provision requests, funds, scopes, or sets a deadline for the National Academies report on which Congress is to act, so the lifting mechanism has no starting point.

38. **Sec. 7(a) — "serious harm" is undefined and a "joint resolution" is just a new statute. [U]** A joint resolution requires bicameral passage and presentment, which Congress could do anyway; the clause binds no future Congress and adds nothing. It also provides no fast-track procedure.

39. **Sec. 7(a) vs. 7(b) — the duration provisions contradict each other. [L/U]** Subsection (a) says the prohibition "shall remain in effect until" a congressional finding; subsection (b) says the Act ceases to have effect after 10 years regardless. The sunset controls as the more specific and later provision, so the "indefinite" prohibition is in fact a 10-year one (and, with Sec. 8, roughly 9.5 years of operative life).

40. **Sec. 7(b) — the sunset has no savings clause. [L]** "This Act shall cease to have effect" extinguishes Sec. 5 as well, so pending penalty actions and liability for violations in the final years are arguably wiped out at sunset; no provision preserves accrued liability, pending proceedings, or the registry data.

## Effective Date (Sec. 8) and global issues

41. **Sec. 8 and Sec. 3(b) — a 180-day window with mismatched benchmarks. [L]** Mining during the 180 days after enactment is not prohibited at all (Sec. 3 isn't effective), yet grandfathering turns on licenses issued "before the date of enactment." Operators can lawfully mine during the window but cannot obtain grandfathered status during it — an incoherence worth highlighting, and one that encourages a rush of license applications before enactment.

42. **No severability clause. [U]** If Sec. 2(1)(B), Sec. 5(a), or the extraterritorial reach of Sec. 3(a) is struck, there is no instruction to preserve the remainder.

43. **No rulemaking authority anywhere in the Act. [U]** None of the agencies mentioned (the undefined "Secretary," NOAA, State) is authorized to issue regulations defining terms, setting the tonnage metric, or running the registry, so none of the ambiguities above can be cured administratively.

44. **No treatment of imports, downstream processing, or supply-chain activity. [L]** Nodules mined by foreign operators can be imported, refined, and sold in the United States without restriction; the bill addresses only the act of recovery.

45. **Title/structure mismatch. [U]** A bill styled as a "Prohibition Act" in fact establishes a per-vessel tonnage allowance, three open-ended exemptions, a registry that presumes ongoing recovery, and a 10-year sunset — a point for the "unworkable and incoherent" messaging.

Two suggestions for how to use this: items 1, 10–11, 16–17, 20–22, and 44 are the ones that most directly answer "could we keep operating," and they are strong enough that opponents will likely try to fix them at markup — so legal may want to prepare for second-degree amendments on aggregation, affiliate attribution, and the exemptions. Items 6, 7, 8, 30, 31, and 39 are the cleanest "this bill does not work as written" points for members and staff who are not deep in the subject.
