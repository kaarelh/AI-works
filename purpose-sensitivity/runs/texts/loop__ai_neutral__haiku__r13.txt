**Drafting problems and loopholes in S. 2817 (text as amended October 6, 2026)**

1. **§ 5(a), § 3(b), § 8:** The Act's effective date conflicts with its enforcement and grandfathering dates. § 8 says the Act takes effect 180 days after enactment, but § 5(a) applies penalties to violations "on or after the date of enactment," and § 3(b) keys the grandfather clause to enactment. Read literally, penalties could run for six months before the prohibition itself applies.

2. **§ 7(a), § 7(b):** The duration clauses contradict each other. § 7(a) keeps the § 3 prohibition in force "until" Congress finds by joint resolution that covered systems can be developed safely, but § 7(b) says the entire Act "shall cease to have effect" 10 years after enactment, which would end the prohibition regardless of any finding.

3. **§ 7(b):** The 10-year sunset runs from enactment, but the prohibition doesn't take effect until 180 days later (§ 8). The prohibition would therefore be in force for roughly 9.5 years, and the drafting doesn't say whether that is intended.

4. **§ 2(1)(B):** The "Secretary" is never identified, and the test ("substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks") has no metric, benchmark, or procedure. There is no notice requirement or standard for judicial review, which makes the provision vulnerable to vagueness and nondelegation challenges and leaves companies unable to know in advance whether they are covered.

5. **§ 2(1)(A):** The compute threshold applies to a "single training run," which invites evasion by splitting training into sequential or staged runs that each fall below 10^26. The definition also doesn't address fine-tuning, post-training, distillation, or whether inference compute counts.

6. **§ 2(1)(A):** The 10^26 threshold is fixed in statute, and the Act gives no mechanism for updating it. Algorithmic efficiency gains could let capable systems be trained below the line without any change to the law.

7. **§ 2(1)(A):** "Operations" is not specified as a precision-weighted or otherwise standardized measure, and there is no method for verifying compute used. Developers can self-report, and the Act does not say how the Secretary would check.

8. **§ 2(3):** "Data center" and "advanced AI chips" are undefined. The 50,000-chip threshold also measures hardware rather than compute, so it doesn't track whether a facility is actually training a covered system.

9. **§ 2(3), § 4:** The registry covers only facilities above 50,000 chips. A developer could train a covered system at a facility below that count, or split a run across several sites each under the threshold, and never be reported.

10. **§ 2(2):** The "U.S. person" definition covers citizens and state-chartered corporations only. It does not clearly cover LLCs, partnerships, or foreign-organized subsidiaries of U.S. firms, and "State" is undefined, so it's unclear whether D.C. and territories are included.

11. **§ 3(a):** "Train," "develop," and "deploy" are undefined. The Act doesn't say whether API access, open-weight release, hosting, or providing compute count as deployment, leaving substantial room for dispute over what is prohibited.

12. **§ 3(a):** The prohibition reaches "any person in the United States" and "any U.S. person anywhere," but not foreign-organized entities operating abroad. That gap is left to the international agreement in § 6, which has no enforcement consequence (see item 24).

13. **§ 3(b):** The grandfather clause protects any training that "began" before enactment, but "began" is undefined. It also doesn't address deployment of grandfathered systems, which appears to be allowed indefinitely, and it could cover training started during the 180-day window before the prohibition takes effect.

14. **§ 3(c):** The "safety evaluation" exemption is undefined and has no good-faith requirement, no limit on scope, and no disclosure duty. It doesn't say whether evaluation can include deployment to users, and a developer with mixed purposes could likely invoke it.

15. **§ 4(a):** The Act sets no deadline for initial registration, and the registration duty carries no penalty because § 5 penalizes only violations of § 3.

16. **§ 4(b):** "Computing workloads" is undefined, and the report need not say whether any workload is a covered AI system. The operator's certification has no stated false-statement penalty, and there is no confidentiality protection for proprietary information in the reports.

17. **§ 4 (general):** The registry has no verification or inspection authority, and nothing in the Act says what Commerce must do with the reports. Without those provisions, the registry may be a reporting exercise with no enforcement behind it.

18. **§ 5(a):** It is unclear whether "$1,000,000 for each day" means per model, per deployment, per user, or per calendar day of a continuing violation. The Act also doesn't name the enforcing agency or authorize injunctions, so a company could continue operating while contesting penalties.

19. **§ 5 (general):** The Act gives no authority to seek injunctions, shutdowns, or seizure of compute. The civil and criminal penalties are the only remedies, and they may be slow to stop an ongoing training run.

20. **§ 5(b):** "Willfully" is undefined, and the Act says nothing about corporate criminal liability or aiding and abetting. It also doesn't address whether cloud providers or financiers of covered systems can be liable.

21. **§ 5 (general):** The Act specifies no limitations period for civil penalties, so the general federal five-year period under 28 U.S.C. § 2462 would likely apply. Drafters should decide whether that's intended.

22. **§ 6:** "Shall seek to negotiate" is a hortatory duty with no reporting requirement and no consequence for failure. "Equivalent" is undefined, so even a successful agreement might not match the domestic standard.

23. **§ 7(a):** The NASEM report has no deadline, no funding, and no requirement that Congress act on it. The standard "can be developed safely" is undefined, and the Act doesn't say how a joint resolution would move through presentment and veto.

24. **§ 6 and § 7 (general):** The Act's extraterritorial reach and long-term duration both depend on future action, either an international agreement or a congressional finding, and neither is guaranteed. The Act doesn't say what happens if those actions don't occur.

25. **Whole Act:** The Act has no severability clause. If a court strikes § 2(1)(B) as vague, the surviving provisions could be challenged as incomplete.

26. **Whole Act:** The Act is silent on preemption of state AI laws and on judicial review. Both will matter for how the prohibition interacts with existing and future state rules and for how quickly challenges can be resolved.

27. **Whole Act:** The Act provides no appropriations or staffing for Commerce to run the registry or make § 2(1)(B) determinations. Without funding, those functions may not be carried out.
