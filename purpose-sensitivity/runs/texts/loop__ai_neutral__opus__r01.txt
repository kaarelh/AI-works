# S. 2817: Loopholes, Ambiguities, and Drafting Problems

Below are the issues I found in the text as pasted (manager's amendment of Oct. 6). They're grouped by section. Some are clear drafting errors; others are judgment calls that sponsors may defend as intentional. I've flagged which is which where it matters for fair framing.

## Sec. 2: Definitions

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A): Key terms are undefined.** "Artificial intelligence," "artificial intelligence model," and "training run" are never defined. The whole compute threshold depends on what counts as one "run" and what counts as an AI model.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A): The "single training run" threshold can be split.** A developer could stay under 10^26 operations per run by dividing training into stages, such as pretraining, continued pretraining, and reinforcement learning post-training, or by merging separately trained models. Nothing aggregates compute across runs or across derivative models.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A): The threshold is fixed, and efficiency gains erode it.** Algorithmic improvements and distillation from larger models let developers reach a given capability with less compute over time. Highly capable models could fall under 10^26, and nothing lets an agency adjust the number. Sponsors may respond that subparagraph (B) is the backstop, but see the next two items.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(B): "The Secretary" is never identified.** The bill doesn't say which Secretary, presumably Commerce, makes capability determinations. That creates real uncertainty about who has authority.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(B): The capability standard is vague and has no process.** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no benchmark, no notice-and-comment procedure, no timing rule, and no judicial review provision. Because the definition carries criminal penalties (Sec. 5(b)), it is a likely target for a void-for-vagueness challenge. It is also unclear whether a determination can apply retroactively to work already done.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(A)–(B): The terminology is inconsistent.** Subparagraph (A) covers a "model" and (B) covers a "system." It's unclear whether a system built on a below-threshold model, such as one with scaffolding, tools, or agents, is covered under (A).

7. **Sec. 2(2): The definition of "U.S. person" is narrow.** It omits lawful permanent residents and arguably excludes LLCs, partnerships, and other non-corporate entities. Entities organized under federal, D.C., or territorial law are also excluded, since none of those is a "State" and the bill doesn't define the term.

8. **Sec. 2(2) with Sec. 3(a): Foreign subsidiaries create an offshore route.** A foreign-incorporated subsidiary of a U.S. company is not a "U.S. person." It could train abroad, outside the reach of Sec. 3(a), as long as no covered activity occurs in the United States.

9. **Sec. 2(3): The facility definition is undefined and easy to avoid.** "Data center" and "advanced AI chips" are undefined. The 50,000-chip threshold could be avoided by spreading hardware across several smaller sites, including through distributed training.

10. **Sec. 2(3): Facility and model thresholds don't line up.** A 10^26 training run can be done with fewer than 50,000 chips over a longer period. Prohibited training could therefore happen at facilities that never have to register.

## Sec. 3: Prohibition

11. **Sec. 3(a): "Develop" and "deploy" are undefined.** "Deploy" could be read to cover any business that uses a covered model through an API, which would be very broad. Alternatively, it may not reach a foreign developer offering a model to U.S. users from abroad. "Develop" could arguably reach preparatory research.

12. **Sec. 3(a): Government application is unclear.** "Person" is undefined. It's unclear whether federal agencies, national labs, or defense contractors working for the government are covered.

13. **Sec. 3(b): The grandfather clause is open-ended.** It covers any system whose training "began" before enactment, with no limit on continued training, fine-tuning, deployment, or derivative models. "Began" is undefined, so developers have an incentive to start nominal runs before enactment.

14. **Sec. 3(b) with Sec. 8: There is a gap between enactment and effective date.** The grandfather date is *enactment*, but the Act takes effect 180 days *later*. Training started in that window is lawful when it begins but not grandfathered, and the bill doesn't say whether continuing it after the effective date is a violation.

15. **Sec. 3(c): The safety-evaluation exemption is undefined and purpose-based.** "Safety evaluation" is undefined, and the bill requires no approval, registration, oversight, or limit on scope. Because the test is the actor's stated purpose, nearly any covered activity could be characterized as safety-related. This is arguably the largest exemption in the bill.

16. **Sec. 3: Nothing addresses foreign-trained models.** The bill doesn't clearly address U.S. persons acquiring, hosting, or fine-tuning covered models trained abroad by foreign entities, apart from the ambiguous "deploy."

## Sec. 4: Registry

17. **Sec. 4: The registry has no penalties.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3. Failing to register, or filing a false certified report, carries no penalty under this Act, though general federal false-statement law (18 U.S.C. § 1001) might apply.

18. **Sec. 4(a): Registration has no deadline or contents.** No registration deadline is set, "operator" is undefined, and nothing specifies what registration must include.

19. **Sec. 4(b): The reporting standard is minimal.** "Describing the computing workloads" sets no level of detail. Commerce gets no authority to audit, inspect, or verify reports, and nothing addresses confidentiality of proprietary information.

20. **Sec. 4: Geographic scope is unclear.** It's unclear whether the registry covers facilities abroad operated by U.S. persons, or foreign-operated facilities in the United States.

## Sec. 5: Penalties

21. **Sec. 5(a) vs. Sec. 8: The dates conflict.** Civil penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the Act takes effect 180 days after enactment. This is a direct internal contradiction, and penalizing conduct before the effective date invites a fair-notice challenge.

22. **Sec. 5: No enforcement agency or procedure.** The bill doesn't say who assesses civil penalties, whether through an administrative process or a civil suit by the Attorney General. It also grants no rulemaking, investigative, or subpoena authority to any agency.

23. **Sec. 5(a): The penalty may be small relative to the activity.** A $1 million-per-day cap may be small compared with the budgets of frontier developers. Some will argue it works as a cost of doing business. This is a policy judgment, so it is best attributed to critics rather than stated as fact.

24. **Sec. 5(b): Criminal liability rests on vague definitions.** Ten-year criminal exposure tied to undefined terms ("develop," "deploy," "safety evaluation," and the Sec. 2(1)(B) standard) raises the stakes of every ambiguity above. It strengthens due-process challenges.

## Sec. 6: International Agreement

25. **Sec. 6: The deadline is ambiguous and the obligation unenforceable.** It's unclear whether the 18 months is a deadline to *begin* seeking negotiations or to *conclude* an agreement. Either way, "shall seek to negotiate" has no consequence if nothing results, and "equivalent" prohibitions is undefined.

26. **Sec. 6: The form of agreement is unspecified.** The bill doesn't say whether this would be a treaty requiring Senate ratification or an executive agreement.

## Sec. 7: Duration

27. **Sec. 7(a): The lifting mechanism is largely symbolic.** A joint resolution must be presented to the President, so it is functionally a new law, which Congress could pass anyway. The bill also doesn't direct or fund the National Academies to produce the required report.

28. **Sec. 7(a) vs. 7(b): The two provisions interact unclearly.** Sec. 7(a) implies the ban lasts until Congress finds safety, but Sec. 7(b) ends the entire Act after 10 years regardless. Companies can simply wait out the sunset.

29. **Sec. 7(b): The sunset clock runs from enactment.** Because the Act takes effect 180 days after enactment, the prohibition is effective for about 9.5 years, not 10.

30. **Sec. 7(b): Pending cases at sunset are unaddressed.** With no savings clause, it's unclear whether prosecutions or penalty actions still pending at sunset survive. The general savings statute (1 U.S.C. § 109) may apply, but courts have differed on how it treats expiring statutes.

## Sec. 8 and Overall Structure

31. **Sec. 8: The 180-day window encourages a race.** Combined with Sec. 3(b), the delay gives developers time to restructure, move work offshore through subsidiaries, or split training runs before enforcement begins.

32. **Missing provisions.** The bill has no severability clause, no preemption clause addressing state AI laws, no whistleblower protections, no general rulemaking authority, and no judicial review procedure. Without a severability clause, a successful challenge to one provision, such as Sec. 2(1)(B), leaves more uncertainty about the rest.

33. **Possible constitutional challenges.** Beyond vagueness, expect arguments that restrictions on developing and publishing model weights implicate the First Amendment, by analogy to the code-as-speech cases. These arguments are contested and untested, so they should be framed that way.
