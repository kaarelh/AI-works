RESPONSE:
**Priority fixes (if the list must be short):**
1. §5 has no penalty for violating §4 (registry and reporting).
2. §3 does not reach foreign developers acting outside the U.S.
3. §7(a) and §7(b) conflict, so the prohibition could expire after 10 years.
4. §3(c) lets any developer label training as "safety evaluation."
5. The Act names no agency with enforcement authority or power to seek injunctions.

**Full list**

1. **§2(1)(A), compute threshold, "single training run":** Developers can evade the threshold by splitting a run into several smaller ones, or by counting only pretraining and not continued training, fine-tuning, or post-training compute. Aggregate compute across related runs and all training stages, and define "training run" and how compute is measured.

2. **§2(1)(A), static threshold:** A fixed 10^26 FLOP line will lose coverage as algorithmic efficiency improves, so a dangerous system could fall below it. Give the Secretary authority to update the threshold by rule, with (B) as a backstop.

3. **§2(1)(B), capability determination:** "Secretary" is undefined, and the Act sets no process, notice, evidentiary standard, or evaluation method for finding that a system "substantially exceed[s] human performance across most cognitive tasks." This invites both vagueness and arbitrary-and-capricious challenges. Specify the agency, procedure, and standard.

4. **§2(1), "system" versus "model":** Clause (A) covers only a "model," while (B) covers any "system." Developers could assemble several sub-threshold models into a scaffolded system that falls under neither clause. Define coverage to include composite systems.

5. **§2(2), "U.S. person":** The definition excludes LLCs, partnerships, and other non-corporate entities, as well as foreign-incorporated subsidiaries of U.S. firms and lawful permanent residents. "State" is also undefined, so it is unclear whether D.C. and the territories count. Cover any entity organized under U.S. federal or state law or with its principal place of business in the U.S., and include controlled affiliates.

6. **§3(a), extraterritorial reach:** The "anywhere" clause binds only U.S. persons, so a foreign-incorporated developer training abroad is outside the prohibition altogether. Extend coverage to any person who offers covered systems in the U.S., uses U.S. compute or chips, or serves U.S. users, and pair it with export-control authority as a backstop.

7. **§3(a), undefined verbs and no facilitation clause:** "Train," "develop," and "deploy" are undefined, and the section does not reach people who supply the means of covered training, such as chips, cloud compute, data, funding, or weights. Add a clause prohibiting knowing provision of material support, with the terms defined.

8. **§3(a), weights and open release:** Nothing prohibits publishing or transferring model weights, and it is unclear whether internal use or release abroad counts as "deployment." Add an express prohibition on releasing or transferring weights of covered systems, and define "deploy."

9. **§3(b), grandfathering:** "Training began" is undefined, and the exemption covers the whole system, including deployment. A long run started before enactment could be deployed indefinitely. Define the start of training, require existing systems to be registered within a deadline, and limit the exemption to training, not deployment.

10. **§3(c), safety-evaluation exemption:** The exemption turns on the developer's stated purpose, with no notice, compute cap, independence requirement, or bar on commercial use. It could cover a full-scale frontier training run. Require advance notice to Commerce, an objective scale limit, independent oversight, and a bar on deploying the resulting system.

11. **§4(a), registration scope:** "Operator" is undefined, and the registration duty covers only facilities above 50,000 "advanced AI chips," a term that is also undefined. Covered training spread across smaller or distributed sites would go unreported. Define the terms, cover distributed and cloud-based training, and set a registration deadline.

12. **§4(b), content of reports:** The quarterly reports describe "computing workloads" in general and do not require identifying covered training runs, their compute totals, or their purpose. The certification also carries no stated penalty for a false statement. Require reporting of each run above the threshold and make knowingly false certifications punishable.

13. **§2(3) and §4 versus §3, orphaned definition:** "Covered facility" appears only in §4, while the prohibition in §3 turns entirely on "covered AI system." The registry therefore has no link to what is prohibited. Tie the reporting duty to the prohibited activity.

14. **§5, no penalty for §4 violations:** Both §5(a) and §5(b) reach only violations of §3, so a company that never registers or reports faces no sanction. Extend civil penalties to §4 violations.

15. **§5(a), no enforcer or enforcement tools:** The Act does not say who brings civil actions, and it gives no authority to seek injunctions, shutdown orders, inspections, subpoenas, or seizure of compute. Designate the enforcing agency and the Attorney General's role, and grant injunctive and inspection powers.

16. **§5(a), penalty size and unit:** It is unclear whether a violation is counted per day or per run, and $1,000,000 per day may be small relative to the cost and revenue of frontier training. Specify the unit of violation and consider per-run penalties, penalties scaled to revenue, or an inflation adjustment.

17. **§5(b), "willfully" and individual liability:** "Willfully" is undefined and sets a high proof bar, and the section does not address whether officers and directors can be held personally liable. Add a knowledge or recklessness standard, attach liability to responsible officers, and add attempt and conspiracy language.

18. **§5(a) versus §8, penalty timing:** Penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the prohibition takes effect 180 days later, so penalties could run before the prohibition does. Align the two dates.

19. **§3(b) and §8, the 180-day gap:** Grandfathering is keyed to enactment, but the prohibition takes effect 180 days later. A training run begun in that window is neither clearly grandfathered nor clearly covered. Key both provisions to the same date and address runs that straddle it.

20. **§6, weak international mandate:** "Shall seek to negotiate" imposes no duty to succeed, requires no report to Congress, and leaves "equivalent" undefined. Require reports at 12 and 18 months, define "equivalent," and consider consequences for non-cooperation, such as trade measures.

21. **§7(a) versus §7(b), conflicting duration provisions:** Section 7(a) keeps the prohibition in force until Congress passes a joint resolution, but §7(b) ends the entire Act after 10 years regardless. Developers could wait out the sunset. Resolve the conflict by deleting the sunset or making it expressly subject to §7(a).

22. **§7(a), "developed safely" and the NASEM report:** "Developed safely" is undefined, no deadline is set for the National Academies report, and no one is charged with requesting it. The joint-resolution route also does not say whether expedited procedures apply or how presentment to the President works. Define the standard, set a report deadline, and specify the resolution procedure.

23. **Missing rulemaking authority:** Nothing authorizes Commerce to issue regulations, define terms, update thresholds, or create registration forms. Add express rulemaking authority.

24. **Missing implementation funding:** The Act creates a registry and enforcement duties without any appropriation. Authorize funding for the responsible agency.

25. **Missing severability, savings, and judicial review clauses:** Without a severability clause, a court striking one provision (for example, §2(1)(B)) could void the whole Act. Add severability, a savings clause, and a clear judicial review provision for capability determinations.

26. **Missing confidentiality and information-sharing terms:** Companies may claim trade-secret protection over the §4 reports, and the Act does not say whether Commerce may share them with other agencies or with Congress. Specify confidentiality protections and authorized disclosures.

27. **Missing whistleblower protection:** Employees are the most likely source of information about covert training, yet the Act offers them no protection against retaliation. Add a whistleblower provision.

28. **Challenge exposure, "develop" and speech:** A broad ban on "develop" could be challenged as restricting publication of research or code, and the undefined terms add to vagueness risk. Clarify that the prohibition regulates training and deployment of covered systems, not publication of research, and note the carve-out in the findings.

**Caveats:** This review is based only on the text you provided. I have not seen the October 6 manager's amendment as a redline, Commerce's existing authorities, or relevant case law on vagueness and extraterritoriality, so Senate legislative counsel should check the proposed fixes before they go to the office. Two items, #3 and #22, involve policy choices about the capability standard and the safety-finding process, and the office may want options rather than a single drafted fix.
