RESPONSE:
# Drafting Problems in S. 2817 (as amended October 6, 2026)

**Sections 2 and 3 (scope)**

1. **§2(1)(A), "by a single vessel":** A mining operation could split its recovery across several vessels, each under the threshold, and still be outside the definition. The text should cover aggregate recovery by one operator, affiliates, or a coordinated fleet.

2. **§2(1)(A), "more than 10,000 metric tons per year":** The annual threshold can be avoided by stockpiling or by shifting activity between years. Disturbance without large-scale recovery (for example, sediment plumes from collector passes) is also not covered. The definition should add a disturbance-based trigger and a cumulative or multi-year test.

3. **§2(1)(A), "other mineral resources":** This phrase is undefined. Cobalt crusts, seafloor massive sulfides, and phosphorites are not clearly covered, and a narrow reading would exclude them.

4. **§2(1)(A), "depths greater than 200 meters":** The depth floor leaves shallower seabed unregulated. Unless there is a documented basis for 200 meters, the line should be tied to ecosystem criteria or removed.

5. **§2(1)(B), "the Secretary":** The Act never identifies which Secretary, or which agency, makes this determination.

6. **§2(1)(B), "poses risks… comparable to":** The determination has no criteria, procedure, public notice, or review standard, and "comparable" is undefined. An operator could lobby for a finding that its activity falls outside the prohibition, and the decision could not be clearly challenged.

7. **§2(1), "commercial":** Beyond the numerical thresholds, the term is undefined. It is unclear whether "commercial" requires sale, profit, or intent, which matters for the exemptions in §3(c).

8. **§2(2), "U.S. person":** The definition excludes foreign subsidiaries of U.S. firms, LLCs and partnerships, and foreign-incorporated entities controlled by U.S. persons. "A State" is also undefined and does not clearly include territories or D.C.

9. **§2(3), "covered vessel":** The 5,000 gross ton threshold is inconsistent with the 10,000-ton trigger in §2(1)(A), since a smaller vessel can still exceed the production threshold. "Equipped for the recovery" is also undefined, so the definition could be avoided by vessel design or modification.

10. **§3(a), "in the United States":** The phrase is undefined and does not clearly reach the EEZ, the continental shelf, or the Area beyond national jurisdiction. Foreign-flagged vessels run by foreign operators outside U.S. jurisdiction are not clearly covered.

11. **§3(a), "engage in":** The prohibition does not reach financing, insurance, equipment supply, vessel chartering, processing, or the purchase or import of minerals. It also lacks attempt or conspiracy language. Without these, the prohibition could be undermined from outside the mining operation itself.

**Section 3 (exemptions)**

12. **§3(b), heading versus text:** The heading says "Existing Operations," but the text exempts "mining conducted under an exploration license." Exploration licenses do not authorize commercial recovery, and the text does not prevent commercial-scale recovery from being carried out under one.

13. **§3(b), no limit or cutoff:** The exemption has no tonnage cap, time limit, or sunset, and "issued before the date of enactment" creates an incentive to issue licenses between introduction and enactment. The cutoff should be tied to the bill's introduction date, and grandfathered activity should be capped and time-limited.

14. **§3(c), "environmental assessment":** This exemption has no cap on tonnage, duration, or sale of recovered minerals, and it requires no pre-approval, public notice, or reporting. "Environmental assessment" is also a NEPA term of art with its own meaning, which creates ambiguity. Commercial mining could be relabeled as assessment without any check.

**Section 4 (registry and reporting)**

15. **§4(a), registration scope:** Only covered vessels (over 5,000 gross tons) must register, so smaller vessels can mine above the production threshold without registering. "Operator" is also undefined, and there is no beneficial-ownership disclosure, so a shell company could operate a vessel while its owner stays hidden.

16. **§4(b), report contents:** The reports need only "describe the recovery activities." They should require tonnage, location, depth, discharge and sediment-plume data, and monitoring results, with a filing deadline, public availability, and coverage of activities exempted under §3(b) and §3(c).

17. **§4, no penalty:** Section 5(a) penalizes only violations of §3, so failing to register, failing to report, or filing a false certified report carries no civil penalty. A penalty for registry and reporting violations and an explicit false-statement provision are needed.

**Section 5 (penalties and enforcement)**

18. **§5(a) versus §8, timing conflict:** Section 5(a) applies to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §3 does not take effect until 180 days later under §8. The text does not make clear whether penalties can apply to conduct during that window.

19. **§5(a), "each day":** The penalty does not define how violations are counted (per day, per vessel, per ton, or per continuing violation), and it does not state an inflation adjustment or a limitations period.

20. **§5, no enforcement machinery:** The Act does not designate an enforcing agency or authorize civil actions, injunctions, cease-and-desist orders, vessel or equipment seizure or forfeiture, or disgorgement of profits from unlawful recovery. Without these, a violator could treat penalties as a cost of doing business.

21. **§5(b), "willfully":** The standard is undefined, and the Act has no responsible-corporate-officer provision. Imprisonment can apply to a "person," which includes corporations, so individual accountability for officers is unclear.

22. **§5 and §2(1)(B), no citizen-suit, whistleblower, or judicial-review provisions:** Nothing allows third parties to sue to enforce the Act, protects employees who report violations, or sets the standard of review for the Secretary's §2(1)(B) determinations.

23. **Missing import and sale prohibition:** Nothing bars importing, selling, or processing minerals recovered in violation of the Act. An import ban is one of the most practical enforcement tools available.

**Sections 6 and 7 (international agreement and duration)**

24. **§6, "shall seek to negotiate":** The deadline requires only that the Secretary seek negotiations, not that an agreement be concluded. There is no reporting requirement to Congress, and "equivalent to those in section 3" is undefined.

25. **§7(a), "serious harm" and the joint resolution:** "Serious harm" is undefined, and it is unclear whether harm is measured by ecosystem, species, or region. The NASEM report has no deadline or trigger, and the joint resolution has no expedited procedure, no presentment or veto clarity, and no limit on scope, so one resolution could lift the prohibition for all commercial mining.

26. **§7(b), sunset (highest priority):** The sunset provides that "this Act shall cease to have effect" after 10 years. This overrides §7(a), so the §3 prohibition would lapse automatically even if no joint resolution has been passed. It should be deleted.

27. **§7(b), sunset clock:** The 10 years run from enactment, not from the effective date in §8, so the prohibition would be in force for only about 9.5 years. If any sunset remains, it should run from the effective date.

**Section 8 (effective date) and missing provisions**

28. **§8, 180-day delay:** The six-month gap, combined with the license cutoff problem in §3(b), creates an incentive to start or expand operations before the prohibition takes effect. An interim moratorium on new licenses, permits, and recovery activity should apply from introduction.

29. **Missing interaction with DSHMRA (1980):** The Act does not amend, repeal, or override the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act, under which NOAA issues exploration licenses and commercial recovery permits. Without a conflict clause, NOAA could keep issuing or renewing permits under the older statute, and pending applications would not be suspended. (Counsel should confirm the current NOAA framework before this is sent.)

30. **Missing severability clause:** If a court strikes one provision, such as the definition of covered vessel or the Secretary determination, the rest of the scheme could fall with it. A severability clause should be added.

31. **Missing rulemaking authority and funding:** The Act gives no authority to issue implementing regulations, sets no deadline for them, and provides no appropriation for enforcement.

32. **Missing definitions:** Terms that need definitions include "Secretary," "State," "operator," "exploration license," "recovery," "seabed," and "environmental assessment."

---

**Top priorities for the October 15 markup:** (1) delete the §7(b) sunset; (2) close the "single vessel" and aggregation gaps in §2(1)(A); (3) limit the §3(b) grandfathering and §3(c) assessment exemptions; (4) add enforcement tools, including an import ban, penalties for registry violations, and a citizen-suit provision; and (5) add a DSHMRA conflict clause and an interim moratorium.

Before we send this, I recommend that CADM counsel check the statutory references (DSHMRA, the penalty provisions, and the NEPA usage) and compare the October 6 manager's amendment against the introduced text. That comparison will show which changes weakened the bill and should be reversed.
