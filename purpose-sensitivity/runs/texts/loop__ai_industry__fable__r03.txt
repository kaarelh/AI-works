RESPONSE:
Here's a full pass through the text. I've grouped items by section and kept the numbering continuous. Items that are mostly "we could keep operating" arguments are mixed with "this is unworkable/defective" arguments; the legal team can sort them, but I've flagged the strongest ones at the end.

**SEC. 2(1)(A) — Compute threshold**

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A): "single training run" is undefined.** Nothing stops a developer from splitting training into multiple runs each below 10^26 (e.g., pretraining in stages from saved checkpoints, continued pretraining, large-scale post-training/RL run separately), merging separately trained models, or training expert modules independently. The threshold is only meaningful if cumulative compute is counted, and the text points the other way.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A): no counting methodology.** The bill doesn't say whether to count synthetic-data generation, RL environment/rollout compute, evaluation passes, sparse vs. dense operations, failed/restarted runs, or how to treat mixed precision. No agency is given rulemaking authority to fill this in, so the threshold is unenforceable without a shared accounting standard and vulnerable to vagueness challenges given the criminal penalty.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A): "integer or floating-point operations" may not reach non-conventional compute.** Analog, optical, neuromorphic, or other architectures whose work isn't naturally expressed as integer/FP operations arguably fall outside the definition.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A): fixed static threshold with no adjustment mechanism.** Algorithmic efficiency gains mean systems trained under 10^26 will match or exceed today's above-threshold systems within a few years, making the threshold both over-inclusive (barring routine systems) and under-inclusive (missing the systems the bill targets). No agency can revise it.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(A): inference-time compute and test-time training are not counted.** A model under the training threshold scaled with heavy inference-time compute, or updated via online/continual learning outside any "training run," is not a covered system under (A).

6. **Sec. 2(1)(A): distillation gap.** A model distilled from a covered system (e.g., one trained by a foreign affiliate, see item 15) using less than 10^26 operations is itself not covered under (A), regardless of capability, unless the Secretary acts under (B).

7. **Sec. 2(1)(A) vs. (B): inconsistent nouns.** (A) defines a covered "system" as a "model"; (B) uses "system." Neither "artificial intelligence model" nor "artificial intelligence system" is defined, and "artificial intelligence" itself is undefined, so it's unclear whether weights alone, a deployed product, an agent scaffold, or a system of multiple models is the regulated object.

**SEC. 2(1)(B) — Capability-based definition**

8. **Sec. 2(1)(B): "the Secretary" is never defined.** The Act names only the Secretary of State (Sec. 6) and the Department of Commerce (Sec. 4). Read literally, the only "Secretary" in the Act is State, which is plainly not intended; as drafted, nobody has authority to make a (B) determination.

9. **Sec. 2(1)(B): no procedure, standard, timeline, notice, or review for determinations.** There's no rulemaking or adjudication framework, no evidentiary standard, no opportunity for the developer to respond, and no judicial-review provision, which invites APA and due-process challenges to any determination.

10. **Sec. 2(1)(B): coverage is purely prospective and discretionary.** A system is not covered until the Secretary actually determines it is; anything trained and deployed before a determination is lawful at the time, and the Secretary is under no obligation to ever make a determination.

11. **Sec. 2(1)(B): logical impossibility for "train."** The Secretary cannot determine that a system "is capable" of exceeding human performance until it exists, so Sec. 3(a)'s prohibition on *training* a (B)-defined system can never attach before training is complete.

12. **Sec. 2(1)(B): "substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" is void-for-vagueness territory.** Which humans (median? expert?), which tasks, what counts as "substantially," and how "most" is measured are all unspecified, yet the standard carries a 10-year criminal penalty. This also raises nondelegation concerns since the Secretary effectively defines the crime.

**SEC. 2(2) — "U.S. person"**

13. **Sec. 2(2): LLCs, partnerships, LLPs, trusts, and other non-corporate entities are excluded.** Only "a corporation organized under the laws of a State" qualifies. Reorganizing the operating entity as a Delaware LLC (or using one for training activity abroad) removes it from the extraterritorial reach of Sec. 3(a).

14. **Sec. 2(2): entities organized under federal law, D.C., or territorial law are excluded.** "State" is undefined and ordinarily doesn't include D.C., Puerto Rico, or other territories absent a definition; nationally chartered or D.C.-organized entities aren't "U.S. persons."

15. **Sec. 2(2): foreign subsidiaries are not U.S. persons.** A wholly owned subsidiary organized abroad is neither a citizen nor a State-law corporation, so training conducted by it outside the U.S. isn't reached by Sec. 3(a)'s extraterritorial clause. There is no control, ownership, or "owned or controlled by" attribution rule.

16. **Sec. 2(2): lawful permanent residents and U.S. nationals are excluded.** Only "citizens" are covered, so non-citizen employees working abroad for a foreign entity are outside the extraterritorial clause.

**SEC. 2(3) — "Covered facility"**

17. **Sec. 2(3): "advanced AI chips," "data center," and "containing" are undefined.** No performance threshold, no reference to an export-control list, no agency to define the terms; operators can't know whether they're covered.

18. **Sec. 2(3): chip-count threshold is trivially avoided and poorly targeted.** Splitting a cluster across multiple facilities of 50,000 chips or fewer, or interconnecting several sites for distributed training, avoids registration; conversely, large non-AI data centers may be swept in. The definition also has no relationship to the 10^26 threshold it ostensibly supports.

**SEC. 3 — Prohibition**

19. **Sec. 3(a): "train," "develop," and "deploy" are undefined.** "Develop" could reach research, publishing, writing code, or data curation (raising First Amendment issues), while "deploy" may or may not cover API hosting, internal use, licensing, open-weight release, or inference on a third party's model.

20. **Sec. 3(a): the verb list omits key conduct.** There is no prohibition on possessing, importing, acquiring, using, operating, selling, distributing, licensing, hosting, or providing compute for a covered system, and no aiding-and-abetting, conspiracy, or facilitation provision. Supplying a cloud cluster to a foreign developer, or running a foreign-developed covered system, is arguably not "deploying" it.

21. **Sec. 3(a): circularity on when the prohibition attaches.** Under (A), a model is not a "covered AI system" until it has been trained past 10^26 operations; the act of training up to that point is therefore training a non-covered system, and the moment it crosses the line it is arguably already trained. The provision needs an "intends to" or "would result in" formulation to work.

22. **Sec. 3(a): "person in the United States" is undefined for entities.** It is unclear whether a Delaware corporation is "in the United States" when its training compute and personnel are abroad, or whether a foreign company is "in the United States" when it serves U.S. customers from offshore servers. The Act also doesn't bar foreign developers from deploying covered systems to U.S. users from abroad, so the bill disadvantages domestic developers without excluding the technology from the U.S. market.

23. **Sec. 3(a): no sovereign or national-security carve-out and no clear application to the government.** "Person" typically excludes the federal government, so agencies are arguably free to proceed, but contractors are not; DoD and the intelligence community could not procure covered systems from any U.S. developer. This is a strong unworkability argument.

24. **Sec. 3(b): grandfather clause requires only that training "began" before enactment.** A run started the day before enactment may continue indefinitely with no compute cap, and nothing in the text prevents continued pretraining, fine-tuning, or further scaling of a grandfathered system as the same "system." Combined with item 1, a pre-enactment checkpoint can anchor all future work.

25. **Sec. 3(b): grandfathering is keyed to enactment, but the Act takes effect 180 days later (Sec. 8).** Training begun in the 180-day window is neither grandfathered nor prohibited while it occurs, and the text doesn't say whether it becomes unlawful on the effective date. The two dates should be the same.

26. **Sec. 3(b): deployment of grandfathered systems is permanently exempt.** The exemption applies to the whole of 3(a), including "deploy," so any covered system in existence at enactment can be deployed, licensed, and scaled without limit.

27. **Sec. 3(c): the safety-evaluation exception swallows the rule.** "Safety evaluation" is undefined, the test is purpose-based with no requirement that it be the sole or primary purpose, there is no scale limit, no reporting, no licensing, and it covers "activities" generally including training and deployment. A frontier system cannot be safety-evaluated without first being trained, so any training run can be framed as serving evaluation.

28. **Sec. 3(c): no oversight of the exception.** No agency approves, audits, or receives notice of safety-evaluation activity, so the exception is self-executing and unreviewable.

**SEC. 4 — Registry**

29. **Sec. 4(a): "operator" undefined; no registration deadline.** Colocation landlord vs. tenant vs. cloud customer is unresolved, and nothing says when registration is due.

30. **Sec. 4: no penalty for failure to register or report.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3, so Sec. 4 has no enforcement mechanism, no inspection or subpoena authority, and no verification of self-certified reports (beyond generic 18 U.S.C. § 1001 exposure).

31. **Sec. 4(b): report content is unspecified and unprotected.** "Describing the computing workloads" sets no content standard (no FLOP totals, no model identification), and there's no confidentiality or trade-secret protection or FOIA exemption, which is both a competitive problem and a point of unworkability.

32. **Sec. 4: registry is disconnected from the prohibition.** Facility operators report workloads, but trainers using rented compute across multiple facilities report nothing, and Commerce has no authority to use the reports to enforce Sec. 3.

**SEC. 5 — Penalties**

33. **Sec. 5(a): penalties reach back to enactment while the Act takes effect 180 days later (Sec. 8).** The clause "for any violation occurring on or after the date of enactment" purports to penalize conduct before there was any operative prohibition, an internal conflict with ex post facto problems for 5(b).

34. **Sec. 5: no enforcement agency, procedure, or injunctive remedy.** No one is authorized to assess civil penalties or bring suit, there's no administrative process, and there's no injunction, seizure, forfeiture, or shutdown authority; the only tools are after-the-fact fines.

35. **Sec. 5(a): "each day on which the violation occurs" is ambiguous.** It's unclear whether a completed training run is one violation or continuing, whether deployment is continuing, and how multiple systems are counted. The $1M/day cap is also small relative to frontier training budgets, undercutting deterrence.

36. **Sec. 5(b): "willfully" plus vagueness.** Willfulness generally requires knowledge that conduct is unlawful; given the undefined terms in Secs. 2 and 3 and the discretionary (B) determination, criminal liability will be very hard to establish and highly vulnerable to as-applied challenges.

**SEC. 6 — International agreement**

37. **Sec. 6: hortatory with no consequence.** "Shall seek to negotiate" imposes no result, and the 18-month deadline is grammatically ambiguous (begin seeking vs. conclude). Nothing in the Act conditions or adjusts the domestic prohibition on whether other nations adopt equivalent rules, so the U.S. is bound unilaterally regardless of outcome.

**SEC. 7 — Duration**

38. **Sec. 7(a) vs. 7(b): direct conflict.** (a) says the prohibition remains until Congress acts by joint resolution; (b) terminates the entire Act after 10 years. Either the sunset controls (and (a) is surplus) or (a) controls (and (b) is meaningless); the text gives no priority rule.

39. **Sec. 7(a): no mechanism to produce the triggering report.** The National Academies are not directed, funded, or given a deadline to produce a report, so the lifting mechanism may never activate; "can be developed safely" is also an undefined standard.

40. **Sec. 7(a): purported entrenchment is unenforceable.** One Congress cannot bind a future Congress to a specific repeal procedure; the joint-resolution requirement has no legal force beyond ordinary legislation.

41. **Sec. 7(b): sunset terminates the registry and all obligations too.** "This Act shall cease to have effect" removes Sec. 4 along with Sec. 3, and the Act doesn't address what happens to a (B) determination, pending penalties, or runs in progress at sunset.

**Cross-cutting**

42. **No rulemaking or administering agency for the prohibition.** Commerce only runs the registry; no agency is empowered to define terms, set compute-accounting rules, update thresholds, or enforce Sec. 3.

43. **No severability clause.** If any provision (most likely 2(1)(B) or 3(c)) is struck, the whole Act is at risk.

44. **No preemption or relationship-to-state-law provision**, no appropriation, no whistleblower or inspection authority, and no transition rules for in-progress work at the effective date.

**Strongest points to lead with**

For "we could continue operating as written": items 1, 13/15 (entity structure), 24/26 (grandfathering), and 27 (safety-evaluation exception) are each independently sufficient on the text. For "unworkable as drafted": items 8 (undefined Secretary), 25/33 (enactment vs. effective date conflicts), 38 (Sec. 7 internal conflict), 23 (no national-security carve-out), and 30 (registry has no penalty) are the clearest drafting failures and the hardest for proponents to defend without substantial redrafting before Thursday.

One caution for the legal team: several of the "keep operating" readings (items 21, 24, 27 especially) rest on literal text that a court could read purposively, and relying on them under a statute carrying criminal penalties carries real risk. They're stronger as markup arguments that the bill doesn't do what its sponsors claim than as a compliance strategy.
