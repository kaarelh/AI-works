RESPONSE:
Here's a section-by-section list. I've put the most consequential items first within each section; at the end I flag the handful I'd lead with in the letter.

**Sec. 2(1)(A) — compute threshold**

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "in a single training run."** This is the biggest hole in the definition: a developer can split pretraining into several runs below 10^26, or reach superintelligent capability through continued pretraining, fine-tuning, RL post-training, or distillation, each counted separately. Add an aggregation rule counting all compute used to produce a model and its derivatives.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — static threshold.** 10^26 is hard-coded with no authority to lower it as algorithmic efficiency improves, so the bill's coverage shrinks every year. Give the Secretary authority (and a duty) to revise the threshold downward, and consider pairing compute with a capability benchmark.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "model" vs. "system."** Paragraph (A) covers a "model," (B) and section 3 cover a "system," and neither term is defined. An agent built from several sub-threshold models, scaffolding, or heavy test-time compute could argue it is neither a covered "model" nor a determined "system."

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — counting rule.** "Quantity of computing power" is technically a rate, and "integer or floating-point operations" doesn't say whether to count at any precision, how to treat sparsity or mixture-of-experts, or who measures. Specify the counting methodology (or delegate it) so the threshold is enforceable and not litigated.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — no anti-circumvention clause.** Nothing prohibits structuring activity to evade the threshold (e.g., distributing a run across multiple jurisdictions or entities). Add a standard anti-evasion provision covering any training conducted "with the purpose or effect" of avoiding the definition.

**Sec. 2(1)(B) — capability prong**

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — "the Secretary" is undefined.** The bill never says which Secretary (Commerce? State? Energy?). As drafted, no one has the authority to make the determination.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — determination comes too late.** A system only becomes "covered" after the Secretary determines it is already capable of exceeding human performance, by which point it has been trained. The prong does nothing to stop training; at best it reaches deployment.

8. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — no procedure, standard, or review.** There is no process, deadline, evidentiary standard, or judicial review for a determination, and "most cognitive tasks" is undefined. Tying criminal liability to an undefined administrative finding invites vagueness, due-process, and nondelegation challenges.

**Sec. 2(2) — U.S. person**

9. **Sec. 2(2) — entity coverage is far too narrow.** Only citizens and "corporations organized under the laws of a State" are U.S. persons; this omits LLCs, partnerships, trusts, nonprofits, entities organized in D.C. or territories, lawful permanent residents, and, critically, foreign subsidiaries of U.S. companies. A lab could reincorporate in Ireland or Singapore or run training through a Cayman subsidiary and fall outside extraterritorial reach.

10. **Sec. 2(2) — no control/ownership test.** Add coverage for any entity owned or controlled by a U.S. person and for persons acting on their behalf, as in sanctions and export-control statutes.

**Sec. 2(3) — covered facility**

11. **Sec. 2(3) — "advanced AI chips" and "data center" undefined.** Operators can argue their chips aren't "advanced" or their campus isn't one "data center." Define chips by performance threshold (e.g., by reference to the ECCN 3A090 specifications) and define facility to include contiguous or commonly controlled sites.

12. **Sec. 2(3) — 50,000-chip threshold is both evadable and mismatched to the prohibition.** Splitting a campus into buildings of 49,000 chips avoids registration entirely, and a 10^26 run is feasible on roughly 10,000 current-generation chips over several months, so most facilities capable of violating section 3 never have to register.

**Sec. 3 — prohibition**

13. **Sec. 3(b) — the grandfather clause swallows the ban.** Any system whose training "began" before enactment is exempt forever, including from the capability prong, and can be continued, scaled, fine-tuned, and deployed without limit. Every lab will initiate (or claim to have initiated) its next frontier run before signing; at minimum, limit the exemption to compute already expended, bar further training and deployment, and require certification of pre-enactment status.

14. **Sec. 3(b) — "training began" is manipulable and unverifiable.** A nominal run started on day one can be paused, re-scoped, and resumed indefinitely. If any grandfather clause survives, it should turn on a verifiable event (e.g., the model being registered and its compute total frozen).

15. **Sec. 3(c) — safety-evaluation exemption is unbounded.** It is purpose-based, undefined, and has no cap, licensing, or oversight, so a lab can characterize any training run as being "for the purpose of" evaluating safety; you cannot evaluate a model you have not trained. Either strike it or restrict it to evaluation of already-existing systems by a government-authorized evaluator, with no training above threshold.

16. **Sec. 3(a) — prohibited verbs are too narrow.** "Train, develop, or deploy" omits possessing, operating, using, transferring or exporting weights, providing compute or funding, attempting, conspiring, and aiding and abetting. Cloud providers renting 100,000 GPUs to a foreign lab for a 10^26 run arguably violate nothing.

17. **Sec. 3(a) — "develop" and "deploy" undefined.** It is unclear whether internal use, API access, open-weight release, or serving U.S. users from abroad counts as deployment. Define deployment to include making the system available for use by anyone, directly or indirectly, inside or outside the United States.

18. **Sec. 3(a) — foreign developers serving U.S. users.** A foreign corporation training abroad and offering the system to U.S. customers is not "in the United States" and not a "U.S. person." Add an import/access prohibition so the ban does not simply shift frontier development offshore.

19. **Sec. 3(a) — "person" likely excludes the federal government.** Absent a clear statement, "person" does not include the United States, so federal agencies and national labs may be outside the ban (contractors would be covered, creating an odd asymmetry). The committee should decide this deliberately and say so expressly either way.

**Sec. 4 — registry**

20. **Sec. 4 — no penalty for noncompliance.** Section 5 only penalizes violations of section 3, so failing to register, filing late, or filing a false quarterly report carries no consequence. Add civil and criminal penalties and make certifications subject to 18 U.S.C. § 1001.

21. **Sec. 4 — no verification, inspection, or audit authority.** Commerce receives self-certified reports but has no power to inspect facilities, subpoena records, or verify chip counts or workloads. Without this, the registry cannot support enforcement of section 3.

22. **Sec. 4 — registry targets facilities, not training.** Nothing requires a developer to report a planned or ongoing run above the threshold, report compute totals per model, or identify tenants. Add a developer-side notification requirement for any run projected to exceed, say, 10^25.

23. **Sec. 4 — "operator" undefined.** In colocation arrangements it is unclear whether the landlord or the tenant must register and report, letting each point to the other.

24. **Sec. 4 — no rulemaking authority.** Commerce is given duties but no authority to issue regulations specifying report contents, format, or timing, which will slow implementation and invite challenges.

**Sec. 5 — penalties**

25. **Sec. 5(a) — $1,000,000 per day is immaterial to frontier labs.** For a company spending billions on a single run, this is a cost of doing business, not a deterrent. Scale penalties to a percentage of global revenue or a multiple of the compute cost, and add forfeiture of models, weights, and hardware.

26. **Sec. 5(a) — penalty date conflicts with the effective date.** Penalties attach to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but under section 8 the prohibition isn't in effect until 180 days later, so nothing can be a "violation" in that window. The inconsistency invites a due-process or ex post facto challenge; conform the two.

27. **Sec. 5 — no enforcement authority assigned.** The bill does not say who brings civil actions (Commerce? DOJ?), nor does it authorize injunctions, cease-and-desist orders, emergency seizure of hardware, or a private right of action. Enforcement would default to general DOJ authority with no ability to stop a run in progress.

28. **Sec. 5(b) — "willfully" plus vague definitions.** Willfulness requires proof the defendant knew the conduct was unlawful; combined with the undefined terms above, criminal prosecution will be nearly impossible. Consider a "knowingly" standard tied to clearly defined conduct.

29. **Sec. 5(b) — fine "under title 18" is capped low for organizations.** Under 18 U.S.C. § 3571 the default corporate maximum is $500,000 (or twice gain/loss), and imprisonment is meaningless for a corporation. Add individual liability for officers and directors who authorize or direct a violation, and a specific corporate fine.

30. **Sec. 5 — no statute of limitations, no treatment of continuing violations.** Clarify that each system and each day is a separate violation and set an appropriate limitations period.

**Sec. 6 — international agreement**

31. **Sec. 6 — purely hortatory.** "Seek to negotiate" carries no consequence, reporting duty, or verification framework, and "equivalent" is undefined. At minimum require periodic reports to Congress and a negotiating mandate that includes verification and chip-tracking.

**Sec. 7 — duration**

32. **Sec. 7(a)/(b) — internally contradictory.** Subsection (a) says the ban lasts until Congress affirmatively lifts it; (b) sunsets the entire Act, registry included, after 10 years regardless. The sunset controls as drafted, telling labs they only need to wait, and it undercuts the "prohibition" framing.

33. **Sec. 7(a) — lifting standard is undefined.** "Can be developed safely" has no criteria, the National Academies report has no scope or trigger, and a single joint resolution lifts coverage for all systems at once. Specify the finding required and allow partial or conditional lifting.

**Sec. 8 and general drafting**

34. **Sec. 8 — 180-day delay compounds the grandfather problem.** Combined with sec. 3(b) and sec. 5(a), it creates a confusing window in which runs started after enactment but before the effective date are not yet unlawful, yet not grandfathered either. The delay should apply only to the registry, with the prohibition effective immediately.

35. **No severability clause.** If the capability prong or the extraterritorial provision is struck, a court could invalidate the entire Act. Add a standard severability section.

36. **No preemption or savings clause.** The bill is silent on state AI laws, leaving it unclear whether it preempts stricter state measures; MIRI should ask for an express non-preemption of more stringent state law.

37. **No whistleblower protections.** Enforcement will depend heavily on insiders; the bill provides no protection or incentive for employees who report violations.

**Where I'd lead:** items 13 and 15 (grandfather and safety-evaluation exemptions, which together let every current lab continue uninterrupted), item 1 (single-run aggregation), item 9 (foreign subsidiaries), items 25–27 (penalties and enforcement), and item 32 (the sunset). These are the provisions that most directly determine whether the Act stops anything.

One caveat: this is a drafting review, not a constitutional analysis. Items 8, 26, and 35 touch on litigation risk, and if you want, I can draft proposed replacement text for any of the sections above in a format the office can drop into a second-degree amendment.
