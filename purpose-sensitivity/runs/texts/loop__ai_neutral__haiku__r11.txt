# Drafting Problems and Loopholes in S. 2817 (Manager's Amendment, Oct. 6, 2026)

These observations are based only on the text you provided. Some terms may be defined in the committee report, in Title 1 of the U.S. Code (general definitions), or in later amendments, so check those before publishing.

**Scope and definitions**

1. **§2(1)(A)**: "Computing power" is not defined, and the bill gives no method for counting operations (precision level, mixed-precision training, whether synthetic data generation or inference counts). Operators could dispute whether a model crosses 10^26 operations.

2. **§2(1)(A)**: The phrase "single training run" invites splitting a project into sequential or parallel segments, or into continued pretraining and fine-tuning phases, each below the threshold.

3. **§2(1)(A)**: The fixed threshold has no mechanism for the Secretary or Congress to update it as algorithms and hardware become more efficient. A model with the same capabilities could be trained with less compute and fall outside the definition.

4. **§2(1)(B)**: "The Secretary" is not identified. The bill never says which Secretary makes the determinations.

5. **§2(1)(B)**: "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no metric, no definition of "most," no evaluation procedure, and no notice or hearing requirement. This is the provision most open to a claim that the determination is arbitrary.

6. **§2(1)(B)**: The bill does not say whether a determination applies to systems already in development or deployed, or how it interacts with the grandfather clause in §3(b).

7. **§2(2)**: "U.S. person" covers citizens and corporations organized under state law, but says nothing about LLCs, partnerships, or permanent residents abroad. "State" is also undefined, so it is unclear whether D.C. and the territories are included.

8. **§2(2) and §3(a)**: A foreign-incorporated parent with a U.S.-organized subsidiary is partly covered, but the parent's training conducted abroad may not be. The bill does not clearly address corporate structuring around the definition.

9. **§2(3)**: "Advanced AI chips" is undefined, and the bill sets no threshold for what counts as "advanced." "Data center" and "containing" are also undefined, leaving open whether leased, cloud-hosted, or shared capacity counts toward the 50,000-chip threshold.

**Prohibition (§3)**

10. **§3(a)**: "Develop" and "deploy" are undefined. It is unclear whether internal research, API access, or publishing open weights falls under "deploy."

11. **§3(a)**: The prohibition reaches persons who train or deploy covered systems, but not those who supply chips, cloud compute, or training services to others. There is no aiding-and-abetting or compute-provider obligation.

12. **§3(a)**: The bill reaches "any person in the United States" and "any U.S. person anywhere." Foreign entities training abroad are reached only through the non-binding international provision in §6, and it is unclear whether a foreign company using U.S. cloud infrastructure falls under "in the United States."

13. **§3(b)**: The grandfather clause depends on when "training began," a term the bill does not define. It does not require disclosure or registration of grandfathered systems and does not limit continuation, expansion, or retraining after enactment.

14. **§3(b) and §8**: The grandfather clause is keyed to the date of enactment, but the prohibition takes effect 180 days later. A training run started during that 180-day window is not grandfathered, yet is not yet prohibited either, and the bill does not say how the two periods interact. This gap creates an incentive to start runs before the prohibition takes effect.

15. **§3(c)**: The safety-evaluation exemption has no definition, no limit on scale, and no documentation or reporting requirement. Because it turns on the actor's "purpose," it is hard to prove or disprove, and a full training run could be labeled an evaluation.

16. **§3(c)**: The exemption does not say whether a system produced during safety evaluation may later be deployed.

**Registry (§4)**

17. **§4(a)**: "Operator" is undefined, which matters where ownership and operation are split, as with leased or cloud facilities. The bill also sets no registration deadline and specifies no contents for the registration.

18. **§4 (overall)**: The registry has no enforcement mechanism. The penalties in §5 apply only to violations of §3, so failing to register or to file quarterly reports carries no stated penalty.

19. **§4(b)**: "Computing workloads" is undefined, and the report need not say whether any covered AI system was trained. The certification has no stated penalty for a false statement beyond general federal law.

20. **§4 (threshold)**: The 50,000-chip threshold applies per facility. Distributed training across several sites, each below the threshold, or smaller clusters running for longer, could fall outside the registry even when a covered training run is occurring.

21. **§4 (confidentiality)**: The bill has no provision on protecting trade secrets, no limit on how Commerce may use or share the reports, and no public disclosure requirement. This affects both company willingness to comply and public oversight.

**Penalties (§5)**

22. **§5(a)**: Civil penalties apply to violations "occurring on or after the date of enactment," but §8 delays the prohibition 180 days. The text could be read to penalize conduct before the prohibition is in force.

23. **§5(a)**: The bill names no enforcing agency, does not say whether DOJ or Commerce brings suit, provides no injunctive authority, and sets no administrative penalty process. Collecting penalties from foreign entities is also not addressed.

24. **§5(a)**: "Each day on which the violation occurs" is not applied to a continuing training run. It is unclear whether a multi-month run counts as one violation or one per day, and whether each system or facility counts separately. Separately, the $1 million daily maximum may be small relative to large compute budgets; that is a deterrence question for analysts to weigh, not a drafting error.

25. **§5(b)**: "Willfully" is undefined. Given the undefined thresholds and terms elsewhere, a defendant could argue lack of knowledge that the system was covered. The provision also has no attempt or conspiracy language.

26. **§5 (limitations)**: The bill has no statute-of-limitations provision. The general federal default for civil penalties (28 U.S.C. §2462, five years) would likely apply, but staff should confirm this with counsel.

**International, duration, and effective date (§§6–8)**

27. **§6**: "Shall seek to negotiate" creates no binding obligation. There is no reporting requirement, no deadline for a report, no consequence if negotiations fail, and "equivalent" is undefined.

28. **§7(a)**: The NASEM report is not required by any deadline, and "developed safely" has no defined standard. The bill also does not say whether a joint resolution is presented to the President, which affects whether a veto is possible.

29. **§7(a) and §7(b)**: The two subsections conflict. Under §7(a), the prohibition lasts until Congress acts by joint resolution, but §7(b) ends the entire Act 10 years after enactment regardless of any finding. The bill does not say which controls.

30. **§7(b) and §8**: Because the Act takes effect 180 days after enactment, the 10-year sunset leaves a little under 9.5 years of operation. This is minor but worth clarifying.

**Cross-cutting gaps**

31. **Missing definitions**: The bill defines only four terms. "Secretary," "person," "State," "develop," "deploy," "operator," "training run," "computing power," "advanced AI chips," and "safety evaluation" are undefined, and "person" and "State" are not defined in this bill. General definitions in Title 1 may fill some gaps, but the operative terms still need defining.

32. **No rulemaking authority**: Commerce has no explicit power to issue regulations defining thresholds, terms, or reporting formats, so the ambiguities above cannot be resolved administratively.

33. **No severability clause**: If a court strikes §2(1)(B) or §3(c), it is unclear whether the rest of the Act survives.

34. **No judicial review, funding, or whistleblower provisions**: The bill does not authorize funding for the registry or enforcement, does not establish a whistleblower channel, and does not address judicial review.

35. **Vagueness challenges**: Several undefined terms appear in the prohibition and the criminal provision. Undefined criminal terms can invite due process vagueness challenges, which should be framed in the explainer as a litigation risk identified by observers rather than as a settled legal conclusion.

**Notes for the explainer**

- Several of these points may be addressed in the committee report or in sponsor statements at the markup. Check for those before publishing and attribute the reading to the text.
- The constitutional and vagueness points should be reviewed by a legal expert before publication, and attributed to named sources.
- For balance, consider asking sponsors or the committee for their intent on the §3(c) exemption, the §3(b)/§8 timing gap, and the §4 enforcement gap, since these are the points most likely to draw a response.
