# Drafting Issues in S. 2817 (Manager's Amendment, Oct. 6, 2026)

I worked from the text you provided. I haven't seen the manager's amendment redline or the committee report, so some points may already be addressed there. Items are grouped by section and numbered continuously.

**Section 2 (Definitions)**

1. **§2(1)(A), fleet splitting.** The 10,000-metric-ton threshold applies "by a single vessel." An operator could spread production across several vessels, each under the threshold, and stay outside the definition.
2. **§2(1)(A), measurement.** "Per year" doesn't specify wet or dry tonnage, whether gangue or sediment counts, or whether tonnage is measured at recovery or at delivery. There is also no rule for ramp-up years or averaging across years.
3. **§2(1)(A), "recovery" undefined.** It's unclear whether lifting material to the surface, collecting test samples, or pilot collection counts as "recovery." This matters for both the threshold and §3(c).
4. **§2(1)(A), "commercial" not defined apart from the volume test.** Revenue-generating activity below 10,000 tons per year appears to fall outside the definition.
5. **§2(1)(A), depth floor.** The 200-meter limit excludes shallower seabed activity. The bill also doesn't say whether depth is measured at the collection site, at the vessel, or along the water column.
6. **§2(1)(B), undefined "Secretary."** The bill doesn't say which Secretary (Commerce, Interior, or State) makes the determination. It also gives no standard for "comparable" risk, no notice or comment process, and no judicial review standard.
7. **§2(1)(B), vagueness and delegation.** A criminal prohibition whose scope depends on an agency's later determination invites vagueness and nondelegation challenges. It also lets scope expand after enactment without a vote of Congress.
8. **§2(2), "U.S. person" is narrow.** It covers citizens and corporations organized under state law, but not LLCs, partnerships, trusts, or foreign-incorporated subsidiaries. "State" is also undefined, so it's unclear whether D.C. and the territories count.
9. **Undefined "person."** The Act doesn't define "person," so the default in 1 U.S.C. §1 applies. Whether foreign state-owned sponsors or government instrumentalities are covered is unresolved.
10. **§2(3), "covered vessel" doesn't match the prohibition.** A vessel of 5,000 gross tons or less can mine in violation of §3 yet fall outside the registry and reporting requirements in §4.
11. **§2(3), "vessel" and "equipped for" undefined.** Modern systems often pair a surface vessel with a seabed collector that may not be a "vessel." It's also unclear whether "equipped for" means installed, present on board, or intended, and how chartered or leased vessels are treated.

**Section 3 (Prohibition)**

12. **§3(a), "engage in" undefined.** The prohibition doesn't clearly reach financing, contracting, equipment supply, processing, or other support activity.
13. **§3(a), no import or sale ban.** Nothing bars importing, processing, or selling minerals recovered abroad, so the trade side of the supply chain is unaddressed.
14. **§3(a), extraterritorial reach is limited.** The Act reaches "any person in the United States" and "U.S. persons anywhere," but not non-U.S. persons operating abroad. Combined with the narrow "U.S. person" definition, this leaves significant room for operations conducted through non-U.S. entities or foreign-flagged vessels.
15. **§3(a), "in the United States" for persons.** It's unclear whether a person is "in the United States" based on where they are located or where they direct operations. This matters for a U.S.-based parent overseeing a foreign subsidiary.
16. **§3(a), no permitting pathway.** The prohibition is absolute, apart from the exemptions in §3(b) and §3(c), with no conditional or permit route. This supports an unworkability argument but also means the bill offers no compliance path.
17. **§3(b), exemption has no scale limit.** It exempts "mining conducted under an exploration license" without limiting volume, duration, or purpose. It also uses "mining" rather than the defined term "commercial deep seabed mining," so the exemption may cover commercial-scale activity. This is the largest loophole in the bill.
18. **§3(b), "exploration license" undefined.** The bill doesn't say whether this means NOAA licenses under the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (DSHMRA), International Seabed Authority contracts, or sponsoring-state licenses. *(Counsel should verify the DSHMRA references.)*
19. **§3(b), renewals and amendments.** The exemption covers licenses "issued before the date of enactment," but says nothing about renewals, extensions, expanded areas, amendments, or transfers. Those changes may be argued to fall under the exemption.
20. **§3(b) and §8, mismatched dates.** The exemption is keyed to enactment, but the prohibition takes effect 180 days later. Licenses issued during that window are outside the exemption, and it's unclear whether they're covered by §3(a) when it takes effect.
21. **§3(c), "environmental assessment" undefined.** The exemption has no limits on volume, duration, sample size, or certification. Because it turns on the activity's "purpose," it's largely subjective, and commercial recovery could be recast as assessment.
22. **§3(c), no reporting or verification.** Activities under §3(c) aren't reported, and §4 covers only "recovery activities" by covered vessels. Nothing lets NOAA or anyone else verify the stated purpose.

**Section 4 (Registry)**

23. **§4, no penalty for noncompliance.** §5 penalizes only violations of §3, so failing to register or file a report carries no penalty. As drafted, §4 is unenforceable.
24. **§4, "operator" undefined.** Owners, charterers, operators, and contractors may each claim or disclaim the obligation.
25. **§4(b), no content or deadline requirements.** The reports don't specify tonnage, location, depth, or method. They also set no NOAA review deadline, public disclosure rule, or confidentiality rule, and "certified" is undefined.
26. **§4, no registration consequences.** The Act doesn't say whether registration is a precondition to operating, what happens if NOAA refuses registration, or whether NOAA can inspect vessels.

**Section 5 (Penalties)**

27. **§5(a), no enforcement mechanism.** The civil penalty doesn't name an enforcing agency, specify whether penalties are assessed administratively or through a DOJ action, or set a limitations period.
28. **§5(a), "day" undefined.** It's unclear how to count days for a continuing violation, or whether the penalty runs per vessel, per project, or per operator.
29. **§5(a) and §8, inconsistent effective dates.** Penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §3 takes effect 180 days later under §8.
30. **§5(b), "willfully" undefined.** The Act has no "knowingly" tier and no provision for aiding and abetting, conspiracy, or attempt.
31. **§5, no forfeiture or injunctive authority.** The Act doesn't provide for forfeiture of vessels, equipment, or minerals, and doesn't expressly authorize injunctions.

**Section 6 (International Agreement)**

32. **§6, "seek to negotiate" creates no obligation.** Nothing requires an agreement to be concluded, and nothing ties the domestic prohibition to the outcome. "Equivalent to" is also undefined, and the bill doesn't address Senate treaty review or whether an executive agreement would suffice.

**Section 7 (Duration)**

33. **§7(a) and §7(b), direct conflict.** §7(a) keeps the prohibition in effect until a joint resolution is enacted. §7(b) ends the entire Act after 10 years, regardless. The bill doesn't say which controls, and in practice §7(b) caps the prohibition at 10 years.
34. **§7(a), "serious harm" and the NAS report.** "Serious harm" is undefined, and the bill sets no standard of proof. It also sets no deadline for the National Academies report and doesn't require Congress to act, so the prohibition could persist indefinitely.
35. **§7(a), mechanics of the joint resolution.** The bill doesn't say whether a joint resolution lifts the prohibition automatically, requires further action, or must be presented to the President.
36. **§7(b), no savings clause.** The Act doesn't say whether violations before the sunset remain prosecutable or whether §4 obligations end. The general savings statute (1 U.S.C. §109) may help, but its application to a sunset is uncertain.

**General**

37. **No relationship to existing law.** The Act doesn't say whether it repeals, amends, or overrides DSHMRA or NOAA's exploration and commercial recovery regime. Existing licenses and permits are therefore left in an ambiguous position, which supports arguments in either direction.
38. **No severability clause.** If a court strikes a provision, such as the delegation in §2(1)(B), it's unclear how much of the Act falls with it.
39. **Key terms undefined.** The Act doesn't define "vessel," "exploration license," "environmental assessment," "recovery," "operator," "State," or "willfully," or specify which "Secretary" is meant throughout.

**Notes for the legal team**

- **Strongest arguments:** Items 17, 21, 23, 33, and 37 are the clearest drafting defects. Items 23 and 33 are internal inconsistencies that a committee can fix quickly, so expect them to be fixed.
- **Risk of the "keep operating" readings:** Items 1, 8, 14, 17, and 21 depend on structuring choices, such as splitting fleets, using non-U.S. entities, or recasting commercial recovery as assessment. Courts often read statutes to prevent evasion of their purpose, and these readings could increase enforcement attention and willfulness exposure. Counsel should weigh those risks before relying on them.
- **Markup dynamics:** Pointing out gaps will likely prompt drafting fixes. Arguments that the bill is unworkable are most credible when they rest on defects that can't be cured easily, such as the absent permitting pathway (item 16) and the unresolved relationship to DSHMRA (item 37).
