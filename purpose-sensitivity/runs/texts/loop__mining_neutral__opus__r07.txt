RESPONSE:
# S. 2817 Drafting Issues: Loopholes, Ambiguities, and Enforcement Gaps

These are issues of textual analysis, not legal conclusions. Some may be deliberate choices or may be addressed by other statutes, such as the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (DSHMRA) or the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act. Before publication, have a maritime or environmental lawyer check them and give the sponsors' offices a chance to respond.

## Definitions (Sec. 2)

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A): Per-vessel threshold invites fleet-splitting.** The 10,000-ton cap applies "by a single vessel," so an operator could spread recovery across several vessels, each under the cap, and mine far more in total with no violation. There is no aggregation rule by operator, company, or affiliate.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A): Tonnage measurement is unspecified.** The bill doesn't say whether tonnage is wet or dry, or raw nodules or processed ore. Nodules hold a lot of water, so this choice could move an operation above or below the threshold. "Per year" is also undefined: calendar year, fiscal year, or rolling 12 months.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A): "Depths greater than 200 meters" is ambiguous.** It could mean water depth or depth beneath the seabed surface. The second reading would narrow the bill to sub-seabed extraction and exclude most nodule collection.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A): "Recovery" and "other mineral resources" are undefined.** It's unclear whether recovery requires lifting material to the surface. Operations that collect and stockpile on the seafloor, or process at depth, might fall outside it. It's also unclear whether cobalt crusts and sulfide deposits are covered.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(A): Ambiguity about which "vessel" counts.** Nodule mining uses seabed collector vehicles, riser systems, and surface ships, sometimes with separate transport vessels. Operators could argue the collector or each transport ship is the relevant "vessel."

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B): "The Secretary" is never identified.** No section names which department head (Commerce? Interior?) holds this power. That invites disputes over who can act and challenges to any determination made.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(B): Open-ended catch-all is vulnerable to legal challenge.** Letting an official define what counts as a crime by a "comparable risks" standard, with no criteria, rulemaking process, or notice requirement, invites vagueness and nondelegation challenges, especially since Sec. 5(b) attaches criminal penalties.

8. **Sec. 2(2): "U.S. person" is narrow.** It covers only citizens and corporations "organized under the laws of a State." That arguably leaves out LLCs, partnerships, permanent residents, and entities organized in D.C. or the territories (the bill doesn't define "State").

9. **Sec. 2(2): Foreign subsidiaries are not covered.** A U.S. company could mine through a foreign-incorporated subsidiary or a foreign-flagged vessel. The bill has no "owned or controlled by" language and no ban on directing, financing, or facilitating mining.

10. **Sec. 2(3) vs. Sec. 2(1)(A): Two inconsistent vessel concepts.** The prohibition applies to "a single vessel" of any size, while the registry applies only to "covered vessels" over 5,000 gross tons. Smaller vessels could mine with no registration or reporting, and the two definitions don't connect.

## Prohibition (Sec. 3)

11. **Sec. 3(a): Territorial reach is unclear.** "Person" and "the United States" are undefined, so it's unclear whether foreign-flagged vessels mining in the U.S. Exclusive Economic Zone are "in the United States." The same question applies to foreign companies whose U.S. offices direct operations abroad.

12. **Sec. 3(a): No ban on importing or buying seabed minerals.** Foreign companies can mine and sell into the U.S. market, and U.S. buyers and processors face no restriction.

13. **Sec. 3(b): Grandfather clause is undefined and could swallow the rule.** "Exploration license" doesn't say who issued it: NOAA under DSHMRA, the International Seabed Authority, or any foreign sponsoring state. A broad reading could exempt a large share of existing industry activity.

14. **Sec. 3(b): Exploration license may become a mining license.** Exploration licenses normally don't authorize commercial recovery. Exempting "mining conducted under" one could be read to let license holders begin commercial-scale extraction.

15. **Sec. 3(b): No limits on grandfathered licenses.** There's no expiration, cap on scale, or bar on renewal, transfer, or expansion of the licensed area. A grandfathered license could become a long-term, tradable exemption.

16. **Sec. 3(c): Environmental-assessment exemption is self-certifying.** It turns on the activity's "purpose," with no definition, permit, tonnage limit, agency approval, or oversight. An operator could label commercial-scale recovery as assessment.

17. **Sec. 3: No relationship to existing law.** The bill doesn't amend or repeal DSHMRA, so NOAA's authority to issue new exploration and commercial recovery permits appears intact. That invites conflicting-statute arguments.

## Registry (Sec. 4)

18. **Sec. 4: No penalty for failing to register or report.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3. Not registering, not reporting, or filing false reports carries no penalty under this Act (general false-statement law under 18 U.S.C. § 1001 might apply).

19. **Sec. 4(a): No deadline or jurisdictional scope.** The bill doesn't say when registration is due. It also doesn't say whether it applies to foreign operators or only U.S. persons and vessels in U.S. waters.

20. **Sec. 4(b): Reports are self-certified with no required content.** Nothing requires tonnage, location, depth, or vessel-tracking data. There is no independent verification, no observers or monitoring, and no audit authority, which makes the per-vessel threshold nearly impossible to police.

21. **Sec. 4: NOAA vs. "the Secretary."** The registry goes to NOAA, while Sec. 2(1)(B) empowers an unnamed Secretary. The bill doesn't assign enforcement, investigation, or rulemaking authority to any agency, and it doesn't address whether reports are public.

## Penalties (Sec. 5)

22. **Sec. 5(a) vs. Sec. 8: Conflicting start dates.** Penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the Act takes effect 180 days later. Applying penalties to conduct in that window could raise fair-notice or ex post facto-style objections.

23. **Sec. 5(a): Daily penalty doesn't match an annual threshold.** A violation is defined by exceeding 10,000 tons per year, but penalties accrue "for each day." It's unclear which days count: every day of the year, only days after the threshold is crossed, or only days of actual recovery.

24. **Sec. 5: No enforcement tools or procedure.** There's no designated enforcing agency, administrative hearing process, inspection or subpoena power, vessel seizure or forfeiture, injunctive relief, or citizen-suit provision. Collecting penalties from foreign-based operators would be difficult.

## International Agreement (Sec. 6)

25. **Sec. 6: Weak, ambiguous mandate.** "Shall seek to negotiate" requires only effort, not a result. The 18-month deadline could apply to starting negotiations or finishing them. There's no reporting requirement and no consequence if nothing happens.

## Duration (Sec. 7)

26. **Sec. 7(a) vs. 7(b): Contradictory duration provisions.** Subsection (a) implies the ban lasts until Congress makes a finding, but (b) ends the whole Act after 10 years regardless. A future Congress could lift the ban without (a), and any joint resolution needs the President's signature, like an ordinary law.

27. **Sec. 7(a): The National Academies report is never commissioned.** No provision directs or funds the study, sets a timeline, or defines "serious harm." The condition for lifting the ban may never be triggered.

28. **Sec. 7(b): Sunset shortens the ban and may cut off pending cases.** Because of the 180-day delay, the ban runs about 9.5 years, and the registry expires too. With no savings clause, defendants could argue that prosecutions or penalty actions pending at sunset abate. Whether the general savings statute (1 U.S.C. § 109) covers expiring statutes is contested.

## Effective Date and Structure

29. **Sec. 8: 180-day window.** Unlimited mining is lawful between enactment and the effective date. Operations already underway could ramp up in that window, then argue continuity under the Sec. 3(b) exemption.

30. **Whole bill: No severability clause.** If a court strikes down a provision such as the Sec. 2(1)(B) delegation, there's no express instruction that the rest survives. Challengers would have more room to argue the whole Act fails.
