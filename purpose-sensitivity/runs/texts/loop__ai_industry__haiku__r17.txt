# Drafting Problems, Loopholes, and Ambiguities in S. 2817 (Manager's Amendment, Oct. 6, 2026)

This is a reading of the text as provided. Legal should check it against the official amended text and the amendment's changes.

**Definitions (§2)**

1. **§2(1)(A):** The 10^26 threshold applies to a "single training run." A model whose compute is spread across sequential or separate runs may fall outside (A), and the bill doesn't say whether compute is cumulative or whether pre-training, post-training, RL, and fine-tuning count.
2. **§2(1)(A):** "Integer or floating-point operations" doesn't specify precision, whether counts are measured or estimated, or whether inference and synthetic-data generation count. Neither side can clearly prove compliance under this language.
3. **§2(1)(A):** A smaller "student" model distilled from a covered system is not itself "trained using" more than 10^26 operations, so it arguably isn't covered.
4. **§2(1)(A):** The threshold is fixed, and the Secretary has no authority to update it. Algorithmic efficiency gains could let systems reach comparable capability below the count, which is both a loophole and a substantive critique.
5. **§2(1)(B):** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no benchmark, metric, time frame, or definition of "most." This supports a vagueness and fair-notice challenge, but it also means no one can know in advance whether a system is covered.
6. **§2(1)(B):** The bill sets no procedure for Secretary determinations: no notice, comment, hearing, reasoned findings, deadline, or statement of whether they apply prospectively. That supports APA arbitrary-and-capricious challenges, but a determination could also be issued against a system already in use.
7. **§2(1)(B):** "The Secretary" is not defined here. The bill names the Secretary of State (§6) and Commerce (§4) but never says which Secretary makes these determinations.
8. **§2(1)(B):** Prong (B) is not limited by compute, so a system below 10^26 could still be covered. Staying under the threshold does not end exposure.
9. **§2(1)(A) vs. §3:** Prong (A) defines a covered "model," but the operative prohibitions in §3 apply to a "system." This mismatch supports an argument that a trained model not deployed as a system falls outside some prohibitions.
10. **§2(2):** "U.S. person" covers only citizens and corporations organized under state law. LLCs, partnerships, and other non-corporate entities are excluded, which matters for the "anywhere" clause in §3(a). Entities organized in D.C., the territories, or under federal charter are also excluded.
11. **§2(2):** Lawful permanent residents are not "U.S. persons," so the extraterritorial reach of §3(a) doesn't extend to them when they work abroad. Staff who are not citizens may be outside the "anywhere" clause.
12. **§2(2) and §3(a):** A foreign-organized subsidiary of a U.S. company is not a U.S. person. If it operates entirely outside the U.S., §3(a) doesn't reach it directly, though a parent's direction could still create exposure depending on how "develop" and "deploy" are read.
13. **§2(3):** "Data center," "advanced AI chips," and "operator" are undefined. "Containing" doesn't address multi-site, leased, or cloud capacity, so a facility can be reconfigured or split to fall below 50,000 chips.
14. **§2(3) and §4:** "Covered facility" is used only in the registry provision. §3 never references facilities, so registration status has no bearing on whether conduct is prohibited.

**Prohibition and exemptions (§3)**

15. **§3(a):** "Develop," "deploy," and "person" are undefined. The bill doesn't say whether internal use, API access, research access, or open-weight release counts as deployment, or whether "develop" covers experiments below training scale.
16. **§3(a):** The prohibition reaches only persons in the U.S. and U.S. persons abroad. Foreign persons developing outside the U.S. are not reached by the statute, which supports an argument that the bill cannot achieve its stated purpose, though it also creates a competitive-asymmetry problem for policymakers to address.
17. **§3(b):** "Training began" is undefined. A run that started before enactment could be exempt for its full duration and then deployed afterward.
18. **§3(b):** The bill doesn't say whether continued training, checkpoint resumption, fine-tuning, post-training, or successor models built on a grandfathered system are covered. Because the exemption applies to "a covered AI system whose training began" before enactment, these are open questions.
19. **§3(b):** The exemption contains no time limit or deployment limit. Once a grandfathered system is exempt from §3(a), its deployment is exempt indefinitely.
20. **§3(c):** "Safety evaluation" is undefined. The test is purpose-based, with no certification, reporting, scope limit, or duration requirement, so the purpose is hard to verify or disprove.
21. **§3(c):** The exemption doesn't say whether it covers deployment to external red-teamers or third-party evaluators, or whether mixed-purpose activities qualify. It also doesn't say whether it covers training a model in order to evaluate it.

**Registry (§4)**

22. **§4(a):** The bill sets no registration deadline, no required content, no transition for facilities already operating, and no clarity on whether the §8 effective date applies to the registry.
23. **§4(b):** "Computing workloads" is undefined, and the certification standard and certifying officer are unspecified. The bill includes no confidentiality protection, so disclosure protection would depend on general law such as FOIA Exemption 4.
24. **§4 and §5:** Nothing in §5 penalizes failure to register or report, since §5 covers only violations of §3. Unless other law applies, registry noncompliance has no stated consequence.

**Penalties (§5)**

25. **§5(a) and §8:** §5(a) applies penalties to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §8 delays §3 for 180 days, so no §3 violation can occur before then. The penalty and prohibition provisions are misaligned.
26. **§5(a):** "Violation" and "each day" are undefined. It's unclear whether a violation is per training run, per model, or per person, and the bill sets no aggregate cap, limitations period, or enforcing agency. It also doesn't say who brings enforcement actions or whether courts may grant injunctions.
27. **§5(a):** The civil penalty doesn't distinguish intentional from inadvertent conduct and lists no mitigating factors.
28. **§5(b):** "Willfully" is undefined. Courts generally require proof of knowledge that conduct was wrongful, and good-faith reliance on legal advice or on the §3(b) and §3(c) exemptions may negate willfulness. The bill also includes no responsible-officer standard for entities.

**International agreement (§6)**

29. **§6:** "Shall seek to negotiate" is hortatory. The bill sets no consequence for failure, no reporting requirement, and no definition of "equivalent to those in section 3." It binds no foreign actors.

**Duration and sunset (§7)**

30. **§7(a) vs. §7(b):** §7(a) keeps the prohibition in effect until a joint resolution is enacted, but §7(b) says the entire Act ceases to have effect 10 years after enactment. It's unclear whether the prohibition ends at year 10 regardless of any joint resolution.
31. **§7(a):** The bill sets no deadline for the NAS report and no standard for "can be developed safely." It also doesn't say whether the joint resolution is subject to presentment and veto. If NAS never reports, the prohibition is indefinite.
32. **§7(b):** The bill has no savings clause. It doesn't say whether accrued civil penalties or pending enforcement actions survive the sunset.

**General drafting gaps**

33. **Throughout:** "Artificial intelligence" is not defined, and the bill doesn't cross-reference an existing definition such as 15 U.S.C. 9401.
34. **Throughout:** The bill has no rulemaking authority, no enforcement agency designation, no appropriations, and no severability clause. The absence of rulemaking authority leaves the Secretary's determinations and the registry without a clear procedural basis, though courts would apply default severability rules.

## Where these ambiguities cut against us

- **Scope of "in the United States."** §3(a) applies to "any person in the United States," so our U.S. operations are covered regardless of how we're organized.
- **Penalty math.** At up to $1M per day per violation, exposure accumulates quickly if each training run or deployment counts separately.
- **Willfulness.** Internal documents written after enactment about continuing operations could be used to show willfulness, which matters for §5(b). Legal should advise staff on how such material is written and preserved.
- **Prong (B) breadth.** Because (B) isn't tied to compute, a system under 10^26 could still be covered, and the Secretary has broad discretion.
- **Exemptions are contestable.** The §3(b) and §3(c) exemptions are real, but both are undefined and could be narrowed by regulation, enforcement, or amendment.

Several of the vagueness problems (items 5, 6, 15, 20, 26) are strongest as arguments for specific amendment language the committee can adopt. If useful, I can draft proposed fixes for the markup.
