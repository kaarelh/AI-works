RESPONSE:
**Capitol Policy Review: S. 2817 drafting and enforcement issues (text as amended October 6, 2026)**

I worked only from the text you provided. I haven't checked the amendment against the original bill, the committee report, or outside law. Items marked "verify" depend on statutes or doctrines the reporter should confirm. Some items may be deliberate policy choices (the sunset, the research exemption), so the explainer should include sponsors' stated rationale alongside any critique.

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A):** The 10,000-metric-ton threshold is measured "per year" without saying whether it counts actual recovery, permitted capacity, wet or dry weight, or ore versus contained metal. Operators could argue they fall below it or plan around it.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A):** The threshold applies to "a single vessel," so an operator could split a project across several vessels that each stay under 10,000 tons, or run affiliated companies with separate vessels.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A):** The 200-meter depth floor excludes shallower seabed, including parts of the continental shelf. The bill doesn't say how it interacts with shallow-water seabed leasing, such as under the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (verify).

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A):** "Recovery," "seabed," and "other mineral resources" are undefined. Whether the bill reaches seafloor massive sulfides, cobalt-rich crusts, or other non-nodule resources is unclear.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(B):** The Secretary can extend the definition to any activity judged to pose "comparable" risk, but the bill sets no evidentiary standard, notice requirement, or process. This invites an arbitrary-and-capricious or vagueness challenge, and the comparison point is itself a tonnage-defined activity.

6. **Sec. 2 (general):** "Secretary" is never identified. The bill doesn't say whether it means Commerce, Interior, NOAA, or State, which matters for Sections 2(1)(B), 4, and 5.

7. **Sec. 2(2):** "U.S. person" covers citizens and state-chartered corporations but not LLCs, partnerships, or trusts, which are common mining vehicles. It also doesn't address foreign-incorporated subsidiaries or sponsored vehicles, which narrows the reach of the "anywhere" language in Sec. 3(a).

8. **Sec. 2 (general):** "State" is undefined, so it's unclear whether D.C., Puerto Rico, Guam, or other territories count for purposes of U.S. person status.

9. **Sec. 2(3):** "Equipped for the recovery of seabed mineral resources" is vague. The bill doesn't say whether it covers ore carriers, support ships, or retrofitted vessels, and it doesn't specify which gross-tonnage measure applies.

10. **Sec. 2(3) and Sec. 4(a):** The 5,000-GT threshold creates a registry blind spot. A vessel of 4,999 GT could mine more than 10,000 tons a year, be prohibited under Sec. 3, yet never register or report under Sec. 4.

11. **Sec. 3(a):** The prohibition covers only "engag[ing] in" mining. It doesn't reach financing, insurance, equipment manufacturing, procurement, port services, or the import and sale of minerals or products from mining elsewhere.

12. **Sec. 3(a):** The bill doesn't explain how it applies to the high seas or to the international seabed area beyond national jurisdiction, or how it relates to International Seabed Authority licensing. Non-U.S. persons acting abroad are outside its reach, and the bill has no enforcement tools against them, such as vessel arrest or service provisions.

13. **Sec. 3(b):** "Exploration license" is undefined, and the bill doesn't say whether an exploration license authorizes commercial recovery. It also doesn't address renewal, transfer, or expansion of pre-enactment licenses.

14. **Sec. 3(b) and Sec. 8:** Licenses issued between the markup and enactment would be grandfathered, which creates an incentive to rush license issuance while the bill is pending.

15. **Sec. 3(b) (general):** The bill doesn't mention the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act, which already governs licensing and permitting for U.S. citizens' deep seabed mining. It's unclear whether that law is preempted, amended, or left in place alongside this bill (verify).

16. **Sec. 3(c):** The "environmental assessment" exemption has no limit on volume, duration, location, or whether recovered minerals may be sold. It has no approval or reporting requirement, so it could be used to recover commercial quantities. The term "environmental assessment" is also undefined.

17. **Sec. 4(a):** "Operator" is undefined, which creates room to split ownership from operation through charters or shell companies. The bill also sets no registration deadline and specifies no required content.

18. **Sec. 4 (general):** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3. The bill sets no penalty for failing to register or report under Sec. 4, which leaves the registry hard to enforce.

19. **Sec. 4(b):** Quarterly reports must "describe" recovery activities, but the bill doesn't list required data such as tonnage, location, depth, or operating days. It also doesn't require public disclosure or say how NOAA may use the reports.

20. **Sec. 4(b):** Reports are "certified by the operator," but the bill doesn't state a penalty for false certification, so enforcement would depend on general federal false-statement law.

21. **Sec. 5(a):** The "$1,000,000 for each day" penalty doesn't define a day for continuing violations or multi-vessel operations. The bill has no per-ton metric, inflation adjustment, disgorgement of profits, vessel forfeiture, or officer liability.

22. **Sec. 5(a) and Sec. 8:** Sec. 5(a) applies penalties to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but Sec. 8 delays the Act's effect by 180 days. It's unclear whether Sec. 3 is enforceable from enactment or only from the effective date.

23. **Sec. 5(b):** "Willfully" is undefined, and the bill doesn't address corporate criminal liability, responsible corporate officers, or attempt and conspiracy.

24. **Sec. 5 (general):** The bill doesn't name an enforcing agency, provide an administrative penalty process, set a statute of limitations, or grant injunctive authority or rulemaking power. Without these, enforcement depends on implied authority.

25. **Sec. 6:** "Shall seek to negotiate" creates no required outcome, reporting deadline, or consequence for failure. "Equivalent" prohibitions is undefined, and the bill doesn't say what happens to domestic law if an agreement is reached (verify the U.S. treaty status under UNCLOS and the ISA framework).

26. **Sec. 7(a):** "Serious harm" is undefined, and the bill doesn't require NASEM to produce a report or set a deadline for it. Who decides whether a finding has been met is therefore unclear.

27. **Sec. 7(a):** It's unclear whether a joint resolution would go to the President for signature or veto, or whether "finds by joint resolution" would have binding legal effect on its own. This may raise presentment questions under Chadha (verify).

28. **Sec. 7(a) and Sec. 7(b):** The two subsections conflict. Sec. 7(a) keeps the prohibition in effect until Congress acts, but Sec. 7(b) ends the entire Act after 10 years regardless of any finding. It's unclear which provision controls.

29. **Sec. 7(b) and Sec. 8:** With the 180-day delay, the Act would be in effect for about 9.5 years. The bill has no savings clause for violations, pending enforcement, or licenses issued during that period, and it doesn't say whether the registry or reporting requirements survive the sunset.

30. **General (missing provisions):** The bill has no severability clause, no general preemption or savings clause, and no statement of how it relates to the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act, the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act, or other environmental statutes. Each gap could be used in litigation.
