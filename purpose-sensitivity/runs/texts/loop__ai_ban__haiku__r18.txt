RESPONSE:
Here are the issues I found in the October 6 text, grouped by section. The top priorities for Thursday are items 1, 3, 11, 13, 19, and 25.

**Definitions (§2)**

1. **§2(1)(A).** The 10^26 threshold applies to "a single training run," so a developer could split one frontier effort into several sub-threshold runs or stages. The definition should count cumulative compute across all training stages for a model lineage, including fine-tuning and post-training.

2. **§2(1)(A).** A fixed compute threshold doesn't account for algorithmic efficiency gains, so dangerous capabilities could be reached below 10^26. The Secretary should be authorized to lower the threshold by rule as efficiency improves.

3. **§2(1)(A) and §3(a).** The definition covers a "model," but the prohibition covers a "covered AI system." Systems built from several sub-threshold models, agent scaffolds, or ensembles may fall outside both.

4. **§2(1)(B).** "The Secretary" has no antecedent. The Act names the Department of Commerce in §4 and the Secretary of State in §6, but never says which Secretary makes the §2(1)(B) determinations.

5. **§2(1)(B).** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" leaves "substantially," "most," and "cognitive tasks" undefined, which invites a vagueness challenge. The bill should specify the evaluation criteria or benchmarks and require a published rule.

6. **§2(1)(B).** The provision sets no duty, deadline, notice, or hearing for determinations, so the Secretary could never act or could act arbitrarily. It should require periodic assessments, notice and comment, and a stated standard for judicial review.

7. **§2(2).** "U.S. person" excludes foreign-incorporated affiliates of U.S. companies, LLCs, partnerships, and lawful permanent residents, and it does not define "State," so D.C. and the territories are unclear. A developer could route training through an entity outside the definition. Coverage should extend to any entity controlled by U.S. persons or with substantial U.S. operations.

8. **§2(3).** "Advanced AI chips" and "data center" are undefined, and "containing" doesn't address leased or distributed capacity. A campus can be split into facilities under 50,000 chips, and training spread across networked sites may never meet the facility test.

**Prohibition (§3)**

9. **§3(a).** "Train," "develop," and "deploy" are undefined. It's unclear whether publishing open weights, fine-tuning a covered model, or providing API access from abroad counts as deployment in the United States. The bill should define these terms and expressly cover making weights available for download.

10. **§3(a).** The prohibition does not reach those who knowingly provide compute, chips, cloud capacity, or other services to a prohibited training effort. Add aiding-and-abetting liability for compute and service providers.

11. **§3(a) and (b).** Extraterritorial reach is limited to U.S. persons. A foreign-incorporated developer training abroad and serving U.S. users is not clearly covered, and §6 does not fill that gap.

12. **§3(b).** The grandfather clause exempts any covered system whose training "began" before enactment from all of §3(a), including deployment and further training. "Began" is undefined, so developers could start runs now and deploy them indefinitely. The exemption should be limited to systems already deployed as of a fixed date, with no further scaling and with registration required.

13. **§3(b).** Keying the grandfather date to enactment, which may be months away, invites a race to start runs before the bill becomes law. Use the bill's introduction or a fixed announced date instead.

14. **§3(c).** The safety-evaluation exemption is self-defined, with no compute cap, notice, registration, or limit on deploying the resulting model. Any capability training can be labeled safety evaluation. The exemption should require advance notice to Commerce, a compute cap, a bar on deploying resulting covered systems, and independent oversight.

15. **§3(a), §5(a), and §8.** The effective dates conflict. §8 delays the Act's effect 180 days, but §5(a) applies penalties to violations on or after enactment, and §3(b) is keyed to enactment. It's unclear whether training during the 180-day window is prohibited.

**Registry and reporting (§4)**

16. **§4(a).** Registration covers only "operators of covered facilities," not developers or specific training runs. A developer using leased or cloud capacity, or a facility under 50,000 chips, falls outside the registry. "Operator" should be defined to include lessees and developers.

17. **§4(b).** The quarterly report must describe "computing workloads" but does not require compute per run, a flag for runs exceeding §2(1)(A), or any specific content. There is no verification mechanism or audit right.

18. **§4.** The Act sets no registration deadline and no penalty for failing to register or for a false certification, since §5 penalizes only §3 violations. It also lacks a confidentiality clause, leaving unclear what companies can withhold and what the public will see.

**Enforcement and penalties (§5)**

19. **§5 (as a whole).** The Act names no enforcing agency or litigating authority, gives no investigative powers such as subpoenas or access to logs and weights, and authorizes no injunction or order to halt training. As written, a company could keep training and simply pay civil penalties, so the Act should add injunctive relief, cease-and-desist authority, and whistleblower protections.

20. **§5(a).** "Violation" is undefined, so it's unclear whether a violation is a run, a day, or a deployment. A $1 million daily maximum is small against frontier training budgets of billions, so penalties should be scaled to compute cost or revenue, with disgorgement.

21. **§5(b).** "Willfully" is undefined. The fines default to general Title 18 corporate maximums (roughly $500,000 per felony count; counsel should verify), which are trivial for frontier developers. The Act should add a fine scaled to the company and individual liability for officers who knowingly direct violations.

**International agreement (§6)**

22. **§6.** "Seek to negotiate" imposes no duty to succeed, no reporting, and no consequence if talks fail. "Equivalent" is undefined. Add periodic reports to Congress and a defined decision point on whether to keep or revise the domestic prohibition.

**Duration and sunset (§7)**

23. **§7(a).** The prohibition lasts until Congress "finds by joint resolution" that systems "can be developed safely," but the bill defines neither "safely" nor the NASEM study. It also doesn't say how a joint resolution becomes law (presidential signature or veto override) or set a deadline for the report.

24. **§7(a) and (b).** The two subsections conflict. §7(a) keeps the prohibition in effect until Congress acts, while §7(b) ends the entire Act after 10 years regardless. Industry will argue the sunset controls, so the bill should either drop the sunset or make it expressly subject to §7(a).

**Structure and challenge risk (general)**

25. **Missing severability clause.** If a court strikes one provision, such as §2(1)(B) or §3(c), nothing says the rest survives, which invites an all-or-nothing challenge. Add a severability clause.

26. **Missing findings and purpose section.** Without congressional findings tying the prohibition to national security and interstate commerce, courts have less basis for deference on the Commerce Clause and national-security rationales. Add a findings section.

27. **Constitutional exposure.** The Act's language on "deploy," "develop," and open-weight publication may draw First Amendment challenges, and §2(1)(B)'s open-ended Secretary determination and §3(c) invite vagueness and nondelegation arguments. Legislative counsel should review these before the markup, because the same wording that creates the loopholes also creates challenge risk.

**Caveats:** I worked only from the text you provided. I haven't seen the amendment's redline, the committee's drafting history, or Commerce's views, and some statutory cross-references (especially the Title 18 fine amounts) should be checked by counsel. Some fixes, such as the grandfather date and the sunset, may be politically sensitive, so it may help to ask the Senator's staff which changes are feasible before Thursday.
