RESPONSE:
Here's the review. Items are numbered continuously and grouped by section; the most consequential ones are flagged with **[critical]**.

**Sec. 2(1)(A) — Definition of "commercial deep seabed mining"**

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "by a single vessel" [critical].** The 10,000-ton threshold is measured per vessel, so an operator can run two or more vessels each recovering 9,999 tons and never be covered. Thresholds should aggregate across all vessels, affiliates, and contractors operating under common ownership or control, or the per-vessel limit should be dropped entirely.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A), the 10,000-ton threshold itself.** 10,000 metric tons per year per vessel is well above pilot-scale (industry collector trials have recovered a few thousand tons in weeks) and permits substantial unregulated extraction. Consider defining the prohibited activity by purpose (any recovery for sale or commercial use) rather than by tonnage, with a far smaller research allowance if one is kept.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "per year" undefined.** It is unclear whether this is a calendar year, a rolling 12-month period, or a fiscal year; an operator could recover 9,999 tons in December and 9,999 in January. Specify "in any 12-month period."

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "metric tons" of what.** The bill does not say whether tonnage is wet weight, dry weight, ore, or contained metal, and nodules can lose a large fraction of mass on drying. Specify the measurement basis and who measures it.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "recovery ... from the seabed" undefined.** "Recovery" could be read to mean only material lifted to a surface vessel, excluding seabed collection, in-situ crushing, slurry pumping, or material staged on the seabed and later retrieved by a different vessel. Define recovery to include any disturbance, collection, or removal of seabed minerals and any associated lifting, transport, or processing.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "at depths greater than 200 meters" ambiguous.** It is unclear whether this refers to the water depth where the vessel sits, the depth of the collector, or the depth of the deposit. Tie the threshold to the depth of the seabed at the point of extraction.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "other mineral resources" may sweep in oil and gas [critical, from the other direction].** Federal statutes such as OCSLA treat oil and gas as "minerals," and a deepwater FPSO recovers far more than 10,000 tons a year from the seabed below 200 meters. Without an express exclusion of hydrocarbons, the bill invites a litigation and lobbying fight that could sink it; conversely, the definition should expressly include polymetallic sulfides and cobalt-rich crusts so sulfide mining of the subsoil isn't argued to fall outside "the seabed."

**Sec. 2(1)(B) — Secretary's determination**

8. **Sec. 2(1)(B), "the Secretary" is never defined [critical].** No section of the Act identifies which Secretary makes this determination; the only Secretary mentioned anywhere is the Secretary of State in Sec. 6, which is almost certainly not the intent. Add a definition (presumably Commerce, given NOAA's role) or substitute "the Administrator of NOAA."

9. **Sec. 2(1)(B), discretionary trigger with no process or duty.** Because coverage depends on a determination the Secretary is never required to make, an unsympathetic administration can neutralize the clause by doing nothing. Add a mandatory determination with a deadline, notice-and-comment procedure, and judicial review.

10. **Sec. 2(1)(B), criminal exposure based on an agency determination.** Sec. 5(b) attaches a 10-year felony to conduct defined only by an open-ended "comparable risk" finding, inviting void-for-vagueness and nondelegation challenges. Either confine (B) to civil liability or require that a determination be published by rule before any penalty attaches.

**Sec. 2(2) — "U.S. person"**

11. **Sec. 2(2), entity coverage is far too narrow [critical].** The definition reaches only corporations "organized under the laws of a State," omitting LLCs, partnerships, trusts, and entities organized under D.C., territorial, tribal, or federal law. Most deep-sea mining ventures are structured through LLCs and foreign subsidiaries.

12. **Sec. 2(2), no coverage of foreign subsidiaries or U.S.-controlled entities.** A U.S. corporation can mine through a Canadian or Tongan subsidiary without triggering the prohibition. Extend the definition to any entity owned or controlled, directly or indirectly, by a U.S. person, and make parents liable for subsidiary conduct.

13. **Sec. 2(2), individuals limited to citizens.** Lawful permanent residents and persons resident in the United States are excluded, unlike in sanctions and export-control statutes, which cover both.

14. **Sec. 2(2), U.S.-flagged vessels not covered.** A foreign-owned company operating a U.S.-flagged vessel is neither "in the United States" nor a "U.S. person." Add vessels documented under U.S. law, and any vessel operating from a U.S. port.

**Sec. 2(3) — "Covered vessel"**

15. **Sec. 2(3), 5,000-gross-ton floor.** Vessels at or below 5,000 GT, and fleets of smaller vessels, escape the registry entirely. Drop the tonnage floor or set it well below any plausible mining platform.

16. **Sec. 2(3), "equipped for the recovery" undefined.** Operators can argue a vessel with removable collector gear, or one that only receives slurry from a separate collector platform, is not "equipped." Define by capability or intended use and include support and transport vessels.

**Sec. 3 — Prohibition**

17. **Sec. 3(a), activity in the U.S. EEZ and on the outer continental shelf may be uncovered [critical].** A foreign-owned, foreign-flagged vessel mining the U.S. continental shelf is not a "person in the United States" (the mining occurs offshore) and not a "U.S. person." Add an express prohibition on any mining in waters or seabed subject to U.S. jurisdiction, regardless of the actor's nationality.

18. **Sec. 3(a), "engage in" reaches only direct operators.** The bill does not prohibit financing, insuring, chartering, crewing, transshipping, processing, importing, or selling minerals, so U.S. capital and ports can support foreign mining. Add "directly or indirectly engage in, facilitate, finance, or provide services for," and consider an import/landing ban on deep-seabed minerals.

19. **Sec. 3(a), no attempt or conspiracy liability.** An operator caught en route or mid-deployment, before the tonnage threshold is crossed, has violated nothing.

20. **Sec. 3, no amendment or supersession of the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act [critical].** DSHMRA (30 U.S.C. 1401 et seq.) still authorizes NOAA to issue exploration licenses and commercial recovery permits, and nothing here repeals that authority or directs NOAA to stop. The Act should expressly amend DSHMRA to bar issuance, renewal, or transfer of licenses and permits, and bar federal sponsorship or financing (e.g., DFC, EXIM, Defense Production Act).

21. **Sec. 3(b), grandfather clause converts exploration into permanent mining rights [critical].** Exploration licenses do not authorize commercial recovery, yet this exempts any "mining conducted under" one, so an existing DSHMRA licensee (or a pending applicant who obtains a license before enactment) can scale to full commercial extraction forever. Existing licenses, pending applications, and any licenses issued after introduction will be the industry's main channel, so the exemption should be struck or at minimum limited to exploration activity itself.

22. **Sec. 3(b), "exploration license" undefined and unbounded.** It does not say who issues the license (NOAA, ISA, a foreign state), and nothing prevents amendment, expansion, renewal, assignment, or extension of a pre-enactment license. If the clause survives, confine it to the original licensed area, term, and licensee.

23. **Sec. 3(b), pre-enactment cutoff creates a race.** The bill text is public now, and there are 180 days between enactment and effect (Sec. 8), so the incentive to obtain licenses quickly is strong. Use the date of introduction, not enactment, as the cutoff.

24. **Sec. 3(c), environmental-assessment exemption has no limits [critical].** Any extraction can be labeled "for the purpose of environmental assessment," and there is no tonnage cap, prior authorization, independent oversight, or bar on selling the recovered material. Cap it, require a permit with a scientific purpose finding, and require that recovered material not be commercialized.

**Sec. 4 — Registry**

25. **Sec. 4, no penalty for noncompliance.** Sec. 5 penalizes only Sec. 3 violations, so failure to register or report carries no consequence under this Act. Add civil penalties and make false certifications expressly actionable.

26. **Sec. 4, "operator" undefined and no jurisdictional hook.** The obligation nominally applies to every covered vessel worldwide, which is unenforceable against foreign operators and invites challenge. Tie registration to U.S. persons, U.S.-flagged vessels, and vessels entering U.S. ports or waters.

27. **Sec. 4, no deadlines.** Neither registration nor the quarterly reports has a due date.

28. **Sec. 4(b), self-certified reports with no verification.** There is no requirement for independent tonnage measurement, AIS tracking, onboard observers, inspection, or public disclosure, so the registry cannot support enforcement of the Sec. 2(1)(A) threshold. Add inspection and records-access authority and publication of reports.

**Sec. 5 — Penalties**

29. **Sec. 5(a), penalty may be below the profit of violating.** $1 million per day with no minimum can be a cost of doing business for an operation grossing more than that. Add a per-ton penalty, disgorgement of proceeds, and forfeiture of recovered minerals and vessels.

30. **Sec. 5(a), effective-date conflict with Sec. 8.** Penalties attach to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the prohibition does not take effect until 180 days later, so either the penalty clause is meaningless for that window or it invites an ex post facto challenge. Conform the two.

31. **Sec. 5, no enforcement machinery.** The Act does not say which agency assesses civil penalties, whether assessment is administrative or judicial, who may bring suit, or whether courts may enjoin violations. Add enforcement authority (NOAA/Coast Guard, with boarding and seizure powers), injunctive relief, and a citizen-suit provision.

32. **Sec. 5, no officer or parent liability.** Corporate officers who direct violations, and parents of violating subsidiaries, are not reached.

33. **Sec. 5(b), "willfully" plus a determination-based definition.** Proving willfulness for conduct covered only through a Sec. 2(1)(B) determination will be nearly impossible; use "knowingly" at least for the 2(1)(A) prong.

**Sec. 6 — International agreement**

34. **Sec. 6, aspirational and ambiguous.** "Seek to negotiate ... not later than 18 months" could mean the effort must begin within 18 months or conclude within it, and there is no report-back requirement or consequence. Add a timeline, periodic reports to Congress, and direction regarding the U.S. position at the ISA and in bilateral talks.

**Sec. 7 — Duration**

35. **Sec. 7(b), the sunset swallows the prohibition [critical].** Subsection (a) says the ban lasts until Congress affirmatively finds mining safe, but (b) terminates the entire Act, including the ban, automatically after 10 years. The sunset converts a prohibition into a moratorium and should be struck; if a review is wanted, require a report, not termination.

36. **Sec. 7(a), "serious harm" undefined and NASEM report uncommissioned.** No one is directed or funded to produce the report, so it could be sponsored by industry, and the finding standard has no content (irreversible? significant? to what?). Define the standard and have the Act commission and fund the study.

37. **Sec. 7, no transition after termination.** If the ban ends under (a) or (b), the Act provides no replacement regulatory regime, so mining would proceed under DSHMRA as it stands.

**Sec. 8 — Effective date**

38. **Sec. 8, 180-day delay.** The delay gives operators time to license, deploy, and establish "existing" status under Sec. 3(b), and it is unclear whether it also delays Secs. 4 and 6. Make Sec. 3 effective on enactment, or at least make Sec. 3(b)'s cutoff earlier (see item 23).

**Missing provisions (Act-wide)**

39. **No severability clause.** If Sec. 2(1)(B) or the criminal penalty falls to a vagueness challenge, the Act's structure gives a court no instruction to preserve the rest.

40. **No savings clause for state law.** Several coastal states have their own deep-sea mining restrictions; an express non-preemption clause prevents argument that the federal scheme displaces them.

41. **No rulemaking authority or appropriation.** No agency is authorized to issue implementing regulations or funded to run the registry and enforce the ban.

42. **No definition of "person" or "seabed."** "Person" in Sec. 3(a)'s first prong defaults to 1 U.S.C. 1, which is adequate but should be stated for consistency with "U.S. person"; "seabed" should be defined to include the subsoil so sulfide and crust mining is clearly covered.
