RESPONSE:
Here's a section-by-section list. I've put the most damaging items (the ones that effectively gut the bill) in **bold** so the cover memo to Whitfield's office can lead with them.

**Sec. 2(1) — Definition of "commercial deep seabed mining"**

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "by a single vessel."** The 10,000-ton threshold is measured per vessel, not per operator, project, or license area. A company can run a fleet of vessels each recovering 9,999 tons (or use a seabed collector plus several shuttle/transport vessels so that no "single vessel" is the one "recovering") and never trigger the prohibition. Aggregate across all vessels under common ownership or control and across the whole operation.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A), tonnage threshold at all.** Defining the prohibited activity by volume means any recovery below the threshold is lawful regardless of commercial purpose. Since the bill's premise is that no one knows how to mine without irreversible harm, the prohibition should turn on purpose (recovery for sale, processing, or commercial development) rather than tonnage, or at minimum set a far lower threshold with a rebuttable presumption.

3. Sec. 2(1)(A), "per year." "Year" is undefined (calendar, fiscal, rolling 12 months, license year). A vessel could recover 10,000 tons in December and 10,000 more in January, or rotate vessels at year-end.

4. Sec. 2(1)(A), "recovery" and "metric tons" undefined. It is unclear whether "recovery" means collection on the seabed, lifting to the surface, or offloading, and whether tonnage is wet or dry weight (nodules lose substantial weight on drying). Both ambiguities are exploitable and complicate proof in an enforcement action.

5. Sec. 2(1)(A), "from the seabed." This arguably excludes sub-seabed extraction (e.g., drilling or cutting into massive sulfide deposits or crusts below the sediment surface). Define to include the seabed, subsoil, and overlying water column.

6. Sec. 2(1)(A), volume-only metric ignores disturbance. Collector tests and plume-generating trials that disturb large areas but recover little material escape the definition entirely. Consider covering seabed disturbance (area or sediment volume) as an alternative trigger.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(B), "the Secretary" is undefined.** The bill never says which Secretary (Commerce, State, Interior). This is a plain drafting error, and as written the catch-all provision cannot operate.

8. Sec. 2(1)(B), discretionary and standardless. Whether an activity is prohibited under (B) depends entirely on whether the Secretary chooses to make a determination; nothing requires one, sets a procedure, timeline, or "comparable risk" standard, or provides for public petition or judicial review. A hostile administration can simply never make a determination, nullifying (B).

9. Sec. 2(1)(B), vagueness/nondelegation exposure for criminal liability. Tying a 10-year felony (Sec. 5(b)) to an undefined official's determination that risks are "comparable" invites void-for-vagueness and nondelegation challenges, and absent a requirement that determinations be published by rule, fair-notice problems. Require notice-and-comment rulemaking and publication in the Federal Register before (B) determinations have criminal effect.

**Sec. 2(2) — Definition of "U.S. person"**

10. **Sec. 2(2), entity types.** Only "corporation[s]" are covered; LLCs, partnerships, LLPs, trusts, and joint ventures are not. Most project vehicles in this industry are LLCs or foreign-organized entities.

11. **Sec. 2(2), foreign subsidiaries and controlled entities.** Nothing reaches a foreign-organized entity owned or controlled by U.S. persons, so a U.S. parent can mine through an offshore subsidiary. Add an ownership-or-control test (and cover U.S. nationals serving as officers, directors, or controlling shareholders).

12. Sec. 2(2), "laws of a State." Entities organized under the laws of the District of Columbia, Puerto Rico, Guam, CNMI, American Samoa, the Virgin Islands, or a federally recognized tribe are not covered.

13. Sec. 2(2), individuals. Lawful permanent residents and non-citizen U.S. nationals are excluded; standard sanctions-style definitions include both.

14. Sec. 2(2), no vessel-flag hook. A U.S.-flagged or U.S.-documented vessel operated by a foreign entity is not covered. Add U.S.-flag vessels to the jurisdictional reach.

**Sec. 2(3) — Definition of "covered vessel"**

15. Sec. 2(3), 5,000 gross ton floor and "equipped for." Vessels under 5,000 GT, and vessels not permanently "equipped" (e.g., supporting a separately deployed collector or ROV), fall outside the registry. The registry definition also doesn't track the Sec. 3 prohibition at all, so a vessel can be covered by one and not the other.

**Sec. 3 — Prohibition**

16. **Sec. 3(a), "in the United States" undefined.** Under ordinary usage this likely does not include the EEZ or outer continental shelf, so a foreign entity mining nodules or crusts in the U.S. EEZ around Hawaii, the Pacific territories, or Johnston Atoll may not be covered at all. Define "United States" to include the territorial sea, EEZ, and continental shelf, and all territories and possessions.

17. Sec. 3(a), "engage in" covers only the direct actor. The prohibition does not reach financing, insuring, chartering, contracting for, supplying equipment to, or transporting the product of deep seabed mining, nor attempt, conspiracy, or aiding and abetting on the civil side. A U.S. company can fund and buy from a foreign operator with impunity.

18. Sec. 3(a), no import, possession, or sale ban. Minerals mined in violation of the Act (or by foreign persons) can be landed, processed, and sold in the United States. A Lacey Act-style prohibition on importing, possessing, or trading in deep-seabed minerals, with forfeiture, is the most enforceable tool the bill lacks.

19. Sec. 3(a), no federal-government or federal-permitting coverage. "Person" generally excludes the United States, so federal agencies, contractors, and federally financed projects may not be covered, and nothing bars a federal agency from issuing permits. Add: "No Federal agency may issue, renew, extend, or recognize any license, permit, or authorization for commercial deep seabed mining," and bar federal procurement, financing (EXIM, DFC), and stockpile purchases.

20. **Sec. 3(a), no relationship to DSHMRA.** The Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (30 U.S.C. 1401 et seq.) still authorizes NOAA to issue exploration licenses and commercial recovery permits, and NOAA is actively processing applications under the April 2025 executive order. Without an express amendment, repeal, or supersession clause, the two statutes conflict and the later-in-time/specific-statute fight will be litigated.

21. **Sec. 3(b), "exploration license" grandfathering.** An exploration license (under DSHMRA or elsewhere) does not authorize commercial recovery, so "mining conducted under an exploration license" is either surplusage or a backdoor that lets any pre-enactment licensee proceed to full commercial mining. Either way it should be struck.

22. **Sec. 3(b), "exploration license" undefined and unlimited.** The exemption does not say which authority's licenses count. If ISA exploration contracts or any foreign state's licenses qualify, essentially every serious prospective miner is grandfathered. There is also no volume cap, no expiration, and no bar on transferring or amending the license.

23. Sec. 3(b), cutoff date creates a rush. The cutoff is the date of enactment, which may be months away, during which NOAA and foreign regulators can issue new licenses that will then be exempt. If any grandfathering survives, key it to a fixed past date (e.g., the date of introduction).

24. **Sec. 3(c), "environmental assessment" exemption.** The exemption turns on the actor's stated "purpose," is uncapped in volume, and has no requirement of government authorization, independent oversight, public data release, or a bar on selling recovered material. Industry already labels large collector trials as environmental assessment. Note that Sec. 2(1)(A)'s threshold already exempts small-scale assessment, so this exemption only matters for assessment above 10,000 tons per vessel per year, i.e., commercial scale.

**Sec. 4 — Registry**

25. Sec. 4, no penalty for noncompliance. Sec. 5 penalties attach only to violations of Sec. 3, so failing to register or report has no consequence. The registry is unenforceable as drafted.

26. Sec. 4(a), "operator" undefined. Owner, charterer, manager, and the entity directing mining activity can all be different; each will point to the other.

27. Sec. 4(b), self-certification with no content requirements. Reports need only "describe" activities; they are not required to state tonnage, location, depth, dates, or sale of recovered material, which is exactly what is needed to prove a Sec. 2(1)(A) violation. No verification, inspection, observer, VMS/AIS, or public disclosure requirements.

28. Sec. 4, conceptual mismatch. Registering vessels for an activity the Act prohibits implies the activity is contemplated and lawful, which weakens the prohibition's message and may be cited to argue sub-threshold mining is affirmatively authorized. Compelling individuals to report "recovery activities" that are criminal also raises a Fifth Amendment self-incrimination challenge.

**Sec. 5 — Penalties**

29. **Sec. 5(a), "on or after the date of enactment" conflicts with Sec. 8.** The prohibition does not take effect until 180 days after enactment, so this clause either penalizes conduct that was not yet unlawful (a due-process/retroactivity problem) or is meaningless. Conform to the effective date or, better, eliminate the delay.

30. Sec. 5(a), no enforcing agency or procedure. The bill does not say who assesses civil penalties, whether administratively or in court, or who may seek injunctions. Designate an agency, authorize DOJ to seek injunctive relief, and add a citizen-suit provision so enforcement does not depend on a willing administration.

31. Sec. 5(a), no floor and no disgorgement or forfeiture. A "not more than" cap with no minimum, and no forfeiture of the vessel, equipment, minerals, or proceeds, lets a profitable operation treat penalties as a cost of doing business. Add forfeiture and penalties tied to the value of recovered material.

32. Sec. 5(b), "willfully." Combined with the undefined Secretary determination and purpose-based exemptions, the willfulness standard will be very hard to meet. Consider "knowingly," and add explicit officer/director liability.

**Sec. 6 — International agreement**

33. Sec. 6, "seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months." It is ambiguous whether negotiations must begin or conclude within 18 months, and "seek to" is aspirational with no reporting requirement or consequence. Add periodic reports to Congress and a required U.S. position in relevant international fora.

**Sec. 7 — Duration**

34. **Sec. 7(b), 10-year sunset guts the Act.** The entire Act, including the prohibition, expires in 10 years regardless of what Sec. 7(a) says, so companies can simply plan around 2036. It also sunsets the penalty provisions with no savings clause, creating a potential argument that pre-sunset violations cannot be prosecuted afterward. Strike it.

35. Sec. 7(a) and 7(b) contradict each other. 7(a) says the prohibition stays in effect "until Congress ... finds by joint resolution"; 7(b) says it ends automatically. Courts will have to reconcile the two, and the sunset will likely control.

36. Sec. 7(a), weak lifting standard. "Acting on a report" does not require that the National Academies report actually conclude mining is safe, no one is directed or funded to commission the report, and "serious harm" is undefined. Require that the report itself find, by a defined standard, that mining can be conducted without serious or irreversible harm to deep-sea ecosystems.

**Sec. 8 — Effective date**

37. Sec. 8, 180-day delay. The delay gives operators a window to ramp up and conflicts with Sec. 5(a) (item 29). The Act uses three different trigger dates (enactment for the grandfather clause and penalties, effective date for the prohibition) with no apparent reason; use one.

**Missing provisions**

38. No severability clause. If Sec. 2(1)(B) or another provision is struck (see item 9), a court could take down more of the Act. Add a standard severability clause.

39. No definitions of "person," "Secretary," "United States," "recovery," "operator," "exploration license," "year," "mineral resources," or "serious harm." Every one of these is load-bearing (see items above).

40. No enforcement tools. The Act provides no inspection, boarding, or detention authority, no observer or electronic monitoring requirement, no whistleblower provision, and no requirement that enforcement actions or registry data be public.

**Suggested lead items for the memo:** 1, 7, 11, 16, 20–22, 24, 29, and 34. Items 21–22 and 34 alone would allow most existing license holders to proceed and would end the prohibition in a decade; items 1 and 11 would let anyone else structure around it.
