**Drafting issues and loopholes in S. 2817 (text as amended October 6, 2026)**

1. **§2(1)(A):** The 10,000-ton annual threshold applies to "a single vessel," so an operator could split recovery across several vessels that each stay below it and fall outside the definition.

2. **§2(1)(A):** The definition covers only "recovery" measured per year. Processing, dewatering, and discharge of tailings are not addressed, and an operator could mine just under 10,000 tons a year indefinitely.

3. **§2(1)(A):** "Other mineral resources" is undefined. It is unclear whether cobalt-rich crusts, seafloor massive sulfides, or phosphorites are covered, since only polymetallic nodules are named.

4. **§2(1)(A):** The 200-meter depth floor excludes shallower operations without explanation, and the bill doesn't say how depth is measured or whether the floor is intended to match any existing boundary.

5. **§2(1)(B):** "The Secretary" is undefined. The bill does not say which Secretary (Commerce, Interior, or State) makes the determination that an activity is "comparable."

6. **§2(1)(B):** "Comparable" has no evidentiary standard, procedure, or notice requirement. This catch-all is vulnerable to a vagueness challenge, especially where it backs a criminal penalty under §5(b).

7. **§2(2):** "U.S. person" covers citizens and corporations organized under state law but not LLCs, partnerships, or trusts, which are common vehicles for offshore ventures.

8. **§2 (general):** "Person" is not defined. The general definition in 1 U.S.C. §1 would likely apply, but that should be confirmed, since the answer determines whether foreign entities and unincorporated firms are covered.

9. **§2(3) and §4(a):** The 5,000-gross-ton "covered vessel" definition is used only for registration, not for the prohibition. A 4,000-ton vessel could exceed the 10,000-ton annual threshold in §2(1)(A) and be unlawful but unregistered. "Equipped for the recovery of seabed mineral resources" is also undefined.

10. **§3(a):** "In the United States" is undefined for the seabed. It is unclear whether it includes the Exclusive Economic Zone and outer continental shelf, where much deep-seabed activity would occur.

11. **§3(a):** The prohibition reaches "any U.S. person anywhere" but only "any person in the United States" for everyone else. Foreign persons and foreign-flagged vessels operating beyond U.S. jurisdiction are not covered by the Act itself, so enforcement against them depends on the agreement in §6.

12. **§3(a):** "Engage in" is undefined. The prohibition does not clearly reach financing, investment, equipment supply, insurance, or contracting, and it does not restrict importing, selling, or processing minerals recovered abroad.

13. **§3(b):** An exploration license does not ordinarily authorize commercial recovery, so it is unclear whether this grandfather clause permits commercial-scale extraction under such a license. The bill also names no issuing authority and sets no time limit or volume cap.

14. **§3(b):** Licenses issued before enactment are exempt, with no requirement to publish a list of covered licenses. A surge of issuance before enactment could create exempt operations, and the bill does not say how grandfathered status would be verified.

15. **§3(c):** The environmental-assessment exemption has no cap on scale, duration, or volume recovered, and no notice or reporting requirement. Commercial recovery could be labeled "for the purpose of environmental assessment," and the purpose is hard to prove or disprove.

16. **§4(a):** "Operator" is undefined. Charter or ownership arrangements could shift responsibility away from the entity actually conducting recovery, and the bill sets no deadline for registration.

17. **§4(b):** The quarterly report has no content requirements (tonnage, location, depth, discharges), no public availability requirement, and no audit or verification authority. Reports are self-certified.

18. **§4 (general):** Failure to register or report is not a violation of §3, and §5 penalizes only §3 violations. As written, the registry and reporting duties carry no penalty.

19. **§5(a) and §8:** Penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §8 delays the Act's effective date by 180 days. It is unclear whether conduct during that window is penalized.

20. **§5(a):** The bill names no enforcing agency, administrative penalty process, or venue. "Per day" does not define how a continuing violation is counted, and there are no assessment factors, no inflation adjustment, and no provision for injunctions, vessel forfeiture, or citizen suits.

21. **§5(b):** "Willfully" is undefined, and the bill sets no standard for corporate officer liability. The fine is set by reference to title 18 without a corporate-specific amount.

22. **§6:** "Shall seek to negotiate" imposes no obligation to reach an agreement and no consequence if none is reached. "Equivalent" is undefined, and there is no reporting requirement to Congress.

23. **§7(a):** "Serious harm" is undefined. The National Academies report is referenced, but the bill does not require it, sets no deadline for it, and provides no expedited procedure for the joint resolution. A joint resolution also requires presidential signature or a two-thirds override, which affects how the standard would work in practice.

24. **§7(a) and §7(b):** The two subsections conflict. §7(a) keeps the prohibition in force until Congress acts, but §7(b) ends the entire Act 10 years after enactment. Because §8 delays effectiveness, the operative period is roughly 9.5 years.

25. **§7 and §8:** The sunset and grandfathered licenses are not addressed. The bill has no savings clause preserving liability for violations committed before the sunset, and it does not say what happens to licenses or registrations when the Act expires.

26. **Act-wide:** The bill has no severability clause. If a court strikes one provision (such as the "comparable" catch-all in §2(1)(B)), it is unclear whether the rest of the prohibition survives.
