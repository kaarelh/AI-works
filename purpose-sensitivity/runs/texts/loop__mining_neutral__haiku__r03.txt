RESPONSE:
Below are the drafting problems I found in the text as amended on October 6. I've listed them in bill order, with global issues at the end. Items that depend on other statutes or on how the Secretary or courts would read a term are marked as interpretive.

**Section 2 (Definitions)**

1. **§2(1)(A):** The 10,000-ton threshold applies "by a single vessel," so several vessels each under the threshold could together recover far more without being covered. The text also doesn't say whether the threshold is measured per calendar year, per operation, or across affiliated companies.

2. **§2(1)(A):** "Other mineral resources" is undefined, and the text doesn't say whether tonnage is measured wet or dry or as ore or as metal content. It is unclear whether cobalt crusts, seafloor massive sulfides, or sand are covered.

3. **§2(1)(A):** The 200-meter depth floor excludes all shallower seabed activity, and the text doesn't say where depth is measured (at the point of recovery, or the deepest point of an operation).

4. **§2(1)(B):** The "Secretary" is not identified (the Commerce Secretary is the likely reading, but the text doesn't say so). The Secretary's power to find activities "comparable" in risk has no criteria, notice, hearing, or reasons requirement, which invites vagueness and delegation challenges. It is also unclear whether a determination must come before enforcement or can apply retroactively.

5. **§2(2):** "U.S. person" covers citizens and State-chartered corporations but not LLCs, partnerships, trusts, or foreign-incorporated affiliates of U.S. companies. Companies could structure around the "anywhere" reach in §3(a). "State" is also undefined (it's unclear whether D.C. and the territories are included).

6. **§2(3):** "Equipped for the recovery of seabed mineral resources" is undefined and could be read to exclude support or mixed-use vessels. The 5,000-gross-ton threshold also means smaller vessels that could exceed 10,000 tons a year fall outside the registry in §4.

7. **§2 (general):** Several key terms are undefined or only partly defined, including "person," "deep seabed," "seabed mineral resources," "exploration license," "recovery activities," "environmental assessment," and "engage in." Courts would have to construe each one, and the bill gives them little guidance.

**Section 3 (Prohibition)**

8. **§3(a):** "Engage in" doesn't say whether financing, investing, purchasing, contracting, or processing counts. The bill also contains no ban on importing, selling, or possessing minerals recovered in violation of the Act, which leaves a gap for buyers and financiers.

9. **§3(a):** "In the United States" is undefined. It's unclear whether it reaches the territorial sea, the EEZ, the extended continental shelf, or the high seas. Foreign-owned, foreign-flagged vessels operated abroad are reached only if their operator is a U.S. person (see item 5).

10. **§3(b) (interpretive):** "Exploration license" is undefined. The text doesn't say whether it means permits under the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act of 1980 (which, to my knowledge, NOAA administers; please verify), licenses from other countries, or International Seabed Authority contracts. It also doesn't say whether an exploration license authorizes commercial recovery at all.

11. **§3(b):** The grandfather clause covers "mining conducted under" a license but sets no volume cap, no sunset, no requirement that the license be valid, and no requirement that the holder be a U.S. person. It doesn't address transfers of licenses or expansion of existing operations.

12. **§3(c):** The "environmental assessment" exemption is the broadest gap in the bill. It has no limit on volume, duration, or sale of recovered material, and no permit, notice, or reporting requirement. Commercial-scale recovery could be labeled as assessment.

**Section 4 (Registry)**

13. **§4(a):** "Operator" is undefined, and the section sets no registration deadline, no required content, and no consequence for failing to register. Registration also doesn't authorize or deny anything.

14. **§4(b):** "Recovery activities" is undefined, and the section doesn't require any specific content, such as volumes, locations, depths, discharge, plume effects, or monitoring data. It sets no due dates, no public-availability requirement, and no verification or audit authority, so reports are effectively self-certified.

15. **§4 (enforcement):** Section 5 penalizes only violations of §3, so failing to register or filing a false or late report under §4 carries no stated penalty.

16. **§4 and §5:** The bill doesn't say whether registry or report data are public or whether businesses can withhold them as confidential commercial information. That affects both transparency and how the data can be used.

**Section 5 (Penalties)**

17. **§5(a):** No agency or DOJ is named to enforce the penalty. The text also doesn't specify the enforcement procedure (administrative or civil action), venue, or limitations period, and it doesn't say how "each day" is counted for continuous operations. It's unclear whether the $1,000,000 figure is a cap or a fixed amount, and the bill doesn't address inflation adjustment (staff should check whether the general federal inflation-adjustment rules apply).

18. **§5(a) vs. §8:** Penalties apply to violations "occurring on or after the date of enactment," but §3 doesn't take effect until 180 days later. The text is internally inconsistent and could be read to suggest liability begins immediately.

19. **§5(a):** The penalty reaches only "any person" who violates §3. It doesn't address officers, directors, or parent companies, so corporate structure could limit exposure.

20. **§5(b):** "Willfully" is undefined, and the section doesn't address attempts, conspiracy, or whether each day of continued mining is a separate offense. Corporate criminal liability is left to general law.

21. **§5 (remedies):** The bill contains no forfeiture or seizure of vessels, equipment, or recovered minerals, no express authority for injunctions to halt operations, no citizen-suit provision, and no whistleblower protection. These are significant enforcement gaps.

**Section 6 (International agreement)**

22. **§6:** "Shall seek to negotiate" is an obligation to try, not to conclude an agreement. "Not later than 18 months" is ambiguous (it may refer to starting or concluding negotiations), and the section has no consequence if no agreement is reached. "Equivalent" is undefined, and the text doesn't say whether an agreement would override §3 or whether it would require Senate consent.

**Section 7 (Duration)**

23. **§7(a) vs. §7(b):** These provisions conflict. Under §7(a), the prohibition stays in effect until Congress passes a joint resolution, but under §7(b), the entire Act ceases after 10 years regardless. The text doesn't say which controls, and read literally, the prohibition lapses at year 10 even without the joint resolution.

24. **§7(a):** "Serious harm" is undefined, and the bill sets no standard of proof for the finding. It also has no deadline for the National Academies report, no funding or request mechanism for it, and no statement of what the report's role is. The exit trigger may never be met.

25. **§7(a) (interpretive):** The bill doesn't say whether the joint resolution must be presented to the President under Article I, Section 7. A resolution that makes findings without presentment could face a legislative-veto challenge under *INS v. Chadha*. Staff should check whether drafters intended presentment.

26. **§7(b):** The 10-year sunset runs from enactment, not from the effective date, so the prohibition would last only about 9½ years. The section also lacks a savings clause preserving liability for pre-sunset violations and enforcement actions, and it says nothing about existing licensees, minerals already recovered, or the registry.

**Section 8 (Effective date)**

27. **§8:** The 180-day delay with no interim standstill means licensing and mining can continue, and may expand, during the window. Licenses issued after enactment aren't grandfathered under §3(b), but they also aren't prohibited until the effective date, which could encourage a licensing rush.

**Global issues**

28. **No findings or purpose section:** The bill doesn't state its justification. That matters in litigation, since courts often look to findings to evaluate whether a restriction is reasonable.

29. **No severability clause:** If a provision is struck down (for example, §2(1)(B) or §7(a)), the text doesn't say whether the rest of the Act survives.

30. **No statement on existing law:** The bill doesn't address the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act, existing NOAA permits, or the International Seabed Authority regime, and it contains no preemption clause or conforming amendments.

31. **No rulemaking, funding, or inspection authority:** NOAA is assigned registry duties but gets no rulemaking power, no appropriation, and no inspection or boarding authority.
