# S. 2817 drafting problems, by section

**Section 2 (Definitions)**

1. **§2(1)(A), per-vessel threshold.** The 10,000 t/year test applies to "a single vessel," so an operator can run several vessels, each under the cap, or spread vessels across affiliated entities, and none of it counts as "commercial." There is no aggregation rule for vessels under common ownership or control.

2. **§2(1)(A), volume-only definition.** "Commercial" is defined purely by tonnage, not by sale, profit motive, or intent to scale up. Pilot, test, or "demonstration" mining below the threshold is lawful even though it can still cause serious harm, and the threshold is far below the scale of a real commercial operation.

3. **§2(1)(A), measurement ambiguity.** The text doesn't say whether tonnage is wet or dry weight, material lifted, retained on board, or processed. It also doesn't say whether "per year" means a calendar year, a rolling 12 months, or a pro-rated period for partial-year operations. Nodules carry a lot of water, so the choice can swing compliance, and each ambiguity gives a defendant a litigation argument.

4. **§2(1)(A), undefined "recovery," "mineral resources," and "seabed."** It's unclear whether the bill covers cobalt crusts, seafloor massive sulfides, rare-earth muds, sediment, or subsoil extraction. It's also unclear whether "recovery" includes collection that is discarded or processed at sea rather than brought aboard.

5. **§2(1)(A), 200-meter depth test.** The bill doesn't say how depth is measured (water depth at the site, the mining tool, or the seabed surface) or what happens on terrain that crosses the line. Mining at shallower sites, including some hydrothermal systems and shelf areas, falls outside the definition entirely.

6. **§2(1)(B), "the Secretary."** The bill never says which Secretary. The determination is also purely discretionary, with no deadline, no required process, no duty to act, and a vague "comparable risks" standard. A future administration could simply never use it, and any use invites an arbitrary-and-capricious challenge.

7. **§2(2), "U.S. person."** The definition omits LLCs and other non-"corporation" entities, partnerships, trusts, territories (the bill says "State"), and U.S. nationals and permanent residents. It also omits foreign-incorporated subsidiaries controlled by U.S. parents, so reincorporating abroad or operating through an overseas affiliate escapes the extraterritorial reach.

8. **§2(3), "covered vessel."** The 5,000 gross ton floor and the "equipped for recovery" test let smaller vessels, or larger ones with equipment temporarily removed or installed, avoid registration. Support ships, platforms, and seabed crawlers or other non-vessel systems are not covered at all.

**Section 3 (Prohibition)**

9. **§3(a), jurisdictional reach.** "In the United States" is undefined, so it is unclear whether it covers the territorial sea, the EEZ, the outer continental shelf, or U.S.-flag vessels. A foreign person on a foreign vessel in U.S. waters may not be reached, and U.S.-flag vessels operated by non-U.S. persons abroad aren't clearly covered. A "vessel subject to the jurisdiction of the United States" hook would close some of this.

10. **§3(a), no ancillary conduct.** "Engage in" is undefined, and the bill doesn't prohibit financing, supplying, onshore processing, importing, or selling seabed minerals. Foreign-mined material can still be processed and sold in the U.S., and nothing bars agencies from issuing permits.

11. **Interaction with existing law.** The bill doesn't amend or repeal the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (administered by NOAA) or address OCSLA authorities. This leaves a possible conflict with the existing licensing and permitting regime and no instruction on pending applications. Please have counsel confirm the exact provisions and add conforming amendments.

12. **§3(b), scope of the grandfather clause.** The exemption covers "mining conducted under" a pre-enactment exploration license, but exploration licenses generally authorize exploration, not commercial recovery. The text could be read to permit commercial-scale extraction under an exploration label. It has no limit on area, volume, or activity, and no expiration.

13. **§3(b), rush and modification risk.** Nothing freezes licensing between now and enactment, so there's an incentive to rush licenses through. The text also doesn't bar renewals, extensions, amendments, expansions, or transfers of grandfathered licenses, or conversion into commercial permits. "Exploration license" is undefined and doesn't say which authority must have issued it.

14. **§3(c), environmental assessment exemption.** The exemption turns on the "purpose" of the activity, with no cap on tonnage, duration, area, or number of vessels, no independent or agency approval, and no bar on selling recovered material. A company could label bulk sampling or test mining as assessment, and a purpose test is hard to disprove.

**Section 4 (Registry)**

15. **§4(a), registration.** There is no deadline, no required content, no bar on operating unregistered, and no rulemaking authority. "Operator" is undefined, so ownership can be layered to avoid it, and "NOAA" is named without saying which official is responsible.

16. **§4(b), reporting.** Only "registered" operators must report, so an operator that never registers never reports. The bill sets no required content (tonnage, location, dates, methods), no verification or audit, no public disclosure, and no recordkeeping requirement. "Certified by the operator" is self-attestation only.

17. **§4, no monitoring or inspection authority.** Nothing authorizes inspections, observers, vessel tracking, or data access. Without independent monitoring, the registry can't detect unreported activity or confirm compliance with the tonnage threshold or the exemptions.

**Section 5 (Penalties)**

18. **§5(a) vs. §8, retroactivity conflict.** Civil penalties reach violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the Act doesn't take effect until 180 days later. That is internally inconsistent, and it invites due process and fair-notice challenges to any penalty for conduct before the effective date.

19. **§5(a), no enforcement mechanism.** The bill doesn't say who assesses penalties or what process applies (notice, hearing, appeal). It also doesn't authorize the Attorney General to sue, provide injunctive relief, vessel or equipment seizure or forfeiture, or disgorgement of proceeds, and it has no citizen-suit provision.

20. **§5, no penalties for registry violations.** Both subsections penalize only violations of §3. Failing to register, failing to report, or filing false reports carries no penalty under this Act.

21. **§5(b), "willfully."** Prosecutors would have to prove the defendant knew the conduct was unlawful, which is a high bar for complex corporate operations. The bill has no knowledge-based alternative and no explicit responsible-corporate-officer liability.

**Sections 6–8 (International, Duration, Effective Date)**

22. **§6, international agreement.** "Shall seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months" is ambiguous about what must happen by the deadline: the start of negotiations or a concluded agreement. "Equivalent" is undefined, there is no reporting to Congress, no consequence for missing the deadline, and no mention of the International Seabed Authority or of limits on U.S. sponsorship or support of foreign mining.

23. **§7(a), lifting the prohibition.** "Serious harm" is undefined, and "can be conducted without serious harm" is a low bar compared with a precautionary standard. The bill doesn't say who commissions the National Academies report, its scope, or its timeline, and it's unclear whether a joint resolution ends the prohibition automatically or just authorizes later action.

24. **§7(b), sunset.** The 10-year sunset contradicts §7(a)'s "until Congress finds" and means the prohibition expires automatically with no scientific finding at all. The bill has no savings clause, so pending enforcement actions and penalties could lapse, and it conflicts with our goal of a lasting prohibition.

25. **§8, 180-day gap.** Nothing restrains activity between enactment and the effective date. Companies can ramp up operations, stockpile, or move vessels, and the grandfather clause freezes in whatever licenses exist at enactment.

**General**

26. **Missing definitions.** Beyond those flagged above, "Secretary," "person," "vessel," "operator," "exploration license," and "environmental assessment" are all undefined or unspecified, each a point of dispute in litigation.

27. **Missing standard provisions.** The bill has no severability clause, findings or purpose section, general rulemaking authority, appropriations authorization, or judicial review or standing provisions. Without findings and explicit delegations, agency implementation is easier to challenge.

28. **Litigation exposure.** Curtailing existing or pending license rights may draw takings and due process claims, and the bill doesn't address vested rights or pending applications. I'd have counsel review this and consider express language on pending applications.

## Suggested priorities

If the office can only take a handful of fixes, I'd lead with these:

- **Sunset (#24):** it undermines the whole bill.
- **Grandfather and assessment exemptions (#12–14):** these are the biggest operational loopholes.
- **Per-vessel threshold and volume-only definition (#1–2):** these allow easy structuring around the ban.
- **Enforcement gaps (#18–20):** as written, penalties lack a mechanism and a clear start date.
- **"U.S. person" and jurisdictional reach (#7, #9):** these allow offshoring.

Please have counsel verify the statutory references (especially DSHMRA and OCSLA) before this goes to Senator Whitfield's office.
