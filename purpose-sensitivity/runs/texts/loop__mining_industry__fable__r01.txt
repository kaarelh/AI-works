RESPONSE:
Below is a section-by-section list. I've grouped items by the provision they arise from, and flagged at the end which ones I'd lead with. Note that a few of these cut both ways (a gap for the company can also be an exposure for individual employees), so I've marked those.

**SEC. 2(1)(A) — Tonnage definition**

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "by a single vessel."** The 10,000-ton threshold is measured per vessel, not per operator, project, or site. An operator can run several vessels each recovering just under 10,000 tons per year and never trigger the definition, no matter the aggregate.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "recovery" is undefined and the production chain involves multiple vessels.** In a nodule system the collector vehicle lifts material off the seabed, a riser delivers it to a production vessel, and a bulk carrier transships it. The bill does not say which of these "recovers" the nodules, and a defendant can argue the collector (arguably not a "vessel," or a vessel under 5,000 GT) is the only one recovering "from the seabed."

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "metric tons" with no wet/dry basis.** Nodules are roughly 25–35% water by weight as recovered. The bill does not say whether the threshold is measured wet, dry, or as processed, which makes the line both unenforceable and easy to argue around.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "per year" is undefined.** Calendar year, fiscal year, rolling 12 months, or the 12 months after the effective date are all plausible. Tonnage straddling the 180-day pre-effective window (Sec. 8) is unaddressed.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "or other mineral resources" is overbroad.** "Mineral resources" ordinarily includes oil, gas, sand, and gravel; a deepwater drilling unit at >200 m recovering more than 10,000 tons of hydrocarbons per year arguably meets the literal definition. That is a strong "unworkable as drafted" argument for the Commerce Committee.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "from the seabed" and "depths greater than 200 meters" are unmeasured.** The bill does not say whether "seabed" includes subsoil, or how depth is measured (chart datum, mean sea level, depth at the collector). Minor, but it compounds the vagueness problem given the criminal penalty.

**SEC. 2(1)(B) — Secretary's "comparable risk" determination**

7. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — "the Secretary" is never defined.** The only Secretary named in the Act is the Secretary of State (Sec. 6), and nothing says that is who is meant here. On its face the authority belongs to no one and cannot be exercised.

8. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — no standard, procedure, or review.** The determination requires no rulemaking, notice, publication, findings, or judicial review, and "comparable" risk to "deep-sea ecosystems" is undefined. An agency determination that converts lawful activity into a felony carrying 10 years' imprisonment, with no procedural safeguards, invites void-for-vagueness, fair-notice, and nondelegation challenges.

9. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — no temporal rule.** The bill does not say whether a determination applies prospectively only, so conduct could be swept in retroactively (or, read the other way, every determination can be contested as ex post facto when applied to ongoing operations).

**SEC. 2(2) — "U.S. person"**

10. **Sec. 2(2) — only "corporations" organized under State law are covered.** LLCs, limited partnerships, trusts, cooperatives, and joint ventures are not corporations. A State-law LLC, or a company reorganized as one, is not a U.S. person under this text.

11. **Sec. 2(2) — "State" excludes DC, territories, and federal charters.** A corporation organized under the laws of the District of Columbia, Puerto Rico, Guam, or by federal charter is not "organized under the laws of a State" as written. (Compare the standard definition in 1 U.S.C. § 1 and the DSHMRA, which the drafters did not use.)

12. **Sec. 2(2) — foreign subsidiaries and foreign-flagged operations are outside the definition.** A subsidiary incorporated abroad, even if 100% owned and controlled from Houston, is not a U.S. person, and nothing in the Act attributes the subsidiary's conduct to its parent. *Caution, cuts both ways:* individual U.S.-citizen officers and crew are U.S. persons and "engage in" is undefined, so employees could still face exposure (see item 16).

13. **Sec. 2(2) — lawful permanent residents are not covered.** The individual prong is limited to "citizen[s]," so green-card holders working for a foreign entity are outside the Act even while physically abroad.

**SEC. 2(3) — "Covered vessel"**

14. **Sec. 2(3) — 5,000 gross-ton threshold and "equipped for."** Vessels at or under 5,000 GT are never "covered," and "equipped for" is undefined (a vessel that can be fitted with recovery gear versus one carrying it). Combined with item 1, a fleet of smaller vessels escapes both the registry and the per-vessel tonnage trigger.

15. **Sec. 2(3) — the "covered vessel" definition does no work in Sec. 3.** The prohibition is tied to "commercial deep seabed mining," not to covered vessels, so a vessel can be covered (registered and reporting) while its activity is lawful, and vice versa. The registry therefore captures the wrong population and the two definitions are not harmonized.

**SEC. 3 — Prohibition**

16. **Sec. 3(a) — "engage in" is undefined.** Read narrowly, it reaches only the entity physically recovering the minerals, leaving financing, chartering, equipment supply, processing, transport, and sale uncovered. Read broadly, it reaches every employee aboard, which is a fair-notice problem the Committee should have to confront.

17. **Sec. 3(a) — "in the United States" is undefined.** Mining at >200 m occurs at sea, and the Act does not say whether the EEZ, the outer continental shelf, or U.S.-flagged vessels on the high seas are "in the United States." If they are not, the first prong of the prohibition has almost no application, and foreign nationals and foreign companies directing operations from U.S. offices and ports are untouched.

18. **Sec. 3(a) — no reach to U.S.-flagged vessels, U.S. ports, or U.S. financing.** The bill has no flag-state prong, no port-entry restriction, no import ban on recovered minerals, and no prohibition on U.S. persons facilitating foreign operations. Every standard jurisdictional hook in comparable statutes (e.g., the Magnuson-Stevens Act, DSHMRA) is absent.

19. **Sec. 3(b) — grandfathers "mining conducted under an exploration license," with no issuer specified.** The exemption is not limited to NOAA/DSHMRA licenses, so a pre-enactment exploration license from any national authority (or an ISA contract sponsored by another state) appears to qualify. It is also the single largest window: operations that already hold such a license are exempt as written.

20. **Sec. 3(b) — exploration licenses do not authorize commercial recovery, and recovery permits are not mentioned.** Under DSHMRA, exploration is licensed and commercial recovery is separately permitted; the exemption references only the former. The result is incoherent: it exempts "mining" under an instrument that does not authorize mining, and says nothing about a commercial recovery permit issued before enactment.

21. **Sec. 3(b) — no cut-off for renewals, extensions, amendments, or transfers.** A license "issued before" enactment but renewed, expanded in area, or transferred to a new holder afterward is still, on the text, a license issued before enactment. The exemption also has no tonnage cap.

22. **Sec. 3(b) — uses "mining" rather than the defined term.** Sec. 3(a) prohibits "commercial deep seabed mining"; Sec. 3(b) exempts "mining." The broader, undefined word in the exemption can be read to cover more than the prohibition covers.

23. **Sec. 3(b)/Sec. 8 — licensing rush and a 180-day gap.** The grandfather date is enactment, but the prohibition does not bite until 180 days later (Sec. 8). Licenses obtained in the interval are not grandfathered, yet operations during that interval are not unlawful either, and tonnage recovered then is unregulated.

24. **Sec. 3(c) — "for the purpose of environmental assessment" has no limits.** There is no tonnage cap, no approval requirement, no definition of environmental assessment, and no rule for mixed-purpose activity. A pilot or test-mining campaign conducted to generate environmental baseline and impact data fits the exemption as written, at any scale.

25. **Sec. 3 — no attempt, conspiracy, aiding-and-abetting, or evasion provisions.** Structuring operations to stay under thresholds, or arranging for a non-U.S. person to do the recovery, is not itself a violation.

**SEC. 4 — Registry**

26. **Sec. 4 — no penalty for failure to register or report.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3. Sec. 4 is a duty without a sanction.

27. **Sec. 4 — "operator" is undefined and there is no deadline.** Registration has no due date, "operator" could mean owner, charterer, or manager, and the quarterly reports have no start date or form. NOAA is given no rulemaking authority, inspection authority, or appropriation to run the registry.

28. **Sec. 4 — no jurisdictional limit.** Read literally, every operator of every >5,000 GT recovery-equipped vessel in the world must register with NOAA, which is unenforceable and will be cited as overreach.

29. **Sec. 4(b) — reports describe "recovery activities" the Act purports to prohibit.** The reporting requirement assumes ongoing recovery, which undercuts the bill's characterization as a prohibition and raises self-incrimination questions for any operator whose reports would document a Sec. 3 violation.

**SEC. 5 — Penalties**

30. **Sec. 5(a) — penalty applies to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the Act is not effective for 180 days (Sec. 8).** The two provisions conflict: there cannot be a Sec. 3 violation before Sec. 3 is in effect. If the Sec. 5(a) language controls, the bill imposes retroactive civil liability for conduct that was lawful when it occurred.

31. **Sec. 5(a) — "each day on which the violation occurs" is incoherent with an annual tonnage threshold.** The offense is defined by exceeding 10,000 tons "per year," so arguably it "occurs" only on the day the threshold is crossed, yielding a single day's penalty. Alternatively every day of the year counts, including days before the threshold was reached; the text supports both readings.

32. **Sec. 5 — no enforcement agency, procedure, or forum.** The bill does not say who assesses the civil penalty (NOAA, Coast Guard, DOJ), whether it is administrative or judicial, what the standard of review is, or whether vessels or recovered minerals are subject to seizure or forfeiture.

33. **Sec. 5(b) — "willfully" plus the Sec. 2(1)(B) determination.** Criminal liability requires willfulness, but the scope of the offense can turn on an unpublished agency determination with no notice requirement, so willful violation of a 2(1)(B)-based offense would be very difficult to prove.

**SEC. 6 — International agreement**

34. **Sec. 6 — "seek to negotiate ... not later than 18 months" is ambiguous and aspirational.** It is unclear whether negotiations must begin or an agreement must be concluded within 18 months, and nothing follows if neither happens. "Prohibitions equivalent to those in section 3" is also indeterminate given that Sec. 3 includes the exemptions and the undefined Secretary's determination.

**SEC. 7 — Duration**

35. **Sec. 7(a) and 7(b) conflict.** 7(a) says the prohibition remains in effect "until" Congress makes a finding; 7(b) says the whole Act ceases after 10 years. If no finding is ever made, the text does not say which controls, and the "until" clause can be read as overriding the sunset.

36. **Sec. 7(a) — the condition may be impossible to satisfy.** Nothing in the Act commissions, funds, or sets a deadline for a National Academies report, so the predicate for the congressional finding may never exist. "Without serious harm" is undefined.

37. **Sec. 7(a) — the joint-resolution mechanism is legally superfluous.** A joint resolution is ordinary legislation subject to presentment, so Congress can already repeal or amend the Act at any time; the provision adds a procedural hurdle with no legal effect but creates an argument that other repeal routes are foreclosed.

38. **Sec. 7(b) — sunset is measured from enactment, not the effective date, and ends the entire Act.** The prohibition therefore runs about 9.5 years, and the registry, definitions, and penalty provisions expire with it. (Pending enforcement is likely preserved by 1 U.S.C. § 109, but the drafters did not include a savings clause.)

**Structural / omitted provisions**

39. **No treatment of DSHMRA.** The bill neither repeals nor amends the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (30 U.S.C. § 1401 et seq.), under which NOAA retains authority to issue exploration licenses and commercial recovery permits. Implied repeal is disfavored; the result is two active federal regimes pointing in opposite directions, with NOAA still obligated to process applications.

40. **No definition of "person," "vessel," "mining," "United States," "exploration license," or "Secretary."** The Act leans on the Dictionary Act for some of these and leaves others wholly undefined, which is unusual for a statute carrying a 10-year criminal penalty.

41. **No severability clause.** If the Sec. 2(1)(B) delegation or Sec. 5(a) retroactivity clause is struck, the Act is silent on whether the rest survives.

42. **No rulemaking authority for any agency.** No one can issue regulations to fill the gaps above, so the ambiguities will be resolved only through litigation.

**Items I'd lead with for the markup:** 1, 7, 10–12, 17, 19–20, 24, 30, 35, and 39. Items 5, 28, and 42 are the strongest "unworkable as drafted" points for members who are not focused on seabed mining specifically. Items 12 and 16 should be handled carefully in any public-facing material because they implicate individual exposure for staff.

This is a textual analysis to support the legal team's review, not a substitute for their judgment on litigation risk or on how a court would resolve the ambiguities.
