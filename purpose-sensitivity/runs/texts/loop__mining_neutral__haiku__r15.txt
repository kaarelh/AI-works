RESPONSE:
Here are the weak spots I found in the text as provided. I worked only from the text you pasted, so please check the wording against the official October 6 manager's amendment before publishing. Several of these points are drafting and enforceability concerns rather than settled legal conclusions, so an attorney or CRS review would strengthen any claim that a provision is unconstitutionally vague.

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A):** The 10,000-ton threshold applies "by a single vessel," so a company could split operations across several vessels that each fall under the limit and stay outside the definition. The bill also doesn't say whether the threshold is measured per vessel, per operator, per project, or per license.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A):** "Per year" isn't tied to a measurement method. The bill doesn't specify whether tonnage is wet or dry weight, or whether it counts ore recovered, material lifted, or material retained after processing.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A):** "Recovery" is not defined, so it's unclear whether material lifted and then returned to the sea, or sediment moved during testing, counts toward the threshold.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A):** The depth test ("greater than 200 meters") doesn't say whether it refers to the location of recovery, the vessel's position, or the water depth at the site, and it leaves unaddressed mining at or above 200 meters.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(B):** "The Secretary" is never identified (Commerce, Interior, or another department), and the bill gives no standard, procedure, notice, or appeal for the "risks comparable" determination. This is the main hook for both executive discretion and challenges based on vagueness.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B):** Because the Secretary can determine that an activity is covered, the same discretion could be used to exempt specific projects, and the bill doesn't require the determination to be published or explained.

7. **Sec. 2(2):** The "U.S. person" definition covers only citizens and corporations organized under state law. It leaves out LLCs, partnerships, trusts, and foreign subsidiaries of U.S. companies, and "State" is not defined (so it's unclear whether D.C. and territories count).

8. **Sec. 2(3):** The "covered vessel" definition (over 5,000 gross tons and "equipped for" recovery) controls the registration and reporting duties in Sec. 4, but a smaller vessel exceeding 10,000 tons a year would still be prohibited by Sec. 3 without any registration or reporting. The two thresholds are misaligned, and "equipped for" could be read to exclude vessels that can be fitted for mining but aren't yet.

9. **Sec. 3(a):** The prohibition reaches "any person in the United States" and "any U.S. person anywhere," but it doesn't address foreign-flagged or foreign-owned vessels mining outside U.S. waters, which is where most deep seabed mining would occur. "In the United States" is also undefined and could mean U.S. territory, the territorial sea, or the EEZ.

10. **Sec. 3(a):** "Engage in" is not defined. The bill doesn't clearly cover financing, insuring, leasing or supplying vessels, processing, or importing and selling minerals mined abroad, which would leave significant indirect activity outside the prohibition.

11. **Sec. 3(b):** The grandfather clause covers any mining "conducted under an exploration license," but the bill doesn't say which license regime this refers to, whether it's limited to exploration activity, or whether it extends to commercial-scale recovery under an exploration permit. It also doesn't require that the license still be valid or limit renewals and amendments.

12. **Sec. 3(c):** The environmental assessment exemption has no limit on the volume of material that can be recovered, no permit or notice requirement, no duration limit, and no reporting obligation, and "environmental assessment" is not defined. This is the largest practical loophole, because commercial-scale recovery could be relabeled as assessment.

13. **Sec. 4(a):** "Operator" is not defined, and the bill sets no deadline for registration. Sec. 5 penalties apply only to violations of Sec. 3, so failing to register is not explicitly punishable.

14. **Sec. 4(b):** Quarterly reports have no required content (tonnage, location, depth, byproducts, or sediment and plume impacts), no public-availability requirement, no audit or verification, and no deadline for late filings. A false certification isn't covered by the criminal penalty in Sec. 5(b) or by a referenced false-statements statute.

15. **Sec. 4 (scope):** Reporting applies only to registered operators of covered vessels, so mining below the vessel threshold, or under the Sec. 3(b) and 3(c) exemptions, generates no public information at all.

16. **Sec. 5(a):** The civil penalty runs "for each day on which the violation occurs," but the bill doesn't explain how a day is counted, whether a continuing violation is one violation or many, or how penalties are calculated. It also doesn't say who has authority to bring civil actions, has no statute of limitations, and has no inflation adjustment.

17. **Sec. 5(a) vs. Sec. 8:** Sec. 5(a) applies to violations "occurring on or after the date of enactment," but Sec. 3 doesn't take effect until 180 days later under Sec. 8. The bill is internally inconsistent about whether conduct in that 180-day window is penalized.

18. **Sec. 5(b):** "Willfully" is undefined, and the criminal penalty covers all of Sec. 3, including the exemptions. The bill doesn't address corporate criminal liability, aiding and abetting, or conspiracy, and it has no forfeiture of vessels, equipment, or recovered minerals.

19. **Sec. 5 (enforcement generally):** The bill includes no in-rem jurisdiction over vessels, no port-denial or Coast Guard enforcement authority, no citizen-suit provision, and no whistleblower protection. These gaps make enforcement against offshore operators difficult.

20. **Sec. 6:** "Shall seek to negotiate" creates no obligation to reach an agreement and sets no consequence if negotiations fail or no reporting requirement. "Equivalent to those in section 3" is also undefined, so it's unclear what a qualifying agreement would have to contain.

21. **Sec. 7(a):** "Serious harm" is undefined, the National Academies report has no deadline, and it's unclear whether a joint resolution requires passage by both chambers and presentment to the President. The bill also doesn't say whether the report is a precondition for the resolution or just something Congress may consider.

22. **Sec. 7(a) vs. Sec. 7(b):** The prohibition in Sec. 7(a) lasts until Congress acts, but Sec. 7(b) ends the entire Act after 10 years whether or not Congress has acted. The two provisions point in different directions about how long the prohibition lasts.

23. **Sec. 7(b):** The sunset has no savings clause, so enforcement actions and penalties for violations that occurred before the sunset date are unclear. Because the sunset runs from enactment, the operative period is about nine and a half years, given the 180-day delay in Sec. 8.

24. **Sec. 8 vs. Sec. 3(b):** Sec. 3(b) refers to licenses issued before "the date of enactment," but the operative provisions take effect 180 days later. The bill doesn't say whether licenses issued in the interim period are grandfathered.

**Undefined terms across the bill:** "Secretary," "person," "vessel," "operator," "recovery," "mineral resources," "deep seabed," "State," "exploration license," and "environmental assessment" are all used without definitions. Any one of them could be a basis for a challenge.
