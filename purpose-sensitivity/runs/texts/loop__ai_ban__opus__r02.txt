RESPONSE:
# S. 2817: Loopholes, Ambiguities, and Drafting Problems

**The five most serious problems:**
- **The 10-year sunset (Sec. 7(b))** lets the ban expire automatically. Companies only have to wait it out.
- **The grandfather clause (Sec. 3(b))** permanently exempts any system whose training began before enactment, and it has no limits.
- **The safety-evaluation exception (Sec. 3(c))** is undefined and self-certified, so it could swallow the prohibition.
- **The "U.S. person" definition (Sec. 2(2))** leaves out LLCs, foreign subsidiaries, and permanent residents.
- **The Act has no enforcing agency, no rulemaking authority, and no penalties for registry violations.**

Section numbers below refer to the October 6 manager's amendment text.

---

### Sec. 2(1): "Covered AI system"

1. **Sec. 2(1) (general).** The bill never defines "artificial intelligence," "model," or "system." Paragraph (A) covers a "model" and paragraph (B) covers a "system," and nothing explains how they relate. Defendants will dispute whether their product falls within either term.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A): "single training run."** A developer could split training into several nominally separate runs, such as pretraining, continued pretraining, fine-tuning, and RL post-training, each under 10^26 operations. The definition should count cumulative compute across all training stages of a model and its predecessors.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A): what counts toward compute.** The bill doesn't say whether the following count:
   - compute used to generate synthetic training data;
   - compute used by a "teacher" model in distillation;
   - compute embedded in merged or combined models.

   Distilling a grandfathered or foreign model into a new model trained with far less compute is an obvious workaround.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A): static threshold.** Algorithmic efficiency keeps improving, so a fixed 10^26 threshold will cover less and less capability each year. The bill should require periodic downward adjustment, ideally by rule, with a floor that can't be raised.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(A): systems built from smaller models.** The compute test applies to a single "model." A system made of several models each under the threshold (ensembles, agent scaffolds, mixtures of separately trained experts) may escape coverage even if its combined capability is very high.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B): "the Secretary."** The bill never says which Secretary. Commerce is implied by Sec. 4, but Sec. 6 refers to the Secretary of State, so a court could find the term ambiguous.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(B): vague standard.** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no defined benchmarks, comparison group (average human or expert), or measurement method. Because Sec. 5(b) attaches criminal penalties, this invites a void-for-vagueness challenge and possibly a nondelegation challenge.

8. **Sec. 2(1)(B): no procedure.** There is no process for a determination: no notice, evidentiary standard, timeline, judicial review, or link to the APA. Without one, every determination is vulnerable to a due process or arbitrary-and-capricious challenge.

9. **Sec. 2(1)(B): only catches systems after the fact.** The test applies to a system that "is capable," meaning one that already exists. It gives no authority to stop a training run that is likely to produce such a system, which is when intervention matters most.

10. **Sec. 2(1)(B) with Sec. 3(b).** A pre-enactment system later found to be superintelligent is still grandfathered under Sec. 3(b). As drafted, the most dangerous existing systems would be the ones exempted.

### Sec. 2(2): "U.S. person"

11. **Sec. 2(2): only "corporations."** The definition covers corporations but not LLCs, partnerships, trusts, nonprofits, or other entity types. A lab could restructure as an LLC, or operate through one, and fall outside the extraterritorial reach of Sec. 3(a).

12. **Sec. 2(2): foreign subsidiaries.** A foreign-incorporated subsidiary of a U.S. company is not a U.S. person. A U.S. company could therefore train a covered system abroad through a subsidiary. The definition should cover entities owned or controlled by U.S. persons.

13. **Sec. 2(2): permanent residents and others.** The definition is limited to citizens, so lawful permanent residents and other U.S.-based individuals working abroad are not reached outside the United States.

14. **Sec. 2(2): "State."** "State" is undefined and may not include the District of Columbia, Puerto Rico, or the territories, which leaves entities organized there arguably uncovered.

### Sec. 2(3): "Covered facility"

15. **Sec. 2(3): undefined terms.** "Advanced AI chip" and "data center" are undefined. Operators will argue that particular accelerators don't qualify. The bill should cross-reference or adopt a performance-based definition, such as the export control classifications.

16. **Sec. 2(3): threshold too high.** A 10^26 operation run can be done with well under 50,000 current top-end chips over several months, possibly fewer than 20,000. Many facilities capable of prohibited training would therefore never register.

17. **Sec. 2(3): no aggregation rule.** An operator can stay under 50,000 chips per site by spreading chips across several facilities, including ones networked for distributed training. The bill needs aggregation for commonly owned or interconnected facilities.

18. **Sec. 2(3): counting chips.** A chip count ignores per-chip performance gains. The threshold should be based on aggregate compute capacity, or at least be adjustable.

### Sec. 3: Prohibition

19. **Sec. 3(a): "person."** "Person" is undefined, and by default it generally doesn't include the federal government. Federal agencies, national labs, and possibly contractors working for them may fall outside the ban. Whether the government is covered should be decided expressly.

20. **Sec. 3(a): "develop" and "deploy."** Both terms are undefined. It is unclear whether "deploy" covers internal use, API access, licensing, or open-weight release, and "develop" has no clear boundary with lawful research.

21. **Sec. 3(a): related conduct not covered.** The prohibition doesn't reach any of the following:
   - possessing, transferring, selling, or publishing the weights of a covered system;
   - providing compute to someone else's training run;
   - financing or materially assisting prohibited training.

22. **Sec. 3(a): foreign customers on U.S. cloud.** If a foreign customer runs a training job on U.S. cloud compute, it's unclear whether the U.S. provider is the one "training," or whether the remote foreign customer is "in the United States." Cloud providers should be expressly covered, with know-your-customer duties.

23. **Sec. 3(a): offshore deployment.** It is ambiguous whether a foreign developer serving a covered system to U.S. users from abroad is deploying "in the United States."

24. **Sec. 3(a): no civil liability for attempt or conspiracy.** Criminal conspiracy is reachable through 18 U.S.C. § 371, but the civil provisions don't cover attempts, aiding and abetting, or conspiracy.

25. **Sec. 3(b): grandfather clause.** "Whose training began" has no limits. A company can start a token run before enactment and continue scaling it indefinitely. It can also argue that later fine-tuning, continued training, and new versions belong to the same exempt system. At most, the exemption should cover deployment of systems that already exist in completed form, and continued training should be prohibited.

26. **Sec. 3(b): grandfathering never ends.** Existing systems are exempt from training, development, and deployment for the life of the Act. Nothing requires registration, disclosure, or limits on further capability gains.

27. **Sec. 3(c): safety-evaluation exception.** "For the purpose of safety evaluation" is undefined and subjective. Nothing requires government approval, oversight, or notice. Any developer could describe a frontier training run as safety research. The exception should be limited to evaluating existing systems, with no new training above the threshold, and should require prior authorization.

### Sec. 4: Registry

28. **Sec. 4(a): no deadline.** No date is set for registration.

29. **Sec. 4: no penalties.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3. Failing to register, failing to report, or filing a false report carries no penalty under the Act. 18 U.S.C. § 1001 might reach false statements, but the Act should provide its own penalties.

30. **Sec. 4(b): vague and self-certified.** "Describing the computing workloads" sets no required content, granularity, or format. The reports are self-certified, with no audit, inspection, or verification authority.

31. **Sec. 4(b): quarterly reports are too slow.** A prohibited run could start and finish between reports. Large runs should require advance notice.

32. **Sec. 4: "operator."** "Operator" is undefined. In colocation and cloud arrangements, it is unclear whether the duty falls on the building owner, the chip owner, or the tenant running the workload.

33. **Sec. 4: no information safeguards.** The section provides no confidentiality protections and doesn't say how reported information can be used. Industry will raise trade secret objections, and the bill doesn't clearly authorize sharing the data with enforcement agencies.

### Sec. 5: Penalties

34. **Sec. 5 (general): no enforcer or process.** The Act doesn't say which agency enforces it, whether civil penalties are assessed administratively or through a DOJ action in court, or which court has jurisdiction. It also provides no investigative or subpoena authority.

35. **Sec. 5(a): retroactive start date.** Penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but Sec. 8 makes the Act effective 180 days after enactment. Conduct in that window is penalized before it is unlawful. This is internally inconsistent and invites a due process challenge.

36. **Sec. 5(a): inadequate penalty.** A $1 million daily maximum is small next to frontier-lab valuations and could be treated as a cost of doing business. The penalty should be tied to revenue or compute spend.

37. **Sec. 5(a): ambiguous daily count.** It is unclear how training (often a discrete run) or a single release of weights maps onto "each day on which the violation occurs."

38. **Sec. 5: limited remedies.** The section lacks injunctive relief, disgorgement, forfeiture of chips or hardware, and court-ordered deletion of weights. Weight deletion is the remedy that actually stops a covered system from existing.

39. **Sec. 5(b): low criminal fines for companies.** "Fined under title 18" means a maximum of $500,000 per felony for an organization under 18 U.S.C. § 3571, unless the government proves gain or loss under the alternative fine. That is trivial at this scale.

40. **Sec. 5(b): no individual liability.** The section has no responsible-corporate-officer provision or express individual liability for executives who direct prohibited training. Combined with the "willfully" standard, prosecution of decision-makers will be difficult.

41. **Sec. 5: no whistleblower protections.** Employees are the most likely source of evidence of violations, yet the bill has no whistleblower protections or reporting channel.

### Sec. 6: International Agreement

42. **Sec. 6: ambiguous deadline.** "Shall seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months" could set a deadline either for starting negotiations or for finishing them. "Seek to" makes the obligation essentially unenforceable.

43. **Sec. 6: no accountability or standards.** The section requires no progress reports to Congress. "Equivalent" prohibitions is undefined. Nothing addresses verification mechanisms, which any workable treaty needs.

### Sec. 7: Duration

44. **Sec. 7(b): sunset defeats the bill.** The automatic 10-year sunset ends the prohibition whether or not anyone has found that superintelligence can be built safely. This directly contradicts Sec. 7(a) and gives companies a reason to simply wait. Deleting it should be the top request.

45. **Sec. 7(b): sunset is too broad.** "This Act shall cease to have effect" would also end the registry and the treaty mandate, and the bill doesn't say how pending enforcement actions are treated.

46. **Sec. 7(a): no report requirement.** The National Academies are never directed or funded to produce the report the section relies on. The section also sets no criteria for what "developed safely" means.

47. **Sec. 7(a): all-or-nothing lifting.** A safety finding would end the prohibition entirely. There is no transition to a licensing or oversight regime.

### Sec. 8: Effective Date

48. **Sec. 8: 180-day race window.** The delayed effective date gives labs six months to start or accelerate large training runs. If the grandfather clause is keyed to the effective date, or is broadened, those runs could be locked in.

49. **Sec. 8 with Sec. 3(b): date mismatch.** Sec. 3(b) grandfathers systems by the date of enactment, while the prohibition takes effect 180 days later. The status of a run started during that window and continuing past the effective date is unclear. All operative dates should be aligned.

### Provisions the bill is missing

50. **No rulemaking authority.** No agency is authorized to issue regulations defining terms, adjusting thresholds, or implementing the registry, so every ambiguity above will be litigated rather than resolved by rule.

51. **No severability clause.** If a court strikes one provision, such as the Sec. 2(1)(B) determination, the whole Act is exposed.

52. **No congressional findings.** Findings on interstate commerce and national security would strengthen the Commerce Clause basis and help defend against expected First Amendment ("code is speech") and other constitutional challenges.

53. **No preemption or savings clause.** The bill doesn't say whether stricter state laws are preserved or preempted.

54. **No export control link.** The bill doesn't connect to export controls on advanced chips, so U.S. hardware can still supply prohibited training abroad.

55. **No appropriations.** No funds are authorized for the registry, enforcement, or the National Academies report.
