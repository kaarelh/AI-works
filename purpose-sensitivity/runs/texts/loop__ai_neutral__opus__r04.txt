# S. 2817 (as amended Oct. 6, 2026): Loopholes, Ambiguities, and Drafting Problems

These are vulnerabilities on the face of the text. Many are "could be argued" points rather than settled legal outcomes. For a nonpartisan explainer, it may help to frame them as questions the committee may face and to seek comment from the sponsors' offices, since some may be addressed at markup.

## Definitions (Sec. 2)

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A): "single training run."** A developer could split training into multiple runs, each under 10^26 operations, or build on a smaller base model through continued pretraining, fine-tuning, or distillation, and argue that no single run crossed the line.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A): fixed compute threshold.** The 10^26 figure is hard-coded, and no agency can update it. As algorithms become more efficient, models of comparable capability could be trained below the threshold, so the line may grow less meaningful over time.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A): counting method unspecified.** The bill counts "integer or floating-point operations" but doesn't say how. It doesn't address mixed precision or whether operations from data generation, failed runs, or reinforcement learning stages count. Developers could choose the most favorable accounting.

4. **Sec. 2(1): "artificial intelligence" undefined.** Neither "artificial intelligence model" nor "artificial intelligence system" is defined. Clause (A) also covers a "model," while Sec. 3 prohibits work on a "system," which leaves unclear whether products built on a model are covered.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(B): "the Secretary" unidentified.** The bill never says which Cabinet secretary makes capability determinations. Sec. 4 points to Commerce, but Sec. 2 does not say so. An action could be challenged as taken by the wrong official.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B): vague capability standard.** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no defined metrics, benchmarks, or procedure. Because violations carry criminal penalties (Sec. 5(b)), this clause is exposed to a void-for-vagueness challenge, and possibly a nondelegation challenge, since Congress gives the Secretary little guidance.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(B): after-the-fact determination.** The bill provides no advance ruling process, notice, or appeal. A developer may learn its system is "covered" only after training, which raises fair-notice and due process problems, especially for criminal liability.

8. **Sec. 2(2): narrow "U.S. person."** The definition omits lawful permanent residents and covers only corporations organized under State law. That leaves out LLCs and partnerships (arguably), entities organized under federal or D.C. law (since "State" is undefined), and, most importantly, foreign subsidiaries of U.S. companies. A U.S. firm could do the prohibited work through an offshore affiliate.

9. **Sec. 2(3): "covered facility" terms undefined.** Neither "advanced AI chips" nor "data center" is defined. A fixed chip count is easy to avoid by spreading hardware across several sites with 50,000 chips or fewer each, or by relying on newer chips that do more per unit.

10. **Sec. 2(3) and Sec. 4: registry doesn't match the prohibition.** Facility coverage depends on chip count, while the prohibition depends on compute or capability. Training spread across many smaller or foreign facilities could cross 10^26 without any registered facility ever being involved.

## Prohibition (Sec. 3)

11. **Sec. 3(a): key verbs undefined.** "Train," "develop," and "deploy" are not defined. It's unclear whether "develop" reaches preparatory research or publishing techniques (which could also raise First Amendment arguments), or whether "deploy" covers offering a foreign-hosted model to U.S. users or a U.S. company merely using one through an API.

12. **Sec. 3(a): territorial ambiguity.** "Person" and "United States" are undefined. The bill doesn't say whether someone physically in the U.S. who remotely directs a training run on foreign servers is training "in the United States," or whether a foreign company with no U.S. presence is reached.

13. **Sec. 3(b): broad grandfather clause.** Any system whose training "began" before enactment is exempt from all of Sec. 3(a): training, development, and deployment, with no time limit. This invites a rush to start training runs before enactment, and companies could argue that continued training or new versions of a grandfathered system are also exempt.

14. **Sec. 3(b): "began" undefined.** Without a definition, a token run started before enactment could be cited to shelter a much larger effort that continues long afterward.

15. **Sec. 3(c): open-ended safety-evaluation exception.** The exception covers any "activities conducted for the purpose of safety evaluation." It sets no limits on who qualifies, no approval or oversight, and no restriction on what happens to the system afterward. Companies could describe frontier training and deployment as safety evaluation, and the exception could swallow the rule.

## Registry (Sec. 4)

16. **Sec. 4: no penalty for violating the registry.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3. Failing to register, failing to report, or filing an inaccurate report carries no penalty under this Act (though 18 U.S.C. § 1001 on false statements might apply).

17. **Sec. 4: missing basics.** The section sets no registration deadline, doesn't define "operator," and says nothing about what reports must contain beyond "describing the computing workloads." It also lacks verification, inspection, and confidentiality provisions, so reports could be vague, and companies may resist filing out of concern for trade secrets.

## Enforcement and Penalties (Sec. 5)

18. **Sec. 5: no designated enforcer.** No agency is named to enforce the Act, assess or sue for civil penalties, investigate, issue subpoenas, or write rules. Nothing in the Act grants rulemaking authority, so terms like "advanced AI chips" can't be clarified by regulation.

19. **Sec. 5(a) and Sec. 8: penalties may reach pre-effective-date conduct.** Civil penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the Act takes effect 180 days after enactment. The provisions conflict, and penalizing conduct during that window could face due process challenges.

20. **Sec. 5(a): unclear "each day" counting.** A training run is a discrete act, while deployment is ongoing, and the bill doesn't say how violation-days are counted. A maximum of $1 million per day may also be small relative to the value of a frontier model, so some firms could treat it as a cost of doing business.

21. **Sec. 5(b): criminal liability tied to vague definitions.** Criminal liability for "willful" violations rests on the undefined terms above, particularly Sec. 2(1)(B). That strengthens vagueness challenges and may make prosecutions hard to win.

## International Agreement (Sec. 6)

22. **Sec. 6: weak, ambiguous mandate.** "Shall seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months" doesn't say whether negotiations must begin or conclude within 18 months, and only an effort is required. There is no consequence if no agreement is reached, "equivalent" is undefined, and any treaty would separately need Senate ratification.

23. **Sec. 6: foreign development unaddressed.** Apart from the Sec. 6 negotiation mandate, the bill contains no export controls, import restrictions, or limits on using foreign-trained models. Development abroad would continue unaffected while negotiations proceed.

## Duration and Effective Date (Sec. 7 and Sec. 8)

24. **Sec. 7(a) vs. Sec. 7(b): conflicting end dates.** Subsection (a) says the prohibition stays in effect "until" Congress finds otherwise, but subsection (b) ends the entire Act after 10 years regardless. The "until" language is effectively overridden, and readers could misunderstand the prohibition as indefinite.

25. **Sec. 7(a): no report is ever commissioned.** Termination depends on a National Academies report, but the bill doesn't direct, fund, or schedule one. Also, a joint resolution must be signed by the President, so this is functionally no different from passing a new law.

26. **Sec. 7(b) and Sec. 8: shorter real duration.** The sunset runs from enactment, while the Act takes effect 180 days later, so the prohibition is actually in force for about 9.5 years.

27. **Sec. 7(b): no savings clause.** When "this Act shall cease to have effect," it's unclear whether pending prosecutions or penalty actions for earlier violations survive. The general savings statute (1 U.S.C. § 109) addresses repeal, and how it applies to an expiring statute is less certain. Defendants could try to run out the clock.

28. **Sec. 8 vs. Sec. 3(b): mismatched dates.** The grandfather clause is keyed to enactment, but the prohibition starts 180 days later. A system whose training begins during that window isn't grandfathered, yet no prohibition applies to it until the effective date. This creates uncertainty about whether completing or deploying it after the effective date is a violation.

## Structural Omissions

29. **No preemption provision.** The bill doesn't say whether it displaces, sets a floor for, or coexists with state AI laws, which invites litigation over overlapping rules.

30. **No severability clause.** Courts generally presume severability anyway, but an explicit clause would reduce the risk that striking a vulnerable provision such as Sec. 2(1)(B) jeopardizes the rest.

31. **No whistleblower or reporting mechanism.** Given the registry's weaknesses, the bill provides no protected channel for employees to report prohibited training, which may make violations hard to detect.
