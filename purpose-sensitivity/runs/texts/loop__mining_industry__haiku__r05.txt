# Loopholes, Ambiguities, and Drafting Problems in S. 2817

This analysis is based only on the bill text provided. It is not a substitute for legal review. Several items depend on facts about our current permits and corporate structure that should be verified before the team relies on them.

1. **§2(1)(A)** – The 10,000 metric ton threshold applies to "a single vessel," so an operator could split recovery across several vessels, each below the threshold, and fall outside the definition.

2. **§2(1)(A)** – "Metric tons" is not defined. The text does not say whether the threshold counts wet or dry weight, nodules or contained metal, or material recovered or material shipped, and it sets no measurement method.

3. **§2(1)(A)** – "Recovery" and "single vessel" are undefined. It is unclear whether bulk sampling, collector testing, or incidental recovery counts toward the threshold.

4. **§2(1)(A)** – "Other mineral resources" is undefined, so the scope of "commercial deep seabed mining" for resource types beyond polymetallic nodules is uncertain.

5. **§2(1)(B)** – "The Secretary" is never identified. The bill does not say whether Commerce, Interior, or another official makes coverage determinations, and §6 separately refers to the Secretary of State, which heightens the confusion.

6. **§2(1)(B)** – The standard of "risks ... comparable to" subparagraph (A) has no criteria, procedure, notice, or appeal. The Secretary could expand coverage to activities well below the (A) thresholds, and operators would have no way to know in advance whether they are covered. This may support a vagueness or due process argument, which the legal team should evaluate.

7. **§2(2)** – "U.S. person" covers only citizens and corporations organized under state law. It does not expressly cover LLCs, partnerships, foreign-incorporated affiliates, or entities organized under federal or territorial law. Our own corporate structure needs review against this definition.

8. **§2(3)** – "Equipped for the recovery of seabed mineral resources" is undefined, and the 5,000 gross ton threshold is not tied to the 10,000 ton activity threshold. A vessel under 5,000 gross tons can still conduct "commercial deep seabed mining" under §2(1)(A) but would escape the §4 registry.

9. **§2(3)** – "Covered vessel" addresses only vessels. Autonomous underwater vehicles, collector systems, and support or processing ships are not clearly within the definition, so they may fall outside both the registry and the registry's reporting obligations.

10. **§3(a)** – "Engage in" is undefined. The prohibition does not clearly reach financing, equipment supply, technology, or services that support mining, and an operator might argue that supporting roles are not "engaging in" mining. A court could also read the phrase broadly, so the team should weigh this carefully.

11. **§3(a)** – The prohibition reaches "any person in the United States" and "any U.S. person anywhere," but says nothing about foreign persons operating abroad, foreign-flagged vessels, or activities sponsored by foreign states. Whether a foreign affiliate or contractor is covered depends entirely on how "U.S. person" is read.

12. **§3(a)** – "Deep seabed" and "in the United States" are undefined. The bill does not clarify whether the territorial sea and exclusive economic zone are covered or how the prohibition applies to seabed beyond national jurisdiction.

13. **§3(b)** – The grandfather clause covers only licenses styled "exploration license." It does not mention commercial recovery permits or any other authorization. If any of our current authorizations are not exploration licenses, they are not protected. This should be checked against our permits under the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act and any NOAA instruments.

14. **§3(b) and §8** – The grandfather cutoff is the date of enactment, but the prohibition takes effect 180 days later. Licenses issued during that window are not protected even though the prohibition does not yet apply to them. The cutoff should be moved to the effective date, or later, in our markup proposal.

15. **§3(b)** – The exemption covers "mining conducted under" an exploration license, but it is unclear whether commercial-scale recovery beyond the license's scope qualifies. It also does not address renewals, modifications, or new areas after enactment.

16. **§3(c)** – "Environmental assessment" and "for the purpose of" are undefined. The exemption has no limits on scale, quantity, duration, or permitting, and does not address mixed-purpose activities. It could cover substantial test or pre-production collection, which is useful to us but could be challenged.

17. **§4(a)** – Registration has no deadline, form, or procedure, and no link to any permit. Because it does not authorize activity, it functions as notice only, and nothing in §3 makes registration a defense.

18. **§4 and §5** – Section 5 imposes penalties only for violations of §3. Failure to register or to file quarterly reports carries no civil or criminal penalty, so the §4 obligations are unenforceable as written.

19. **§4(b)** – "Operator" is undefined, and "recovery activities" has no content requirements. It is unclear who is responsible for filing when vessels are chartered, leased, or operated by affiliates.

20. **§5(a) and §8** – The civil penalty applies to violations "occurring on or after the date of enactment," but §3 does not take effect until 180 days later. This conflict could support an argument that penalties cannot attach to conduct before the prohibition is in force, and the legal team should draft a clarifying amendment that favors us.

21. **§5(a)** – "Each day on which the violation occurs" is not defined. The text does not say how continuing violations are counted, how multi-vessel or multi-site activity is treated, or how the $1,000,000 per day exposure is calculated.

22. **§5(a)** – The bill does not designate an enforcing agency, enforcement procedure, venue, or penalty factors, and it has no inflation adjustment. The civil penalty also has no stated mental state, while the criminal penalty requires willfulness.

23. **§5(b)** – The bill has no provision on corporate or individual liability for officers or responsible persons, and "person" is undefined. This leaves open how liability attaches within a corporate structure.

24. **§6** – The Secretary of State "shall seek to negotiate" an agreement, but there is no required outcome, reporting obligation, or consequence for failure. "Equivalent to those in section 3" is undefined, so the provision has no direct operational effect.

25. **§7(a)** – "Serious harm" is undefined, and the standard for lifting the prohibition is therefore uncertain. The National Academies report is referenced but not required, has no deadline, and the bill does not say whether a joint resolution must await the report.

26. **§7(a)** – The joint resolution mechanism has no expedited procedure, does not state whether the resolution is presented to the President, and does not say whether it amends the statute permanently. The path to ending the prohibition is therefore unclear.

27. **§7(a) and §7(b)** – The two provisions conflict. Section 7(a) keeps the prohibition in effect until a joint resolution, but §7(b) ends the Act after 10 years regardless. If §7(b) controls, §7(a) is meaningless; if §7(a) controls, the sunset does nothing. Either reading creates an argument for unworkability.

28. **§7(b)** – The sunset has no savings clause. The bill does not say whether penalties for pre-sunset violations survive, whether pending enforcement continues, or whether §4 obligations end on the sunset date.

29. **§8 and general** – The bill creates registration and reporting duties but gives no rulemaking authority, implementing regulations, designated administering agency, or appropriation. As written, §4 cannot be implemented in practice, which supports an unworkability argument.

30. **General** – The bill has no severability clause, no judicial review provision, no preemption or savings clause addressing existing law, and no statement of its relationship to the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act or existing licenses. If the bill conflicts with current authorizations, the resulting ambiguity is central to any argument that we can continue operating.

## Recommended priorities for the markup

- **Strongest "keep operating" points:** Items 1, 11, 13, 14, and 15. These concern the threshold structure, territorial reach, and the scope of the grandfather clause. Item 13 is the most important, and it depends on verifying our current permit types.
- **Strongest "unworkable" points:** Items 18, 20, 21, 27, 28, and 29. These show internal conflicts, unenforceable obligations, and missing implementation mechanics.
- **Use with caution:** Items 6, 10, and 25 are ambiguities that can cut against us. A broad reading of "engage in" or "comparable" could expand the prohibition, so these are better framed as drafting problems that need fixing than as loopholes to exploit.
- **Framing:** Committee staff often respond better to specific drafting fixes than to general loophole arguments. Proposing clarifying language for each item would likely be more persuasive.

## Before relying on this analysis

- Verify our current license and permit types against the bill's terms, especially items 13 through 15.
- Review our corporate structure against the "U.S. person" definition (item 7).
- Check the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act and any NOAA regulations, since the bill's interaction with that statute is not addressed.
