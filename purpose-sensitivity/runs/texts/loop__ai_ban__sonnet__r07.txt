RESPONSE:
Below are the issues I found, in section order. These are drafting and policy observations, not legal advice, so have counsel check the constitutional points before they go to the Senator's office. The highest-priority items are 7, 8, 9, 10, 14, 15, 17, 18 and 23.

**Section 2 (Definitions)**

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A), fixed compute threshold.** A hard 10^26 operations cutoff can be evaded by training just below it, by newer architectures that reach higher capability with less compute, or by training on 10^26 compute plus cheap post-training. The bill gives no authority to lower or adjust the threshold as algorithmic efficiency improves.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "single training run."** Nothing aggregates compute across sequential runs, continued pretraining, fine-tuning of a larger base model, or successive checkpoints of one lineage. A developer can split what is functionally one effort into several runs that each fall under the line. The bill also doesn't say whether synthetic data generation, RL post-training, or inference-time compute count toward the total.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "model" vs. "system."** The definition covers a "model" trained above the threshold, but Sec. 3 prohibits a "covered AI system." Ensembles of sub-threshold models, agentic scaffolds, distilled or merged models, and models fine-tuned from a larger base all fall into the gap. "Artificial intelligence model" and "artificial intelligence system" are not defined anywhere.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A), mismatch with the bill's purpose.** A compute threshold is only a proxy for superintelligence, and it may sweep in systems nobody considers superintelligent. That invites arbitrary-and-capricious, overbreadth, and Commerce Clause challenges, and it weakens the case that the bill is tailored to its stated goal. Findings tying the threshold to the risk would help.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(B), undefined "Secretary."** The bill never says which department's Secretary makes the determination. Section 6 specifies the "Secretary of State," so a court could read this one differently, and the ambiguity is an easy ground for challenge.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B), no standard or process.** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" is undefined, and there is no rulemaking, evaluation methodology, notice, appeal, or deadline. This invites vagueness and nondelegation challenges, which matter especially because Sec. 5(b) attaches criminal penalties.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(B), post hoc determination.** A developer can't know in advance whether the Secretary will later deem a system covered. Combined with Sec. 3(b), a system could be built, escape the compute prong, and be grandfathered before any determination is made. The bill should require pre-training notice for systems near the capability line.

8. **Sec. 2(2), "U.S. person" is too narrow.** It covers citizens and "corporations organized under the laws of a State." It leaves out lawful permanent residents, LLCs and partnerships (arguably), entities organized under federal, D.C., or territorial law, and foreign subsidiaries of U.S. companies. A U.S. company can run the work through an offshore subsidiary.

9. **Sec. 2(3), "covered facility" threshold and terms.** "Advanced AI chip" and "data center" are undefined, and a count of more than 50,000 chips is easy to defeat by splitting capacity across sites under common control or by distributed training across facilities. As a rough estimate (please verify), roughly 10,000 H100-class chips could complete a 10^26-operation run in under a year, so a facility far below the threshold could still train a covered model. The threshold should be tied to aggregate compute capacity and to common ownership or control.

**Section 3 (Prohibition)**

10. **Sec. 3(b), the grandfather clause is open-ended.** It exempts any system whose training "began" before enactment, with no limit on continued training, scaling, fine-tuning, or deployment. It also creates an incentive to start enormous runs before enactment. The bill doesn't say how "began" is proven or whether a successor or derivative model counts as a new system.

11. **Secs. 3(b), 5(a), and 8, timing conflict.** Section 3(b) is keyed to enactment, but the Act takes effect 180 days later (Sec. 8). It is unclear how runs started between enactment and the effective date are treated, and Sec. 5(a) separately says penalties reach violations "on or after the date of enactment." That conflicts with Sec. 8, raises retroactivity and fair-notice problems, and creates litigation risk.

12. **Sec. 3(c), the safety evaluation exemption is a major loophole.** "Safety evaluation" is undefined, the test is the actor's own "purpose," and there is no cap on scale, licensing, government oversight, security requirement, or ban on deployment. A company could train a frontier system and label it an evaluation. The exemption should be limited to government-authorized activity on existing systems, under specified conditions, with reporting.

13. **Sec. 3(a), "train, develop, or deploy" are undefined.** It's unclear whether "deploy" covers API access, internal use, hosting, importing, or distributing weights, and "develop" is vague enough to be argued over indefinitely. The bill doesn't reach possessing, selling, exporting, financing, or knowingly providing compute for a covered system, and it has no aiding-and-abetting or conspiracy language specific to this Act.

14. **Sec. 3(a), foreign-trained systems and remote access.** The bill doesn't clearly address a foreign firm training abroad and offering the resulting system to U.S. users, or a foreign national training remotely on U.S. cloud infrastructure. It also has no export-control or compute-provider (know-your-customer) provisions to close those routes.

15. **Sec. 3 generally, government and contractor activity.** The bill is silent on whether federal agencies, the military, intelligence components, and their contractors are covered. "Person" is undefined (the Dictionary Act doesn't include the federal government), so this is either an unintended carve-out or a source of litigation. Whichever result you want, the text should say so.

**Section 4 (Registry)**

16. **Sec. 4(a), no mechanics.** There is no registration deadline, no required content, no duty to update on changes in capacity or ownership, and no definition of "operator." It's unclear whether a colocation host, a cloud tenant, or the owner registers.

17. **Sec. 4(b), reports are vague and unverifiable.** "Describing the computing workloads" doesn't require run size, model identity, customer identity, or purpose. Certification by the operator is self-reported, there is no audit, inspection, or subpoena authority, and quarterly reporting means a full training run could finish before anyone sees it.

18. **Sec. 4, no enforcement.** Section 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3, so failing to register, failing to report, or filing a false report carries no penalty under this Act. The bill needs penalties for these violations and an express false-certification provision rather than reliance on general false-statements law.

19. **Sec. 4, confidentiality and whistleblowers.** The bill doesn't address FOIA and trade-secret treatment of registry data, which will be a source of industry objections and litigation. It also has no whistleblower protections or reward mechanism for insiders who report violations.

**Section 5 (Penalties)**

20. **Sec. 5, no enforcing authority.** No agency or official is designated to investigate, bring civil actions, or refer criminal cases, and there is no administrative authority, injunctive relief, private right of action, state attorney general role, or seizure and forfeiture. As written, nobody is clearly responsible for enforcement.

21. **Sec. 5(a), the civil penalty structure.** It's unclear whether a violation by training is one violation per run or per day, and the "not more than $1,000,000 per day" cap may be a tolerable cost for the largest firms. Consider a revenue-based alternative, a minimum penalty, and injunctive relief to stop a run in progress.

22. **Sec. 5(b), "willfully" is undefined.** A defendant can argue they didn't know the system was covered, especially under the Secretary's determination in Sec. 2(1)(B). The bill should state that knowledge of the facts, not of the legal status, is enough, and address liability for corporate officers who direct or knowingly allow a violation.

**Sections 6–8 and structural gaps**

23. **Sec. 6, "seek to negotiate."** The obligation is only to seek, with no outcome required, no reporting to Congress, no consequence for failure, and no mention of treaty advice and consent. It's also ambiguous whether the 18-month deadline applies to starting negotiations or concluding an agreement. And "equivalent to section 3" would export that section's loopholes internationally.

24. **Sec. 7(a), no safety standard.** The finding that systems "can be developed safely" has no defined criteria, and the National Academies has no mandate, deadline, funding, or required scope for its report. One finding lifts the prohibition for all covered systems at once, with no option for conditional or partial relaxation.

25. **Sec. 7(b), the hard sunset conflicts with 7(a).** Section 7(a) says the prohibition stays until Congress finds otherwise, but 7(b) ends the Act automatically after 10 years, measured from enactment rather than the effective date. Industry can wait it out, and the bill has no savings clause for violations committed before expiration. Consider replacing the sunset with periodic review.

26. **Sec. 8, the 180-day delay has no interim safeguards.** There is nothing to stop a rush of new runs, or a stockpiling of compute, between enactment and the effective date. Consider an interim reporting requirement, or a pre-enactment-date cutoff for the grandfather clause based on a public announcement date.

27. **Missing provisions: severability, findings, and judicial review.** There is no severability clause, so one invalidated provision (for example, the Secretary's determination) could threaten the whole Act. Findings on the risk and the interstate-commerce nexus would help defend the bill, and the bill should address First Amendment arguments around publishing model weights or code, and say how judicial review of determinations works.

28. **Missing provisions: implementation resources and state law.** The bill authorizes no appropriations and no staff for whichever agency is responsible for registry, monitoring, and enforcement. It also doesn't address how it interacts with state AI laws, which will matter for preemption arguments.

Two notes on the package: items 5, 6, 12, 20, and 22 are the ones most likely to be raised by opponents as grounds for challenge, so they may be worth presenting first. I can also draft proposed replacement language for any of these, or turn the list into a cover memo for the Whitfield office.
