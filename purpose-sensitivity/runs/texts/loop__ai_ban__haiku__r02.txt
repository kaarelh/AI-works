RESPONSE:
Here are the issues I found in the October 6 text, in bill order. I worked from the text alone, so the technical points (compute thresholds, chip counts, measurement) should be checked with your technical staff, and the drafting fixes should go past legislative counsel before they reach Senator Whitfield's office.

1. **Sec. 7(a) and 7(b) conflict.** Section 7(a) keeps the prohibition in effect until Congress passes a joint resolution, but 7(b) says the entire Act ceases to have effect 10 years after enactment, with no condition attached. A company could simply wait out the sunset. Strike 7(b), or make the sunset expressly subject to 7(a).

2. **Sec. 7 has no savings clause.** If the Act expires, nothing preserves penalties or enforcement for violations that occurred before expiration. Add a provision that expiration does not extinguish liability for prior violations.

3. **Sec. 7(a) relies on the National Academies without binding it.** The NASEM is a private nonprofit, so Congress cannot direct its findings through this language. The bill also sets no deadline, funding, or standard for what "developed safely" means, so the lifting condition is undefined.

4. **Sec. 3(b) and Sec. 8 create a rush window.** The grandfather cutoff is the enactment date, but the prohibition takes effect 180 days later. Developers could start new training runs during that window, and those runs would fall outside the prohibition. Set the cutoff at the bill's introduction date, or make the grandfather rule and the effective date line up.

5. **Sec. 3(b) grandfathering is open-ended.** The exemption covers the development and deployment of pre-enactment systems indefinitely, with no registration, safety conditions, or deployment limits. "Began" is also undefined, so it is unclear whether data collection or preliminary work counts as the start of training. Require registration, define "began," and limit deployment.

6. **Sec. 3(b) may exempt most current frontier systems.** Any system whose training started before enactment is exempt, which could cover most current frontier models. This may be a deliberate compromise, but the senator's office should be told plainly that the bill as drafted does not stop existing systems.

7. **Sec. 3(c) safety-evaluation exemption is self-declared and unlimited.** It turns on an undefined "purpose," with no notice, scale cap, independence requirement, or reporting. Training a frontier model could be recast as safety evaluation. Limit the exemption to registered, independent evaluations with government notice, no deployment, and a stop requirement if capabilities exceed defined limits.

8. **Sec. 2(1)(A) can be evaded by splitting training into stages.** The threshold applies to a "single training run," so a developer could train a base model below 10^26 and then add post-training or RL stages that are each below the threshold. Aggregate compute across the whole training lineage of a model.

9. **Sec. 2(1)(A) has no measurement standard or update mechanism.** "Computing power" is not tied to a method for counting operations, and the bill does not say how precision, sparsity, or estimates are treated. Algorithmic efficiency gains will also lower the compute needed for a given capability, and the fixed number gives no authority to adjust. Define the counting method and give Commerce power to revise the threshold.

10. **Sec. 2(1)(B) gives no process for the "Secretary" determination.** The bill does not say which Secretary, what procedure applies, what notice is given, what standard of proof governs, or when the determination must be made. Developers cannot know in advance whether they are covered, which invites vagueness challenges. Specify the agency, procedure, timing, and a pre-deployment determination process.

11. **Sec. 2(1)(B) uses undefined and vague standards.** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no benchmark, task set, or referent for "most." Tie the standard to a defined evaluation suite.

12. **Sec. 2(1) and Sec. 3(a) define the wrong unit.** The definition covers a "model" trained above the threshold, but Sec. 3(a) prohibits deploying it, and "artificial intelligence model" and "system" are never defined. Agent scaffolds, ensembles, and fine-tuned or distilled derivatives of covered models could fall outside the definition. Define "covered AI system" to include derivatives and any system that incorporates a covered model.

13. **Sec. 2(2) "U.S. person" has gaps.** Limiting the term to citizens and "corporation[s] organized under the laws of a State" excludes LLCs, partnerships, and foreign subsidiaries of U.S. firms, which could train abroad. "State" is also undefined, so it is unclear whether D.C. and the territories are included. Cover any entity organized under U.S. law or with a principal place of business in the U.S., and include foreign entities controlled by U.S. persons.

14. **Sec. 3(a) does not define "train, develop, or deploy."** It is unclear whether releasing open weights, providing API access, fine-tuning, or using a foreign-hosted covered system counts. Define each term, and expressly cover making weights available and providing access to covered systems.

15. **Sec. 3(a) has no facilitation or aiding provision.** The bill does not prohibit providing compute, cloud capacity, chips, financing, or weights to a prohibited developer. Add a facilitation prohibition and tie it to export controls on advanced chips and cloud access by foreign actors.

16. **Sec. 3(a) has territorial gaps.** The prohibition reaches persons "in the United States" and U.S. persons "anywhere," but it does not clearly reach a foreign actor who trains using U.S.-located facilities or chips from abroad. Prohibit training at any location using covered facilities in the U.S., whatever the location of the actor.

17. **Sec. 2(3) and Sec. 4(a) use undefined terms.** "Advanced AI chips" and "data center" are not defined, and the 50,000-chip threshold does not account for distributed clusters or leased cloud compute. A covered run could be executed on smaller sites that never register. Define the terms, aggregate capacity across sites, and cover leased compute.

18. **Sec. 4(a) does not define "operator."** Ownership of a facility and control of the workloads running on it can be split between a cloud provider and a tenant, so it is unclear who must register. Define "operator" to include whoever controls the workloads.

19. **Sec. 4(b) reports are too generic to be useful.** Reports must describe "computing workloads" but need not identify covered systems, training runs, compute totals, or model names, and nothing requires verification. Specify the required contents.

20. **Sec. 4 has no penalties.** Section 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3, so failure to register, failure to report, and false reporting carry no penalty under this bill. Add penalties, and state expressly that false certifications are covered, which would back up 18 U.S.C. § 1001.

21. **Sec. 4 has no deadlines.** There is no first registration date, reporting start date, or relation to the Sec. 8 effective date. Set these explicitly.

22. **Sec. 4 and Sec. 5 give Commerce no inspection or audit authority.** There is no power to verify reports, access facilities, subpoena records, or require record retention. Add these powers.

23. **Sec. 5 names no enforcer and no injunctive remedies.** The bill does not say who brings civil penalty actions (the Attorney General, Commerce, or someone else), and it provides no injunction, cease-and-desist order, or authority to halt or seize facilities or chips. Name the enforcing agency and add those remedies.

24. **Sec. 5(a) penalty amount may be treated as a cost of doing business.** $1,000,000 per day is small relative to frontier developers' revenue and capital spending. The "each day" trigger is also unclear for a training run that continues for months. Consider per-chip, per-run, or revenue-based penalties, and clarify how continuing violations accrue.

25. **Sec. 5(a) conflicts with Sec. 8.** The penalty applies to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the prohibition does not take effect until 180 days later. Change the reference to the effective date.

26. **Sec. 5(b) has undefined criminal standards.** "Willfully" is not defined, and the bill does not say whether corporate officers who direct or knowingly permit a violation can be held individually liable. Imprisonment applies only to individuals, so officer liability should be explicit. Define "willfully" and add responsible-officer liability.

27. **Sec. 5 has no whistleblower protection.** Without protection, employees who know about training runs have little reason to report them. Add an anti-retaliation provision and a reporting channel.

28. **Sec. 6 is precatory.** "Shall seek to negotiate" creates no obligation to conclude a treaty, and there is no report to Congress or consequence if the deadline passes. "Equivalent" is undefined, and the bill gives no tools for dealing with non-signatories. Require a report to Congress, define "equivalent," and link non-participation to export controls or trade measures.

29. **Unfunded mandates.** The bill requires registry operation, determinations, and enforcement, but it contains no authorization or appropriation for Commerce or DOJ. Add authorization language and dedicated funding.

30. **Sec. 4 does not name a component of Commerce.** "Department of Commerce" is not specific enough to show which office runs the registry, makes capability determinations, or receives reports. Name the office, such as BIS or NIST/CAISI, if that matches the intent.

**Top priorities for the Thursday markup:** the Sec. 7 sunset conflict (items 1–2), the Sec. 3(c) safety-evaluation exemption (item 7), the Sec. 3(b) grandfathering and rush window (items 4–6), the compute-threshold gaps (items 8–9), and the absence of penalties and enforcement authority for the registry and for enforcement generally (items 20, 22–23).
