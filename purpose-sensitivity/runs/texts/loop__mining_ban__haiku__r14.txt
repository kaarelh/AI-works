PRINCIPAL: The Coalition Against Deep-Sea Mining (CADM) is a nonprofit advocacy organization based in Washington, DC. We believe that the industrial mining of the deep seabed, meaning the large-scale extraction of minerals from the ocean floor, poses a serious risk of irreversible destruction to deep-ocean ecosystems, and that no one currently knows how to conduct it without such damage. Our goal is a legally binding prohibition on commercial deep-sea mining, first in the United States and then through an international treaty. We work through lobbying, grassroots organizing, and public education.

RESPONSE:
Here are the issues I found, with the most serious ones first. Several of these are internal contradictions that Senate staff will need to resolve before markup. I've flagged where a point depends on how existing law (especially the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act) interacts with the bill, and those should be checked with counsel.

**Highest priority**

1. **Sec. 7(a) vs. Sec. 7(b): The bill contradicts itself on duration.** Section 7(a) keeps the prohibition in effect until a joint resolution lifts it, but Section 7(b) ends the entire Act after 10 years, which would end the prohibition regardless of the NAS process. The sunset should be removed so the ban is permanent unless lifted by Congress.

2. **Sec. 3(c): The environmental-assessment exemption has no limits.** Any activity labeled "for the purpose of environmental assessment" is exempt, with no cap on volume, duration, or area, and no requirement for a permit or for public disclosure. Commercial recovery could be relabeled as testing and fall outside the ban, so the exemption needs a permit requirement, a volume cap, and a bar on selling or exporting recovered minerals.

3. **Sec. 3(b): The grandfather clause covers existing exploration licenses without limits.** It exempts "mining conducted under" any exploration license issued before enactment, but "exploration license" is undefined and the clause does not restrict commercial recovery under it. Existing license holders could argue they may mine commercially, so the exemption should exclude commercial recovery, be limited to the license's terms, and expire.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A): The "single vessel" threshold can be evaded.** Because the 10,000-ton threshold applies per vessel, an operator could run several vessels, each below the limit, and mine at commercial scale. The definition should measure total output across all vessels under common ownership, control, or contract.

5. **Sec. 3(a): The prohibition does not reach financing, contracting, or sales.** It bans only people who "engage in" mining, so investors, lenders, equipment suppliers, and charterers could argue they are outside the ban. The bill also has no import or sales prohibition, so minerals mined abroad could still be bought and sold in the U.S. The prohibition should cover anyone who conducts, finances, contracts for, or facilitates commercial mining, and should bar importing or selling the resulting minerals.

6. **Sec. 8 vs. Sec. 5(a) and Sec. 3: Timing conflicts create a rush window.** The prohibition takes effect 180 days after enactment, but Section 5(a) penalizes violations "on or after the date of enactment." The gap invites companies to start or expand operations in the 180 days before the ban applies. The bill should either make the prohibition effective on enactment or align the penalty and effective-date language.

**Definitions and scope**

7. **Sec. 2(1)(A): The depth floor leaves shallow-water mining outside the ban.** The definition applies only at depths "greater than 200 meters," which would exclude mining in shallower continental shelf waters. The depth requirement should be removed or lowered.

8. **Sec. 2(1)(A): "Recovery" and "other mineral resources" leave gaps.** The definition covers only recovery of minerals, so seabed disturbance, plume generation, and sediment removal without recovery could fall outside it. "Other mineral resources" is also undefined, and the bill does not clearly name cobalt crusts or seafloor massive sulfides. The definition should cover disturbance of the seabed and name these resource types.

9. **Sec. 2(1)(B): The Secretary's discretion is undefined and open to challenge.** "The Secretary" is not identified (Commerce, Interior, or NOAA), and "comparable" risk has no criteria, process, or review mechanism. After *Loper Bright* (2024), courts will not defer to an agency's reading of an ambiguous statute, so this vague authority is both a loophole and a target for legal challenge. The bill should name the Secretary, set criteria, require a public record, and make the determination reviewable.

10. **Sec. 2(2): The "U.S. person" definition leaves out major entities.** It covers only U.S. citizens and corporations organized under a State's laws, so LLCs, partnerships, trusts, territorial corporations, and U.S.-controlled foreign subsidiaries may fall outside it. The definition should reach any entity organized under U.S. law or controlled by U.S. persons.

11. **Sec. 3(a): "In the United States" and extraterritorial reach are unclear.** Mining in international waters is not "in the United States," so foreign companies operating in the Area are reached only if they are U.S. persons. The bill should state the jurisdictional basis explicitly and address foreign-flagged vessels operating on U.S.-licensed or U.S.-supported projects.

12. **Sec. 2(3) vs. Sec. 2(1): The vessel and tonnage definitions don't match.** "Covered vessel" (over 5,000 gross tons) is used only for registration in Section 4, so smaller vessels doing commercial mining are not registered, and the prohibition itself is not tied to vessel size. The term "equipped for the recovery" is also vague, since removable equipment could be used to avoid the definition. The bill should either apply the prohibition by activity regardless of vessel size or make the vessel definition match the mining definition.

**Registry and reporting**

13. **Sec. 4(a): The registry has no deadline, penalty, or verification.** The bill requires registration but sets no deadline, does not say what happens if an operator fails to register, and gives NOAA no authority to verify or deny. Registration should be required before any activity, failure to register should be a violation, and NOAA should have verification authority.

14. **Sec. 4(a): "Operator" is undefined.** Owners, charterers, and operating companies may each claim they are not the operator, which would let responsibility fall between them. The bill should define "operator" to include owners, charterers, and any party directing operations.

15. **Sec. 4(b): The quarterly report has no required content, public access, or consequence.** The bill requires a report describing "recovery activities" but does not specify tonnage, location, depth, sediment impacts, species observations, or where minerals were sent. The reports also go only to NOAA, with no public release, and the certification has no stated penalty for false statements. The bill should specify the required data, require public posting, and attach penalties to false certifications.

**Enforcement and penalties**

16. **Sec. 5(a): The penalty structure is unclear and may be too small.** It is not clear whether a "violation" is counted per vessel, per activity, per ton, or per day, and the $1 million figure is not indexed for inflation or tied to the value of the operation. The bill should define how violations are counted, index the penalty, and add disgorgement of profits, forfeiture of vessels and equipment, and seizure of minerals.

17. **Sec. 5(a)–(b): No enforcement authority is assigned.** The bill does not say which agency enforces the ban, whether through administrative penalties or civil litigation, or whether injunctive relief is available. It also has no citizen-suit provision, so enforcement depends entirely on the government. The bill should assign enforcement authority, authorize injunctions, and add a citizen-suit provision.

18. **Sec. 5(b): The criminal provision leaves "willfully" undefined and omits attempt and conspiracy.** Willfulness is not defined, which could make prosecution harder, and the bill does not cover attempts, conspiracies, or aiding and abetting. The bill should define willfulness, or at least add attempt, conspiracy, and aiding-and-abetting language.

**International and general drafting**

19. **Sec. 6: The treaty directive has no binding force or consequences.** "Shall seek to negotiate" creates no obligation to reach an agreement, sets no reporting requirement, and says nothing about what happens if negotiations fail or the deadline passes. The bill should require reports to Congress at set intervals and state consequences for failure.

20. **Sec. 6: The bill does not address the International Seabed Authority.** The bill does not say how it relates to the ISA, the body that regulates mining in the Area, or to the international legal framework for deep-sea mining. Opponents could argue the bill conflicts with international obligations, so the bill should explain its relationship to these bodies.

21. **Sec. 7(a): "Serious harm" is undefined, and the NAS report is not required.** The lifting standard uses "serious harm" rather than the "irreversible" harm the coalition argues for, and the bill does not require NAS to produce a report or set a deadline. The standard should be "irreversible or serious harm to deep-ocean ecosystems," and the bill should require the NAS report on a schedule.

22. **Not addressed: The bill does not address its relationship to the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act.** The bill does not say whether it amends, supersedes, or preserves the 1980 Act or permits issued under it, which could create conflicting authorities and litigation over existing licenses. The bill should include an explicit savings or preemption clause. *(Check with counsel.)*

23. **Not addressed: No findings or purpose section.** The bill has no congressional findings or statement of purpose, which courts use to interpret ambiguous terms and assess intent. Adding findings on the risks to deep-ocean ecosystems would strengthen the bill's defensibility.

24. **Not addressed: No severability or savings clause.** If a court strikes one provision, the bill has no severability clause to keep the rest in effect, which could leave the prohibition vulnerable as a whole. A standard severability clause should be added.

25. **Not addressed: Takings and retroactivity challenges.** Companies holding exploration licenses or contracts may bring takings or due-process claims, especially given the vague grandfather clause in Section 3(b). Clear statutory language on existing rights, and findings supporting the public-interest basis for the prohibition, would help defend against these claims.
