RESPONSE:
I reviewed only the text you pasted. I haven't checked it against the underlying statutes (for example, the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act, 30 U.S.C. §§ 1401 et seq.), so counsel should verify the cross-references before this goes to Senator Whitfield's office.

**Section 2 (Definitions)**

1. **§2(1)(A), volume threshold.** A bright line of 10,000 metric tons per year means any operation below it is not "commercial deep seabed mining," so large-scale test, pilot, or "demonstration" extraction is lawful. The bill also never says whether the weight is wet or dry, or how it is measured, which invites disputes.

2. **§2(1)(A), "by a single vessel."** The threshold applies per vessel, not per company, project, or affiliate group, so an operator can run several vessels under the line. Add aggregation across vessels, affiliates, joint ventures, and contractors working on the same project.

3. **§2(1)(A), "per year."** The bill doesn't say whether this means a calendar year, a rolling 12 months, or a license year. An annual measure also makes it unclear on which day a violation occurs, which complicates the per-day penalty in §5(a).

4. **§2(1)(A), undefined terms.** "Recovery," "seabed," "mineral resources," and "depth" are all undefined. It is unclear whether sulfides, crusts, and sediments are covered, how depth is measured, and whether "mineral resources" reaches unrelated materials such as sand, gravel, or hydrocarbons, which could invite overbreadth challenges.

5. **§2(1)(A), the 200-meter floor.** Extraction at or shallower than 200 meters is outside the definition entirely. The definition also ignores associated activities such as midwater discharge of tailings and sediment plumes, which are major sources of harm.

6. **§2(1)(B), "the Secretary."** The bill never says which Secretary. Because this is the catch-all that closes the gaps in (A), the ambiguity is a significant weakness.

7. **§2(1)(B), standardless discretion.** "Comparable risks" has no criteria, procedure, deadline, or public-notice requirement. It is vulnerable to nondelegation and vagueness challenges, especially since §5(b) attaches criminal penalties. It also depends on a discretionary determination that a future administration may never make.

8. **§2(2), "U.S. person."** This covers only citizens and corporations organized under a State's law. It leaves out LLCs, partnerships, trusts, DC and territorial entities, lawful permanent residents, and, critically, foreign-incorporated subsidiaries of U.S. companies and foreign entities controlled by U.S. persons.

9. **§2, "person" is undefined.** §3 and §5 apply to "any person," but the bill doesn't say whether this includes foreign entities, subsidiaries, joint ventures, officers, financiers, or federal contractors. Define it expressly.

10. **§2(3), "covered vessel."** A 5,000 gross ton floor excludes smaller vessels, and "equipped for the recovery" can be dodged by temporary or modular equipment. It also omits platforms, seafloor collectors and crawlers, riser systems, and support or ore-transport vessels.

**Section 3 (Prohibition)**

11. **§3(a), geographic scope.** "In the United States" is undefined and may not clearly cover the EEZ, the outer continental shelf, extended continental shelf areas, or U.S.-flag vessels at sea. Define the covered waters and seabed.

12. **§3(a), conduct covered.** Only "engag[ing] in" mining is prohibited. There is no prohibition on financing, aiding or abetting, conspiracy, attempt, processing or importing seabed minerals, or knowingly purchasing them, so foreign operators backed by U.S. capital or supply chains are untouched.

13. **§3(a), no bar on new authorizations.** The bill doesn't direct agencies to stop issuing or processing licenses and permits under existing authorities such as the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act and the OCS Lands Act. It also has no preemption or repeal language, leaving those statutes' permitting pathways intact and potentially in conflict.

14. **§3(b), grandfather clause.** Exempting "mining conducted under an exploration license" swallows the rule: it has no volume cap, no expiry, no compliance condition, and no bar on renewals, extensions, modifications, or transfers. Exploration licenses typically don't authorize commercial recovery, so the language also muddies what is actually exempted.

15. **§3(b), "an exploration license."** The term is undefined and not limited to licenses issued by the United States. It is also unclear whether it covers foreign-sponsored licenses held by U.S. persons. Because the cutoff is the enactment date, there is an incentive to rush new licenses before enactment.

16. **§3(b), takings risk.** If you tighten the grandfather clause, expect takings and due-process claims from license holders. The fix should state that licenses confer no vested right to commercial recovery, and findings should support that.

17. **§3(c), environmental assessment exemption.** It turns on the "purpose" of an activity, with no permit, notice, independence requirement, volume cap, duration limit, or ban on retaining or selling material. It can be used as a pretext for test mining. Also, §3(c) says "activities," while the rest of §3 uses "mining."

**Section 4 (Registry)**

18. **§4(a), no deadline or content.** There is no registration deadline, no required information (owner, flag, equipment, area, target resource), and no rulemaking authority for NOAA. "Operator" is undefined, so owners, charterers, and managers can point at each other.

19. **§4(b), reporting content.** Reports aren't required to include tonnage, location, depth, or processing and discharge data. Without them NOAA can't tell whether the 10,000-ton threshold is crossed.

20. **§4, no verification.** There are no audit, inspection, recordkeeping, or tracking requirements (such as AIS or VMS) and no public disclosure. Compliance rests entirely on operator self-certification.

21. **§4, no penalty.** §5 penalizes only violations of §3, so failure to register, failure to report, and false certification carry no stated consequence. Add civil and criminal penalties for §4 violations.

22. **§4, coverage mismatch.** Registry duties attach only to vessels over 5,000 gross tons, but §3 applies to recovery by any vessel. Smaller vessels can operate unregistered and unmonitored.

**Section 5 (Penalties)**

23. **§5(a), conflict with the effective date.** Penalties run "on or after the date of enactment," but §8 delays the Act's effect by 180 days, so it's unclear whether §3 can be violated during that window. The language also invites retroactivity arguments.

24. **§5(a), civil enforcement mechanics.** The bill doesn't say who assesses or collects penalties, what procedures or hearing rights apply, or whether the cap adjusts for inflation. A flat cap per day may also be trivial for a large operator.

25. **§5, missing remedies.** There is no forfeiture of minerals, proceeds, or vessels, no injunctive authority, no citizen-suit provision, and no judicial review provision. Without them, enforcement depends entirely on agency discretion.

26. **§5(b), "willfully."** It is unclear whether this requires knowledge of the facts, knowledge of the law, or both. That makes prosecutions harder and invites a defense based on reliance on the §3(b) exemptions or an uncertain Secretary determination under §2(1)(B). The bill also doesn't address venue for offenses committed outside the United States or liability of responsible corporate officers.

**Section 6 (International Agreement)**

27. **§6, "seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months."** It's ambiguous whether the deadline is for starting talks or concluding an agreement, and it has no reporting requirement or consequence. As drafted it is purely hortatory.

28. **§6, "equivalent to those in section 3."** This imports §3's loopholes, including the thresholds and the exploration-license and assessment exemptions, into the treaty target. It also ignores the existing International Seabed Authority framework and has no trade or diplomatic tools for non-participants.

**Section 7 (Duration)**

29. **§7(a), "serious harm."** The standard is undefined and low. Congress needs only a joint resolution (a simple-majority path, subject to veto) "acting on a report" of the National Academies, and nothing requires the report to be favorable, funded, or even commissioned. A precautionary standard, with the burden on proponents, is missing.

30. **§7(a), what happens after the finding.** The bill doesn't say whether the prohibition lifts automatically, or what regulatory regime then governs, leaving a possible vacuum with no permitting or environmental standards. It also doesn't say whether the finding is technology-specific or sweeps in all mining.

31. **§7(b), sunset.** This directly contradicts §7(a): the prohibition expires after 10 years with no scientific finding, which defeats the "until Congress finds" structure. Because §8 delays the effective date, the real period of effect is about 9.5 years.

32. **§7(b), no savings clause.** On sunset, pending enforcement actions and penalties for earlier violations could be argued to abate. Add a savings clause.

**Section 8 and general issues**

33. **§8, 180-day delay.** The gap gives companies time to ramp up, obtain licenses, or lock in "existing operations," and nothing restrains new authorizations in the interim. It also does not address operations already underway on the effective date.

34. **No findings or purpose section.** Findings on ecological harm, the commerce-power basis, and extraterritorial intent would strengthen the bill against legal challenge and guide interpretation.

35. **No severability clause.** If a provision such as §2(1)(B) is struck down, the whole Act's operation could be contested. Add an express severability clause.

36. **No implementation machinery.** The bill designates no lead enforcement agency, doesn't coordinate NOAA, the Coast Guard, and DOJ, authorizes no appropriations, and gives no general rulemaking authority.

**Highest-priority fixes**
- The §3(b) grandfather clause and §3(c) assessment exemption.
- The §7(b) sunset and the weak §7(a) standard.
- The §2(1)(A) threshold and "single vessel" language.
- The §2(2) "U.S. person" definition.
- The §5/§8 timing conflict and the missing §4 penalties.
- The lack of any bar on new licenses or permits under existing statutes.
