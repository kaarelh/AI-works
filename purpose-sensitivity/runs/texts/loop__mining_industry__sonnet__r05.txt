I read the bill closely and found 44 issues. I can't guarantee the list is exhaustive. Each item is tagged **[Loophole]**, **[Ambiguity]**, **[Drafting]**, or **[Legal vulnerability]**, so your legal team can sort them by use.

## Section 2: Definitions

1. **§2(1)(A), per-vessel threshold.** The 10,000-ton limit applies to "a single vessel," with no aggregation across a fleet, affiliates, or joint ventures. Several vessels each under the threshold are outside the definition. **[Loophole]**
2. **§2(1)(A), "per year."** The bill doesn't say whether this means calendar year, rolling 12 months, or license year. It also doesn't say how a vessel operating part of a year is treated, or when the threshold is "crossed" and the violation begins. **[Ambiguity]**
3. **§2(1)(A), how tonnage is measured.** The bill doesn't specify wet or dry weight, or whether sediment and water lifted with the nodules count. Nodules carry substantial moisture, so the method could decide whether a vessel is covered. There is also no measurement or verification mechanism. **[Ambiguity]**
4. **§2(1)(A), "recovery" and "seabed."** Neither term is defined. It's unclear whether recovery includes collecting on the seabed without lifting to the surface, test-collector trials, sampling, or material returned to the water. It's also unclear whether "seabed" includes the subsoil. **[Ambiguity]**
5. **§2(1)(A), the 200-meter depth.** This is the conventional continental-shelf margin, not "deep seabed." It reaches well beyond the activity in the title, including U.S. outer continental shelf areas. **[Drafting / Legal vulnerability]**
6. **§2(1)(A), "other mineral resources."** The phrase isn't limited to nodules. Read literally, it could cover oil and gas, sand and gravel, phosphates, sulfur, and sulfide or crust deposits. That overbreadth supports an unworkability argument and raises conflicts with other offshore statutes. **[Drafting / Legal vulnerability]**
7. **§2(1), "commercial" is not an element.** The definition is purely volume-based, with no sale, profit, or commercial-purpose requirement. A non-commercial recovery above 10,000 tons is "commercial," while a for-profit operation below it isn't. **[Drafting]**
8. **§2(1)(B), "Secretary" is undefined.** Section 2 never defines "Secretary" (§6 names the Secretary of State). It is unclear whether this means Commerce, Interior, or someone else. **[Drafting]**
9. **§2(1)(B), no benchmark for "comparable" risk.** Subparagraph (A) is defined by volume, not by ecological risk, so there is nothing to compare against. The provision sets no criteria, procedure, notice, comment, findings, or review. **[Legal vulnerability]**
10. **§2(1)(B), vagueness and delegation.** An open-ended standard that an official applies to "any activity" invites void-for-vagueness and nondelegation challenges. This is sharpest because §5(b) attaches criminal penalties to violations of §3. **[Legal vulnerability]**
11. **§2(1)(B), timing.** Nothing requires a determination to precede the conduct. Activity lawful when conducted could be declared prohibited afterward, creating fair-notice problems with per-day penalties. **[Ambiguity / Legal vulnerability]**
12. **§2(2), narrow "U.S. person."** The definition covers only citizens and corporations organized under State law. It omits LLCs, partnerships, trusts, and other non-corporate entities, DC- or territory-chartered entities, permanent residents, and foreign-organized subsidiaries of U.S. parents. It has no ownership or control test. **[Loophole]**
13. **§2(2) and §3(a), "person" and "in the United States."** Neither term is defined in the bill. It's unclear whether "in the United States" includes the territorial sea, the EEZ, or the outer continental shelf. It's also unclear what makes a person "in the United States" when the activity occurs at sea. **[Ambiguity]**
14. **§2(3) and §2(1), mismatch.** The prohibition applies to any vessel regardless of size, but the registry reaches only vessels over 5,000 gross tons. Smaller vessels can conduct covered activity without being registered. **[Drafting / Loophole]**
15. **§2(3), "equipped for the recovery of seabed mineral resources."** The standard turns on equipment, not on actual activity. It may capture research vessels, dredgers, and drillships that never mine, while missing seabed collectors or platforms that aren't "vessels." The bill also doesn't say which tonnage measure applies. **[Drafting / Loophole]**

## Section 3: Prohibition and exemptions

16. **§3(a), "engage in" is undefined.** The bill doesn't say whether owning, financing, chartering, supplying, or buying from an operator counts. A U.S. company contracting with a foreign-flag, foreign-operated vessel may fall outside it. **[Loophole / Ambiguity]**
17. **§3(a), recovery only.** The bill doesn't prohibit onshore processing, refining, or purchase of nodules or minerals recovered elsewhere. It also doesn't reach non-U.S. persons operating abroad, so the environmental harm moves offshore of U.S. jurisdiction. **[Loophole / Policy argument]**
18. **§3(a), "any U.S. person anywhere."** The bill asserts extraterritorial reach over high-seas activity with no flag-state mechanism or enforcement tools. This also sits in tension with high-seas freedoms, which the U.S. treats as customary international law. **[Legal vulnerability]**
19. **§3(b), "an exploration license" is undefined.** The bill doesn't limit the issuing authority to the U.S. A license from a foreign state or an international body could arguably qualify. It also doesn't require that the license be valid, unexpired, or unrevoked. **[Loophole]**
20. **§3(b), "mining" is lowercase and uncapped.** The exemption covers "mining conducted under" a pre-enactment license, not the defined term, and sets no cap on volume, area, or duration. A licensee could argue it covers full-scale operations. **[Loophole]**
21. **§3(b), renewals, transfers, and pending applications.** The bill doesn't address renewals, extensions, amendments, assignments, or successor licenses after enactment. It also doesn't address pending applications or licenses issued during the 180-day window, since only licenses issued "before the date of enactment" qualify. **[Ambiguity / Loophole]**
22. **§3(b), commercial recovery permits.** The exemption mentions only exploration licenses. Under the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (30 U.S.C. ch. 26), as I understand it, commercial recovery requires a separate NOAA permit. The bill doesn't say whether a licensee may obtain or use one. Please have counsel verify against the statute. **[Ambiguity]**
23. **§3(c), "environmental assessment."** The term is undefined, with no volume cap, duration limit, plan, or agency approval requirement. "For the purpose of" is subjective, and the bill doesn't bar selling recovered material. Large-scale test recovery could be characterized this way. **[Loophole]**
24. **§3 generally, no transition or compensation provisions.** There is no wind-down for operations in progress, vessels at sea, or existing contracts. There is also nothing on compensation, which is relevant to reliance and takings arguments. **[Drafting / Legal vulnerability]**

## Section 4: Registry

25. **§4, no enforcement mechanism.** §5 penalizes only violations of §3. Failure to register, late reports, and false certifications carry no stated penalty under this Act. The bill also gives no rulemaking authority, deadlines, forms, or fees. **[Drafting]**
26. **§4(a), "operator" is undefined.** It could mean the owner, charterer, or day-to-day manager. The registration duty applies to every covered vessel with no U.S. nexus and no stated extraterritorial intent, so enforcement against foreign operators is doubtful. **[Ambiguity / Legal vulnerability]**
27. **§4(b), reports.** The required content is unspecified, with no tonnage, location, or depth data. That means NOAA couldn't verify the §2 threshold. The bill doesn't say who certifies or to what standard. It doesn't define "quarter," set a first due date, or provide a filing deadline. **[Drafting]**
28. **§4(b), no confidentiality protection.** Operators must report proprietary operational data with no express trade-secret protection. They would have to rely on default FOIA exemptions. **[Drafting / Industry concern]**
29. **§4, no authority, funding, or purpose.** NOAA is given no enforcement authority or appropriation. The bill doesn't state what the registry is for, or whether vessels operating under §3(b) or §3(c) must register. **[Drafting]**

## Section 5: Penalties

30. **§5(a) vs. §8, conflicting dates.** Penalties run for violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §8 delays the entire Act, including §3, by 180 days. It's unclear whether conduct during the gap is penalizable, which creates retroactivity and fair-notice problems. **[Ambiguity / Legal vulnerability]**
31. **§5(a), "each day on which the violation occurs."** A per-day penalty fits poorly with an annual-volume threshold. The bill doesn't say which days count, whether before or after the threshold is crossed. **[Ambiguity]**
32. **§5(a), no assessment procedure.** The bill names no assessing agency and provides no administrative process, hearing, penalty factors, mitigation, or inflation adjustment. **[Drafting]**
33. **§5(a), $1,000,000 per day.** With no proportionality factors, the maximum exposes the penalty to an Excessive Fines Clause challenge. **[Legal vulnerability]**
34. **§5, thin enforcement toolkit.** There is no injunctive relief, vessel or cargo forfeiture, citizen suit, or stated enforcing agency (DOJ or otherwise). Penalties alone leave practical gaps. **[Loophole / Drafting]**
35. **§5(b), "willfully" is undefined.** There is no express knowledge element for the §2(1)(B) determination and no good-faith reliance defense for conduct authorized by a federal license. Together with item 10, this is a strong due process argument. **[Legal vulnerability]**
36. **§5(b), inconsistencies.** The provision omits §5(a)'s "on or after enactment" language. It also doesn't address corporate-officer liability or organizational fines beyond a general cross-reference to title 18. **[Drafting]**

## Section 6: International agreement

37. **§6, ambiguous mandate.** "Seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months" could set a deadline to begin talks, conclude them, or bring an agreement into force. There is no reporting requirement, consequence for missing the date, definition of "equivalent," or specified form (treaty or executive agreement). **[Ambiguity]**
38. **§6, no link to §3.** The ban applies whether or not any other country joins. §6 concedes that a unilateral U.S. prohibition leaves foreign operators unaffected, which supports the leakage argument. **[Policy argument]**

## Sections 7 and 8: Duration and effective date

39. **§7(a) vs. §7(b), direct contradiction.** §7(a) keeps the ban until Congress acts, while §7(b) ends the whole Act 10 years after enactment. The sunset makes the §7(a) mechanism meaningless and means the ban lasts only about 9.5 years after the delayed §8 effective date. **[Drafting]**
40. **§7(a), the release mechanism.** There is no expedited joint-resolution procedure (so a filibuster applies), no deadline or funding for the NASEM report, and no designated requester. "Serious harm" is undefined, and "can be conducted without serious harm" is close to unfalsifiable. Congress also can't bind future Congresses, so the mechanism has no legal force beyond ordinary legislation. **[Drafting / Legal vulnerability]**
41. **§7(b), no savings clause.** The bill doesn't say whether penalties incurred before sunset survive, or whether pending enforcement continues. Counsel should assess how 1 U.S.C. §109 applies to an expiring Act rather than a repealed one. **[Ambiguity]**
42. **§8, the 180-day window.** Operations can continue and ramp up before §3 takes effect. Licenses or permits issued in that window aren't grandfathered under §3(b), and nothing addresses surges or wind-down. **[Loophole / Ambiguity]**

## Cross-cutting

43. **No findings, purpose, or constitutional authority.** The title says "Prohibition," but the operative text prohibits only a narrow category. The absence of a purpose clause weakens the case for reading ambiguities broadly. **[Drafting]**
44. **No coordination with existing law or implementation machinery.** There is no amendment, repeal, or savings language for the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act, the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act, or NEPA. The bill has no rulemaking authority, designated administering agency, judicial review provision, appropriations, or severability clause. Sections 3 and 4 may conflict with NOAA's existing authority. **[Drafting / Legal vulnerability]**
