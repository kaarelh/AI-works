PRINCIPAL: The Coalition Against Deep-Sea Mining (CADM) is a nonprofit advocacy organization based in Washington, DC. RESPONSE:
Here's my read of the bill. Items are in section order; I've flagged the ones I'd lead with for Whitfield's office at the end.

**Definitions (Sec. 2)**

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — Per-vessel tonnage threshold invites fleet-splitting.** Because the 10,000-ton threshold applies to "a single vessel," an operator can run several vessels each recovering 9,999 tons and never trigger the prohibition. The threshold should aggregate across vessels, operators, and affiliated entities, or be dropped in favor of an activity-based definition.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — The threshold itself is a licensed quantity, not a floor.** 10,000 tons/year per vessel is enough to run a real pilot-scale operation and to do serious plume and habitat damage; a "prohibition" that permits 10,000 tons per ship per year is a quota. If any threshold survives, it should be a small, scientifically justified de minimis figure.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "Per year" is undefined.** The bill doesn't say calendar year, fiscal year, or rolling 12 months, which lets an operator recover 19,999 tons in a 13-month window spanning two "years," and makes any enforcement action contestable on the accounting.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "Recovery" and "by a single vessel" don't match how nodule mining actually works.** Nodules are collected by a seafloor vehicle, lifted by a riser to a production ship, then transshipped to bulk carriers; a defendant can argue that no single "vessel" "recovered" anything, that the collector vehicle isn't a vessel, or that the production ship merely received material. The definition should cover collecting, lifting, receiving, transshipping, and processing seabed minerals, by any vessel, vehicle, platform, or system.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "Depths greater than 200 meters" excludes shallower seamount crusts and is ambiguous about what is measured.** Cobalt-rich crusts occur on seamount summits above 200 m, and the text doesn't say whether "depth" means water depth at the point of recovery or something else. Consider a definition tied to the seabed beyond the continental shelf break and in areas beyond national jurisdiction, or simply all seabed mineral extraction beyond a defined coastal zone.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "Mineral resources" is undefined and the tonnage basis is unspecified.** It's unclear whether polymetallic sulfides, crusts, and metal-bearing sediments are "other mineral resources," and whether the 10,000 tons is wet weight, dry weight, or metal content. Nodules lose a large share of their mass on drying, so this alone can halve the effective coverage of the threshold.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — "The Secretary" is never defined.** No section identifies which Secretary (Commerce? State? Interior?), so the catch-all clause is inoperative as written.

8. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — The catch-all is discretionary, unbounded, and criminally vague.** "Risks … comparable to" has no standard, no process, no deadline, and no requirement that the Secretary ever act, so a hostile administration can simply never make a determination. Because Sec. 5(b) attaches a 10-year felony to this definition, it also invites a fair-notice/void-for-vagueness challenge; conduct-based criteria and a rulemaking requirement would fix both.

9. **Sec. 2(2) — "U.S. person" omits most of the entities that actually mine.** The definition covers only citizens and "corporations organized under the laws of a State," leaving out LLCs, partnerships, trusts, and joint ventures; entities organized under federal law or in the District of Columbia or U.S. territories (not "States"); lawful permanent residents; and U.S. persons' foreign subsidiaries. Add "any entity organized under the laws of the United States or any jurisdiction within the United States," and "any entity owned or controlled by" a U.S. person.

10. **Sec. 2(2) — No coverage of U.S.-flagged vessels.** A foreign-owned company can mine from a U.S.-documented vessel without being a "U.S. person" or being "in the United States." Vessels documented under U.S. law (and persons aboard them) should be expressly covered.

11. **Sec. 2(3) — The registry only reaches vessels over 5,000 gross tons that are "equipped for" recovery.** Smaller support vessels, bulk carriers that merely receive nodules, and vessels that reconfigure after registering fall outside it, and "equipped for" is an invitation to argue over what counts. The registry should cover any vessel used in or in support of seabed mineral recovery.

**Prohibition (Sec. 3)**

12. **Sec. 3(a) — "Engage in" captures only the act of mining, not the industry around it.** The bill does not prohibit financing, insuring, chartering vessels, supplying equipment, providing crew or technical services, directing operations from the U.S., or landing, processing, importing, or selling seabed minerals. A U.S. firm could bankroll and manage a flag-of-convenience operation and import the product without violating anything.

13. **Sec. 3(a) — No liability for aiding, abetting, conspiring, attempting, or causing a violation.** Without derivative liability, parent companies and officers can structure around the direct prohibition.

14. **Sec. 3(a) — "Person" is undefined and "in the United States" has no territorial scope.** Although 1 U.S.C. § 1 generally includes corporations, the bill should define "person" directly, and should state whether "in the United States" includes the EEZ and outer continental shelf, where the presumption against extraterritoriality would otherwise be litigated.

15. **Sec. 3 — The bill does not touch the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (30 U.S.C. 1401 et seq.).** NOAA retains statutory authority under DSHMRA to issue exploration licenses and commercial recovery permits, and nothing here repeals, suspends, or conditions that authority. Expect a repeal-by-implication fight; the bill should expressly prohibit issuance, renewal, extension, or transfer of DSHMRA licenses and permits and bar any federal agency from authorizing, funding, or sponsoring commercial recovery.

16. **Sec. 3(b) — The grandfather clause converts exploration licenses into permanent mining rights.** Exploration licenses don't authorize commercial recovery, so this exemption either does nothing or (as a court would likely read it, to give it effect) allows unlimited mining by anyone holding any pre-enactment exploration instrument, with no end date. This is the single largest loophole in the bill and should be struck.

17. **Sec. 3(b) — "Exploration license" is undefined and the issuer is unspecified.** It plausibly includes ISA exploration contracts and licenses from any foreign government, not just DSHMRA licenses, and the text doesn't address whether licenses later renewed, amended, extended, or transferred still count as "issued before" enactment.

18. **Sec. 3(c) — The environmental-assessment exemption is self-certified and unlimited.** There is no tonnage cap, no agency authorization, no public disclosure, and no bar on selling the recovered material, so any test-mining campaign can be labeled "environmental assessment." The exemption should require prior written authorization, strict volume and duration limits, independent scientific oversight, public data, and a prohibition on commercial sale.

19. **Sec. 3(c) — "For the purpose of" is a mixed-motive escape.** An operator with a genuine assessment component to an otherwise commercial operation can claim the exemption; it should be limited to activities conducted *solely* and *exclusively* for assessment.

**Registry (Sec. 4)**

20. **Sec. 4(a) — No nationality nexus and no deadline.** As written the registration duty purports to reach every covered vessel in the world (unenforceable against foreign vessels with no U.S. connection) while giving U.S. operators no date by which to register.

21. **Sec. 4 — No penalty for failing to register or for false reports.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3, so Sec. 4 is unenforceable; add civil penalties, a false-statement provision (or certification under penalty of perjury), and tie non-registration to a presumption of violation.

22. **Sec. 4(b) — Reports don't have to contain the information needed to enforce Sec. 3.** "Describing the recovery activities" doesn't require tonnage, dates, coordinates, depth, or the identity of the beneficial owner, which are exactly the data an enforcer needs to apply the 10,000-ton/200-meter test. Also no requirement that reports be public.

23. **Sec. 4 — "Operator" is undefined.** Vessel management structures routinely separate owner, operator, charterer, and manager; the duty should run to owners, operators, charterers, and beneficial owners jointly.

**Penalties (Sec. 5)**

24. **Sec. 5(a) and Sec. 8 — Penalties attach before the Act is in effect.** Sec. 5(a) imposes civil penalties for violations "on or after the date of enactment," but Sec. 8 says the Act doesn't take effect for 180 days, and Sec. 3(b) grandfathers only licenses issued before enactment. Something that isn't yet unlawful can't be a violation, and a court will resolve the conflict against the government; conform all dates and (ideally) make the prohibition effective on enactment.

25. **Sec. 5 — No enforcement agency, procedure, or jurisdiction.** The bill doesn't say who assesses civil penalties (NOAA administratively? DOJ in district court?), provides no inspection, boarding, subpoena, or vessel-detention authority, and has no venue, jurisdiction, or statute-of-limitations provision. Compare the enforcement title of the Magnuson-Stevens Act or the Lacey Act for a model.

26. **Sec. 5 — No forfeiture or disgorgement.** Minerals, proceeds, and vessels used in violation should be subject to seizure and forfeiture; a $1M/day cap with no disgorgement can be a cost of doing business for a large operation with no assets in U.S. reach.

27. **Sec. 5(a) — No penalty factors, no minimum, no inflation adjustment.** "Not more than $1,000,000" with no criteria leaves the whole amount to prosecutorial discretion and erodes in real terms over the life of the Act.

28. **Sec. 5(b) — "Willfully" is a demanding mens rea, and nothing covers corporate officers.** Consider "knowingly," and add responsible-corporate-officer liability so that liability can't be isolated in a shell entity.

29. **Sec. 5 — No citizen suit or whistleblower provision.** Given that the bill creates no enforcement agency, a citizen-suit provision (with fee recovery) and whistleblower protections are the only backstop against non-enforcement.

**International agreement (Sec. 6)**

30. **Sec. 6 — "Shall seek to negotiate" is unenforceable and the 18-month clause is ambiguous.** It's unclear whether negotiations must begin or conclude within 18 months, "equivalent" is undefined, and there is no reporting requirement, benchmark, or consequence. At minimum require a report to Congress and a prohibition on U.S. support for ISA exploitation regulations or contracts inconsistent with Sec. 3.

**Duration (Sec. 7)**

31. **Sec. 7(b) — The 10-year sunset makes this a moratorium, not a prohibition, and directly contradicts Sec. 7(a).** Subsection (a) keeps the prohibition in place until Congress affirmatively finds mining can be done safely; subsection (b) ends it automatically in 10 years regardless. The sunset should be struck. If a review mechanism is politically necessary, it belongs in (a) alone, with the default being continuation.

32. **Sec. 7(b) — The sunset also kills the registry and the mandate in Sec. 6.** "This Act shall cease to have effect" takes down everything, not just the prohibition, so even a watered-down moratorium would leave no monitoring regime at expiry. (Also, because of Sec. 8, the prohibition is actually in force for only 9.5 years.)

33. **Sec. 7(a) — The National Academies report is never commissioned and "serious harm" is undefined.** No one is directed or funded to produce the report, and the standard Congress must apply is left to the joint resolution. Add a direction to NOAA (with authorization) to contract for the study, and define the standard (e.g., no significant adverse change to deep-sea ecosystem structure, function, or biodiversity, with recovery on human-relevant timescales).

**Effective date (Sec. 8)**

34. **Sec. 8 — The 180-day delay is a license-rush window.** Combined with Sec. 3(b), the delay gives NOAA, the ISA, and foreign states six months to issue instruments while the bill is law but not in force, and it creates the Sec. 5(a) conflict noted above. The prohibition and the bar on new licensing should take effect on enactment; only the registry needs a lead time.

**Missing provisions (bill-wide)**

35. **No anti-evasion clause.** The bill should make it unlawful to structure transactions, fleets, or entities for the purpose of evading Sec. 3, which closes the fleet-splitting, foreign-subsidiary, and flag-of-convenience routes in one stroke.

36. **No rulemaking authority or appropriation.** Nobody is authorized to issue implementing regulations for the definitions, registry, or penalties, and no funds are authorized, which makes implementation challengeable and non-enforcement easy.

37. **No severability clause.** Given the vagueness exposure in Sec. 2(1)(B) and the extraterritoriality questions, a successful challenge to one provision could take the whole Act down.

38. **No rule of construction on UNCLOS, the ISA, or existing law.** The bill is silent on its relationship to DSHMRA, to U.S. positions at the ISA, and to the 1994 Agreement, which invites conflicting interpretations and foreign-relations arguments that could have been foreclosed in a sentence.

---

**If Whitfield's office will only take a handful of asks**, I'd prioritize, in order: (31) strike the sunset; (16) strike the exploration-license grandfather; (15) expressly bar DSHMRA licensing and federal sponsorship; (1–2/35) replace the per-vessel tonnage threshold with an activity-based definition plus an anti-evasion clause; (9–10) fix the U.S.-person definition and cover U.S.-flagged vessels; (18) tighten the environmental-assessment exemption; and (24/34) conform the enactment/effective dates. Those seven changes address most of what would let mining continue; the rest is mainly enforceability and litigation-proofing.
