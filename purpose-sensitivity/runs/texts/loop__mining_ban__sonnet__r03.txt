## Section 2: Definitions

1. **§2(1)(A), 10,000-ton threshold.** The ban only reaches recovery above 10,000 metric tons per year, so pilot and "test" operations just under that line are legal. Test collections have historically been in the low thousands of tons, so the threshold may exempt much of what companies actually do before scaling up.
2. **§2(1)(A), "by a single vessel."** A company can split recovery across several vessels, each under the threshold, or use shuttle vessels, and never trigger the definition. The text should aggregate across vessels under common ownership or control.
3. **§2(1)(A), measurement ambiguity.** The text doesn't say whether tonnage is wet or dry weight (nodules hold substantial water), where it is measured (seafloor or surface), or what "per year" means (calendar year, rolling 12 months, or annualized for shorter operations). Each gap is an opening for a company to argue it was under the line.
4. **§2(1)(A), "recovery" and "seabed" undefined.** The Act doesn't say whether "recovery" covers collection on the seafloor, lifting, or only landing, or whether "seabed" includes the subsoil and sediments. Nor does it say where the prohibition applies (U.S. EEZ, extended continental shelf, or the international Area).
5. **§2(1)(A), 200-meter depth floor.** Mining at depths of 200 meters or less is entirely uncovered, and the text doesn't say how or where depth is measured for an operation that spans the line. Separately, "other mineral resources" could be read to sweep in oil and gas or sand and gravel, which invites opposition and a vagueness challenge.
6. **§2(1)(B), "the Secretary" is undefined.** The Act never says which Secretary (Commerce? Interior?), and §6 separately refers to the Secretary of State, so the reference is ambiguous.
7. **§2(1)(B), unbounded and discretionary.** The Secretary "determines" comparable risk, but there is no standard, procedure, deadline, or duty to act. This invites a nondelegation challenge, and an activity isn't clearly unlawful until a determination is made, which raises fair-notice problems for criminal enforcement.
8. **§2(2), "U.S. person" is too narrow.** It covers only citizens and corporations organized under a *State's* law, so it omits LLCs and partnerships (depending on state law), trusts, permanent residents, D.C. and territorial entities, and foreign-incorporated subsidiaries of U.S. companies. A U.S. parent can simply run operations through an offshore shell, so the definition needs an ownership-or-control test.
9. **§2(3), "covered vessel" threshold.** The 5,000-gross-ton floor lets smaller vessels mine without registering, and §2(1)(A) has no matching size limit, so a sub-5,000-ton vessel could exceed 10,000 tons a year unregistered. "Equipped for the recovery of seabed mineral resources" is also undefined, and non-vessel platforms and seafloor equipment are not covered.

## Section 3: Prohibition

10. **§3(a), jurisdictional reach.** "In the United States" isn't defined and doesn't clearly include the EEZ, the outer continental shelf, or the territories. Foreign-flag vessels operated by non-U.S. persons in U.S. waters may fall outside the prohibition.
11. **§3(a), only "engage in … mining" is prohibited.** The Act doesn't reach financing, supplying, processing, importing, selling, or landing minerals mined elsewhere. It also doesn't reach exploration, prospecting, or testing below the §2 threshold.
12. **§3, no bar on issuing authorizations.** Nothing stops NOAA or other agencies from accepting or granting licenses or permits under existing law, and nothing amends or repeals the existing statutory permitting scheme. That leaves a direct conflict between the old licensing authority and the new ban, and pending applications could be argued to proceed.
13. **§3(b), open-ended grandfathering.** The exemption has no sunset, volume cap, area limit, or environmental conditions. It also doesn't address renewals, extensions, amendments, expansions, or transfers of an existing license.
14. **§3(b), "exploration license" is undefined and doesn't match "mining."** The Act doesn't say who issued the license (NOAA, a foreign government, or the International Seabed Authority). An exploration license ordinarily doesn't authorize commercial recovery, yet the text exempts "mining conducted under" it, which companies can read as permission to extract at scale.
15. **§3(b), rush-to-license risk.** The cutoff is the date of *enactment*, so any license issued between now and enactment is exempt. Nothing freezes issuance or voids licenses granted in the interim.
16. **§3(c), environmental-assessment exemption.** It turns on the "purpose" of the activity, with no cap on volume, duration, or area and no government approval, oversight, or independent review. Commercial-scale extraction can be labeled "assessment," and the Act doesn't bar selling what is recovered.

## Section 4: Registry

17. **§4(a), "operator" undefined, no deadline.** The Act doesn't say who counts as the operator (owner, charterer, or contractor) or when registration must occur, such as before any recovery begins.
18. **§4, no implementing authority.** NOAA isn't given rulemaking, inspection, or data-collection powers, and the Act doesn't specify what registration information is required.
19. **§4(b), self-certified, thin reports.** Reports are certified only by the operator, with no required content (tonnage, location, depth, dates), audit, verification, or independent monitoring such as AIS or vessel tracking. There are also no record-retention rules and no public disclosure.
20. **§4, no enforcement hook.** §5 penalizes only violations of §3, so failing to register, filing a late report, or filing a false one carries no penalty under this Act.

## Section 5: Penalties

21. **§5(a) vs. §8, conflicting dates.** Civil penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §8 makes the Act effective 180 days later. This creates confusion about whether conduct in the gap is penalized, and an easy fair-notice or retroactivity argument for defendants.
22. **§5(a), no enforcement mechanism.** The Act doesn't say who assesses or collects civil penalties, whether through an administrative process or a civil action by the Attorney General. It also doesn't provide for injunctions, vessel and equipment forfeiture, seizure of recovered minerals, or disgorgement of profits.
23. **§5, no restoration or damages liability.** Nothing requires remediation of harm or provides natural-resource damages, and there is no citizen-suit provision to back up agency enforcement.
24. **§5(a), fixed dollar cap.** The $1,000,000 maximum has no inflation adjustment, and the "not more than" language gives no minimum or guidance, which could produce token penalties.
25. **§5(b), "willfully" and corporate liability.** The willfulness standard may let a defendant claim it believed an exemption (§3(b) or (c)) applied. The Act has no responsible-corporate-officer or aiding-and-abetting language, and no venue or jurisdiction provision for offenses committed far offshore.

## Section 6: International Agreement

26. **§6, soft mandate.** The Secretary of State need only "seek to negotiate," with no obligation to conclude an agreement, report to Congress, or face any consequence. It is also unclear whether the 18-month deadline applies to starting talks or finishing them.
27. **§6, vague terms.** "Equivalent" prohibitions is undefined, the text doesn't say whether the result is a treaty requiring Senate consent or an executive agreement, and it doesn't address coordination with the International Seabed Authority or other mining states.

## Section 7: Duration

28. **§7(b) contradicts §7(a).** Subsection (a) makes the ban indefinite, but (b) ends the whole Act after 10 years, with no extension mechanism. The industry gets a built-in expiration date, and a court would have to reconcile the two provisions.
29. **§7(b), no savings clause.** When the Act "ceases to have effect," pending enforcement actions and accrued penalties may be extinguished. Because the clock runs from enactment, not the effective date, the ban also operates for only about 9.5 years.
30. **§7(a), lifting the ban.** "Without serious harm" is undefined, nothing requires the National Academies to produce a report or sets a timeline or funding, and the Act doesn't say whether a joint resolution lifts the ban generally or for particular operations. There is also no regulatory framework in place, so lifting would leave mining effectively unregulated.

## Section 8 and Whole-Bill Issues

31. **§8, 180-day gap.** Companies can ramp up operations, secure licenses (see item 15), or move assets during the delay. The Act has no interim moratorium on new authorizations and doesn't say how operations underway at the effective date are treated.
32. **No findings or purpose section.** Congressional findings on ecological risk and scientific uncertainty would support the statute against challenge and guide courts on ambiguous terms.
33. **No severability clause.** If a court strikes a provision (for example §2(1)(B) as an unconstitutional delegation), nothing says the rest of the Act survives.
34. **No relationship to existing law.** The Act has no savings or preemption language addressing existing deep seabed mining and offshore leasing statutes, NEPA, or other regimes. Conflicting authorities will be argued by industry.
35. **No rulemaking, judicial review, or appropriations provisions.** No agency is directed to issue implementing regulations, and nothing authorizes funds for NOAA or the Coast Guard to monitor and enforce.

## Highest Priority

- **Close the threshold loopholes:** items 1–3 (volume, single-vessel, and measurement gaps) and 9.
- **Narrow the exemptions:** items 13–16, which as drafted could allow substantial mining.
- **Fix the dates and sunset:** items 21, 28, 29, and 31.
- **Make the Act enforceable:** items 20, 22, and 25.
- **Cover foreign shells and the supply chain:** items 8 and 11.
