# S. 2817: Loopholes, Ambiguities, and Drafting Problems

This analysis is based only on the text you pasted. For publication, please check it against the official manager's amendment text and have legislative counsel or legal scholars review the constitutional points (items 21–23). Several items are arguable rather than clear defects, so I've phrased those as challenges critics or litigants could raise.

**Section 2: Definitions**

1. **Sec. 2 (generally): Key terms are undefined.** The bill never defines "artificial intelligence," "artificial intelligence model," "operator," "data center," or "advanced AI chip." It also never says which "Secretary" it means (Commerce is implied by Sec. 4 but not stated). Each gap invites disputes over coverage and makes enforcement harder.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A): "Single training run" can be split.** A developer could stay under 10^26 operations per run by dividing training into separate runs, such as pretraining, continued pretraining, and fine-tuning. Other options are merging separately trained models or distilling from a larger model. The bill doesn't say whether compute is added up across runs or across a model's lineage.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A): The compute threshold is fixed.** Algorithmic efficiency keeps improving, so models trained under 10^26 operations may eventually match today's frontier systems. The bill gives no authority to adjust the threshold.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A) vs. (B): "Model" vs. "system."** Prong (A) covers "models" and prong (B) covers "systems." This leaves open whether a system built from several sub-threshold models (for example an agent, ensemble, or tool-using scaffold) falls under prong (A).

5. **Sec. 2(1)(B): The capability standard is vague.** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no benchmark. It doesn't say whether "human" means the average person or experts, what counts as a "cognitive task," or what "most" or "substantially" mean. No process, notice, or appeal is set out for the Secretary's determination.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B): Coverage can attach after the fact.** A system becomes covered only once the Secretary makes a determination. Developers could argue they had no notice that their work was unlawful while they were doing it. That matters especially given the criminal penalties in Sec. 5(b).

7. **Sec. 2(2): The "U.S. person" definition is narrow.** It covers citizens and "corporation[s] organized under the laws of a State." It appears to leave out lawful permanent residents, LLCs, partnerships, and entities organized in DC or U.S. territories, since "State" is undefined. It also excludes foreign subsidiaries of U.S. companies, which could train covered systems abroad.

8. **Sec. 2(3): The covered-facility threshold can be avoided.** Operators could stay under 50,000 chips per facility by spreading compute across several sites. Distributed training across multiple data centers would then escape the registry, and "facility" isn't tied to common ownership or control.

**Section 3: Prohibition**

9. **Sec. 3(a): "Train, develop, or deploy" are undefined.** "Develop" could reach anything from research to fine-tuning. "Deploy" doesn't clearly say whether it covers offering API access, releasing open weights, or a U.S. business using a foreign company's covered system.

10. **Sec. 3(a): Offshore training is a gap.** A foreign entity with no U.S. presence can train a covered system abroad. The bill doesn't clearly bar importing its model weights or serving U.S. users from outside the country.

11. **Sec. 3(b): The grandfather clause has no limits.** Any system "whose training began before" enactment is exempt indefinitely. The clause doesn't say whether later fine-tuning, scaling up, new versions, or continued deployment of that system remain exempt. Developers could also start nominal training runs just before enactment to qualify.

12. **Sec. 3(b) and Sec. 8: The timing is mismatched.** The grandfather date is enactment, but the ban takes effect 180 days later. Training that starts during that window isn't grandfathered and isn't yet banned, so it's unclear whether continuing it after the effective date is a violation.

13. **Sec. 3(c): The safety-evaluation exception could swallow the rule.** It exempts activities conducted "for the purpose of safety evaluation" with no definition, scope limit, approval process, or oversight. A company could describe frontier training as safety work, and proving otherwise would require proving its intent.

**Section 4: Registry**

14. **Sec. 4: The registry has no penalties.** Sec. 5 penalizes violations of Sec. 3 only. Failing to register, failing to report, or filing an inaccurate report carries no penalty under the Act itself, though 18 U.S.C. § 1001 might apply to false statements.

15. **Sec. 4(a): There is no registration deadline or procedure.** The bill sets no timeline for registering. It also gives Commerce no rulemaking authority to define the process.

16. **Sec. 4(b): Report contents are vague, and there's no way to verify them.** "Describing the computing workloads" sets no level of detail and doesn't require compute figures. Commerce gets no authority to inspect, audit, or subpoena, so the reports amount to self-certification.

17. **Sec. 4: The registry doesn't connect to the ban.** The registry tracks facilities, not covered AI systems or training runs. Training at unregistered or sub-threshold facilities, or abroad, wouldn't show up.

**Section 5: Penalties**

18. **Sec. 5(a): The penalty date conflicts with the effective date.** Civil penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but under Sec. 8 the Act doesn't take effect until 180 days later. That creates a direct conflict and a fair-notice argument for anything done during the gap.

19. **Sec. 5(a): No enforcer or process is named.** The bill doesn't say which agency assesses civil penalties, whether through administrative proceedings or a court action, or who brings suit. "Each day on which the violation occurs" is also unclear when applied to training as opposed to ongoing deployment.

20. **Sec. 5(a): The penalty may be too small to deter.** A cap of $1 million per day may be modest compared with what large developers spend on and earn from frontier models. Critics may argue it works as a cost of doing business rather than a deterrent.

21. **Sec. 5(b): Criminal liability meets vague definitions.** Criminal penalties attached to the vague capability standard in Sec. 2(1)(B) and the undefined "safety evaluation" exception in Sec. 3(c) raise a potential void-for-vagueness challenge under the Due Process Clause.

**Section 6: International Agreement**

22. **Sec. 6: The negotiation mandate is ambiguous and non-binding.** It's unclear whether the 18-month deadline applies to *seeking* negotiations or *concluding* them. Nothing happens if no agreement is reached, "equivalent" is undefined, and the domestic ban doesn't depend on other countries adopting one.

**Section 7: Duration**

23. **Sec. 7(a) vs. 7(b): The two end conditions conflict.** Sec. 7(a) says the ban lasts "until" Congress acts by joint resolution, while Sec. 7(b) sunsets the whole Act after 10 years. The bill doesn't say which controls. Sec. 7(b) also ends the Act entirely, not just the ban, including the registry and the treaty mandate.

24. **Sec. 7(a): The off-ramp is largely redundant and depends on a report nobody is directed to write.** A joint resolution needs presentment like any other law, so Sec. 7(a) adds little to Congress's existing power to repeal. Nothing directs or funds the National Academies to produce the report it relies on.

25. **Sec. 7(b) and Sec. 8: The ban runs about 9.5 years, not 10.** The sunset counts from enactment but the Act takes effect 180 days later, so the prohibition is in force for roughly 9.5 years.

**Issues the bill doesn't address**

26. **No preemption clause.** The bill doesn't say whether it preempts state AI laws, which could produce overlapping or conflicting regimes and litigation.

27. **Other potential constitutional arguments.** Litigants might raise First Amendment claims (code or model weights as speech, particularly for open-weight release) or nondelegation claims over the Secretary's open-ended designation power. Neither is clearly winning, but either could delay enforcement.
