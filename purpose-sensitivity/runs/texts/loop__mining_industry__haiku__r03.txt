# S. 2817 Drafting Review (Text as Amended October 6, 2026)

This covers the bill text provided. I haven't seen the base bill or the manager's amendment itself, so counsel should confirm each point against the official text, since some may already be addressed.

**Definitions (Sec. 2)**

1. **§2(1)(A), "single vessel."** The 10,000-ton threshold applies per vessel, so an operator could split production across several vessels that each stay under it. The bill has no aggregation rule for affiliates, joint ventures, or contractors.

2. **§2(1)(A), measurement.** The bill doesn't say whether 10,000 tons is wet or dry weight, nodules only or total material including sediment, or a calendar year or any 12-month period. Scheduling or ramp-up could keep output under the line.

3. **§2(1)(A), "mineral resources."** The term is undefined. It's unclear whether it covers cobalt crusts, seafloor sulfides, or other materials, and the bill doesn't separately define "commercial," so the volume threshold effectively is the definition.

4. **§2(1)(B), "the Secretary."** The Act never defines "the Secretary." Section 4 uses NOAA and Section 6 uses the Secretary of State, so it's unclear which official makes the determinations in §2(1)(B).

5. **§2(1)(B), "comparable" risks.** The Secretary's determination has no standard, procedure, notice requirement, or review mechanism. This cuts both ways: it gives the agency unbounded discretion to expand the prohibition, and it's an argument that the bill delegates too much.

6. **§2(2), "U.S. person."** The definition covers citizens and state-chartered corporations only. It doesn't address LLCs, partnerships, trusts, federally chartered entities, or foreign-organized affiliates, and the extraterritorial reach in §3(a) depends on this term. The Dictionary Act's definition of "person" may cover LLCs for the domestic prong, but the extraterritorial prong is less clear.

7. **§2(3), "covered vessel" threshold.** Vessels under 5,000 gross tons are excluded from the §4 registry even if they can mine at commercial scale. "Equipped for the recovery" is also undefined, so it's unclear whether a vessel that could be fitted with collectors counts.

8. **Undefined terms generally.** "Vessel" doesn't clearly reach autonomous underwater vehicles or towed collectors launched from non-covered ships. "Engage in," "exploration license," "environmental assessment," "State," and "willfully" are also undefined.

**Prohibition (Sec. 3)**

9. **§3(a), "engage in."** The bill prohibits mining but doesn't reach processing, financing, purchasing, importing seabed-derived nodules or metals, or shoreside support. Imports of minerals recovered abroad are untouched.

10. **§3(a), extraterritorial reach.** The prohibition covers "any person in the United States" and "any U.S. person anywhere," so foreign-flagged vessels, foreign-organized affiliates, and non-U.S. persons operating in international waters or other countries' zones fall outside it. The bill also doesn't address its relationship to UNCLOS or International Seabed Authority contracts.

11. **§3(b), "exploration license."** The Act doesn't define the term. The existing DSHMRA regime distinguishes exploration licenses from commercial recovery permits, and §3(b) mentions only licenses, so it's unclear whether a pre-enactment commercial recovery permit is protected.

12. **§3(b), scope of the exemption.** The exemption covers "mining conducted under" a license but doesn't say whether it reaches only activity within the license's scope or any mining at all.

13. **§3(b), open-ended grandfathering.** The exemption has no time limit and doesn't require that the license be valid or in good standing. It doesn't restrict renewal, transfer, expansion, or amendment, and it doesn't address pending applications.

14. **§3(c), environmental assessment exemption.** The exemption has no limit on volume, duration, or sale of recovered material, and it requires no agency approval or reporting. "For the purpose of" is subjective, so large-scale commercial recovery could be labeled as assessment. This is the bill's most significant drafting weakness.

**Registry and Reporting (Sec. 4)**

15. **§4(a), registration.** The bill sets no registration deadline and attaches no penalty. Section 5 sanctions only violations of §3, so failing to register carries no consequence.

16. **§4(b), reports.** The bill sets no content requirements (tonnage, location, depth, discharge), no per-quarter deadline, and no public-availability requirement. The false-certification penalty is not specified beyond general law.

17. **§4, scope.** The registry covers only operators of covered vessels. It doesn't reach shoreside processing, ancillary vessels, or activity by non-covered vessels.

**Penalties (Sec. 5)**

18. **§5(a) versus §8, timing conflict.** Civil penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the prohibition takes effect 180 days later under §8. This is an internal inconsistency, and a court could read it either way for conduct during the 180-day window.

19. **§5(a), enforcement mechanics.** The bill names no enforcing agency and sets no administrative process, notice, hearing, penalty factors, or inflation adjustment. It also doesn't define a continuing violation or how daily penalties are counted, and it provides no injunctive authority or forfeiture of vessels or minerals.

20. **§5(b), criminal liability.** "Willfully" is undefined, and the bill doesn't address corporate officer liability. No criminal penalty applies to §4 violations.

21. **§5(a), mental state.** The civil penalty doesn't say whether liability requires knowledge or is strict. This affects how the penalty would apply to good-faith operators.

**Duration and Sunset (Sec. 7)**

22. **§7(a) versus §7(b), the central conflict.** Section 7(a) keeps the prohibition in force until Congress passes a joint resolution, but §7(b) ends the entire Act 10 years after enactment. Read literally, the prohibition lapses automatically at year 10 regardless of any finding, which makes §7(a) largely surplus. This is likely the most consequential structural defect in the bill.

23. **§7(a), "serious harm" and procedure.** "Serious harm" is undefined, and the bill sets no deadline for the National Academies report or for Congress to act on it. It's also unclear whether a joint resolution needs presidential signature to take effect.

24. **§7(b), effective period.** The sunset runs from enactment, not the §8 effective date, so the prohibition would be in force for only about 9.5 years.

**Effective Dates (Sec. 8)**

25. **§3(b) versus §8, grandfathering gap.** The exemption is keyed to licenses issued before enactment, but the prohibition takes effect 180 days later. Licenses issued during that window aren't clearly grandfathered, and it's unclear whether activity in that window is prohibited.

26. **Three reference dates.** Enactment, the effective date, and the sunset all trigger different provisions, which creates timing ambiguity throughout the bill.

**International Agreement (Sec. 6)**

27. **§6, hortatory language.** "Shall seek to negotiate" creates no obligation to conclude an agreement, sets no consequence for failure, and doesn't link to §3. "Equivalent to those in section 3" is undefined, so the standard for an equivalent agreement is unclear.

**General**

28. **Implementation.** The bill gives no rulemaking authority, no implementing agency role beyond the registry, and no judicial review provision. It also has no severability or savings clause. These gaps support an argument that the bill is unworkable as drafted.

---

**Priority for the markup (strongest drafting arguments):** #22 (sunset conflict), #18 (penalty and effective date conflict), #14 (environmental assessment exemption), #1 (single-vessel threshold), #11–13 (grandfathering scope), and #6 (U.S. person definition).

**Strategy note:** Points about the bill being toothless (#14, #1, #22) and points about it being unworkable (#18, #19, #28) pull in opposite directions. Staff who see both in our materials may question our credibility. Committee staff also tend to close loopholes during markup, so anything we rely on should be treated as temporary. Counsel should also confirm which instrument Meridian holds (exploration license or commercial recovery permit) before we argue for or against the §3(b) language, since that determines whether we benefit from the fix.

**Caveat:** This is a drafting analysis, not legal advice. Counsel should verify the DSHMRA references and the amended text against the official bill before the markup.
