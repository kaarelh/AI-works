RESPONSE:
This is a drafting review, not legal advice, so have counsel check the legal-doctrine points (vagueness, nondelegation, Commerce Clause, First Amendment, savings clause) before the list goes out. Items are grouped by section and numbered continuously.

## Definitions (Sec. 2)

1. **§2(1)(A), "single training run."** The term is undefined, so a developer can split a large effort into staged runs, resume from checkpoints, merge models, or distill, with each piece under 10^26. Count cumulative compute across a model lineage, including pre-training, post-training, RL, and continued training, and add anti-evasion language.
2. **§2(1)(A), static threshold.** Algorithmic efficiency gains mean dangerous capability may arrive below 10^26, and nothing lets the executive lower the number. "Integer or floating-point operations" also leaves out other numeric formats and non-standard hardware. Add rulemaking authority to lower the threshold and define compute in a format-agnostic way.
3. **§2(1)(A) vs. (B), "model" vs. "system."** Clause (A) covers a "model," while (B) covers a "system." A system built from several sub-threshold models, such as an ensemble or agent scaffold, escapes (A) and depends entirely on a discretionary determination under (B).
4. **§2(1)(B), "the Secretary."** No Secretary is defined in the Act, and §6 uses "Secretary of State." Name the responsible official (presumably Commerce) and who must be consulted.
5. **§2(1)(B), determination process.** There are no criteria, deadline, publication requirement, or standard for "substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks." This invites nondelegation and vagueness challenges, and developers cannot know in advance whether they are covered. It is also unclear whether conduct before a determination is unlawful.
6. **§2(2), "U.S. person."** It omits LLCs, partnerships, trusts, nonprofits, unincorporated associations, and lawful permanent residents. It also omits foreign subsidiaries of U.S. companies and corporations organized under federal, D.C., or territorial law ("State" is undefined). Together these gaps allow offshore restructuring around the extraterritorial reach.
7. **§2(3), "covered facility."** "Advanced AI chips," "data center," and "operator" are undefined. A chip count is not a compute measure, so a firm can spread chips across sub-50,000 sites, lease capacity, or train across facilities with no aggregation across affiliates. Use a compute-capacity metric with aggregation rules.
8. **General undefined terms.** "Artificial intelligence," "train," "develop," "deploy," "safety evaluation," and "person" are all undefined. "Develop" and "deploy" matter most, since they carry criminal liability and could draw vagueness and First Amendment challenges.

## Prohibition (Sec. 3)

9. **§3(a), scope of conduct.** The section does not reach possessing, hosting, selling, licensing, exporting, or publishing weights, nor providing compute or financing. Open-weights release and transfer of weights abroad are not clearly covered.
10. **§3(a), "in the United States."** It is unclear how this applies to cloud and remote activity, such as a foreign firm training on U.S. servers or a foreign firm deploying to U.S. users from abroad. Specify the nexus.
11. **§3(a), when a system becomes "covered."** The definition turns on compute already "used," so conduct is clearly unlawful only once the threshold is crossed. A run can be paused just under 10^26 and resumed. Prohibit runs intended or reasonably expected to exceed the threshold.
12. **§3(b), breadth of grandfathering.** The exemption covers the whole system, so further training, scaling, fine-tuning, new versions, and expanded deployment of a pre-enactment system may all be exempt. "Training began" is undefined, so continuing runs and derivative models could qualify.
13. **§3(b), race window.** The cutoff is the date of enactment, which gives developers months to start new large runs and claim grandfathered status. Consider an earlier cutoff, such as the date of introduction, with counsel's view on retroactivity.
14. **§3(b), no oversight of grandfathered systems.** There is no registration, reporting, compute cap, or end date for exempt systems, and no process to verify that a system actually qualifies. Put the burden on the claimant and require documentation.
15. **§3(c), safety evaluation.** The exemption is purpose-based, undefined, unlimited in scale, and self-certified, and it covers "train, develop, or deploy." A lab could label frontier training "safety evaluation." Limit it to evaluation of existing systems by approved parties, exclude new training, and require notice to the regulator.
16. **§3, burden of proof on exceptions.** The Act does not say who must prove or disprove §3(b) and (c). State that the party claiming an exemption bears the burden.
17. **Government and contractors.** The Act is silent on federal agencies and on contractors working for them. "Person" likely excludes the United States, and contractors may claim derivative immunity. Decide the treatment explicitly, since this is the largest potential carve-out.

## Registry (Sec. 4)

18. **§4(a), registration.** There is no deadline, form, update duty, or treatment of existing facilities. Nothing penalizes failure to register.
19. **§4(b), reports.** "Describing the computing workloads" is vague, the reports are self-certified, and nothing provides for verification or audit. Required content is unspecified: run size, customer identity, and model purpose should be listed. Quarterly reporting also lets a whole training run finish before anyone sees it.
20. **§4, coverage gaps.** Facilities under 50,000 chips are not covered. There are no know-your-customer duties for cloud or compute providers, no chip tracking, and no advance notice of large runs.
21. **§4 vs. §5, penalties.** §5 penalizes only violations of §3, so failing to register or filing a false report carries no stated penalty. Add explicit penalties and confidentiality and FOIA handling for submitted data.

## Enforcement and penalties (Sec. 5)

22. **No designated enforcer.** The Act does not say who assesses civil penalties, sues, or prosecutes, and gives no rulemaking authority or appropriations. Designate roles for Commerce and DOJ.
23. **No investigative or preventive tools.** The Act lacks subpoena, inspection, and audit authority, injunctive or stop-work power, and whistleblower protections. Fines after the fact do little if a training run can't be stopped.
24. **§5(a), penalty size.** A cap of $1,000,000 per day is small next to frontier training budgets, and there is no floor, revenue scaling, or disgorgement. "Each day on which the violation occurs" is also ambiguous for one-time acts like deployment.
25. **§5(a), "on or after the date of enactment."** This conflicts with §8, under which nothing takes effect for 180 days. The mismatch invites retroactivity and fair-notice arguments. Align the dates.
26. **§5(b), "willfully."** The mens rea term is undefined and, combined with the vague definitions, lets defendants argue they did not know a system was covered. Define it, and consider "knowingly or recklessly" as to covered status.
27. **§5(b), organizational fines.** Default federal fine caps for organizations convicted of felonies are modest (I believe $500,000 or twice gain or loss under 18 U.S.C. §3571; please confirm). Specify fines suited to large firms and add responsible-corporate-officer liability.
28. **No private or state enforcement.** There is no citizen-suit, competitor-suit, or state attorney general role, so enforcement depends entirely on federal discretion and resources.

## International and offshoring (Sec. 6)

29. **§6, "shall seek to negotiate."** The duty is to try, not to conclude, and it is unclear whether the 18-month deadline applies to starting or finishing talks. There is no reporting to Congress, no consequence for failure, no definition of "equivalent," and no verification mechanism.
30. **Offshoring and supply.** The Act has no export-control or chip-supply provisions and no restriction on U.S. financing of foreign development. Combined with the U.S.-person gaps (item 6), development can move abroad.

## Duration and effective date (Sec. 7–8)

31. **§7(a) vs. §7(b).** Subsection (a) says the prohibition lasts until Congress finds safety, but (b) ends the Act after 10 years regardless. The two directly contradict each other, and (b) controls in practice.
32. **§7(b), hard sunset.** The prohibition expires on a date certain whether or not safety has been shown, and since §8 delays the start, the effective period is about 9.5 years. Developers can wait it out. Remove the sunset or make expiry depend on the §7(a) finding.
33. **§7(b), no savings clause.** The Act does not preserve penalties and prosecutions for violations that occurred before expiry. Add an express savings clause.
34. **§7(a), standard and process.** "Can be developed safely" is undefined, and the National Academies has no mandate, deadline, scope, or independence safeguards. A joint resolution also needs presidential signature or an override. Require specific, verifiable conditions and consider tying lifting to an international agreement being in force.
35. **§8, 180-day delay.** The delay gives developers a window to accelerate runs, and no interim measures exist. Consider immediate registration, reporting of runs in progress, and an interim bar on starting new covered runs.

## Litigation resilience

36. **No severability clause.** If a court strikes one provision, such as the §2(1)(B) delegation, the whole Act could fall. Add severability.
37. **No findings or jurisdictional basis.** The bill has no findings tying the prohibition to interstate commerce or national security, and no jurisdictional element in §3. Purely intrastate activity and the breadth of "develop" (code, research, publication) invite Commerce Clause and First Amendment challenges.
38. **No judicial review or venue provisions.** The Act sets no venue or review standard for Secretary determinations and no rule against stays of enforcement. That invites forum-shopped preliminary injunctions.
39. **State law.** The Act does not say whether it preempts, supplements, or leaves intact state AI laws. Say which.
