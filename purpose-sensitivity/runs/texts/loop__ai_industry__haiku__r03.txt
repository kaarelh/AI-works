# Drafting Problems and Gaps in S. 2817 (as amended October 6, 2026)

These are analytical notes for your legal team, not legal advice. Counsel should verify each point against the official text and the legislative record.

**Definitions (§2)**

1. **§2(1)(A):** The compute threshold applies per "single training run," but the bill doesn't say whether continued pretraining, checkpoint continuation, or staged training programs count as one run. A program split into sub-threshold runs may fall outside the definition.
2. **§2(1)(A):** The bill doesn't specify how FLOPs are measured (precision, dense vs. sparse operations, whether inference is included, or who does the counting). Any of these choices could move a model above or below 10^26.
3. **§2(1)(A) vs. (B):** Clause (A) covers a "model," while clause (B) covers a "system." Ensembles, agentic scaffolds, or distilled systems built from sub-threshold models may fall outside (A), and it's unclear whether (B) reaches them.
4. **§2(1)(B):** "The Secretary" has no stated referent, and the bill sets no procedure, notice, standard, or appeal for the capability determination. It also doesn't say whether a determination applies to systems already trained or deployed.
5. **§2(1)(B):** "Substantially exceeding," "most," and "cognitive tasks" are undefined and have no benchmark, so covered systems can't be identified in advance. This is a strong basis for the "unworkable" argument.
6. **§2(2):** "U.S. person" excludes foreign-organized subsidiaries, foreign partnerships, and non-citizen U.S. residents abroad. Work done through these entities falls outside the "anywhere" clause of §3(a).
7. **§2(3):** "Data center" and "advanced AI chips" are undefined, and the bill doesn't say how to count owned vs. leased or cloud capacity across multiple sites. The 50,000-chip trigger can therefore be avoided by distributing capacity, or be hard to apply.
8. **§2 (general):** "Person," "State," "train," "develop," and "deploy" are undefined. "Deploy" in particular doesn't say whether internal use, API access, or open-weight release counts.

**Prohibition (§3)**

9. **§3(a):** "Develop" and "train" appear to reach pre-release research, internal evaluation, and experimentation with no carve-out below deployment. This overbreadth supports an "unworkable" argument.
10. **§3(b):** Grandfathering applies to systems "whose training began before" enactment, but the bill doesn't address continued training, fine-tuning, or post-training after enactment. A run that began before enactment could arguably keep expanding under the exemption.
11. **§3(b):** The exemption removes the entire subsection (a) prohibition, which can be read to permit indefinite deployment of grandfathered systems, not just completion of the original training run. This is one of the largest gaps for existing operations.
12. **§3(b):** Grandfathered systems have no registration or documentation requirement, so the government has no means to verify the exemption.
13. **§3(c):** "Safety evaluation" is undefined and turns on a subjective "purpose" test, with no standards, registration, reporting, or limits on scale or duration. The exemption could arguably cover large-scale training or deployment described as evaluation. This is the most significant loophole in the bill.
14. **§3(c):** The exemption doesn't say whether evaluations must be conducted by the developer or may be conducted by third parties, whether results must be shared, or whether it applies only to covered systems.

**Registry (§4)**

15. **§4(a):** Registration is triggered by covered *facilities*, not covered *AI systems*. A facility with covered models but fewer than 50,000 chips isn't required to register, and a large facility that runs no covered systems must still register.
16. **§4(b):** "Computing workloads" is undefined, and the reports don't have to identify whether covered systems are involved. The registry therefore can't be used to enforce §3.
17. **§4:** Section 5 penalizes only violations of §3, so the bill sets no penalty for failing to register or to file reports. Enforcement of §4 is unclear.
18. **§4:** The bill has no confidentiality or trade-secret protection for reports, and it doesn't name the official who must certify them. The disclosure risk is a legitimate concern to raise.
19. **§4:** The bill doesn't say whether the registry reaches facilities located outside the United States.

**Penalties (§5)**

20. **§5(a) vs. §8:** The civil penalty applies to violations "occurring on or after the date of enactment," but §3 takes effect 180 days later. It's unclear whether conduct during that window can be penalized, and the two provisions point to different start dates.
21. **§5(a):** "For each day on which the violation occurs" isn't defined for continuing violations. The bill also doesn't name an enforcing agency or specify the procedure (DOJ or Commerce, civil action or administrative proceeding, and in which court).
22. **§5(b):** "Willfully" is undefined. Good-faith reliance on the ambiguous terms in §2(1)(B) and §3(c) supports a willfulness defense.
23. **§5:** The bill doesn't say who is liable when a model is trained by a contractor or cloud provider, or whether compute suppliers can be liable for aiding a violation.

**International agreement (§6)**

24. **§6:** "Seek to negotiate" creates no operative obligation, no reporting requirement, and no consequence for failure. "Equivalent to those in section 3" is also undefined. This is more a drafting weakness than a loophole, but it can be cited to argue the provision is aspirational.

**Duration (§7)**

25. **§7(a) vs. §7(b):** §7(a) keeps the prohibition in effect until Congress finds by joint resolution that covered systems can be developed safely. §7(b) says the entire Act ceases to have effect after 10 years, regardless of any finding. As written, the prohibition lapses on a fixed date.
26. **§7(a):** The bill sets no deadline for the National Academies report, doesn't require that it be issued, and doesn't define "developed safely." The joint-resolution path also doesn't say how the resolution is presented to the President. The off-ramp may never trigger.
27. **§7(b):** "Cease to have effect" is ambiguous about whether penalties and registry obligations survive for conduct before the sunset. The bill has no savings clause.

**Effective date and structure (§8)**

28. **§3(b) and §8:** Grandfathering is keyed to enactment, but the prohibition takes effect 180 days later. Training that begins during that window is neither clearly exempt nor clearly covered, and it's unresolved whether a run started in the window becomes covered when §3 takes effect. The gap also creates an incentive to start runs quickly.
29. **General:** The bill has no severability clause, no savings clause, no rulemaking authority for defining key terms or FLOP counting, no appropriation, and no judicial review provision. Core obligations depend on agency determinations made without procedural safeguards.

**Notes for the markup strategy**

- **Separate "loopholes" from "unworkable" points.** Undefined terms, the §2(1)(B) determination process, the FLOP-counting gaps, and the "U.S. person" definition are stronger arguments that the bill can't be implemented. Points that only help us, such as the §3(b) and §3(c) exemptions or the §7(b) sunset, may be fixed by sponsors once they're raised publicly. Decide which to press openly and which to raise privately.
- **Expect amendments.** Pointing out gaps often prompts tightening. The sunset conflict (§7) and the grandfathering and safety-evaluation exemptions (§3(b)–(c)) are the points most likely to be closed quickly.
- **Verify against the official text.** I've worked from the text you provided, so counsel should confirm the section numbering and wording against the official version before anything is cited in testimony or a letter.
