# S. 2817: Loopholes, Ambiguities, and Drafting Problems

This analysis works only from the text you pasted. I can't verify it against the official manager's amendment, so please confirm against the enrolled committee print before relying on it.

## Section 2: Definitions

1. **§ 2(1)(A), per-vessel threshold.** The 10,000-ton trigger applies "by a single vessel," so an operation split across several vessels, each under 10,000 tons a year, falls outside subparagraph (A) on its face. The bill has no aggregation or anti-evasion clause covering commonly controlled vessels or operators.

2. **§ 2(1)(A), "by a single vessel."** Recovery done by something other than a vessel, such as a fixed or moored platform, a seabed crawler, or a riser system attached to a non-vessel structure, may not meet the definition at all.

3. **§ 2(1)(A), "metric tons" undefined.** The bill doesn't say whether tonnage is measured wet (as lifted, including entrained water and sediment) or dry, or before or after shipboard separation. Nodules carry significant water weight, so this choice could move an operation above or below the threshold.

4. **§ 2(1)(A), "per year" undefined.** It's unclear whether this means a calendar year, a fiscal year, or any rolling 12-month period. That affects both when a violation occurs and whether a campaign spanning two calendar years stays under the cap.

5. **§ 2(1)(A), "recovery" undefined.** The bill doesn't say whether recovery means lifting to the surface, collection on the seabed, or retention aboard. Material collected and returned or discharged at sea may not count.

6. **§ 2(1)(A), "from the seabed."** This may not reach material extracted from the subsoil or from crusts and sulfide deposits requiring cutting. The depth measurement is also unspecified, since it could be water depth at the site or depth of the recovery point.

7. **§ 2(1)(A), "other mineral resources" is overbroad.** Undefined, this phrase arguably covers oil and gas (as "minerals" does under OCSLA, 43 U.S.C. § 1331(q)). That would sweep in deepwater production vessels such as FPSOs in more than 200 meters of water. This is a strong unworkability argument and could draw opposition from the oil and gas industry.

8. **§ 2(1)(B), "the Secretary" undefined.** The bill never says which Secretary (Commerce, Interior, or another), and no section defines the term. It's unclear who holds the authority to expand the prohibition.

9. **§ 2(1)(B), vague delegation backed by criminal penalties.** Under this clause, conduct becomes prohibited, and potentially a crime under § 5(b), based on the Secretary's finding of "comparable" risk. There's no standard, procedure, rulemaking requirement, or notice. This invites void-for-vagueness, rule-of-lenity, and due process challenges, and possibly a nondelegation argument.

10. **§ 2(1)(B), no procedure or timing.** The bill doesn't say whether a determination must go through notice-and-comment rulemaking, whether it applies prospectively only, or whether it can target a single operator.

11. **§ 2(2), "U.S. person" is narrow.** The definition covers only citizens and corporations organized under State law. That leaves out lawful permanent residents, LLCs, partnerships, and trusts (arguably not "corporations"), and entities organized under the laws of D.C., the territories, or federal charter.

12. **§ 2(2), foreign subsidiaries.** A foreign-incorporated subsidiary of a U.S. company is not a "U.S. person." If it operates outside the United States, § 3(a) does not reach it. The bill has no "owned or controlled by" language.

13. **§ 2(2), "State" undefined.** It's unclear whether this includes territories such as American Samoa, Guam, the CNMI, or Puerto Rico, which matters given Pacific EEZ interests.

14. **§ 2(3), "covered vessel" disconnected from the prohibition.** The registry uses a different trigger (more than 5,000 gross tons and "equipped for recovery") than the prohibition (10,000 tons recovered per vessel). Vessels under 5,000 GT never have to register, even if they mine.

15. **§ 2(3), "equipped for the recovery" ambiguous.** The bill doesn't say whether temporarily fitted, convertible, or support vessels qualify, or whether removing equipment between campaigns takes a vessel out of coverage.

## Section 3: Prohibition

16. **§ 3(a), "in the United States" undefined geographically.** It's unclear whether this reaches the EEZ and continental shelf. A foreign person on a foreign-flagged vessel in the U.S. EEZ may not be "in the United States."

17. **§ 3(a), "engage in" is limited to direct conduct.** The bill does not expressly prohibit financing, sponsoring, chartering, insuring, purchasing, processing, or importing nodules mined by others. U.S. persons could likely invest in or buy from foreign operations. There is no import ban.

18. **§ 3(b), "mining conducted under an exploration license" is internally contradictory.** Exploration licenses (e.g., under DSHMRA, 30 U.S.C. § 1401 et seq.) authorize exploration, not commercial recovery. The clause either exempts nothing or, read broadly, grandfathers full commercial mining by any license holder. The ambiguity cuts both ways.

19. **§ 3(b), license source unspecified.** The clause doesn't limit itself to licenses issued by the United States. It could arguably cover ISA exploration contracts or licenses issued by other sponsoring states.

20. **§ 3(b), no limits on the grandfather.** It has no expiration and no area or tonnage cap. It also doesn't address renewals, extensions, modifications, or transfers, so it's unclear whether a license renewed or transferred after enactment still counts as "issued before" enactment.

21. **§ 3(b), commercial recovery permits omitted.** DSHMRA commercial recovery permits issued before enactment are not mentioned, while exploration licenses are. This is an odd asymmetry, and it supports a takings or reliance-interest argument for permit holders.

22. **§ 3(b) and § 8, license window.** The grandfather cutoff is enactment, but the prohibition takes effect 180 days later. Licenses issued during that window are not grandfathered, yet activity under them is lawful until the effective date. The structure invites a rush of license applications before enactment.

23. **§ 3(c), environmental assessment exemption is open-ended.** "Environmental assessment" is undefined. The exemption has no tonnage cap, no approval or oversight requirement, no limit on who qualifies, and no rule for dual-purpose activity. As written, any operation with an assessment purpose is arguably exempt.

24. **§ 3, no exemption for scientific research.** Bona fide academic sampling above the threshold, or anything the Secretary deems "comparable" under § 2(1)(B), is prohibited. That is an overbreadth point that may resonate with the research community.

## Section 4: Registry

25. **§ 4, no penalty for failing to register or report.** Section 5 penalizes only violations of § 3, so the registration and reporting duties are unenforceable.

26. **§ 4(b), reporting tied to "registered" operators.** Only operators who have registered must report. An operator who never registers (see item 25) never triggers the reporting duty.

27. **§ 4, no deadline, content, or format requirements.** The bill sets no deadline to register and doesn't define what reports must contain (location, tonnage, methodology). NOAA is given no authority to verify, inspect, or issue regulations.

28. **§ 4(b), self-certification only.** Reports are certified by the operator, with no independent verification, observers, or vessel-monitoring requirement. 18 U.S.C. § 1001 may still apply to false statements.

29. **§ 4, scope as to foreign-flag vessels.** It's unclear whether foreign-flagged covered vessels with no U.S. nexus must register, or how that would be enforced.

## Section 5: Penalties

30. **§ 5(a) and § 8, effective-date conflict.** Section 5(a) applies penalties to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but § 8 says the Act takes effect 180 days later. Conduct in that window is penalized under a provision that isn't yet in effect, and courts would have to resolve which controls.

31. **§ 5(a), per-day penalty against an annual threshold.** The violation is defined by annual tonnage, so it's unclear which "day" a violation occurs on: the day the 10,000th ton is recovered, every day of the year, or every day after crossing the threshold. Penalty exposure could vary enormously depending on the reading.

32. **§ 5(a), no enforcing agency or procedure.** The bill doesn't say who assesses civil penalties, by what process (administrative hearing or civil action), or with what appeal rights. There are no enforcement powers such as boarding, inspection, subpoena, or forfeiture.

33. **§ 5(b), criminal liability tied to secretarial determinations.** "Willfully" violating a prohibition whose scope the Secretary can expand under § 2(1)(B) raises fair-notice problems (see item 9).

34. **§ 5, no penalty for § 4 violations** (see item 25).

## Section 6: International Agreement

35. **§ 6, "shall seek to negotiate."** This imposes only a duty to try, with no requirement to conclude an agreement and no consequence for failure.

36. **§ 6, ambiguous deadline.** The 18-month deadline could apply to beginning negotiations or to concluding an agreement.

37. **§ 6, no forum specified, and the UNCLOS problem.** The bill doesn't name a forum or address the ISA or UNCLOS, to which the U.S. is not a party. It also doesn't say what "equivalent" prohibitions would mean for other states. This supports the policy argument that a unilateral U.S. ban cedes the field to other nations without any guaranteed reciprocity.

## Section 7: Duration

38. **§ 7(a) vs. § 7(b), direct conflict.** Subsection (a) keeps the prohibition in effect until Congress acts by joint resolution, while (b) ends the entire Act after 10 years regardless. The bill doesn't say which controls.

39. **§ 7(a), no NAS study is actually commissioned.** The bill conditions relief on a National Academies report but never directs, funds, or schedules one. Absent separate action, the off-ramp may never become available.

40. **§ 7(a), the joint resolution is largely surplus.** A joint resolution requires presentment to the President, so it is effectively legislation that Congress could pass anyway. The clause also doesn't say whether the "finding" automatically lifts the prohibition.

41. **§ 7(a), "serious harm" undefined.** The bill sets no standard for what the NAS or Congress must find.

42. **§ 7(a) vs. § 7(b), mismatched scope.** Subsection (a) ends only "the prohibition in section 3," while (b) ends the whole Act. After a joint resolution, the registry and other provisions would technically continue.

## Section 8 and Cross-Cutting Issues

43. **§ 8 and § 7(b), effective life of the ban.** The sunset runs from enactment but the prohibition starts 180 days later, so the effective ban lasts about 9.5 years. The various deadlines run from different dates, which complicates compliance.

44. **Throughout, no relationship to DSHMRA.** The bill doesn't amend or repeal the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act. NOAA's licensing and permitting duties under that statute arguably remain intact, and implied repeal is disfavored. The two statutes would have to be reconciled by courts.

45. **Throughout, no relationship to OCSLA.** The bill doesn't address OCSLA or BOEM's authority over hard minerals on the U.S. continental shelf, leaving the jurisdictional overlap unresolved.

46. **Throughout, no rulemaking authority.** No agency is granted authority to issue implementing regulations, define terms, or resolve the ambiguities above.

47. **Throughout, no severability clause.** If a provision such as § 2(1)(B) is struck down, the bill doesn't say whether the rest survives.

48. **Throughout, no takings or compensation provision.** The bill doesn't address investment-backed expectations of license and permit holders not covered by § 3(b).

## Strategic Notes

- **Strongest "unworkable" arguments:** item 7 (oil and gas overbreadth), items 9 and 33 (vague delegation backed by criminal penalties), items 30 and 38 (internal conflicts), items 25–26 (unenforceable registry), and items 39–40 (illusory off-ramp).
- **Most significant operating room if passed as written:** items 1, 12, 17, 18–20, and 23. However, courts often read exemptions narrowly and look past structures that exist mainly to avoid a statute, particularly arrangements like splitting vessels (item 1) or relabeling commercial work as assessment (item 23). Counsel should weigh that litigation risk before treating any of these as safe harbors.
- **Timing:** Raising these drafting problems at markup may simply prompt fixes in a further amendment, especially items 1, 12, 18, 23, and 30. You may want to decide which points to raise publicly as "unworkability" objections and which to hold.
