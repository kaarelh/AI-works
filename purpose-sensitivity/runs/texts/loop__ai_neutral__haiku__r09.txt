# Weak Spots in S. 2817 (Text as Amended October 6, 2026)

## Definitions (§2)

1. **§2(1)(A)**: "Single training run" and the FLOP-counting method are undefined. The bill doesn't say whether pretraining, post-training, fine-tuning, distillation, or synthetic data generation count toward the 10^26 figure, so a developer could split work across runs or count only one stage to stay under the line.
2. **§2(1)(A)**: The threshold cannot be updated. Algorithmic efficiency gains could allow dangerous capabilities below 10^26 FLOP, and the bill gives no rulemaking authority to adjust the number.
3. **§2(1)(B)**: "The Secretary" is undefined. §4 refers to Commerce and §6 refers to the Secretary of State, so it is unclear who makes the §2(1)(B) determination.
4. **§2(1)(B)**: "Substantially exceeding," "most cognitive tasks," and "human performance" have no benchmarks, procedure, or notice requirements. Compliance is hard to predict, and determinations would be vulnerable to arbitrary-and-capricious challenges.
5. **§2(1)(B)**: The bill does not say when a determination takes effect. A developer could train or deploy a system before it is designated as covered.
6. **§2(2)**: "U.S. person" covers only citizens and state-law corporations. It excludes LLCs, partnerships, federally chartered entities, and lawful permanent residents abroad, which creates gaps in extraterritorial coverage.
7. **§2(2)**: Foreign subsidiaries of U.S. companies are not U.S. persons, so the "anywhere" reach in §3(a) stops at the corporate boundary. "State" is also undefined, leaving open whether D.C. and the territories count.
8. **§2(3)**: "Data center" and "advanced AI chips" are undefined. Operators could avoid the 50,000-chip threshold by spreading chips across sites or leasing capacity from cloud providers.
9. **§2(3)**: The 50,000-chip facility threshold is not tied to the 10^26 FLOP threshold. A model trained on fewer chips over a longer period can be a covered system under §2(1)(A) while its facility never triggers registration under §4.

## Prohibition (§3)

10. **§3(a)**: "Develop" and "deploy" are undefined. It is unclear whether releasing open weights, offering API access, or running internal-only systems counts as deployment.
11. **§3(a)**: The prohibition does not reach enablers. It doesn't address providing compute, chips, cloud services, or technical assistance for a prohibited training run, and it doesn't clearly bar possessing or distributing covered weights.
12. **§3(a)**: Foreign actors abroad are outside the prohibition unless they fall within "U.S. person." Foreign labs using U.S.-origin chips or cloud services are not addressed, and §6 is only aspirational.
13. **§3(b)**: The grandfather clause turns on when training "began," which is undefined. Developers could continue or extend a pre-enactment run, produce new checkpoints, or deploy the result indefinitely.
14. **§3(b) and §8**: The grandfather date is the enactment date, but the prohibition takes effect 180 days later. Training started in that window is neither grandfathered nor clearly covered, which invites a rush to start runs before the effective date and leaves the status of in-progress runs unclear.
15. **§3(c)**: "Safety evaluation" is undefined and turns on the developer's stated purpose. The bill sets no limits on scope, duration, independence, documentation, disclosure, or deployment to customers, so this exemption could swallow the prohibition.
16. **§3 (general)**: The bill has no mens rea standard for civil violations, so it is unclear whether civil liability is strict. Its scope for academic and non-commercial research is also unaddressed.

## Registry (§4)

17. **§4(a)**: "Operator" is undefined. It is unclear whether the owner, lessee, or cloud provider must register, and how workloads of tenants are attributed.
18. **§4(a)**: The bill sets no registration deadline and no required content for registration filings, and it does not say whether the registry is public.
19. **§4(b)**: "Describing the computing workloads" has no required content. Reports need not identify models, training runs, FLOP counts, or purpose, so an accurate report could still fail to show whether §3 was violated.
20. **§4(b)**: The reports are self-certified, and the bill gives no audit, inspection, or verification authority. It also creates no penalty for a false certification.
21. **§4 and §5**: The penalties in §5 attach only to violations of §3. Failing to register or file reports carries no penalty, which is a major enforcement gap.
22. **§4**: The bill has no confidentiality or trade-secret protections for filed reports, which could deter accurate reporting or make companies reluctant to comply.

## Penalties (§5)

23. **§5(a)**: "Each day on which the violation occurs" is undefined for continuing training runs, so it is unclear whether a run is one violation or one violation per day. The $1 million daily cap is small relative to frontier training costs and could be treated as a cost of doing business.
24. **§5 (general)**: The bill names no enforcing agency and sets no enforcement procedure. It grants no injunctive, cease-and-desist, or training-halt authority, so the most direct harm (an ongoing training run) has no direct remedy.
25. **§5(a) and §8**: "On or after the date of enactment" conflicts with the 180-day effective date in §8, which is a drafting inconsistency.
26. **§5(b)**: "Willfully" is undefined. The bill also has no provisions on attempt, conspiracy, aiding and abetting, or individual officer liability.
27. **§5 (general)**: The bill has no whistleblower protections and no safe harbor for good-faith disclosure, which limits the information available to enforcers.

## International Agreement (§6)

28. **§6**: "Shall seek to negotiate" creates no obligation to reach an agreement, and the bill sets no reporting requirement or consequence if none is reached. "Equivalent" is undefined, and nothing in the Act changes its reach over foreign actors.

## Duration and Sunset (§7)

29. **§7(a)**: "Developed safely" is undefined. The bill sets no deadline for the NASEM report, requires no congressional action on it, and says nothing about how the joint resolution moves through presentment and veto.
30. **§7(a) vs. §7(b)**: The two subsections conflict. §7(a) keeps the prohibition in place until a joint resolution is passed, while §7(b) ends the entire Act after 10 years. It is unclear whether the prohibition is indefinite or lasts 10 years.
31. **§7(b)**: The sunset has no savings clause. Accrued penalties, pending enforcement actions, and investigations could be extinguished, and the §4 registry would end with the Act.
32. **§7(b) and §8**: The 10-year clock runs from enactment, not the effective date, so the operative period is about 9.5 years.

## General Drafting and Legal Exposure

33. **Effective date (§8)**: Sections 3(b), 5(a), and 7(b) run from enactment while the prohibition runs from the effective date, creating the mismatches noted above.
34. **Missing provisions (general)**: The bill has no severability clause, no judicial review provision, no rulemaking authority to define terms or implement reporting, and no appropriation or authorization for enforcement. It also does not define "artificial intelligence model," "artificial intelligence system," "training," or "computing power."
35. **Constitutional exposure (general, for legal review)**: The vague standards in §2(1)(B) and §5(b) could draw void-for-vagueness challenges, and the discretionary determination could draw nondelegation challenges. Depending on how "deploy" is read, the bill could also raise First Amendment questions about restrictions on research or code publication. These are possible arguments for reviewers to evaluate, not conclusions.
