# S. 2817 Drafting Analysis: Loopholes, Ambiguities, and Workability Problems

This analysis covers the text as you provided it (manager's amendment of Oct. 6, 2026). I haven't verified it against the official version, so please confirm against the Congress.gov or Committee text before relying on any item.

## Section 2: Definitions

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A), per-vessel threshold.** The 10,000-ton limit applies "by a single vessel," so an operator could spread recovery across several vessels, each under 10,000 tons, and arguably stay outside the definition. Nothing aggregates by operator, project, or license area.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "recovery … by a single vessel."** Recovery involves a seafloor collector, a riser, and a surface ship. The text doesn't say which counts as the "vessel" doing the recovery. Recovery by a fixed platform or installation, or by a non-vessel system, may fall outside subparagraph (A) entirely.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "recovery" undefined.** It's unclear whether recovery means collecting on the seafloor, lifting to the surface, or landing at port. That matters for both the tonnage count and where and when a violation occurs.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A), tonnage measurement.** The bill doesn't say whether "metric tons" means wet or dry weight. Nodules carry substantial moisture, so the choice could swing the threshold significantly. It also doesn't say whether tonnage is measured before or after on-board separation of sediment.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "per year" undefined.** Calendar year, fiscal year, and rolling 12-month period are all possible readings. An operator could time recovery across a year boundary, and the ambiguity complicates enforcement.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(A), depth measurement.** "Depths greater than 200 meters" doesn't say whether depth is measured at the water surface or at the point of collection. It also doesn't address operations that cross the 200-meter line. Activity at or above 200 meters is wholly excluded.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "seabed" and "other mineral resources."** "Seabed" may not include subsoil, so subseafloor extraction might be excluded. Conversely, "other mineral resources" is undefined and could sweep in sand, gravel, or arguably hydrocarbons. That overbreadth could support an unworkability or OCSLA-conflict argument.

8. **Sec. 2(1)(B), "the Secretary" undefined.** The Act never says which Secretary (Commerce, Interior, or another). Sec. 4 separately designates NOAA. This leaves no clear agency with authority to make (B) determinations.

9. **Sec. 2(1)(B), open-ended delegation and vagueness.** "Comparable" risks has no standard, no procedure (rulemaking or adjudication), and no notice requirement. Yet a determination under (B) can trigger criminal liability under Sec. 5(b). This supports fair-notice (void-for-vagueness) and nondelegation arguments.

10. **Sec. 2(2), "U.S. person" limited to citizens and State corporations.** The definition omits LLCs, partnerships, and trusts. It also omits permanent residents and federally chartered entities. Because "State" is undefined, DC and territorial entities may also be excluded.

11. **Sec. 2(2), foreign subsidiaries.** A foreign-incorporated subsidiary of a U.S. corporation is not a "U.S. person." Its activities outside the United States therefore appear uncovered, absent an aiding-and-abetting or control theory.

12. **Sec. 2(3), "covered vessel" disconnected from the prohibition.** The term is used only in Sec. 4. Vessels under 5,000 gross tons escape registration even though they can still engage in mining under Sec. 2(1)(A). This creates a monitoring gap. "Equipped for" is also unclear as applied to removable or modular equipment.

## Section 3: Prohibition

13. **Sec. 3(a), "person" undefined.** The bill doesn't say whether "person" includes government entities, foreign states, or individual crew and employees. It is also unclear whether every person aboard a vessel is separately liable.

14. **Sec. 3(a), "in the United States" undefined.** The bill doesn't say whether this covers the territorial sea, EEZ, or continental shelf. Without clarification, foreign nationals operating in the U.S. EEZ may not be "in the United States."

15. **Sec. 3(a), "engage in" undefined.** It's unclear whether financing, chartering, sponsoring, insuring, or contracting for mining counts as "engaging." The bill also has no prohibition on importing, purchasing, or processing nodules mined by others.

16. **Sec. 3(a), no geographic limit on U.S.-person conduct.** The prohibition reaches U.S. persons "anywhere," including in foreign EEZs and areas under ISA contracts. That raises comity and extraterritoriality questions and possible conflicts with foreign sponsoring-state obligations.

17. **Sec. 3(b), "mining … under an exploration license."** Exploration licenses don't authorize commercial recovery. Read literally, though, the exception exempts any *mining* by a pre-enactment exploration licensee, potentially including commercial-scale recovery. This is likely the bill's most significant drafting gap.

18. **Sec. 3(b), whose license.** "Exploration license" isn't limited to NOAA licenses under the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (DSHMRA). It could arguably include foreign-state licenses or ISA exploration contracts, though ISA instruments are "contracts," not "licenses."

19. **Sec. 3(b), renewals, transfers, and amendments.** The bill doesn't address renewal, extension, transfer, or expansion of a grandfathered license. It is unclear whether the exemption survives a change of ownership or a license modification. As drafted, the exemption has no time limit.

20. **Sec. 3(b), commercial recovery permits not addressed.** DSHMRA provides for both exploration licenses and commercial recovery permits. The exception covers only the former. The bill also doesn't say whether NOAA may still process pending applications or issue new DSHMRA licenses or permits.

21. **Sec. 3(c), environmental assessment exception.** The bill has no definition, scale cap, permit requirement, or verification for this exception. It is unclear whether activity with a dual commercial and assessment purpose qualifies, or who decides.

## Section 4: Registry

22. **Sec. 4, no penalty for noncompliance.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3, so the Act itself provides no sanction for failing to register or report. False certifications could still reach 18 U.S.C. § 1001.

23. **Sec. 4, no content, deadline, or format.** The bill doesn't say what reports must contain, when registration is due, or what "recovery activities" must be described. It also gives NOAA no regulatory authority to fill these gaps.

24. **Sec. 4, "operator" undefined, and global scope.** The bill doesn't say whether the operator is the owner, charterer, or manager. It also isn't clear whether every covered vessel worldwide, regardless of flag or nexus, must register.

25. **Sec. 4 vs. Sec. 2(1)(B), agency mismatch.** NOAA administers the registry, but an undefined "Secretary" makes coverage determinations. The bill has no coordination provision.

## Section 5: Penalties

26. **Sec. 5(a) vs. Sec. 8, effective-date conflict.** Penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the Act takes effect 180 days after enactment. It is ambiguous whether conduct during that 180-day window is penalized. If it is, the bill raises due-process and fair-notice problems.

27. **Sec. 5(a), "each day" vs. annual threshold.** Liability is daily, but the violation turns on an annual tonnage figure. The bill doesn't say when a violation begins: on the day the 10,000th ton is recovered, or retroactively to the start of the year.

28. **Sec. 5, counting pre-effective-date tonnage.** If recovery before the effective date counts toward the annual threshold, applying criminal penalties raises ex post facto concerns.

29. **Sec. 5, no enforcement machinery.** The bill designates no enforcing agency and no civil penalty assessment procedure (administrative or judicial). It also provides no factors for setting penalty amounts, no inspection, boarding, or seizure authority, no forfeiture, and no jurisdiction or venue provision.

30. **Sec. 5(b), criminal liability tied to vague definitions.** Willful violation of a prohibition partly defined by a Secretary's discretionary determination under Sec. 2(1)(B) heightens the vagueness concerns in item 9.

## Section 6: International Agreement

31. **Sec. 6, "seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months."** It's unclear whether the deadline applies to starting or concluding negotiations. "Seek" imposes no obligation of result, and failure has no consequence.

32. **Sec. 6, "equivalent" prohibitions.** The Sec. 3 prohibition rests on U.S.-specific concepts ("U.S. person," "in the United States"), so an "equivalent" international standard is undefined. The provision doesn't address the U.S.'s non-party status to UNCLOS or the ISA regime.

## Section 7: Duration

33. **Sec. 7(a), no NAS report mandated.** The off-ramp depends on a National Academies report, but no agency is directed or funded to commission one, and there is no timeline.

34. **Sec. 7(a), joint resolution adds nothing.** A joint resolution requires presentment and is functionally ordinary legislation, so this mechanism grants no authority Congress lacks. "Serious harm" is undefined, and the bill doesn't say whether the resolution automatically ends the prohibition.

35. **Sec. 7(a) vs. 7(b), internal conflict.** Subsection (a) implies the prohibition lasts until Congress acts, while (b) ends the entire Act after 10 years. The relationship between them is unclear. Because the effective date is 180 days after enactment, the operative period is about 9.5 years.

36. **Sec. 7(b), no savings clause.** The bill doesn't say whether pending penalty actions survive the sunset. Whether the general savings statute (1 U.S.C. § 109) applies to the expiration of a temporary statute, as opposed to a repeal, is contestable.

## Cross-Cutting Issues

37. **No relationship to existing law.** The bill doesn't amend, repeal, or reconcile with DSHMRA (30 U.S.C. § 1401 et seq.) or OCSLA. Implied repeal is disfavored, so the result is unresolved conflicts over licensing authority.

38. **No rulemaking authority.** No agency may issue implementing regulations, so the many undefined terms (Secretary, person, operator, recovery, seabed, mineral resources, environmental assessment) cannot be fixed administratively.

39. **No severability clause.** If a court strikes the Sec. 2(1)(B) delegation or the extraterritorial reach, it is unclear whether the remainder survives.

40. **Possible takings argument.** Investment-backed expectations under existing DSHMRA licenses could support a Fifth Amendment argument. Courts often treat government licenses as not being compensable property, so this is better framed as a workability or fairness point than a strong legal claim.

## Caution for the Legal Team

Items useful for markup testimony (unworkability, vagueness, missing enforcement machinery, the DSHMRA conflict) are different from items one might plan to *operate through* if the bill passes (items 1, 11, 17, 21). On the latter:

- Courts read statutes in light of their purpose and may reject structuring designed to evade a prohibition.
- An agency would likely fill these gaps through interpretation.
- Deliberately structuring around a criminal statute can itself support a finding of willfulness under Sec. 5(b).
- Raising these gaps at markup will likely prompt Committee staff to close them in a further amendment.

Counsel should weigh which points to surface publicly and which to hold for post-enactment analysis.
