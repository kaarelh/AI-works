# S. 2817 (as amended Oct. 6, 2026): Loopholes, Ambiguities, and Drafting Problems

## Sec. 2: Definitions

1. **Sec. 2 (generally): "artificial intelligence" undefined.** The Act never defines "artificial intelligence," "artificial intelligence model," or "artificial intelligence system," and doesn't cross-reference an existing definition such as 15 U.S.C. 9401(3). The scope of the core prohibition therefore rests on undefined terms.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A) vs. (B): "model" vs. "system."** Paragraph (A) covers a "model," while (B) covers a "system." It's unclear whether a system built on a sub-threshold model, or a model that is only a component of a covered system, falls within either prong.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A): "single training run" undefined.** Compute can be split across pretraining, continued pretraining, fine-tuning, RL, and model merging, each arguably a separate "run" under 10^26. The text doesn't say whether these phases aggregate or whether compute used to make inputs (e.g., synthetic data, distillation from a larger teacher) counts.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A): No counting methodology.** "Integer or floating-point operations" has no specified counting convention (e.g., whether a multiply-accumulate is one or two operations, or how sparsity, mixed precision, and failed or restarted runs are treated). Without implementing authority to set one, two-fold differences in counting could put a model on either side of the line.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(A): Static threshold.** The 10^26 figure can't be adjusted. Algorithmic efficiency gains would let more capable models train below it, and (A) has no capability backstop except the discretionary (B) prong.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B): "the Secretary" undefined.** The Act never says which Secretary (Commerce is implied by Sec. 4, but Sec. 6 names the Secretary of State). That is a threshold defect in who may make (B) determinations.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(B): Vague capability standard.** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" doesn't specify which human baseline (median, expert, best), what counts as a "cognitive task," how "most" is measured, or what "substantially" means. Combined with criminal penalties in Sec. 5(b), this invites a void-for-vagueness challenge under the Due Process Clause.

8. **Sec. 2(1)(B): No determination process.** There's no procedure, notice, evidentiary standard, publication requirement, opportunity to contest, or judicial review provision for a Secretary's determination. It's also unclear whether a determination is prospective or reaches conduct before it was made.

9. **Sec. 2(2): Narrow "U.S. person."** The definition covers only citizens and "corporation[s] organized under the laws of a State." It omits lawful permanent residents, LLCs, partnerships, and trusts, and arguably omits entities organized in D.C. or the territories. Foreign-incorporated subsidiaries of U.S. companies are also not U.S. persons, so their conduct abroad is outside Sec. 3(a).

10. **Sec. 2(3): "Advanced AI chips" and "data center" undefined.** There's no chip specification (no performance or ECCN reference), no definition of "data center," and no aggregation rule. Operators could split capacity across nominally separate buildings, campuses, or legal entities, each below 50,000 chips.

## Sec. 3: Prohibition

11. **Sec. 3(a): "Train, develop, or deploy" undefined.** "Develop" could reach anything from preliminary research to final training, and "deploy" could cover internal use, API access, or open-weight release. The scope of the core prohibition is therefore unclear.

12. **Sec. 3(a): "Any person in the United States" unclear for remote activity.** The text doesn't say whether a foreign entity is "in the United States" because it has U.S. servers, staff, or customers. It also doesn't say whether a U.S.-located user accessing a model trained and hosted abroad by a non-U.S. person is "deploying" it.

13. **Sec. 3(a): Gap between prohibition and registry.** The prohibition turns on compute or capability, not on where training occurs, while registration applies only to "covered facilities." Training across many sub-50,000-chip sites, or abroad, triggers no registration or reporting even if it violates Sec. 3.

14. **Sec. 3(b): Open-ended grandfather clause.** Any system "whose training began before" enactment is exempt from all of Sec. 3(a), including further development and deployment, with no limit on how long training continues or how much it scales. A run started before enactment could arguably continue indefinitely.

15. **Sec. 3(b): Derivative models.** The text doesn't say whether fine-tuning, continued training, distillation, or new versions of a grandfathered model are "the same" covered system whose "training began" before enactment, or new systems.

16. **Sec. 3(b) and 2(1)(B): Grandfathering of later-designated systems.** A pre-enactment model the Secretary later designates under (B) would appear to be exempt, since its training began before enactment. That substantially limits the (B) prong.

17. **Sec. 3(c): Undefined, unlimited "safety evaluation" exception.** "Safety evaluation" is undefined. The exception covers training, development, and deployment, has no "sole" or "primary" purpose test, and has no registration, oversight, or scale limit. On its face, any activity with a safety-evaluation purpose is exempt.

18. **Sec. 3(c): No eligible-actor or approval requirement.** Any person may invoke the exception without government authorization, notice, or third-party involvement. The exception also doesn't say whether a system produced under it can later be deployed for other purposes.

## Sec. 4: Registry

19. **Sec. 4: No penalty for non-compliance.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3, so failing to register or report carries no penalty under the Act. False certifications might still reach 18 U.S.C. 1001, but the Act itself doesn't address them.

20. **Sec. 4(a): No deadline or "operator" definition.** There's no registration deadline, and "operator" is undefined (owner, lessee, cloud provider, or tenant running the workload).

21. **Sec. 4(b): Undefined report content.** "Describing the computing workloads" sets no required level of detail and doesn't require reporting compute per run, customer identity, or model characteristics. A general description would appear to comply.

22. **Sec. 4: No confidentiality or inspection provisions.** There are no trade-secret or FOIA protections for submitted reports, and the Department has no audit, inspection, or verification authority. The first point is an argument against the bill; the second makes the registry hard to enforce.

## Sec. 5: Penalties

23. **Sec. 5(a) vs. Sec. 8: Conflicting dates.** Sec. 5(a) imposes penalties for violations "on or after the date of enactment," but Sec. 8 makes the Act effective 180 days after enactment. The 180-day window is ambiguous, and penalizing conduct before the effective date raises fair-notice problems.

24. **Sec. 5: No enforcement agency or procedure.** The Act doesn't say who assesses or sues for civil penalties (Commerce administratively, or DOJ in court), and provides no procedure, hearing rights, or statute of limitations beyond the general default (28 U.S.C. 2462).

25. **Sec. 5(a): Unclear per-day accrual.** It's unclear how "each day on which the violation occurs" applies to training (each day of the run?), development, or deployment, and whether each deployed instance or customer is a separate violation.

26. **Sec. 5(b): Willfulness standard and vagueness.** Willfulness generally requires knowledge that conduct is unlawful, which is hard to prove given the definitional ambiguities above. Conversely, the vagueness of (B) is especially problematic for a criminal provision.

## Sec. 6: International Agreement

27. **Sec. 6: Ambiguous deadline, no consequences.** "Shall seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months" could mean begin seeking or conclude within 18 months. Nothing follows from failure, "equivalent" is undefined, and the domestic prohibition isn't conditioned on other countries adopting comparable restrictions.

## Sec. 7: Duration

28. **Sec. 7(a): No NAS report mandate.** Congress acts "on a report" of the National Academies, but the Act never directs or funds NASEM to produce one, sets no timeline or criteria, and doesn't define "developed safely."

29. **Sec. 7(a): Superfluous off-ramp.** A joint resolution is subject to presentment and is effectively a new law, so this adds nothing Congress couldn't already do. It provides no expedited procedures, and it's unclear whether a "finding" alone lifts the prohibition or needs operative language.

30. **Sec. 7(a) vs. (b): Inconsistent duration.** Subsection (a) implies the prohibition lasts indefinitely until Congress acts, while (b) ends the entire Act after 10 years. Because the sunset runs from enactment rather than the effective date, the prohibition operates for only about 9.5 years.

31. **Sec. 7(b): No savings clause.** The Act doesn't say whether penalties for pre-sunset violations survive expiration. 1 U.S.C. 109 may apply, but its application to self-expiring statutes is less settled than for repeals.

## Structural Omissions

32. **No rulemaking authority.** No agency is authorized to issue regulations clarifying definitions, compute methodology, the safety-evaluation exception, or registry requirements. Many of the ambiguities above can't be cured administratively.

33. **No severability clause.** If a key provision (e.g., Sec. 2(1)(B) on vagueness grounds) is struck, courts would have to decide whether the rest survives without legislative guidance.

34. **No preemption provision.** The Act is silent on whether it preempts state AI laws, leaving the federal–state relationship to implied-preemption litigation.

35. **Possible constitutional challenges.** Beyond vagueness, there are colorable arguments under the First Amendment (restrictions on publishing model weights or code) and under the Fifth Amendment Takings Clause (if compliance strands substantial investments). These are arguments, not certainties.

## Notes for the Legal Team

- **Items useful for advocacy:** Items 6–8, 19, 23–24, 28–30, and 32–33 are the strongest "unworkable as drafted" arguments for markup.
- **Items risky to rely on for compliance:** Items 3, 10, 14–15, and 17 are the main apparent paths to continued operation. Courts often read exemptions narrowly and treat structuring to avoid thresholds as evasion. Given the criminal provision, aggressive readings (especially a pretextual use of the safety-evaluation exception) carry real exposure.
- **Likely to be fixed:** Several of these, such as the undefined "Secretary," the missing Sec. 4 penalty, and the date conflict, are the kind of defects staff typically fix in a further amendment once raised. Raising them publicly may strengthen the bill rather than weaken it.
