RESPONSE:
Here are the issues I found in the October 6 text, in bill order.

1. **§2(1)(A):** The 10^26 threshold can be evaded by splitting a training program into several runs, or by moving capability gains into post-training (fine-tuning, RL, distillation), which may not count as a "single training run." The bill also doesn't address algorithmic efficiency, which can reach the same capability with less compute over time.

2. **§2(1)(A):** The bill doesn't say how compute is measured or certified, whether estimates or actual counts control, or how multi-stage training is counted. Without this, the threshold is hard to apply and easy to dispute.

3. **§2(1)(A):** The Secretary's authority to lower or update the compute threshold is missing. Algorithmic progress could make 10^26 too high within a short time.

4. **§2(1)(B):** "The Secretary" is undefined. The bill doesn't say whether this is the Secretary of Commerce or another official.

5. **§2(1)(B):** The capability determination has no procedure, evidentiary standard, deadline, or review mechanism. "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" is vague enough to invite APA challenges to any determination, in either direction.

6. **§2 (missing definitions):** "Train," "develop," and "deploy" are undefined. The bill doesn't say whether releasing model weights, providing API access, or fine-tuning counts as deployment or development, which matters most for open-weight releases.

7. **§2(1)(A)–(B) (scope of "covered AI system"):** Developers could build a covered system from several individually below-threshold models or from scaffolding around them. The bill should aggregate compute across models in a system and across related runs in the same lineage.

8. **§2(2) ("U.S. person"):** The definition excludes foreign-incorporated subsidiaries of U.S. companies, LLCs and partnerships, and lawful permanent residents. "State" is also undefined, so it's unclear whether D.C. and the territories are included.

9. **§3(a) (facilitation):** The prohibition covers only those who train, develop, or deploy. It doesn't reach cloud providers, chip suppliers, data center operators, or financiers who knowingly enable covered training, so there is no aiding-and-abetting or facilitation liability.

10. **§3(a) (model weights):** The bill doesn't address possession, transfer, or export of covered model weights, so a company could move a trained model abroad or give it to a third party without violating the Act.

11. **§3(b) (grandfathering):** Systems whose training "began" before enactment are exempt from the whole prohibition, including continued development and deployment. "Began" is undefined, and grandfathered systems have no registration or reporting obligation, so they can run indefinitely outside oversight.

12. **§3(c) (safety evaluation):** The exemption has no definition, approval process, containment requirement, or limit on scale. It could be used to justify training a covered system, and it doesn't say whether deployment after an evaluation is permitted.

13. **§3 (determination of coverage):** The bill has no process for developers to determine in advance whether a system is covered, or for regulators to verify that determination. Under (A), coverage depends entirely on self-reported compute.

14. **§4(a) (registry scope):** "Advanced AI chip" is undefined, and "data center" is undefined. The 50,000-chip threshold applies per facility, so distributed training across several sites, or across multiple tenants, could stay under it.

15. **§4(b) (quarterly reports):** The bill doesn't specify what "describing the computing workloads" requires. There's no verification, no audit right, no public disclosure, and no specific penalty for a false certification, beyond whatever general law applies.

16. **§4 and §5 (enforceability of the registry):** Penalties under §5 attach only to violations of §3. Failing to register, failing to file reports, or filing false reports carries no direct penalty, so the registry is effectively voluntary.

17. **§5(a) (civil penalty amount):** $1 million per day is small relative to the revenue and valuations of frontier developers, so it could be treated as a cost of doing business. The bill should consider penalties tied to revenue, per model, or per training run.

18. **§5(a) (counting):** It's unclear whether "each day" means each day of a continuing violation or each separate act, which affects how penalties accumulate and how courts will read the provision.

19. **§5 (enforcement authority):** The bill doesn't name the agency or official that brings civil actions, refers criminal cases, or issues cease-and-desist orders. It also has no injunction authority, no asset-freeze or shutdown power, and no appropriation for enforcement.

20. **§5(a) and §8 (mismatch):** Civil penalties apply to violations "occurring on or after the date of enactment," but the prohibition itself doesn't take effect until 180 days later. The two provisions run on different clocks, which invites dispute about what conduct is covered during the gap.

21. **§5(b) (criminal liability):** "Willfully" is undefined, and the bill doesn't address corporate criminal liability, individual officer liability, attempts, or conspiracy. The default statute of limitations also isn't specified.

22. **§6 ("shall seek to negotiate"):** The provision imposes no obligation to reach an agreement, requires no report to Congress, and sets no fallback if talks fail. "Equivalent" is undefined, and the section isn't tied to chip export controls or weight-transfer restrictions, so developers could relocate to a non-signatory country.

23. **§7(a) (joint resolution):** The bill doesn't say that a joint resolution must be presented to the President, which determines whether a veto override is needed. It also doesn't define "developed safely," set a vote threshold, or set a deadline for the NASEM report, and it doesn't require that the report be produced at all.

24. **§7(a) and §7(b) (conflict):** Section 7(a) keeps the prohibition in effect until Congress acts, but §7(b) ends the entire Act after 10 years whether or not Congress has acted. This is the most serious structural problem in the bill, because the sunset overrides the indefinite duration, and the prohibition could lapse while the risk remains.

25. **§7(b) (savings clause):** There's no savings clause for pending enforcement actions, penalties accrued before sunset, or grandfathered systems after the Act expires.

26. **§8 (effective date):** The 180-day delay creates an incentive to start large training runs before the prohibition takes effect, and §3(b) would then exempt them. Runs started between enactment and the effective date are neither prohibited nor covered by grandfathering. The bill should include an immediate freeze on new covered training runs.

27. **§4, §2(1)(B), and §6 (agency designation):** The bill refers to "the Department of Commerce," "the Secretary," and "the Secretary of State" without a clear designation of the implementing agency or grant of rulemaking authority. Regulations will be hard to issue and easy to challenge without that authority.

28. **Whole bill (missing provisions):** The bill has no severability clause, no judicial review provision, no whistleblower protection for employees who report violations, no incident-reporting requirement, and no savings clause preserving state law. Without a severability clause, a court striking one provision could take down the whole Act.

**Priorities for the Whitfield office:** I'd lead with items 24 (sunset conflict), 11 (grandfathering), 16 (registry enforceability), 19 (enforcement authority), 9 (facilitation), and 26 (interim race window). These have the largest effect on whether the bill works at all.

**Before sending:** Have counsel check the enforcement and criminal provisions against current federal statutes, including the default limitations period, §1001 false-statement liability, and any existing Commerce Department export authorities the bill could reference. I've worked only from the text you provided, so please confirm that the October 6 manager's amendment didn't already address any of these points elsewhere in the bill.
