# S. 2817 Drafting Analysis: Loopholes, Ambiguities, and Defects

These are organized by section, with cross-cutting issues at the end. For each item I note whether it mainly **(L)** narrows coverage or creates a gap, or **(U)** supports an unworkability or constitutional argument. Some items do both.

## Sec. 2: Definitions

1. **§2(1)(A), "single training run" (L).** The compute threshold applies per run, so a model built through several runs, each under 10^26, appears to fall outside it. Examples include staged pretraining, continued pretraining from a checkpoint, separate RL and post-training phases, distillation, and model merging. The bill has no aggregation rule.

2. **§2(1)(A), what counts toward the threshold (L/U).** The bill doesn't say whether compute spent on synthetic data generation, teacher models in distillation, failed or restarted runs, or ablations counts. It also gives no method for counting operations under mixed precision or sparse architectures.

3. **§2(1)(A), "quantity of computing power" (U).** "Power" is a rate (operations per second), but the clause measures a total quantity of operations. A court would likely read past this, but it is imprecise drafting in a provision that carries criminal penalties.

4. **§2(1)(A), static threshold (U).** The fixed 10^26 figure has no adjustment mechanism. Algorithmic efficiency gains will let systems of a given capability be trained with less compute over time, so the threshold becomes both over- and under-inclusive. This undercuts the bill's stated purpose.

5. **§2(1)(B), "the Secretary" is undefined (L/U).** No section of the Act says which Secretary. Commerce is the likely inference from §4, but §6 refers to the Secretary of State. This is a serious defect for a provision that triggers criminal liability.

6. **§2(1)(B), vague capability standard (U).** "Substantially exceeding," "human performance" (median human or expert?), and "most cognitive tasks" have no benchmarks, procedures, notice-and-comment process, or appeal rights. This supports void-for-vagueness and due process challenges, and possibly a nondelegation argument, given the 10-year criminal penalty in §5(b).

7. **§2(1)(B), timing of a determination (U/L).** A capability determination can generally be made only after a system exists. The bill doesn't say whether conduct before the determination becomes unlawful after the fact, or whether liability runs only from the determination forward. Retroactive application would raise ex post facto problems for the criminal provision.

8. **§2(1)(A) vs. (B), "model" vs. "system" (L).** Prong (A) covers a "model" and prong (B) covers a "system." It is unclear whether a system built from several sub-threshold models, or a scaffolded or agentic system around a sub-threshold model, is covered under (A). "Artificial intelligence," "model," and "system" are all undefined.

9. **§2(2), narrow "U.S. person" definition (L).** The definition covers only citizens and corporations organized under State law. It omits lawful permanent residents, LLCs, partnerships, trusts, and other non-corporate entities. Read literally, a U.S. LLC operating abroad is not reached by the "anywhere" clause in §3(a).

10. **§2(2), "State" is undefined (L).** Without a definition including D.C. and the territories, entities organized in D.C., Puerto Rico, or other territories, and federally chartered corporations, may fall outside the definition.

11. **§2(2), foreign subsidiaries (L).** A foreign-incorporated subsidiary of a U.S. company is not a "U.S. person." Its activity abroad is reached only if the U.S. parent itself "develops" the system. That depends on the undefined term "develop" (see item 15) and on attribution doctrine.

12. **§2(3), "data center" and "advanced AI chips" are undefined (L/U).** Neither term is defined, and there is no performance threshold or export-control cross-reference such as ECCNs. The facility definition is unadministrable as written.

13. **§2(3), per-facility threshold (L).** The 50,000-chip trigger applies per "data center." Splitting capacity across sites, or training across several sub-threshold facilities, avoids registration. The bill also doesn't say when chips are counted.

14. **§2, "person" and "United States" are undefined (L).** "United States" has no geographic definition (territories? possessions?). "Person" relies on the 1 U.S.C. §1 default, which is probably sufficient but isn't stated.

## Sec. 3: Prohibition

15. **§3(a), "train, develop, or deploy" are undefined (L/U).** "Develop" could reach anything from a single training run to general research on sub-threshold models. "Deploy" doesn't say whether offering API access to foreign users, or a U.S. user accessing a foreign-hosted system, counts.

16. **§3(a), when a system becomes "covered" (L).** Under prong (A), a model arguably isn't "covered" until it crosses 10^26 operations. The bill doesn't say whether the prohibition reaches a run planned or designed to exceed the threshold before it does, or only the operations beyond it.

17. **§3(a), foreign developers serving U.S. users (L/U).** The prohibition reaches persons "in the United States" and U.S. persons abroad. A foreign developer serving U.S. customers remotely may not be covered. This creates a competitive-disadvantage argument and a loophole involving foreign partners.

18. **§3(b), scope of the grandfather clause (L).** The exemption covers the whole of subsection (a), including "develop" and "deploy," for any system whose training began before enactment. This appears to allow indefinite further development and deployment of grandfathered systems, with no cap on additional compute or capability.

19. **§3(b), "training began" is undefined (L).** The bill doesn't say whether a training run counts as "begun" on the first step, when hardware is provisioned, or at some other point. It also doesn't say whether continued training from a grandfathered checkpoint is the "same" system or a new one, or whether an ongoing run can continue forever.

20. **§3(b) vs. §8, mismatched dates (L/U).** The grandfather clause is keyed to *enactment*, but the Act takes effect 180 days later. Training started during that 180-day window is lawful when started, is not grandfathered, and then becomes unlawful on the effective date. This raises reliance and fair-notice problems.

21. **§3(b), (B)-designated systems (L).** A system designated under §2(1)(B) whose training began before enactment is grandfathered regardless of how capable it is.

22. **§3(c), the safety-evaluation exemption is unbounded (L).** "Activities conducted for the purpose of safety evaluation" is undefined. It requires no approval, notice, oversight, or limit on scale, and it turns on a subjective purpose test. It could arguably cover training as well as testing, which risks swallowing the rule.

23. **§3(c), mixed purposes (L/U).** The bill doesn't address activities with both safety and commercial purposes, such as "primary," "sole," or "a" purpose tests. Enforcement would require proving subjective intent.

## Sec. 4: Registry

24. **§4, no penalty for noncompliance (L/U).** Section 5 penalizes only violations of §3. Failing to register or report under §4 carries no penalty, although a false certification could still create liability under 18 U.S.C. §1001.

25. **§4, no deadlines, rulemaking authority, or definition of "operator" (U).** The bill sets no registration deadline or first-report date, and no format or content standards beyond "describing the computing workloads." Commerce receives no rulemaking authority, and "operator" is undefined: owner, lessee, or cloud customer?

26. **§4(b), no confidentiality protection (U).** Workload reports would contain highly sensitive trade secrets, but the bill provides no FOIA exemption, confidentiality requirement, or data-security obligation. This is a strong unworkability argument.

27. **§4, no link to the prohibition (U).** The registry has no stated function. It isn't tied to any §3 enforcement mechanism, and multi-facility training evades it entirely.

## Sec. 5: Penalties

28. **§5(a) vs. §8, effective-date conflict (U).** Civil penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the Act takes effect 180 days later. Penalizing conduct during that window raises fair-notice problems, and courts would likely resolve the conflict against enforcement.

29. **§5, no enforcement agency or procedure (U).** No agency is authorized to assess or sue for civil penalties, and there are no administrative procedures, investigative powers, or judicial review provisions. DOJ could presumably sue, but the bill is silent.

30. **§5(a), penalty unit ambiguity (U).** The bill doesn't say whether "each day on which the violation occurs" means per system, per day, per facility, or per act (train vs. deploy). It also gives no factors for setting the penalty amount.

31. **§5(b), criminal liability on a vague standard (U).** Ten-year felony exposure tied to the §2(1)(B) capability standard and to undefined terms like "develop" strengthens vagueness and rule-of-lenity arguments. The willfulness requirement helps the government somewhat, but doesn't cure the problem.

## Sec. 6: International Agreement

32. **§6, ambiguous deadline (U).** It is unclear whether the 18-month deadline applies to *beginning* negotiations or *concluding* them. The bill attaches no consequence to failure, and there is no reporting requirement.

33. **§6, no conditionality (U).** The domestic prohibition takes effect regardless of whether any other country agrees to restrictions. This supports a unilateral-disarmament and competitiveness argument. "Equivalent" prohibitions is also undefined.

34. **§6, treaty process (U).** The bill doesn't address whether a resulting agreement would require Senate advice and consent, or how it would interact with §3.

## Sec. 7: Duration

35. **§7(a), illusory release mechanism (U).** Congress can always repeal by legislation, so a "joint resolution" (which requires presentment) adds nothing. The bill doesn't direct or fund a NASEM study, and gives no standard for "can be developed safely."

36. **§7(a) vs. §7(b), relationship of the two endpoints (U).** Subsection (a) says the prohibition stays in effect "until" Congress acts, while (b) sunsets the whole Act at 10 years. The natural reading is "whichever comes first," but the drafting is unclear. Note also that a §7(a) release lifts only §3, leaving §4 in place.

37. **§7(b), effect of the sunset on pending enforcement (L).** The bill has no savings clause for violations occurring before the sunset. Whether 1 U.S.C. §109 preserves liability when a statute expires on its own terms, as opposed to being repealed, is unsettled.

## Sec. 8 and Cross-Cutting Issues

38. **§8, inconsistent date anchors (U).** Sections 3(b), 5(a), 6, and 7(b) are keyed to enactment, while the Act takes effect 180 days later. This creates the conflicts noted in items 20 and 28.

39. **No severability clause (U).** If §2(1)(B) or another provision is struck down, it is unclear whether the remainder survives. The default presumption favors severability, but the omission is worth noting.

40. **No preemption clause (U).** The bill doesn't address state AI laws, which leaves possible inconsistent overlapping regimes.

41. **No general rulemaking or definitional authority (U).** No agency can clarify any of the undefined terms above, so every ambiguity would have to be resolved through litigation.

42. **Open-weight and third-party issues (L/U).** The bill doesn't say whether downloading, hosting, or fine-tuning publicly released weights of a grandfathered or foreign covered model is "deployment" or "development." Nor does it say whether the grandfather exemption passes to downstream users.

43. **Possible First Amendment argument (U).** Restricting the development and distribution of model weights may raise code-as-speech arguments (cf. *Bernstein v. DOJ*). These are untested and probably weak against a training restriction, but potentially relevant to the scope of "deploy" and "develop."

## Strategic Notes for the Legal Team

- **Raising loopholes in markup invites fixes.** Items marked (L) are gaps that, if raised publicly, sponsors can close with a manager's amendment. This is especially true of items 1, 9–11, 13, 18, and 22. The legal team may want to separate gaps worth flagging publicly from those to keep internal.
- **Relying on aggressive readings is risky.** Given the willfulness-based criminal provision, operating under a contested interpretation (e.g., the safety-evaluation exemption, foreign-subsidiary structuring, or run-splitting) could be characterized as deliberate evasion. Courts may also read exemptions narrowly in light of statutory purpose. These readings are better treated as evidence of poor drafting than as an operating plan, pending formal outside-counsel opinions.
- **The strongest "unworkable" arguments** are probably items 5–7 (undefined Secretary and vague capability standard, combined with criminal penalties), 20 and 28 (date conflicts), 26 (no trade-secret protection), and 29 and 41 (no enforcement or rulemaking machinery).
