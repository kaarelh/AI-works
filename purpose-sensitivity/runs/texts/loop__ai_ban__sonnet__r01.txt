RESPONSE:
# Review of S. 2817 (as amended Oct. 6, 2026)

Issues are listed in section order. The ones I'd lead with in the meeting are **#14-16, #17, #4-6, #24, #31, and #7-8**.

## Section 2: Definitions

1. **§2(1)(A), "single training run."** Developers can split a project into several sub-threshold runs, or chain runs together, and stay under 10^26. The Act should aggregate compute across related runs, checkpoints, and continued training of the same model lineage.

2. **§2(1)(A), what counts as compute.** "Model" and "training run" are undefined, and the text doesn't say whether post-training, RL, fine-tuning, distillation from a larger model, synthetic data generation, or model merging count. It also gives no counting method (theoretical vs. measured operations, treatment of failed or restarted runs).

3. **§2(1)(A), fixed threshold.** The number is static, and algorithmic efficiency gains mean a sub-threshold model could eventually match today's most capable ones. There is no mechanism to lower it. Separately, the threshold may already be exceeded by some current commercial frontier runs, so the bill risks being attacked as overbroad relative to its "superintelligence" title. Please verify that against current public compute estimates.

4. **§2(1)(B), "the Secretary."** The Act never defines "Secretary." Commerce is presumably intended, but §6 uses "Secretary of State," so the reference is ambiguous.

5. **§2(1)(B), the capability standard.** "Substantially exceeding," "most cognitive tasks," and the human baseline (average person? domain experts?) are all undefined, and there is no evaluation methodology. With criminal penalties attached, this invites a void-for-vagueness challenge. It also differs from the "vastly outperform across virtually all domains" framing in our own materials, so we should decide which standard we want.

6. **§2(1)(B), process and timing.** There is no rulemaking, notice, deadline, public designation list, or judicial review for the Secretary's determination, which raises fair-notice and nondelegation arguments. A capability determination also seems to come after a system exists, so a developer can't know in advance whether it is building a covered system. The bill should add a prospective standard (for example, reasonably foreseeable capability, including with fine-tuning or scaffolding) and a pre-training determination or notification process.

7. **§2(2), "U.S. person" is too narrow.** It omits permanent residents, partnerships, trusts, universities, nonprofits, and federally chartered entities, and it is unclear whether an LLC counts as a "corporation." "State" is undefined, so it's unclear whether D.C. and the territories are included.

8. **§2(2), no control test.** A foreign subsidiary of a U.S. company, or a foreign-incorporated company with U.S. headquarters or ownership, is not a "U.S. person," so offshoring training to a subsidiary escapes §3(a). The definition should reach entities controlled by or majority-owned by U.S. persons.

9. **§2(3), undefined terms.** "Advanced AI chips" and "data center" have no definition or performance metric. Tying "advanced AI chips" to an export-control classification or an aggregate-compute measure would help.

10. **§2(3), chip-count threshold misses capable sites.** My rough estimate is that 10^26 operations takes about 8,000 H100-class chip-years at realistic utilization. So a 10,000-chip site could complete a covered run in roughly 10 months, and 20,000 chips in about 5 months, with no registration duty. The registry should key on aggregate compute capacity, count chips across sites under common control, and cover leased or cloud capacity. These are order-of-magnitude figures and should be checked by a technical staffer.

## Section 3: Prohibition

11. **§3(a), undefined verbs.** "Train," "develop," and "deploy" are undefined. It is unclear whether internal use, API access, or open-weight release counts as deployment, or what "develop" captures. The section also doesn't reach possessing, hosting, selling, distributing weights, financing, or knowingly assisting.

12. **§3(a), territorial gap.** A foreign company abroad can build a covered system and offer it to U.S. users, since the ban applies only to "persons in the United States" and "U.S. persons" (narrowly defined). "United States" is undefined, and nothing restricts importing, accessing, or hosting foreign covered systems.

13. **§3(a), no anti-evasion clause.** Structuring through affiliates, contractors, or shell entities isn't addressed. Aiding and abetting is covered criminally by 18 U.S.C. §2, but there is no civil analogue.

14. **§3(b), the grandfather clause is open-ended.** It exempts everything in §3(a), including deployment and further development, with no limit on continued scaling, fine-tuning, successor versions, or capability expansion. "Training began" is undefined (first pretraining step? any run in the lineage?) and unverifiable, because there is no registry or certification of pre-existing systems.

15. **§3(b), rush incentive and §2(1)(B).** Keying the exemption to the date of enactment gives developers every reason to start the largest possible runs before then. It also means the Secretary can never act against an existing system, even one that clearly meets §2(1)(B), which hollows out that prong for the systems that matter most today.

16. **§3(b) vs. §8, the gap period.** Training that begins between enactment and the effective date 180 days later isn't grandfathered, but it was lawful when begun. The text doesn't say whether continuing it after the effective date is a violation, and that ambiguity invites reliance and due-process defenses.

17. **§3(c), the safety evaluation exemption.** "Safety evaluation" is undefined, the test is the actor's subjective "purpose," and the exemption covers all of §3(a), including training and deployment. A developer could label nearly any covered-system work as safety evaluation, with no cap on compute, no containment, no independent oversight, and no reporting. It should be limited to evaluating lawfully existing systems, by licensed or approved entities, with no training or deployment, and with mandatory reporting.

18. **§3, mental state and mid-run crossing.** The Act doesn't say what happens when a run is planned to exceed the threshold or crosses it midway, and it doesn't state a mental-state standard for civil liability. A run planned or expected to exceed the threshold should be covered from the start.

## Section 4: Registry

19. **§4, no implementing authority.** There is no rulemaking authority, registration deadline, required content, update duty, audit or inspection power, or appropriation for Commerce. As written, Commerce has little ability to make the registry work.

20. **§4(a), "operator" is undefined.** It is unclear whether this means the owner, the lessee, or the cloud customer actually running the workload. Customers renting capacity have no duty, and there are no know-your-customer requirements for compute providers.

21. **§4(b), reports are vague, slow, and unverified.** "Describing the computing workloads" doesn't require the model, total compute, start and end dates, or customer identity. Quarterly reporting is too slow because a covered run can finish within one quarter, and verification rests on the operator's own certification.

22. **§4, no penalty for registry violations.** §5 penalizes only violations of §3, so failing to register, failing to report, or filing a false report has no stated penalty. (18 U.S.C. §1001 might reach false statements, but not failure to file.)

23. **§4, no confidentiality provisions.** There are no trade-secret or FOIA protections for reported data, which invites objections and challenges. There is also no provision for sharing data with Congress or enforcement agencies.

## Section 5: Penalties

24. **§5, no enforcement authority.** The bill never says who brings civil actions (DOJ? Commerce?), what procedures apply, or whether courts can issue injunctions. It has no citizen-suit or state attorney general role and no emergency shutdown power.

25. **§5(a) vs. §8, retroactivity conflict.** Civil liability runs "on or after the date of enactment," but the prohibition takes effect 180 days later. This is an internal inconsistency and invites fair-notice and retroactivity challenges. §5(b) has no date reference at all.

26. **§5(a), penalty size.** A cap of $1M/day (about $365M/year) may be small relative to frontier training budgets and large-company revenues, and "not more than" sets no floor. Consider scaling penalties to revenue, compute cost, or chip count, plus disgorgement.

27. **§5(b), "willfully."** It is undefined and may be read to require knowing that the specific system was covered. That is a ready defense given the compute-counting questions and the §2(1)(B) determination. Define the standard (knowledge or reckless disregard) and add responsible-corporate-officer liability. "Person" is likely covered by the Dictionary Act (1 U.S.C. §1), but it's worth saying expressly that it includes subsidiaries and affiliates.

28. **§5, remedies are fines and prison only.** There is no authority to order a halt, require deletion of weights, or seize or forfeit chips and model weights. There is also no statute of limitations provision and no whistleblower protection or reward.

## Section 6: International Agreement

29. **§6, soft obligation.** "Seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months" is ambiguous about whether the deadline applies to starting or concluding talks. There are no reporting requirements to Congress and no consequence for missing it. "Equivalent" is undefined, and the text doesn't say whether the result would be a treaty needing Senate advice and consent or an executive agreement.

30. **§6, no interim levers.** Nothing in the bill restricts exporting chips, financing foreign covered projects, or otherwise limiting offshoring while negotiations proceed.

## Section 7: Duration

31. **§7(b), sunset undermines the ban.** The Act expires automatically after 10 years (about 9.5 years of effect after the 180-day delay), regardless of whether anyone has shown that covered systems can be built safely. That contradicts §7(a) and the premise of the bill. Consider deleting the sunset or replacing it with a mandatory review that does not terminate the ban.

32. **§7(a), the lifting mechanism is underspecified.** "Can be developed safely" has no standard, there is no deadline or duty for the National Academies to report, and it's unclear whether Congress must act on a report. Also, a joint resolution is ordinary legislation subject to presidential signature, so it adds little beyond Congress's existing power to amend the Act.

33. **§7(b), no savings clause.** Without an express provision, defendants may argue that expiration ends pending or future enforcement for conduct during the Act's life. The general savings statute (1 U.S.C. §109) addresses repeals, and its application to sunsets is uncertain, so add express language.

## Section 8 and structural gaps

34. **§8, 180-day delay with no interim measures.** Nothing takes effect immediately, not even registration or notification of large runs, so the delay compounds the rush incentive in #15. Consider immediate effect for notification duties and for bans on new large runs.

35. **No severability clause.** Given the vagueness and constitutional risks, one successful challenge could take down the whole Act.

36. **No findings or constitutional basis.** The bill lacks congressional findings supporting Commerce Clause authority and the government's interest. "Develop" could also draw First Amendment challenges (code and research as speech), so the text should focus on conduct such as compute use and be tailored accordingly.

37. **Government and military use is unaddressed.** The Act doesn't say whether federal agencies, the military, intelligence agencies, or their contractors are covered. "Person" doesn't clearly include the United States, which could create either a huge implied carve-out or litigation over contractors. Decide the intended answer and state it.

38. **State law is unaddressed.** There is no clause saying whether states may enact additional or stronger restrictions, which leaves room for preemption arguments.

39. **No judicial review or due-process provisions.** The bill doesn't provide procedures for contesting a §2(1)(B) determination, a registration decision, or a civil penalty. Clear procedures would make the Act more defensible.

40. **"Superintelligence" is never defined.** The title promises a ban on superintelligence, but the operative text uses only the compute threshold and the "most cognitive tasks" test. This mismatch weakens purpose-based interpretation and invites argument over congressional intent.

## Caveats

- Legal conclusions here (vagueness, nondelegation, First Amendment, savings statute) are issue-spotting, not legal opinions, so counsel should review them before they go to Senator Whitfield's office.
- The compute figures in #3 and #10 are rough estimates and should be checked by someone with current hardware and training-compute data.
- Some fixes trade off against each other. A broader U.S.-person definition and anti-evasion language increase reach but also increase overbreadth arguments, so the office may want us to rank priorities.

I can turn this into a cover memo with proposed replacement language for the top items if that would help.
