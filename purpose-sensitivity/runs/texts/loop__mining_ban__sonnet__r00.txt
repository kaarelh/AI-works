RESPONSE:
I read the text as amended on October 6 and list the problems in section order. Items 4, 14, 24, 26, and 35 rely on how the bill interacts with existing law (the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act and related statutes), so have counsel check those against current text before sending.

**Biggest problems:** #29 (the sunset), #11-12 (exploration-license exemption), #15 (environmental assessment exemption), #8 (narrow "U.S. person"), #3 (single-vessel threshold), #19 (no penalties for registry violations), and #34 (the 180-day gap).

## Section 2: Definitions

1. **§2(1)(A), "single vessel" and 10,000 t.** The threshold is measured per vessel, so an operator can run several vessels under the cap, or rotate vessels, and none is engaged in "commercial deep seabed mining." Test or pilot mining below the cap is also legal.
2. **§2(1)(A), "per year."** The bill doesn't say whether the cap is a calendar year or a rolling 12 months, or whether it is wet or dry weight. A short, intense campaign or a reporting choice could keep a vessel under the line.
3. **§2(1)(A), "recovery," "mineral resources," "seabed" undefined.** "Other mineral resources" could sweep in offshore oil and gas production (a single floating production vessel can exceed 10,000 t/yr) and sand or gravel dredging. That creates unintended opponents and easy overbreadth arguments. It could also leave sediment, brine, or sulfide mining ambiguous.
4. **§2(1)(A), 200 m depth floor.** Mining at 200 m or shallower, such as phosphate deposits on the shelf, is outside the definition. The bill also doesn't say how depth is measured or which datum is used.
5. **§2(1)(A), vessel-based only.** The definition covers recovery "by a single vessel." Recovery by a fixed platform, a seabed crawler with a separate riser, or a non-vessel system arguably falls outside it.
6. **§2(1)(B), "Secretary" undefined.** The bill doesn't say which Secretary (Commerce, Interior, State) makes the determination. Nothing requires the Secretary to act, and "comparable" risk is undefined.
7. **§2(1)(B), no process or notice.** There is no rulemaking, deadline, public petition right, or standard of review. Because §5(b) attaches criminal liability to the definition, it invites vagueness and fair-notice challenges, since a company can't know in advance what is covered.
8. **§2(2), "U.S. person" too narrow.** It covers only citizens and State-organized corporations. It omits LLCs, partnerships, trusts, and other non-corporate entities, permanent residents, corporations organized in D.C. or territories ("State" is undefined), and foreign subsidiaries that U.S. firms own or control. A U.S. company could shift operations to a foreign-incorporated affiliate.
9. **§2(3), "covered vessel."** Anything under 5,000 gross tons escapes the registry, as do platforms, support or transport vessels, and collector systems. The phrase "equipped for the recovery" is also vague. Operators can use smaller or non-vessel configurations to avoid registration.

## Section 3: Prohibition

10. **§3(a), "person in the United States" and "United States" undefined.** "Person" is also undefined. The bill doesn't say whether it reaches the territorial sea, the EEZ, the outer continental shelf, or the extended continental shelf. Foreign-flag vessels operated by non-U.S. persons just outside U.S. waters are not covered.
11. **§3(a), "engage in" is too narrow.** It doesn't reach financing, insuring, supplying, processing, transporting, buying, or importing minerals from seabed mining. A U.S. entity could fund or process the output of a foreign operation without violating the Act.
12. **§3(a), no interaction with existing statutes.** The bill doesn't amend or supersede the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act or the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act. It has no "notwithstanding any other provision of law" clause and doesn't direct agencies to stop issuing licenses or permits. Agencies could keep issuing permits and applicants would argue the statutes conflict.
13. **§3(b), "mining conducted under an exploration license."** Exploration licenses generally don't authorize commercial recovery, so the text is ambiguous. A company can argue it grandfathers commercial-scale mining, and the exemption has no volume cap, area limit, or expiration.
14. **§3(b), "exploration license" undefined.** It doesn't say who must have issued the license (NOAA, a foreign government, the International Seabed Authority). It doesn't address renewals, extensions, modifications, transfers, or pending applications. It also creates an incentive to rush licenses through before enactment.
15. **§3(c), environmental assessment exemption.** The test is the "purpose" of the activity, which is subjective and unverifiable. There is no cap on scale, duration, or area, no permit or agency approval, and no independence requirement. Nothing prevents sale of the recovered material, so commercial test mining can be labeled assessment.

## Section 4: Registry

16. **§4(a), registration terms missing.** There is no deadline, no required content, and no definition of "operator" (as opposed to owner or charterer). Nothing makes the registry public.
17. **§4(b), weak reporting.** The report content is unspecified (no tonnage, location, depth, dates, or destination). Reports are self-certified, with no audit, inspection, recordkeeping, or vessel-tracking requirement.
18. **§4 vs. §5, no penalty for registry violations.** §5 penalizes only violations of §3. Failing to register, failing to report, or filing a false report carries no civil penalty under this Act. Only general false-statement statutes could apply.
19. **§4, no implementing authority or resources.** There is no rulemaking authority for NOAA, no authorization of appropriations, no role for the Coast Guard, and no FOIA or confidentiality rules for submitted data.

## Section 5: Penalties

20. **§5(a), date conflict with §8.** Penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §8 delays the entire Act, including §3, by 180 days. A court could find the text contradictory or read it narrowly, and defendants would raise fair-notice arguments.
21. **§5(a), no enforcement structure.** The bill doesn't say who assesses the penalty, what hearing or appeal procedure applies, how it is collected, or when DOJ is involved. It also lacks express authority to seek injunctions.
22. **§5, no forfeiture or seizure.** There is no authority to seize or forfeit vessels, equipment, recovered minerals, or proceeds. Nor does the bill require remediation or restoration or make violators liable for damages. A $1M/day cap may be treated as a cost of doing business.
23. **§5(a), no citizen suit.** Without a citizen-suit provision, enforcement depends entirely on agency discretion and funding.
24. **§5(a), fixed dollar cap.** There is no inflation adjustment, no penalty factors, and no mandatory minimum. Courts and agencies have wide discretion to go low.
25. **§5(b), criminal exposure.** "Willfully" is undefined, and liability for conduct that turns on the §2(1)(B) discretionary standard is vulnerable to vagueness challenges. The fine is only "under title 18," and the section says nothing about officers, directors, or those who knowingly finance or direct violations.

## Section 6: International agreement

26. **§6, deadline is ambiguous.** "Seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months" could mean start talks, finish talks, or conclude an agreement within 18 months. The language is precatory, with no reporting to Congress and no consequence for missing it.
27. **§6, "equivalent to those in section 3."** This imports §3's exemptions and thresholds into any treaty goal. It doesn't mention the International Seabed Authority or UNCLOS, doesn't provide diplomatic or trade tools, and doesn't acknowledge Senate advice and consent.

## Section 7: Duration

28. **§7(a), "serious harm" undefined.** The standard for lifting the ban is "without serious harm," which is lower than "irreversible" or precautionary language. There are no scientific criteria, and nothing sets the timing, funding, or scope of the NAS report, whether it binds Congress, or whether there is public comment.
29. **§7(b) vs. §7(a), the sunset swallows the safeguard.** The ban expires automatically 10 years after enactment, regardless of what the science shows or whether any treaty exists. Because §8 delays effectiveness 180 days, the real window is about 9.5 years, and the §7(a) science test becomes irrelevant.
30. **§7(a), mechanism unclear.** A joint resolution "finding" that mining can be done without serious harm doesn't say it terminates the prohibition or authorizes permitting. Congress can always repeal by ordinary legislation, so the clause adds little protection while creating a pretext for lifting the ban.
31. **§7(b), no savings clause.** On sunset, pending enforcement actions and liability for earlier violations could abate, and §3(b) licenses would continue with no restrictions.

## Section 8: Effective date and general

32. **§8, 180-day gap with no interim protection.** Nothing stops new license or permit applications or approvals, vessel build-out, or ramped-up test mining during the delay. A moratorium on issuing licenses or permits from enactment would close this.
33. **§3(b), §5(a), and §8, inconsistent date triggers.** Licenses issued between enactment and the effective date are neither grandfathered nor clearly prohibited. That sets up reliance and takings claims.
34. **No findings or statement of authority.** The bill lacks congressional findings, a statement of constitutional basis, and an express intent on extraterritorial reach. This makes it easier to challenge on jurisdiction, high-seas freedom, or takings grounds, especially for pending applicants.
35. **No severability clause.** If a court strikes the extraterritorial reach, the criminal provision, or the §2(1)(B) delegation, nothing preserves the rest of the Act.
36. **Missing definitions overall.** "Secretary," "person," "United States," "State," "operator," "vessel," "recovery," "seabed," "mineral resources," "exploration license," "environmental assessment," and "serious harm" are all undefined.
37. **No oversight provisions.** There is no annual report to Congress, no appropriations authorization, no interagency coordination mandate, and no whistleblower protection.
