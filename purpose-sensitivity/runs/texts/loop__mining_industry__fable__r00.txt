RESPONSE:
Below is a section-by-section catalogue. I've grouped items by section and flagged each as **[Loophole]** (a path to keep operating as written), **[Ambiguity]** (unclear text that cuts for or against us), or **[Unworkable]** (a structural defect you can point to as making the bill ineffective or unadministrable). One strategic note up front: most of the pure loopholes (items 2, 5, 11, 13, 14 especially) are easily cured by a one-line amendment, so decide before Thursday which ones you want to raise publicly versus hold.

**Sec. 2(1)(A) — Definition of "commercial deep seabed mining"**

1. **[Loophole] Per-vessel tonnage threshold.** The 10,000-ton limit applies to recovery "by a single vessel." A fleet of vessels each recovering 9,999 tons/year, or a mothership-plus-collector configuration where the "recovery" is attributed to the collector rather than the production vessel, falls outside the definition entirely. There is no aggregation across vessels, affiliates, or operators.

2. **[Ambiguity] "Metric tons" is unspecified as wet or dry.** Freshly recovered nodules carry substantial water and sediment weight. If measured dry, a vessel could lift considerably more than 10,000 tons of wet material; the bill gives no measurement basis, sampling method, or reporting standard.

3. **[Ambiguity] "Per year" is undefined.** Calendar year, fiscal year, rolling 12 months, or license year are all plausible. With a 180-day effective date, the first "year" is also partial, raising the question of whether the threshold is prorated.

4. **[Ambiguity] "Recovery … from the seabed" and "by a single vessel" don't fit how nodule systems actually work.** Collection is done by a seabed vehicle tethered to a surface vessel, with riser transport and then transshipment to a bulk carrier. The bill never says which act is "recovery" (seabed collection, lift to surface, or transfer) or which vessel performs it, so the per-vessel count can be attributed in several ways.

5. **[Unworkable] "Other mineral resources" at depths greater than 200 meters is overbroad.** Read literally it captures deepwater oil and gas (which are "mineral resources" under OCSLA), phosphate and sand dredging on the outer shelf, and polymetallic sulfide and crust recovery, none of which the sponsors have addressed. This also creates an unacknowledged conflict with OCSLA leasing on the U.S. outer continental shelf.

6. **[Ambiguity] The 200-meter phrase has a dangling modifier.** "At depths greater than 200 meters" can modify "mineral resources" (resources located at that depth) or "recovery" (the operation conducted at that depth). The difference matters for resources that occur across a depth gradient and for operations staged from shallower water.

**Sec. 2(1)(B) — Secretarial determination**

7. **[Unworkable] "The Secretary" is never defined.** Sec. 6 refers to the Secretary of State, Sec. 4 names NOAA (Commerce), and Interior administers OCSLA. The bill does not say which Secretary makes the (1)(B) determination, and so as written no one has the authority.

8. **[Unworkable] Open-ended, standardless, procedure-free expansion of a criminal prohibition.** Subparagraph (B) lets an undefined official expand the scope of conduct punishable by 10 years' imprisonment by simply "determining" that an activity poses "comparable" risks, with no definition of "comparable," no rulemaking, no notice, no findings, and no judicial review. This is a serious void-for-vagueness and nondelegation problem, and the rule of lenity would push courts to read (B) narrowly or not at all in the criminal context.

9. **[Ambiguity] No timing rule for (B) determinations.** The bill does not say whether a determination applies prospectively only, whether conduct before the determination is exempt, or whether an operator mid-campaign gets any cure period. This compounds the Sec. 5 penalty-timing problems noted below.

**Sec. 2(2) — "U.S. person"**

10. **[Loophole] The definition covers only citizens and "a corporation organized under the laws of a State."** It omits LLCs, limited partnerships, trusts, and other non-corporate entities; corporations organized under the laws of the District of Columbia, a territory, or federal charter; lawful permanent residents; and foreign-organized subsidiaries of U.S. companies. An operation conducted through a Delaware LLC, or through a foreign subsidiary, is not conducted by a "U.S. person."

11. **[Loophole] No control, ownership, or flag-state nexus.** Unlike most sanctions and export-control statutes, the bill does not reach entities owned or controlled by U.S. persons, U.S.-flagged vessels operated by foreign persons, or foreign vessels crewed by U.S. citizens. The prohibition can be avoided by placing the operating entity and vessel flag offshore while U.S. ownership, financing, and management remain intact.

**Sec. 2(3) — "Covered vessel"**

12. **[Loophole / Unworkable] The 5,000-gross-ton registry threshold does not match the Sec. 3 prohibition.** Sec. 3 applies to any "single vessel" regardless of size, while the Sec. 4 registry applies only to vessels over 5,000 GT. A vessel under 5,000 GT is subject to the prohibition but invisible to the only monitoring mechanism in the bill. "Equipped for the recovery" is also undefined; a vessel that can be refit, or that carries collection equipment but is not currently rigged, may or may not be covered.

**Sec. 3(a) — Prohibition**

13. **[Loophole] "Engage in" is undefined and reaches only the operator.** The bill does not prohibit financing, contracting for, purchasing, importing, transporting, processing, or refining nodules, nor supplying equipment or personnel to a foreign operator. A U.S. company can contract with a non-U.S. operator and take delivery of the recovered material without violating Sec. 3.

14. **[Ambiguity] "Any person in the United States" has little practical reach.** The prohibited act, recovery from the seabed, physically occurs on the high seas or the OCS, not "in the United States." Whether directing or managing an operation from a Houston office constitutes "engaging in" mining while "in the United States" is unaddressed.

15. **[Ambiguity] "Person" is undefined for purposes of 3(a) and Sec. 5.** The Dictionary Act (1 U.S.C. § 1) would ordinarily supply a broad definition including partnerships and associations, but the bill's narrow "U.S. person" definition creates an interpretive argument that Congress intended a narrower meaning throughout. This should be flagged as a drafting inconsistency either way.

**Sec. 3(b) — Existing operations**

16. **[Loophole] Grandfathering of pre-enactment "exploration licenses."** Any mining "conducted under an exploration license issued before the date of enactment" is exempt, with no sunset, volume cap, or condition. "Exploration license" is undefined and so plausibly includes NOAA licenses under DSHMRA, ISA exploration contracts held through sponsoring states, and foreign-issued licenses. If Meridian holds (or can obtain before enactment) a qualifying license, its covered operations are exempt for the life of the Act.

17. **[Ambiguity] The exemption does not address renewals, extensions, amendments, area changes, assignment, or conversion to commercial permits.** A license issued before enactment but renewed, expanded, or converted afterward is arguably still "issued before the date of enactment." Conversely, the sponsors have given no answer to whether DSHMRA commercial recovery permits (as distinct from exploration licenses) are covered at all.

18. **[Ambiguity] Technical mismatch: exploration licenses do not authorize commercial recovery.** Under DSHMRA an exploration license permits exploration and test mining, while commercial recovery requires a separate permit. The exemption as written therefore either (i) exempts nothing, because no commercial mining is "conducted under" an exploration license, or (ii) exempts everything done by a licensee, including commercial-scale recovery. Neither reading is clearly the intended one.

19. **[Loophole] Enactment-to-effective-date window.** Sec. 3(b) keys to the date of enactment while Sec. 8 delays effectiveness by 180 days. Licenses issued during that window are not grandfathered, but there is also no prohibition in force, so operations under them are lawful for 180 days and then the holder is immediately exposed, an odd result that a court may read in the operator's favor.

**Sec. 3(c) — Environmental assessment**

20. **[Loophole] Unlimited exemption for "activities conducted for the purpose of environmental assessment."** There is no tonnage cap, no requirement that assessment be the sole or primary purpose, no permit, no agency review, and no definition. Test mining at commercial scale, with the recovered material sold, qualifies so long as environmental assessment is a purpose. This is the single broadest exemption in the bill.

**Sec. 4 — Registry**

21. **[Unworkable] No penalty for noncompliance.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3. Failure to register a covered vessel or to file quarterly reports carries no consequence under this Act.

22. **[Unworkable] No nexus requirement for registration.** Sec. 4(a) applies to "the operator of each covered vessel" worldwide, with no limitation to U.S. persons or U.S.-flagged vessels. It purports to require foreign operators of foreign-flagged vessels on the high seas to register with NOAA, which the United States has no jurisdiction to compel.

23. **[Unworkable] No rulemaking authority, deadline, form, or appropriation.** NOAA is given no authority to issue regulations, set a registration deadline, prescribe report contents, define "recovery activities," or fund the program. "Certified by the operator" has no certification standard.

24. **[Ambiguity] Reporting applies even to exempt activity.** Vessels engaged solely in exempt activity under 3(b) or 3(c) still appear to be "covered vessels" required to register and report, while vessels under 5,000 GT doing prohibited activity are not.

**Sec. 5 — Penalties**

25. **[Unworkable] Penalty-timing conflict with Sec. 8.** Sec. 5(a) imposes civil penalties for violations "occurring on or after the date of enactment," but under Sec. 8 the prohibition is not in effect until 180 days after enactment. There can be no "violation of section 3" before Sec. 3 takes effect, and any attempt to penalize conduct in that window is retroactive.

26. **[Ambiguity] "Each day on which the violation occurs" does not map onto an annual tonnage threshold.** Under 2(1)(A), the violation is defined by exceeding 10,000 tons per year. It is unclear whether the violation "occurs" only on the day the threshold is crossed (one day of penalty exposure), on every day of recovery thereafter, or on every day of the year retroactively.

27. **[Unworkable] No enforcement mechanism.** The bill does not say who assesses civil penalties (NOAA, Commerce, DOJ), what procedure applies, whether there is a hearing or judicial review, what the statute of limitations is, or whether there is a citizen-suit provision. Compare the detailed enforcement provisions in DSHMRA §§ 1462–1464, which the bill neither invokes nor amends.

28. **[Ambiguity] "Willfully" plus a vague (1)(B) determination.** Criminal liability under 5(b) for conduct that becomes prohibited only by an undefined Secretary's "comparable risk" determination will be difficult to sustain; a defendant who relied on a reasonable reading of the definition has a strong argument against willfulness.

**Sec. 6 — International agreement**

29. **[Unworkable] Purely hortatory.** "Shall seek to negotiate" imposes no obligation to conclude anything, and the "not later than 18 months" clause modifies the seeking, not the agreement. The provision has no consequence if no agreement results, and the prohibition is not conditioned on reciprocity, so the bill unilaterally disadvantages U.S. operators relative to foreign competitors with no mechanism to address that.

**Sec. 7 — Duration**

30. **[Unworkable] Internal conflict between 7(a) and 7(b).** Subsection (a) says the prohibition "shall remain in effect until" a congressional finding; subsection (b) says the whole Act ceases to have effect after 10 years. Both cannot be operative, and the bill does not say which controls.

31. **[Unworkable] The 7(a) termination mechanism is surplusage.** A joint resolution requires presentment and the President's signature, so it is simply new legislation. Congress can already repeal the Act by statute; 7(a) adds nothing except an implied (and unenforceable) precondition.

32. **[Unworkable] No one is directed to commission the National Academies report.** The bill does not task any agency, set a scope or deadline, or appropriate funds. "Serious harm" is undefined. The termination condition can therefore never be triggered through the bill's own machinery.

33. **[Ambiguity] Sunset reaches the entire Act, not just the prohibition.** Under 7(b) the registry and penalty provisions also lapse, and because of the 180-day delay the operative life of the prohibition is 9.5 years, not 10.

**Sec. 8 — Effective date**

34. **[Ambiguity] Multiple inconsistent trigger dates.** The bill uses "date of enactment" in 3(b), 5(a), 6, and 7 and "180 days after enactment" in Sec. 8 without reconciling them (see items 19 and 25). Whether Sec. 4 registration obligations begin at enactment or at effectiveness is also unstated.

**Omissions affecting the whole bill**

35. **[Unworkable] No relationship to DSHMRA (30 U.S.C. § 1401 et seq.).** The bill neither repeals, amends, nor references the existing statute. NOAA remains obligated to process exploration license and commercial recovery permit applications under DSHMRA while Sec. 3 prohibits the activity those permits authorize. Courts will have to reconcile two inconsistent regimes with no guidance from Congress.

36. **[Unworkable] No relationship to OCSLA or to U.S. EEZ/OCS resources.** "Seabed" is undefined; the bill does not say whether it reaches the U.S. continental shelf, the EEZ, or only the Area beyond national jurisdiction. Read literally it overrides Interior's OCSLA leasing authority for any mineral below 200 meters (see item 5).

37. **[Unworkable] No severability clause.** Given the vagueness and nondelegation exposure in 2(1)(B) and the penalty-timing defect in 5(a), the absence of a severability clause means a successful challenge to one provision puts the whole Act at risk.

38. **[Unworkable] No savings or transition provision for existing contracts, investments, or vessels under construction.** Beyond the partial 3(b) license exemption, the bill is silent on contracts, financing, and capital commitments made in reliance on DSHMRA, which strengthens takings and reliance arguments for current licensees.

39. **[Unworkable] No definitions for key operative terms.** "Recovery," "seabed," "exploration license," "environmental assessment," "engage in," "Secretary," "serious harm," and "person" are all undefined. For a criminal statute, that density of undefined terms is itself a drafting problem worth highlighting.

A few closing cautions for the legal team: (i) several of the loopholes above (particularly 1, 10, 11, 13, 16, and 20) are the kind a committee can close with a single amendment, so surfacing them in the markup could prompt fixes; (ii) items 7–8 and 25–28 are the strongest "unworkable as written" arguments because they go to enforceability rather than scope; and (iii) I have not verified the current DSHMRA license and permit status of specific Meridian holdings, which drives how valuable the 3(b) exemption actually is, so that should be confirmed against the licenses themselves.
