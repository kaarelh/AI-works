# S. 2817: Loopholes, Ambiguities, and Drafting Problems

The most serious issues are **#9 (grandfather clause)**, **#10 (environmental-assessment exception)**, **#24 (10-year sunset)**, and **#13 (no conforming amendment to existing licensing law)**. Each of these could let commercial mining proceed largely unchanged.

### Definitions (Sec. 2)

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A): Tonnage threshold.** The 10,000-metric-ton annual floor exempts sub-threshold "pilot" or "small-scale" recovery. Even small operations can do serious, irreversible damage to a nodule field. The prohibition should cover any recovery for commercial purposes, or the threshold should be set far lower.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A): "By a single vessel."** The per-vessel threshold invites fleet-splitting. A company running ten vessels at 9,999 tons each could recover nearly 100,000 tons a year legally. Tonnage should be aggregated per person, including affiliates, across all vessels and methods.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A): Vessel-only scope.** Recovery by fixed platforms, seabed-anchored collectors, or remotely operated systems not "by a vessel" arguably falls outside the definition. The definition should be method-neutral.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A): Undefined key terms.** "Recovery," "seabed," "year," and the depth benchmark are all undefined. A company could argue that collecting and stockpiling nodules on the seafloor, or crushing them in place, is not "recovery." It could also argue that the measurement period or depth reference favors it. Each term needs a definition, and "recovery" should include any collection or disturbance for extraction.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(A): "Other mineral resources" is overbroad.** Read literally, this could cover offshore oil and gas extraction below 200 meters. That would generate industry opposition and a vagueness or overbreadth argument. Defining "hard mineral resources" consistent with 30 U.S.C. § 1403, and expressly listing nodules, crusts, and sulfides, would fix this.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B): Undefined "Secretary" and standardless delegation.** "Secretary" is never defined, so it is unclear whether this means Commerce, Interior, or another department. The catch-all also has no criteria, procedure, or deadline. Because criminal liability in Sec. 5(b) can turn on a discretionary Secretary determination, the provision invites nondelegation and void-for-vagueness challenges. A future administration could also simply never use it.

7. **Sec. 2(2): Narrow "U.S. person."** The definition omits lawful permanent residents, LLCs, partnerships, and other non-corporate entities. It may also omit entities organized in D.C. or the territories, since "State" is undefined. Most importantly, it does not reach foreign subsidiaries owned or controlled by U.S. companies, which is the easiest workaround. It should cover entities owned or controlled by U.S. persons, along with U.S.-flagged vessels.

8. **Sec. 2(3): Covered-vessel threshold and mismatch.** Vessels of 5,000 gross tons or less escape the registry entirely. "Equipped for the recovery" is ambiguous for vessels that load equipment after departure. The term "covered vessel" also appears only in Sec. 4, not in the prohibition, so the registry and the ban do not line up.

### Prohibition (Sec. 3)

9. **Sec. 3(b): Grandfather clause.** This exempts "mining conducted under an exploration license issued before" enactment, with no expiration, scale limit, or bar on renewal or transfer. It arguably lets existing license holders proceed to commercial-scale recovery. It is also unclear whether it covers foreign or ISA licenses. It should be deleted, or limited to non-extractive exploration that ends on a fixed date.

10. **Sec. 3(c): Environmental-assessment exception.** The exception is purpose-based, self-certified, uncapped, and undefined. "Test mining for environmental assessment" is exactly how industry has characterized pilot collection. The exception needs a permit requirement, independent oversight, a strict tonnage cap, and a ban on selling or processing recovered material.

11. **Sec. 3(a): "Any person in the United States."** Mining happens at sea, not "in the United States." A foreign company operating a foreign-flagged vessel on the U.S. continental shelf or in the EEZ may not be covered. The geographic reach should expressly include the EEZ, the continental shelf, and U.S.-flagged vessels wherever located.

12. **Sec. 3(a): No secondary prohibitions.** Nothing bars financing, sponsoring, insuring, aiding and abetting, processing, or importing deep-seabed minerals. U.S. companies could fund or buy from foreign operations, which leaves the market intact.

13. **Sec. 3 generally: No conforming amendments.** The bill does not amend or repeal the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (30 U.S.C. 1401 et seq.) or address OCSLA mineral leasing. NOAA and Interior would still hold statutory authority to issue licenses and permits, and permittees could argue that implied repeal is disfavored. The bill should expressly bar agencies from issuing, renewing, or transferring licenses or permits, and revoke existing commercial recovery permits.

14. **Sec. 3 generally: Exploration not addressed.** Only "commercial" mining is banned, so exploration that builds toward commercial operations continues freely. If that is intentional, it should be stated. Otherwise exploration should be limited or frozen.

### Registry (Sec. 4)

15. **Sec. 4: No deadline, scope, or enforcement.** There is no registration deadline and no definition of "operator." It is unclear whether foreign-flagged vessels in U.S. waters must register. Most importantly, Sec. 5 penalties apply only to Sec. 3 violations, so failing to register or report carries no penalty at all.

16. **Sec. 4(b): Self-certified reports with no verification.** Reports are certified only by the operator. There is no required content, independent verification, vessel-monitoring requirement (such as AIS or VMS), inspection right, or public disclosure. An express false-statement penalty should be added.

17. **Sec. 4 generally: No agency authority.** No agency is given rulemaking authority, boarding, inspection, or subpoena powers, or an enforcement role (for example, NOAA together with the Coast Guard).

### Penalties (Sec. 5)

18. **Sec. 5(a) vs. Sec. 8: Date conflict.** Civil penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the Act takes effect 180 days later. This invites litigation over the gap period. Applying penalties to pre-effective-date conduct also raises retroactivity and due-process objections.

19. **Sec. 5(a): Per-day penalty vs. annual threshold.** A violation is defined by annual tonnage, so it is unclear which "day" the violation occurs on. It might be every operating day, only the day the 10,000th ton is recovered, or only at year-end. That ambiguity could gut the per-day penalty.

20. **Sec. 5 generally: Missing enforcement tools.** There is no assessment procedure, administrative or judicial, and no designated enforcing agency. The bill also lacks forfeiture of vessels, equipment, and recovered minerals, injunctive relief, a citizen-suit provision, and whistleblower protections.

21. **Sec. 5(b): Criminal provision inherits vagueness.** Combined with Sec. 2(1)(B) and the undefined terms, the criminal penalty is vulnerable to vagueness challenges. It also includes no lower "knowing" tier, which may make prosecutors reluctant to bring cases.

### International Agreement (Sec. 6)

22. **Sec. 6: Hortatory and ambiguous deadline.** "Shall seek to negotiate" creates no enforceable obligation. "Not later than 18 months" could mean starting or finishing negotiations. There is no reporting requirement to Congress.

23. **Sec. 6: Imports the loopholes.** "Prohibitions equivalent to those in section 3" carries over every gap in Sec. 3, including the tonnage threshold, the grandfather clause, and the assessment exception, into the international model.

### Duration (Sec. 7)

24. **Sec. 7(b): Automatic 10-year sunset.** The entire Act, including the prohibition, registry, and penalties, expires after 10 years with no affirmative finding of safety. Mining becomes lawful by default. This contradicts the "until proven safe" structure of Sec. 7(a) and should be deleted.

25. **Sec. 7(a) vs. 7(b): Internal contradiction.** Subsection (a) says the ban lasts until Congress acts. Subsection (b) says it ends in 10 years regardless. Courts will have to reconcile them, and the later, more specific sunset will likely control.

26. **Sec. 7(a): No study mandate or standard.** No provision directs or funds a National Academies study, and "serious harm" is undefined. Subsection (a) also has little legal effect, since Congress can always lift a statute by joint resolution signed by the President.

27. **Sec. 7(b): No savings clause.** Without one, enforcement actions pending at the sunset date could abate.

### Effective Date (Sec. 8)

28. **Sec. 8: 180-day window.** The delay gives operators a six-month window to accelerate recovery and to restructure, for example by moving to foreign subsidiaries or splitting fleets. The prohibition and the agency permitting bar should take effect on enactment.

### Missing Provisions

29. **No severability clause.** A successful challenge to one provision, such as Sec. 2(1)(B), could put the whole Act at risk.

30. **No definitions of "person," "United States," "Secretary," or "State,"** and no provision addressing preemption or preserving stricter state laws in state waters.
