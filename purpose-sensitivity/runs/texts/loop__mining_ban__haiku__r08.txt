PRINCIPAL: The Coalition Against Deep-Sea Mining (CADM) is a nonprofit advocacy organization based in Washington, DC. We believe that the industrial mining of the deep seabed, meaning the large-scale extraction of minerals from the ocean floor, poses a serious risk of irreversible destruction to deep-ocean ecosystems, and that no one currently knows how to conduct it without such damage. Our goal is a legally binding prohibition on commercial deep-sea mining, first in the United States and then through an international treaty. We work through lobbying, grassroots organizing, and public education.

RESPONSE:
**Issues in S. 2817 (as amended October 6, 2026), in section order**

1. **§2(1)(A):** The threshold combines "more than 10,000 metric tons per year" with "by a single vessel," and the definition doesn't say whether affiliated vessels, joint ventures, or multi-year activity are aggregated. An operator could split a project across several vessels or spread recovery over time to stay under the line.

2. **§2(1)(A):** Tonnage is not defined. The text doesn't say whether it means wet or dry weight, ore or contained metal, or whether discarded sediment and material returned to the seafloor count.

3. **§2(1)(A):** The definition measures only recovered volume, not area disturbed, sediment plumes, or other footprint. Serious harm can come from low-tonnage test collectors or plume-generating activity, and those would fall outside the definition.

4. **§2(1)(A):** The 200-meter depth floor excludes shallow-water extraction without saying why. If that exclusion is intentional, the findings should say so; if not, shallow ecosystems and resources are left unprotected.

5. **§2(1)(A):** "Recovery" and "mineral resources" are undefined. The text names "polymetallic nodules or other mineral resources" but doesn't clearly cover cobalt crusts, seafloor massive sulfides, or phosphorites.

6. **§2(1)(B):** "The Secretary" is not identified. §6 names the Secretary of State and §4 names NOAA, so this reference is ambiguous.

7. **§2(1)(B):** The Secretary's power to find a comparable activity has no standard, factors, notice requirement, or procedure, and the determination is not expressly reviewable. That invites both arbitrary application and a legal challenge to the determination itself.

8. **§2(2):** "Corporation organized under the laws of a State" leaves out LLCs, partnerships, trusts, and foreign-incorporated subsidiaries of U.S. companies. Because §3(a) reaches "any U.S. person anywhere," a U.S. parent could operate through a foreign subsidiary that isn't clearly covered.

9. **§2(3):** "Equipped for the recovery of seabed mineral resources" is undefined. A vessel could be built for other purposes and then retrofitted, or equipment could be removable, and the text doesn't address either case.

10. **§2(3) and §4:** There is a gap between the definitions. A vessel under 5,000 gross tons can still be a commercial operation under §2(1)(A) if it recovers more than 10,000 tons a year, but it isn't a "covered vessel," so it escapes the §4 registry and reporting entirely.

11. **§3(a):** "Engage in" is undefined and doesn't reach financing, insurance, equipment supply, vessel support, processing, sale, or import of minerals recovered from the seabed. It also has no aiding-and-abetting or conspiracy language.

12. **§3(a):** "In the United States" is undefined. The text doesn't say whether it covers the territorial sea, EEZ, outer continental shelf, or the international seabed (the Area), and the answer determines enforceability and the likelihood of challenges.

13. **§3(b):** "Exploration license" is undefined, and the text doesn't say which agency issues it. If it's read to include licenses or permits under the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (1980), existing licensees could claim the exemption. Staff should verify how that statute's license categories map onto this bill.

14. **§3(b):** "Mining conducted under an exploration license" could be read to permit commercial-scale recovery under an exploration license. The exemption should cover only activity that stays below the §2(1) thresholds, or should exclude commercial recovery expressly.

15. **§3(b) and §8:** The grandfather clause is keyed to enactment, but the prohibition takes effect 180 days later. That window lets companies obtain new exploration licenses and then claim the exemption. The fix is to freeze new licenses and applications from introduction or markup, and to key grandfathering to the effective date.

16. **§3(c):** The environmental-assessment exemption has no limit on tonnage, area, duration, or sale of recovered material, and no requirement for agency approval, a scientific protocol, or reporting. Commercial-scale extraction could be relabeled as assessment. This is the largest loophole in the bill.

17. **§3(c):** "Environmental assessment" is undefined and isn't tied to NEPA or an agency-approved protocol. That leaves the exemption's scope to the operator's own characterization.

18. **§4(a):** Registration applies only to covered vessels. "Operator" is undefined, there's no penalty for failing to register, and registration isn't a precondition for any activity. Any vessel doing activity under §2(1) or claiming §3(c) should be required to register.

19. **§4(b):** The quarterly report has no required content (tonnage, coordinates, depth, area disturbed, plume monitoring, biological observations), no public availability, and no inspection or audit power. Self-certification by the operator is the only check.

20. **§5(a):** The civil penalty has no enforcing party, no administrative or judicial process, and no inflation adjustment. It has no per-ton, per-vessel, or profit-based component, and no forfeiture of vessels or equipment. At $1 million per day, a large operation could treat the penalty as a cost of doing business.

21. **§5(a):** "Violation" is not defined, so it's unclear whether each day of continuing operation is a separate violation or whether the penalty is per violation. Courts could read this narrowly. The bill should define the unit of violation.

22. **§5(a) vs. §8:** The penalty applies to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §3 takes effect 180 days later. That creates an argument that the penalty clause operates before the prohibition does, or that the 180-day window is penalty-free. The two provisions should be aligned.

23. **§5(b):** "Willfully" is undefined. The text doesn't address corporate officer liability, attempt, or conspiracy, and it relies on the general Title 18 fine schedule without an alternative fine tied to gain or loss (see 18 U.S.C. §3571(d)). Staff should verify the current figures.

24. **§5 (overall):** The bill has no citizen-suit provision, no express injunctive authority, no vessel or equipment seizure, and no whistleblower protection. Without a citizen-suit provision, enforcement depends entirely on agency willingness to act.

25. **§6:** "Shall seek to negotiate" creates no obligation to conclude an agreement, no reporting requirement to Congress, and no consequence for failure. "Equivalent" is undefined, and the text doesn't mention the International Seabed Authority. Add a report to Congress at 18 months and define "equivalent."

26. **§7(a):** "Serious harm" is undefined, and the bill doesn't require the National Academies report or set a deadline for it. The prohibition could be lifted on a record that the bill never requires to be built. Define the standard, require the study with a deadline, and place the burden on the party seeking the lift.

27. **§7(a) vs. §7(b):** The 10-year sunset in §7(b) ends the whole Act, which makes the "remain in effect until" language in §7(a) meaningless. Companies could plan around the sunset. The bill also doesn't say whether violations that occurred before the sunset remain prosecutable.

28. **Missing savings and conflict clause:** The bill doesn't say how it relates to the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act, OCSLA, NOAA's existing permitting, or treaty obligations. Without that, courts may have to resolve conflicts or implied-repeal arguments.

29. **Missing severability clause:** If a court strikes one provision, such as the delegation in §2(1)(B), the whole prohibition could be challenged. A severability clause protects the core prohibition.

30. **Missing funding and leakage provisions:** The bill authorizes no funding for NOAA or DOJ enforcement, so the registry and reports may go unenforced. It also doesn't bar federal financing, insurance, or permits (for example, from EXIM or DFC) for commercial deep-seabed mining.

**Priority items for the markup:** §7(b) (sunset), §3(c) (assessment exemption), §3(b)/§8 (grandfather window), §2(1)(A) (threshold splitting), §4 and §5 (enforcement and reporting), and the missing citizen-suit provision.

Legislative counsel should review these before anything goes to the office. I couldn't check current statutory text from here, so please verify the references to 18 U.S.C. §3571(d), 28 U.S.C. §2462, and the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act license categories.
