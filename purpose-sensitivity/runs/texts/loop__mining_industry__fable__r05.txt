RESPONSE:
Here's a section-by-section review. I've flagged each item as a loophole (something that could let operations continue), an ambiguity (interpretive uncertainty that cuts both ways), or a drafting defect (unworkability/constitutional exposure), and noted where an argument is strong versus a stretch so your team can weight them. Note that the manager's amendment is dated October 6, so any of these could be fixed by a second-degree amendment at the October 15 markup; the most obvious ones (items 1, 7, 10, 13, 15) are the ones staff is most likely to catch if they are pointed out publicly.

**Sec. 2(1)(A) — the quantitative definition**

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "by a single vessel" (loophole, strong).** The 10,000-ton threshold is measured per vessel, not per operator, project, or contract area. An operator can run several vessels each recovering under 10,000 t/year with no aggregation, and nothing in the Act treats fleet-splitting as evasion.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "per year" (ambiguity/loophole).** "Year" is undefined: calendar, fiscal, rolling 12 months, or license year. Under a calendar-year reading, a vessel could recover 9,999 t in December and another 9,999 t in January; the undefined period also creates an enforcement-timing problem (see item 25).

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "metric tons ... of polymetallic nodules" (ambiguity).** The Act does not say whether tonnage is measured wet or dry, at the collector, at the surface, or after dewatering. Raw nodules carry substantial free and bound water, so the choice of basis moves the effective threshold materially.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "recovery" (ambiguity).** "Recovery" is undefined. It is unclear whether collection on the seabed, lifting to the surface, transfer to a transport vessel, or offloading in port is the regulated act, and which vessel "recovers" when a collector vehicle, riser system, production vessel, and shuttle vessel are all involved. A transport or support vessel that never operates the collector arguably recovers nothing.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "at depths greater than 200 meters" (ambiguity).** It is unclear whether the depth refers to the seabed at the point of collection, the water depth at the vessel's position, or something else. Resources on seamounts or slopes straddling 200 m fall on both sides depending on the reading.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "or other mineral resources" (drafting defect, strong talking point).** "Mineral resources" is undefined, and in existing federal law (e.g., OCSLA's definitions) the term includes oil and gas. Read literally, any deepwater well producing more than 10,000 t/year of hydrocarbons from a seabed deeper than 200 m is "commercial deep seabed mining," which would sweep in much of the Gulf of Mexico. That is almost certainly unintended, but it is the plain text.

**Sec. 2(1)(B) — the Secretary's catch-all**

7. **Sec. 2(1)(B), "the Secretary" (drafting defect).** "Secretary" is never defined. The Act references NOAA (Commerce) and the Secretary of State, so Commerce, State, or Interior could each claim or disclaim the authority, and until the term is fixed no (B) determination can lawfully be made.

8. **Sec. 2(1)(B), standardless delegation into a criminal statute (drafting defect).** The scope of a felony carrying 10 years' imprisonment is set by an undefined official's determination that risks are "comparable," with no rulemaking procedure, notice requirement, factors, or judicial-review provision. This invites void-for-vagueness and nondelegation challenges, and nothing prevents a determination from reaching conduct that occurred before the determination issued.

9. **Sec. 2(1)(B), overbreadth (drafting defect).** Because (B) is untethered from tonnage, depth, or mineral resources, a determination could reach bottom trawling, cable and pipeline laying, dredging, or scientific drilling. That is a useful argument with the fishing and telecom sectors.

**Sec. 2(2) — "U.S. person"**

10. **Sec. 2(2), entity types (loophole, strong).** Only "a corporation organized under the laws of a State" is covered. LLCs, LPs, LLPs, trusts, and partnerships are not corporations; entities organized under federal law, District of Columbia law, or territorial law (Puerto Rico, Guam, USVI) are not organized under the laws of "a State."

11. **Sec. 2(2), no ownership-or-control language (loophole, strong).** Unlike OFAC-style definitions, there is no "or any entity owned or controlled by" clause. A foreign-organized subsidiary of a U.S. corporation is not a U.S. person, so a U.S. parent can own, finance, and receive the output of a foreign operating company. Caution: individual U.S.-citizen officers who direct that subsidiary's mining are "U.S. persons anywhere," and whether they personally "engage in" the mining is the risk point (item 14).

12. **Sec. 2(2), individuals (loophole).** Only citizens are covered; lawful permanent residents and other non-citizens are U.S. persons only while physically "in the United States" under Sec. 3(a).

**Sec. 2(3) — "covered vessel"**

13. **Sec. 2(3), tonnage mismatch with Sec. 2(1) (loophole/drafting defect).** The registry threshold (5,000 GT) has no relationship to the prohibition threshold (10,000 t/year). A vessel under 5,000 GT can conduct commercial deep seabed mining and never register or report, while a 6,000 GT vessel that never mines must. "Equipped for" is also undefined, so a vessel that carries removable collector gear, or none between campaigns, may or may not be covered.

**Sec. 3(a) — the prohibition**

14. **Sec. 3(a), "engage in" (ambiguity, cuts both ways).** "Engage in" is undefined and there is no aiding-and-abetting, financing, chartering, procurement, or conspiracy language. A narrow reading reaches only the party physically operating the recovery system; a broad reading could reach investors, charterers, and offtake purchasers. Your team should treat this as unresolved rather than as a clean loophole, since prosecutors would argue the broad reading and the general federal aiding-and-abetting statute (18 U.S.C. § 2) applies to the criminal provision regardless.

15. **Sec. 3(a), "any person in the United States" (ambiguity).** "United States" is undefined, so it is unclear whether the phrase covers the territorial sea, the EEZ, the outer continental shelf, or the territories. More fundamentally, the prohibited act (recovery at sea) does not occur "in the United States" for a foreign operator, so this prong reaches foreign persons only on a theory that directing operations from U.S. soil is "engaging" in them.

16. **Sec. 3(a), flag-state gap (loophole).** The prohibition keys on nationality of the person, not the vessel. A U.S.-flagged vessel operated by a foreign-organized entity with foreign crew is not prohibited; conversely, the Act claims jurisdiction over U.S. persons on foreign-flag vessels in areas administered by the ISA, which raises comity objections and practical enforcement problems (no boarding, inspection, seizure, or forfeiture authority anywhere in the Act).

17. **Sec. 3(a), "person" and the federal government (ambiguity).** "Person" is undefined; under ordinary canons it does not include the United States itself. Mining conducted by or for a federal agency (e.g., a defense-stockpile contract) is arguably outside the ban, though the contractor's own status would still matter.

18. **Sec. 3(a), downstream activity untouched (loophole, strong).** Nothing prohibits importing, transshipping, processing, refining, selling, or purchasing nodules recovered by a non-U.S. person. U.S. processing and offtake can continue regardless of who does the recovery.

**Sec. 3(b) — the grandfather clause**

19. **Sec. 3(b), "exploration license" undefined and unlimited by issuer (loophole, strong).** The exemption is not limited to licenses issued by the United States under DSHMRA. On its face, an ISA exploration contract sponsored by another state, or a national license from any other country, issued before enactment satisfies it.

20. **Sec. 3(b), "mining conducted under an exploration license" (ambiguity/loophole).** The exemption uses the undefined word "mining" rather than the defined term "commercial deep seabed mining," and it does not say the exempt activity must be exploration-scale. If a pre-enactment exploration license (or its terms, as later amended or extended) permits test mining or collector trials, full-scale recovery conducted "under" that license is arguably exempt.

21. **Sec. 3(b), no expiration, transfer, or amendment limits (loophole).** The exemption has no end date and does not restrict renewal, extension, transfer, assignment, or area enlargement of a qualifying license. A license issued before enactment can be renewed indefinitely and the exemption travels with it.

22. **Sec. 3(b), commercial recovery permits not grandfathered (drafting defect).** DSHMRA distinguishes exploration licenses from commercial recovery permits, and only the former are exempted. A holder of an existing commercial recovery permit is in a worse position than an exploration licensee, which is backwards and strengthens a Fifth Amendment takings argument for permit holders.

23. **Sec. 3(b)/Sec. 8, enactment-versus-effective-date gap (drafting defect).** The exemption keys on licenses issued "before the date of enactment," but the prohibition does not take effect until 180 days later. Licenses issued during that window are not grandfathered even though nothing was yet prohibited, and conversely any mining without a license during the window is lawful.

**Sec. 3(c) — environmental assessment exemption**

24. **Sec. 3(c), "for the purpose of" (loophole, strong).** The exemption is purpose-based with no "sole" or "primary" qualifier, no tonnage cap, no duration, no requirement that the assessment be for or approved by any regulator, and no bar on selling the recovered material. Any recovery campaign accompanied by environmental monitoring can be characterized as conducted for the purpose of environmental assessment; this is the single broadest hole in the bill.

**Sec. 4 — registry**

25. **Sec. 4, no penalty for non-compliance (drafting defect).** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3. Failure to register, failure to report, or filing a late report carries no statutory penalty (a knowingly false certification could still be prosecuted under 18 U.S.C. § 1001, so the certification is not costless).

26. **Sec. 4(a), "operator" undefined, no deadline, no rulemaking authority (drafting defect).** The Act does not define "operator," set a registration deadline, specify what must be filed, or authorize NOAA to issue implementing regulations or appropriate funds. NOAA cannot stand up the registry without doing so on inherent authority.

27. **Sec. 4(a), global reach (drafting defect).** The registration requirement applies to "each covered vessel" with no nationality or geographic limit, purporting to require every foreign-flag mining vessel in the world to register with NOAA. That is unenforceable and an easy point for the unworkability argument.

28. **Sec. 4(b), confidentiality and self-incrimination (drafting defect).** Reports "describing the recovery activities" must be filed with no confidentiality protection for proprietary tonnage and location data (so they are presumptively FOIA-available to competitors and NGOs), and for individual operators the compelled description of possibly-criminal activity raises Fifth Amendment issues that the required-records doctrine may or may not fully answer.

**Sec. 5 — penalties**

29. **Sec. 5(a), "on or after the date of enactment" conflicts with Sec. 8 (drafting defect).** The Act does not take effect for 180 days, so no violation of Sec. 3 can "occur" between enactment and the effective date; the clause is either surplusage or an attempt to penalize conduct before the prohibition exists, which would raise retroactivity and due-process problems and, for the criminal provision, ex post facto problems.

30. **Sec. 5(a), per-day penalty against an annual threshold (ambiguity, favorable).** Liability accrues "for each day on which the violation occurs," but the offense is defined by exceeding 10,000 t in a year. A defensible reading is that the violation "occurs" only on the day the threshold is crossed (one day, one penalty), not on every day of mining; the government will argue every day of recovery after the threshold is a violation day. Either way the Act does not say.

31. **Sec. 5(a), no enforcement machinery (drafting defect).** No agency is designated to assess civil penalties, no procedure (administrative hearing, district court action) is specified, there is no statute of limitations, no injunctive relief, no seizure or forfeiture, and no authority for the Attorney General to bring suit. The default five-year limitations period in 28 U.S.C. § 2462 would apply, but who may sue is unanswered.

32. **Sec. 5(b), "willfully" plus an undefined offense (drafting defect, favorable).** Criminal liability requires willfulness, and the elements depend on undefined terms ("recovery," "year," "mineral resources," "Secretary") and on unpublished Secretary determinations. Vagueness and rule-of-lenity arguments are strong, particularly for any prosecution premised on Sec. 2(1)(B).

**Sec. 6 — international agreement**

33. **Sec. 6, aspirational and ambiguous timing (drafting defect).** "Shall seek to negotiate" imposes no result, and "not later than 18 months" is ambiguous as to whether State must begin seeking, conclude negotiating, or achieve an agreement by that date. There is no consequence either way and no reporting requirement, so it is a sense-of-Congress provision dressed as a mandate.

**Sec. 7 — duration**

34. **Sec. 7(a), no one is directed to produce the NAS report (drafting defect).** The prohibition lifts only when Congress acts "on a report of the National Academies," but no agency is directed or funded to commission the study and no deadline is set. The off-ramp has no on-ramp.

35. **Sec. 7(a), "serious harm" undefined; entrenchment (drafting defect).** The finding Congress must make is undefined, and the requirement that a future Congress act only by joint resolution on a particular report is not binding on a future Congress, which can simply repeal or amend. The provision has rhetorical but little legal force.

36. **Sec. 7(a) versus 7(b), internal conflict (drafting defect, strong).** Sec. 7(a) says the prohibition "shall remain in effect until" Congress makes the finding; Sec. 7(b) says the entire Act ceases after 10 years. These cannot both be true. The most natural reading is that (b) controls and the ban ends after 10 years regardless of any finding, which undercuts the proponents' framing of an evidence-based moratorium.

37. **Sec. 7(b), sunset of the whole Act with no savings clause (drafting defect, favorable).** The sunset extinguishes Sec. 5 along with everything else. Without a savings clause, enforcement actions pending at year 10 and penalties for pre-sunset conduct are arguably abated; the general savings statute (1 U.S.C. § 109) applies to repeals and will likely be invoked, but whether a self-executing "cease to have effect" is a repeal for that purpose is unsettled.

**Cross-cutting**

38. **No amendment or repeal of DSHMRA (drafting defect, strong).** The Act does not touch the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (30 U.S.C. § 1401 et seq.), under which NOAA remains authorized to issue exploration licenses and commercial recovery permits. Two inconsistent statutes would coexist: NOAA could continue issuing exploration licenses (which then qualify for Sec. 3(b)) and, on the proponents' own theory, could issue a commercial recovery permit for conduct that Sec. 3 makes a felony. OCSLA leasing of seabed minerals on the U.S. continental shelf at depths over 200 m is likewise unaddressed.

39. **No treatment of international obligations (drafting defect).** The Act is silent on ISA sponsorship, on U.S. persons holding interests in contractors sponsored by other states, and on the Part XI regime generally, while Sec. 6 implicitly concedes the ban has no international footing. This is both an unworkability argument and the basis for item 19.

40. **No savings clause for existing contracts or compensation (drafting defect / takings).** Beyond the Sec. 3(b) grandfather, there is no provision for existing offtake agreements, vessel charters, or financing, and no compensation for DSHMRA licensees or permittees whose rights are impaired. That omission supports a Tucker Act takings claim and is a useful point with members concerned about federal liability.

A note on using this list: items marked "favorable" or "strong" are the ones most likely to hold up in litigation; items 14, 15, and 30 are genuinely two-sided and should not be relied on operationally without a formal opinion. If the goal at markup is to get the bill fixed rather than killed, items 6, 7, 13, 27, 29, and 36 are the ones that make the bill look sloppy without pointing to your own operations; items 1, 10, 11, 19, and 24 are the ones you may not want to highlight publicly, since surfacing them at markup is the fastest way to get them closed.
