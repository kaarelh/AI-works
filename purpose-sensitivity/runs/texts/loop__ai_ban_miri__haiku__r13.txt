RESPONSE:
Here are the issues I found in the October 6 text, in bill order. I worked from the text alone, so legislative counsel should check the drafting fixes before anything goes to the office.

**Highest priority for the markup**
1. §3(c): the safety-evaluation exemption (item 15)
2. §7: the sunset conflicts with the indefinite prohibition (item 27)
3. §5 and §4: registry duties have no penalties and no enforcer (items 17 and 18)
4. §2(1)(B): the Secretary's capability determination has no procedure (item 3)
5. §3(b): the grandfather clause keyed to when training "began" (item 13)

**Definitions (§2)**

1. **§2(1)(A):** The compute trigger counts only FLOP in "a single training run," so developers can split a run into several or leave out post-training, reinforcement learning, and continued pretraining. Define "training run" and count all compute used to create or substantially modify a model.

2. **§2(1)(A):** A fixed 10^26 threshold will erode as algorithmic efficiency improves, so systems with dangerous capabilities could be built below it. Give the Secretary rulemaking authority to update the threshold on a set schedule.

3. **§2(1)(B):** The capability prong depends on a determination by "the Secretary," which is undefined (presumably the Secretary of Commerce), with no procedure, notice, deadline, or record requirement. Until a determination issues, developers can argue nothing is covered, and an unexplained determination is open to an arbitrary-and-capricious challenge.

4. **§2(1)(B):** "Substantially exceeding human performance" and "most cognitive tasks" are undefined, which invites vagueness challenges and disputes over what counts. Tie them to objective benchmarks or delegate definitions to rulemaking.

5. **§2(1) and §3(a):** The bill alternates between "model" (A) and "system" (B), and §3(a) uses "covered AI system." A model embedded in an agent scaffold or product could be argued outside the definition. Define one term that includes the model, its weights, and the associated inference and scaffolding infrastructure.

6. **§2(2):** "U.S. person" excludes LLCs and partnerships (common among AI startups), foreign subsidiaries of U.S. companies, and lawful permanent residents abroad. Expand it to any entity organized under U.S. law or headquartered in the U.S., and reach controlled foreign affiliates.

7. **§2(2):** It is unclear whether "a State" includes the District of Columbia and the territories. Clarify.

8. **§2(3):** "Advanced AI chips" is undefined, so the facility threshold cannot be applied. Define it by technical specifications or by reference to export-control classifications.

9. **§2(3):** "Data center" and "covered facility" are single-site concepts, so a run can be distributed across several sites, each under 50,000 chips, and avoid registration. Aggregate facilities under common ownership or control, or those linked for a single training run.

10. **§2(3) and §2(1)(A):** The registry threshold is a chip count and is not tied to the training threshold. A 10^26 run could plausibly finish on well under 50,000 chips if it runs longer, so the registry could miss covered training. Set the registry threshold in terms of compute capacity.

**Prohibition (§3)**

11. **§3(a):** "Train, develop, or deploy" is undefined, and the clause does not reach fine-tuning, releasing or distributing weights, providing inference access, or possessing a covered system. Add "possess," "distribute," and "make available," and cover fine-tuning explicitly.

12. **§3(a):** The prohibition does not reach aiding or facilitating covered training, so cloud providers and chip suppliers who knowingly supply compute face no liability. Add liability for knowingly providing material support, with a safe harbor for good-faith lack of knowledge.

13. **§3(b):** The grandfather clause turns on when training "began," so a developer can start a run before enactment and continue it indefinitely, and the clause does not limit later development or deployment of the grandfathered system. Limit the exemption to systems completed before enactment, require their registration within a short deadline, and bar further training on them beyond their existing compute.

14. **§3(a) and §2(1)(B):** The bill has no offshoring provision. A non-U.S. developer training abroad for non-U.S. users is outside §3, and §6 is non-binding. Add restrictions on exporting or remotely providing compute, chips, or model weights for covered training abroad.

15. **§3(c):** The safety-evaluation exemption is purpose-based and self-declared, so almost any training run can be recast as "safety evaluation." This is the largest gap in the bill. Require advance notice to the Secretary, defined scope and compute limits, containment (no public access or deployment), and a time limit, and state that the exemption does not cover deployment.

**Registry (§4)**

16. **§4(a):** Registration covers only "operators" of covered facilities, so developers who rent cloud capacity may not be covered, and the registry does not identify covered training runs. Require developers to register covered systems and training runs, and define "operator" to include cloud tenants.

17. **§4 and §5:** Penalties in §5 apply only to violations of §3, so failure to register, failure to report, and false certifications carry no penalty. Add penalties for §4 violations and for knowingly false statements.

18. **§4(b):** Reports cover "computing workloads" without specifying content, detail, or disclosure thresholds, and do not require operators to identify which workloads are covered systems. Specify minimum contents: training runs, compute used, model identifiers, dates, and deployments.

19. **§4(a) and §4(b):** The bill sets no deadline for initial registration or for the first report, and no effective-date rule for these duties. Add explicit deadlines.

20. **§4:** Commerce has no authority to verify reports through inspection, audit, or information requests, so the certifications cannot be checked. Add that authority.

21. **§4:** Reported information has no confidentiality protection or FOIA exemption, which will provoke industry resistance and make companies more cautious about reporting. Protect the information from public disclosure while guaranteeing congressional access.

**Penalties and enforcement (§5)**

22. **§5(a):** The civil penalty names no enforcing agency, no procedure, and no venue. It also does not define when a continuing daily violation begins or ends. Designate the Attorney General or Commerce as enforcer and set venue.

23. **§5(a):** At $1 million per day, the penalty may be treated as a cost of doing business against frontier-scale training budgets. Scale penalties to a meaningful share of compute spending or revenue, or make them higher.

24. **§5(a) and §8:** The penalty applies to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §3 does not take effect until 180 days later. Conform the language so the penalty and the prohibition take effect together.

25. **§5:** The bill has no injunctive relief, cease-and-desist authority, or power to halt a run, quarantine compute, or seize weights. Because the harm comes from the training run itself, a court order to stop is the most important remedy. Add it.

26. **§5(b):** "Willfully" is undefined, and developers could claim they did not know a run crossed the threshold because compute is hard to measure. Define willfulness to include reckless disregard, and require good-faith, documented compute estimates.

27. **§5:** The bill has no individual liability for officers who direct violations. Add responsible-officer liability.

28. **§5 and §4:** The bill gives no rulemaking authority and no appropriation for enforcement. Add both.

29. **General:** The bill has no severability clause, so a court striking §2(1)(B) or §3(c) as vague could be used to attack the whole Act. Add one.

30. **General:** The bill sets no standard of judicial review for the Secretary's determinations. Specify a review standard and expedited review.

**Duration and lifting the prohibition (§7)**

31. **§7(a) and §7(b):** §7(a) keeps the prohibition in place until Congress acts, but §7(b) ends the entire Act after 10 years whether or not Congress has acted. Developers will argue the prohibition lapses at year 10. Remove the sunset, or state that it does not end the prohibition absent an affirmative joint resolution.

32. **§7(a):** The standard "can be developed safely" is undefined, and the lifting mechanism depends on a NASEM report that has no deadline and that Congress cannot compel. Set a deadline, state that a missing report does not lift the prohibition, and specify that a lifting joint resolution is subject to presentment and veto.

**International agreement (§6)**

33. **§6:** "Seek to negotiate" creates no obligation to conclude an agreement and no reporting on progress, and "equivalent prohibitions" is undefined. Require reports to Congress at 6 and 12 months, and define the minimum content of an equivalent prohibition.

**Effective date (§8)**

34. **§8:** The 180-day delay, combined with a grandfather cutoff at enactment, creates an incentive to start very large runs before the bill takes effect. Set the grandfather cutoff at introduction, and make the reporting requirement effective immediately.

**Strategic note for staff**

Current frontier training runs may already be near or above 10^26 FLOP. If so, the compute prong could sweep in ordinary frontier development, not just superintelligence, and opponents will use that to argue the bill is overbroad. Expect that line of attack at markup, and consider whether the compute trigger should be paired with a capability trigger or framed around systems that are substantially more capable than current ones.
