RESPONSE:
Here's a section-by-section audit. I've grouped items by section and kept numbering continuous. At the end there's a short note on how the list splits between gaps you could actually operate through and points that are better used as "unworkable as drafted" arguments.

## Sec. 2(1)(A) — Compute-threshold definition

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "in a single training run."** "Training run" is undefined, so the threshold can be avoided by segmenting training into multiple runs (pretraining, continued pretraining, mid-training, RL post-training, each under 10^26) from saved checkpoints. There is no cumulative or aggregate compute measure.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "trained using."** It is unclear whether compute spent generating synthetic training data, running teacher models for distillation, or running reward models counts toward the threshold. A distilled model whose own run is under 10^26 is arguably not covered even if it inherits a covered model's capabilities.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A), fine-tuned derivatives.** The bill doesn't say whether a fine-tune of a 10^26 base model is "a model trained using" >10^26 operations (counting the base run) or a new model trained with only the fine-tuning compute. Either reading creates problems: the former makes every downstream fine-tune a covered system; the latter lets derivatives of covered models escape.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A), no measurement methodology.** The text doesn't specify whether operations are counted at theoretical peak or actual utilization, whether backward passes, restarted/failed runs, or discarded ablations count, or how sparsity, mixture-of-experts routing, or low-precision formats are handled. No agency is authorized to issue a counting rule, so the line is unenforceable with precision and vague for criminal purposes.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(A), model merging and ensembles.** Combining several sub-threshold models via merging, routing, or ensembling produces a system with no single qualifying training run and is not captured.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(A), fixed threshold with no adjustment.** The threshold is a static number with no inflation for algorithmic efficiency. As efficiency improves, sub-threshold models will exceed the capabilities the bill is aimed at while remaining outside (A); conversely the threshold will sweep in models that are not "superintelligence" in any sense, which undercuts the Act's stated purpose.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(A), inference-time compute.** The definition looks only at training compute. Test-time scaling (long reasoning, search, many-sample inference) can produce frontier capabilities from a sub-threshold model and is wholly unaddressed.

8. **Sec. 2(1)(A) vs. (B), "model" vs. "system."** (A) covers a "model" and (B) covers a "system"; neither term is defined, and the inconsistency invites argument that an orchestrated system of sub-threshold models is not a "model" under (A).

## Sec. 2(1)(B) — Secretary's capability determination

9. **Sec. 2(1)(B), "the Secretary" is undefined.** Sec. 4 references the Department of Commerce and Sec. 6 references the Secretary of State, but Sec. 2 never says which Secretary makes the determination. As written, no official clearly has the power to designate a system under (B).

10. **Sec. 2(1)(B), no procedure, standard of review, or notice.** There is no rulemaking, notice-and-comment, evidentiary standard, hearing, or judicial review for a determination that can trigger 10-year criminal exposure. This is a strong due-process and APA-reviewability point.

11. **Sec. 2(1)(B), "substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks."** Every operative term is undefined: which humans (average, expert, best), what counts as a "cognitive task," how "most" is measured, and what "substantially" means. Combined with Sec. 5(b)'s criminal penalty, this is a textbook void-for-vagueness vulnerability.

12. **Sec. 2(1)(B), timing and retroactivity.** A capability determination can only be made after a system exists, but Sec. 3(a) prohibits "training" it. A developer cannot know during training that the system will be designated, so liability attaches to conduct that was lawful when undertaken.

## Sec. 2(2) — "U.S. person"

13. **Sec. 2(2), excludes non-corporate entities.** Only citizens and corporations "organized under the laws of a State" are U.S. persons. LLCs, limited partnerships, trusts, and public-benefit entities that aren't formally corporations are outside the extraterritorial prong, as are lawful permanent residents and federally chartered entities.

14. **Sec. 2(2), "a State."** Entities organized under the laws of the District of Columbia, Puerto Rico, or other territories are not "organized under the laws of a State" and are arguably not U.S. persons.

15. **Sec. 2(2), foreign subsidiaries.** A foreign-incorporated subsidiary of a Delaware corporation is not a U.S. person. Training conducted abroad by such a subsidiary is outside Sec. 3(a) unless the parent itself can be said to "train" or "develop" the system, which the bill doesn't address (no attribution, control, or beneficial-ownership rule).

16. **Sec. 2(2), individual citizens.** Because "any U.S. person anywhere" includes citizens, every U.S.-citizen employee of a foreign lab is individually exposed worldwide while the lab itself is not. This is both over- and under-inclusive and practically unenforceable.

## Sec. 2(3) — "Covered facility"

17. **Sec. 2(3), "advanced AI chips" is undefined.** There is no specification, performance threshold, or cross-reference to export-control classifications. Operators cannot know whether they must register, and the number cannot be audited.

18. **Sec. 2(3), "data center" and the 50,000-chip line.** Neither "data center" nor the point in time at which the count is taken is defined. Splitting a campus into multiple buildings or legal entities, or keeping any one site at 49,999 chips, avoids the registry entirely. Distributed training across many sub-threshold sites is invisible to Sec. 4.

## Sec. 3 — Prohibition

19. **Sec. 3(a), "train, develop, or deploy" undefined.** "Develop" could reach research, architecture design, or writing code (sweeping in basic R&D and raising First Amendment concerns), or could be read narrowly to add nothing beyond "train." "Deploy" is undefined as to whether internal use, inference, hosting, or providing API access qualifies.

20. **Sec. 3(a), downstream users.** If "deploy" includes using or integrating a model, then every U.S. business or individual using a covered system developed abroad is a violator subject to $1M/day penalties and criminal exposure. If it does not, foreign labs can serve the U.S. market freely. Either reading is a strong unworkability argument.

21. **Sec. 3(a), conduct not covered.** The bill does not prohibit possessing, acquiring, importing, licensing, using, or running inference on a covered system, nor aiding, abetting, procuring, financing, or conspiring to train one. A U.S. company could fund or commission training by a non-U.S. person and then use the result.

22. **Sec. 3(a), geographic locus of training.** For distributed training across jurisdictions, or a foreign customer renting U.S. cloud compute, it is unclear whether training occurs "in the United States" and who is doing the training (the cloud customer, the facility operator, or both). No attribution rule exists.

23. **Sec. 3(a), government and sovereign actors.** "Person" is undefined; under ordinary rules it does not include the federal government, so DoD, DOE, and intelligence-community projects are either silently exempt or silently prohibited. Either way, the absence of an express national-security provision is a serious drafting gap.

24. **Sec. 3(b), the grandfather clause is keyed to enactment, not the effective date.** Any run whose training "began" before the date of enactment is permanently exempt. Developers can begin as many large runs as they can stand up before signature, and nothing in the text requires those runs to be bona fide or complete.

25. **Sec. 3(b), "whose training began" has no endpoint.** A run that begins before enactment and continues indefinitely (continuous or continual pretraining from the same run) is arguably still the same grandfathered training. Nothing in the text says a grandfathered run must end.

26. **Sec. 3(b), derivatives of grandfathered systems.** It is unclear whether a fine-tune, continued-pretraining checkpoint, or distillation of a grandfathered model is "a covered AI system whose training began before enactment." A reasonable reading is that the derivative inherits the base model's start date, which would exempt an entire lineage.

27. **Sec. 3(b), "began" undefined.** Whether data preprocessing, tokenizer training, a small pilot run, or the first optimizer step counts as training "beginning" is unspecified.

28. **Sec. 3(b), applies to (B) systems as well.** Because the exemption covers any "covered AI system," a system later designated under (B) is exempt from training, development, *and* deployment restrictions if its training started before enactment, regardless of capability.

29. **Sec. 3(c), the safety-evaluation exemption is open-ended.** "Activities conducted for the purpose of safety evaluation" is not limited by scale, actor, duration, or exclusivity ("solely" or "primarily" are absent). Training a covered model in order to evaluate its safety is, literally, an activity conducted for the purpose of safety evaluation. This is the single largest gap in the bill.

30. **Sec. 3(c), covers deployment too.** The exemption applies to all of Sec. 3(a), so deploying a covered system for red-teaming, external evaluation, or "safety research with real users" is exempt. There is no limit on the size of the user population.

31. **Sec. 3(c), subjective purpose standard.** Liability turns on the actor's purpose, which is self-declared and hard to disprove, and which interacts with the "willfully" standard in Sec. 5(b) to make criminal prosecution nearly impossible.

## Sec. 4 — Registry

32. **Sec. 4, no penalty.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3. There is no consequence for failing to register or report, and no deadline for registration.

33. **Sec. 4(a), "operator" undefined.** In colocation and cloud arrangements the facility operator often does not know, and is contractually barred from knowing, what workloads tenants run. Requiring a certification of workloads from a party without visibility is unworkable and invites either meaningless reports or breach of customer contracts.

34. **Sec. 4(b), no content standard or confidentiality protection.** "Describing the computing workloads" sets no granularity, and the bill provides no trade-secret, FOIA-exemption, or CBI protections for highly sensitive data. No inspection, audit, or verification authority is granted.

35. **Sec. 4, no implementing authority.** No rulemaking, appropriation, or staffing is provided to Commerce, and no agency at all is designated to enforce Sec. 3.

## Sec. 5 — Penalties

36. **Sec. 5(a) conflicts with Sec. 8.** Civil penalties attach to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the prohibition is not effective until 180 days later, so no violation can occur in that window. Read literally, the clause either does nothing or is retroactive, raising due-process and (for 5(b)) ex post facto concerns.

37. **Sec. 5(a), no assessment mechanism.** The bill does not say who assesses the civil penalty, in what forum, with what procedures, or whether the agency or DOJ litigates. There is no injunctive relief, forfeiture of weights or hardware, or cease-and-desist authority, so the government's only tool is after-the-fact money.

38. **Sec. 5(a), penalty scale.** $1,000,000 per day caps exposure at roughly $365M per year regardless of the actor's size. For a multi-billion-dollar training run that is a cost of doing business, which undermines the deterrent rationale and is a point lawmakers can be shown.

39. **Sec. 5(b), "willfully."** Given the undefined terms in Secs. 2 and 3, the safety-evaluation exemption, and the absence of any implementing regulations, proving willful violation will be exceptionally difficult. Criminal liability is also not addressed as to corporate officers or individual employees.

40. **Sec. 5, no provisions for aiding and abetting, attempt, or conspiracy, and no private right of action or whistleblower mechanism.**

## Sec. 6 — International agreement

41. **Sec. 6, non-binding and unconnected to the prohibition.** "Seek to negotiate" imposes no obligation of result, no report to Congress, and no consequence if no agreement is reached. Nothing conditions the Sec. 3 prohibition on reciprocity, so the Act operates as a unilateral restriction while foreign developers remain unconstrained.

## Sec. 7 — Duration

42. **Sec. 7(a), internal conflict with 7(b).** 7(a) says the prohibition remains "until Congress … finds by joint resolution," while 7(b) terminates the entire Act after 10 years regardless. The two cannot both be the operative termination rule.

43. **Sec. 7(a), the trigger is non-operative.** No one is directed or funded to commission a National Academies report, so the predicate for a Congressional finding may never exist. And because a joint resolution is just ordinary legislation subject to presentment, 7(a) adds nothing Congress could not already do, while implying a false limitation on repeal.

44. **Sec. 7(b), sunset of "this Act."** The sunset ends the registry and all definitions, not just the prohibition, and leaves no transition rule for grandfathered systems, pending penalties, or registrations.

## Sec. 8 — Effective date

45. **Sec. 8, three inconsistent dates.** The Act uses enactment (Secs. 3(b), 5(a)), enactment plus 180 days (Sec. 8), and enactment plus 18 months (Sec. 6) without reconciling them. Training that begins in the 180-day window is not grandfathered but is also not unlawful until the effective date, and the bill is silent on what happens to such a run when the prohibition kicks in.

## Cross-cutting omissions

46. **No definitions of "artificial intelligence," "model," "system," "person," "Secretary," "operator," "training run," "deploy," or "develop."**

47. **No rulemaking authority, no designated enforcement agency, no judicial-review provision, no severability clause, no preemption clause (so state laws can layer on top), and no exemption for government, academic, open-source, or allied-government activity.**

48. **No controls on the actual inputs.** The Act does not restrict chip acquisition, cloud access by foreign entities, export or import of model weights, or foreign training by U.S.-funded entities, so the conduct it targets can be relocated rather than stopped.

---

**A note on how to use this list.** Items 1–3, 13–15, 21, 24–26, and 29–31 are gaps that would plausibly let a company continue operating under the text as written. Items 4, 9–12, 19–20, 23, 33–38, and 42–45 are better framed as "unworkable or unconstitutional as drafted" arguments. Keep in mind the two uses pull in opposite directions at markup: publicly pointing to an exploitable loophole invites the Committee to close it with a second manager's amendment, while pointing to vagueness and unworkability tends to slow the bill. Legal and policy should decide which items go in the public-facing material versus the internal contingency memo. Several of the constitutional points (vagueness, due process, ex post facto, First Amendment reach of "develop") will need a litigator's assessment before being relied on.
