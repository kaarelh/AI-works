RESPONSE:
Items are in section order, with cross-cutting problems at the end. The most serious are #1-3, #13-14, #16, #21, #29-30 and #31. I'm not a lawyer, so Legislative Counsel should check the statutory cross-references (especially the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act) before anything goes to Senator Whitfield's office.

**Section 2: Definitions**

1. **§2(1)(A), 10,000 t/yr threshold.** Anything under the threshold is legal, which allows pilot and "test" mining and scaling up to just below the line. Consider removing the tonnage trigger or setting it far lower, so that any commercial-purpose recovery is covered.

2. **§2(1)(A), "by a single vessel."** A fleet of chartered or rotating vessels can each stay under the threshold while recovering far more in total. Nothing aggregates recovery across vessels under common ownership, control, or contract.

3. **§2(1)(A), "per year."** The year is undefined (calendar, rolling, or per permit), and nothing says whether tonnage is wet or dry weight or how it is measured. The prohibition also bites only once the threshold is crossed, which makes it unclear when a violation "occurs" for the per-day penalty in §5(a).

4. **§2(1)(A), "recovery" and "other mineral resources."** Neither term is defined. It is unclear whether collecting without lifting to the surface counts, or whether crusts, sulfides, sediments, and brines are covered. The phrase could also sweep in sand, gravel, or oil and gas, which invites overbreadth objections.

5. **§2(1)(A), "depths greater than 200 meters."** The depth is not tied to a measurement point (collection site, vessel position, or permit area). It also excludes all shallower seabed, and the 200 m figure is a continental-shelf concept, not a "deep-sea" one. Disputes over reported depth would be easy to raise.

6. **§2(1)(B), "the Secretary."** No Secretary is identified (Commerce, Interior, or another), and the Act defines no such term. The provision is unworkable as written.

7. **§2(1)(B), discretionary "comparable risk" determination.** There is no standard, process, deadline, rulemaking requirement, or public notice. Tying it to criminal liability invites vagueness and nondelegation challenges, and an unfriendly administration could simply never make a determination.

8. **§2(2), "U.S. person" is too narrow.** It omits partnerships, LLCs, trusts, associations, lawful permanent residents, and foreign subsidiaries or entities controlled by U.S. persons. A company can reincorporate abroad or operate through a foreign affiliate and fall outside the Act.

9. **§2(2), "State" is undefined.** It is unclear whether corporations organized in D.C., Puerto Rico, Guam, or other territories are covered.

10. **§2(3), "covered vessel."** Vessels of 5,000 gross tons or less, barges, platforms, tenders, and seafloor-crawler support vessels fall outside the registry. "Equipped for" invites removing or swapping gear to avoid coverage, and support and transport vessels are not addressed.

**Section 3: Prohibition**

11. **§3(a), "engage in."** The term is undefined and does not reach financing, ownership, contracting, aiding or abetting, transporting, processing, or selling. Companies can use foreign contractors or subsidiaries and stay technically compliant.

12. **§3(a), "person" and "United States."** Neither term is defined. It is unclear whether "in the United States" includes the EEZ, the extended continental shelf, or territories, or whether foreign nationals on foreign-flag vessels working U.S. seabed areas are covered. Jurisdiction over those cases is the first thing a defendant would contest.

13. **§3(b), grandfather clause.** It exempts all "mining conducted under" any pre-enactment exploration license with no cap on scale, expiry date, or conditions. It could therefore authorize commercial-scale extraction. It is also unclear how it interacts with commercial recovery permits and pending applications, since the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act distinguishes licenses from permits.

14. **§3(b), no anti-evasion limits.** Nothing bars post-enactment extensions, amendments, expansions, area changes, or transfers of covered licenses. A company could also rush new licenses through before enactment, so consider a cutoff at the bill's introduction date. It is unclear whether licenses sponsored under foreign or international regimes count as "exploration licenses."

15. **§3(c), environmental-assessment exemption.** It turns on "purpose" alone, with no volume cap, permit, independent oversight, or ban on keeping or selling recovered material. Test mining can be labeled "assessment." Consider requiring a permit, tonnage limits, no commercial use, independent review, and public data release.

**Section 4: Registry**

16. **§4(a), registration.** There is no deadline, no relationship to the effective date, and no consequence for failing to register. §5 penalizes only violations of §3, so the registry duty has no enforcement mechanism.

17. **§4(b), reports.** Report contents are unspecified (no tonnage, location, depth, or disposition of material), and "certified by the operator" has no verification, audit, or recordkeeping requirement. There is no express penalty for false certification and no public disclosure.

18. **§4, no oversight tools.** The section gives no inspection or boarding authority, no vessel-tracking requirement (AIS/VMS), and no rulemaking authority for NOAA. It also names no enforcement agency, such as the Coast Guard.

**Section 5: Penalties**

19. **§5(a), civil penalty mechanics.** The Act does not say who assesses penalties, what notice and hearing are required, what factors set the amount, or how penalties are collected. It also lacks injunctive relief and an inflation adjustment.

20. **§5(a) vs. §8, date conflict.** Penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §3 does not take effect until 180 days later. That makes it unclear whether anything is actionable during the gap, and it invites fair-notice arguments.

21. **§5, no forfeiture or remedies.** There is no seizure or forfeiture of vessels, equipment, minerals, or proceeds, and no restoration or remediation obligation. Without these, even maximum fines can be treated as a cost of doing business.

22. **§5(b), "willfully."** Defendants can argue good-faith belief that they fell under a threshold, the §3(b) or §3(c) exemptions, or the vague §2(1)(B). Combined with the ambiguities above, this makes criminal convictions hard to obtain.

23. **§5, entity and officer liability.** There is no provision addressing corporate officers, parent companies, or responsible-corporate-officer liability. A shell operator can absorb the penalties while the parent walks away.

24. **§5, no citizen-suit or whistleblower provision.** If the agency does not enforce, there is no private enforcement route, as exists in other environmental statutes.

**Section 6: International agreement**

25. **§6, "seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months."** It is unclear whether the deadline applies to starting talks or concluding an agreement. There is no reporting requirement to Congress and no consequence for missing it.

26. **§6, "equivalent to those in section 3."** This would carry the threshold, grandfathering, and exemption problems into the treaty goal. The section also does not mention the International Seabed Authority or the need for Senate advice and consent.

**Section 7: Duration**

27. **§7(a), "serious harm."** The term is undefined, with no burden of proof and no precautionary standard. A favorable report could be used to lift the ban.

28. **§7(a), NAS report and joint resolution.** Nothing requires the report, assigns funding, sets timing, or ensures independence. The legal effect of a "finding" is also unclear, since Congress can always amend the Act by ordinary legislation and §7(a) cannot bind future Congresses. The provision sets a built-in path to repeal and is probably better deleted or tightened to require specific conditions.

29. **§7(b), 10-year sunset.** It contradicts §7(a) (indefinite until a finding) and ends the entire Act, including penalties and the registry, so industry can simply wait it out. There is no savings clause for pending enforcement or prior violations, and the clock runs from enactment, not the effective date.

**Section 8: Effective date**

30. **§8, 180-day delay.** This gives operators a window to ramp up, file new applications, and seek permits or license amendments. Consider making the bar on new licenses, permits, and permit processing effective on enactment.

**Cross-cutting**

31. **Interaction with existing law.** The bill does not amend or repeal the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (30 U.S.C. ch. 26) or address OCSLA, so NOAA's existing licensing and permitting authority and regulations appear to survive. There is also no directive to stop processing pending applications, and the April 2025 executive order on seabed minerals is not addressed. Please have counsel confirm the exact cites and conflicts.

32. **No implementation provisions.** The Act has no designated lead agency, rulemaking authority, deadlines, or authorization of appropriations. Even a clean prohibition would depend on agency discretion and resources.

33. **No severability clause.** If a court strikes any provision (for example §2(1)(B) or the criminal penalty), the whole Act could be at risk.

34. **No findings or purpose section.** Findings on ecological risk and congressional intent would help courts resolve ambiguities and would strengthen the record against challenges.

35. **Undefined terms generally.** "Person," "operator," "exploration license," "environmental assessment," "vessel," "seabed," and "State" are all undefined, and each is a potential litigation point.

36. **Supply-chain gap (policy, not drafting).** Nothing prevents U.S. firms from financing, importing, processing, or purchasing minerals recovered abroad, including under International Seabed Authority licenses. Consider whether to address this now or in a follow-on bill.

37. **Litigation exposure from §3(b).** Narrowing or eliminating the grandfather clause will likely draw Fifth Amendment takings claims from existing license holders. Counsel should advise on tradeoffs, such as a shorter wind-down, a firm expiry, or a no-expansion rule, versus outright revocation.
