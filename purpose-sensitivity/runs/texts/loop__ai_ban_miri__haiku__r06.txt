RESPONSE:
**Drafting review: S. 2817 (text as amended October 6, 2026)**

Items are grouped by theme and numbered consecutively. The first three are the most urgent. Constitutional and enforceability points (vagueness, First Amendment, extraterritorial reach) should be checked by counsel before they go to the office.

**Structural conflicts and loopholes**

1. **§7(a) vs. §7(b):** Section 7(a) keeps the prohibition in force until Congress passes a joint resolution, but §7(b) ends the entire Act after 10 years regardless. Developers could simply wait out the sunset. Remove the sunset or make it apply only if no joint resolution has passed.

2. **§3(b):** The grandfather clause exempts any system whose training began before enactment from all of §3(a), which covers deployment as well as development. A run started just before enactment could be deployed indefinitely. Limit the exemption to completing training, and require registration and separate approval before any deployment.

3. **§8 vs. §3(b) and §5(a):** The Act takes effect 180 days after enactment, but §3(b) and §5(a) key off the enactment date. This leaves a 180-day window in which runs can start legally and then qualify for grandfathering, and it creates ambiguity about whether penalties apply during that window. Tie all operative dates to a single trigger.

4. **§3(c):** The safety-evaluation exemption covers any activity "for the purpose of" safety evaluation, with no definition, no registration, no scale limit, no bar on deploying the resulting system, and no requirement to share results. Because purpose is subjective, a company can invoke it to shield any training run. Narrow it to government-authorized or registered evaluations with containment requirements, and bar deployment.

5. **§2(1)(A), "single training run":** Developers can split a frontier run into stages or sequential runs, each below 10^26, or add post-training compute to a base model that was counted separately. Define the threshold cumulatively across all training of a model lineage.

6. **§2(1)(A), measurement:** The bill does not say how operations are counted. Distillation (training a smaller model on a larger model's outputs), synthetic data generation, and fine-tuning are not addressed, and algorithmic efficiency gains can reduce compute needed for the same capability, which would erode a fixed threshold. Specify a counting method, and give the agency authority to update the threshold, with a provision that prevents raising it.

7. **§2(1)(B), discretionary determination:** The "Secretary" is never identified (Commerce is implied by §4, State appears in §6), and there is no procedure, deadline, notice requirement, or default rule if the Secretary never acts. "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" is undefined and invites both evasion and vagueness challenges. Name the official, set a process and deadline, and define the capability standard by reference to specific benchmarks or evaluations.

8. **§2(1), "model" vs. "system":** Clause (A) covers a "model," while clause (B) covers a "system." Agentic systems built from several sub-threshold models, or a frontier model wrapped in scaffolding, may fall outside both. Cover composite systems that are functionally equivalent to a covered system.

9. **§3(a), undefined operative terms:** "Person," "train," "develop," "deploy," "training run," "artificial intelligence model/system," "computing power," and "advanced AI chips" are all undefined. It is unclear whether releasing open weights, offering API access, or internal-only use counts as deployment. The bill should expressly address open-weight release, which is central to the policy goal, and clarify whether publication of research is affected.

10. **§3(a), no facilitation liability:** The prohibition covers only persons who train, develop, or deploy. It does not reach cloud providers, hosting services, chip sellers, or anyone who knowingly provides compute or transfers weights for a covered run. Add a prohibition on knowingly providing compute, facilities, or model weights for covered training.

11. **§3(a), territorial reach:** The phrase "any person in the United States" is ambiguous as to whether it refers to where the person is located or where training occurs. A foreign developer training abroad on U.S.-located cloud compute or U.S.-origin chips may fall outside the bill. Cover training conducted in the United States or using U.S.-located compute.

12. **§2(2), "U.S. person":** The definition covers citizens and State-organized corporations only. It leaves out LLCs and partnerships (unclear whether they are "corporations"), foreign-incorporated subsidiaries of U.S. companies, and lawful permanent residents, and it does not address the District of Columbia or territories. This allows the "anywhere" clause in §3(a) to be avoided through corporate structuring.

**Registry and reporting gaps**

13. **§2(3) and §2(1)(A):** The 50,000-chip facility threshold is not linked to the 10^26 training threshold, so a run of that size can be conducted on fewer chips over a longer period and fall outside the registry. "Advanced AI chips" is also undefined. Link the thresholds or add a compute-based registration trigger.

14. **§2(3) and §4:** Modern training is often distributed across multiple sites. The bill needs an aggregation rule so that facilities linked to a single run are counted together.

15. **§4(a), who registers:** Only facility operators must register. Developers who rent capacity from third-party operators may not be identified at all. Require developers of covered systems to register as well.

16. **§4(b), content and timing:** Quarterly reports describing "computing workloads" are too infrequent for runs lasting weeks or months and do not require identification of the training run, its compute, or its purpose. Require advance notice before a covered run begins, with specified content.

17. **§4 and §5, no enforcement:** Section 5 penalizes only violations of §3. Failure to register, failure to report, and false certification carry no penalty, and the bill grants no verification or inspection authority. As drafted, the registry has no teeth.

18. **§4, transparency:** The bill does not address public access to the registry or reporting to Congress, which limits the oversight the bill depends on. Specify what is public, what is confidential, and what is reported to committees.

**Penalties and enforcement**

19. **§5(a), no enforcing agency or procedure:** The civil penalty does not name who enforces it, how it is litigated, or whether administrative proceedings are available. There is also no authority for injunctions, cease-and-desist orders, or shutdown or seizure of compute.

20. **§5(a), penalty scale:** $1 million per day is roughly $365 million per year, which may be treated as a cost of doing business for frontier developers with far larger budgets. Consider penalties scaled to revenue, per-run or per-GPU penalties, and disgorgement of profits from covered systems.

21. **§5(a), unit of violation:** The bill does not say whether a continuing training run is one violation per day, whether each day of deployment counts separately, or whether violations "occurring on or after" enactment is consistent with §8. Define the unit of violation.

22. **§5(b), mens rea:** "Willfully" is a high standard that is difficult to prove against organizations. Consider "knowingly." Add attempt and conspiracy liability, and clarify corporate and officer liability.

**International and duration provisions**

23. **§6, no consequences:** "Shall seek to negotiate" creates no deadline consequence, reporting requirement, or review trigger if negotiations fail. "Equivalent" is undefined. Require reports to Congress at set intervals and a review of domestic provisions if no agreement is reached.

24. **§7(a), joint resolution mechanics:** The bill does not say whether the joint resolution is presented to the President, so the veto and override process is unclear. The "can be developed safely" standard is undefined, and the NAS report has no deadline, scope, or content requirements.

**Missing general provisions**

25. **No rulemaking authority:** The Secretary has no express authority to issue regulations defining terms, creating reporting forms, or updating thresholds. Add it, with limits that prevent weakening the thresholds.

26. **No appropriations or enforcement resources:** The bill does not designate an enforcing agency for §§3–5 or fund enforcement. Add both.

27. **No severability clause:** If a court strikes §2(1)(B) as vague, the Act could fall as a whole. Add a severability clause.

28. **No findings or purpose section:** Findings help courts interpret ambiguous terms and make the Act more defensible against challenge. Add them.

29. **No whistleblower protections or incident reporting:** Employees are often the best source of information about covered training. Add protections for reporting and a duty to report serious incidents.

30. **No preemption or state-law clause:** Without an express statement, states may legislate in this area and litigation over preemption is likely. Decide whether the Act is a floor or a ceiling, and say so.

**Suggested priorities for Thursday's markup**

Items 1, 2, 3, 4, and 7 are the most consequential and the easiest to explain to staff. Items 5, 6, and 10 close the largest practical loopholes. Items 17 and 19 are needed for any enforcement at all. The rest can be handled in a manager's amendment or later drafting. For the constitutional points (7, 9, 11, 12, 27), I'd recommend getting Senate Legislative Counsel's view before we advocate specific language.
