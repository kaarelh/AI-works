RESPONSE:
I reviewed the text as amended on October 6. Items are in section order, followed by cross-cutting problems. Fixes are suggested briefly where obvious.

## Section 2: Definitions

1. **§2(1)(A), static threshold.** The fixed 10^26 figure will be overtaken by algorithmic efficiency and hardware gains, so models with frontier capability will fall below it over time. There is no authority to lower the threshold by rule or any capability-based backstop that works without a government determination.

2. **§2(1)(A), "single training run" is undefined.** A developer can split work into sequential runs, continue pretraining from checkpoints, merge models, or distill a larger model, keeping each run under the line. The bill should count cumulative compute in a model's lineage.

3. **§2(1)(A), what counts as compute.** It is unclear whether RL, post-training, synthetic-data generation, and inference-time compute are included. The bill also gives no method for measuring compute or resolving disputes about it.

4. **§2(1)(A), "model" versus "system."** Prong (A) covers a "model," so a system assembled from several sub-threshold models (ensembles, agent scaffolds, separately trained components) escapes it. Only prong (B) could reach such a system.

5. **§2(1)(B), "the Secretary" is undefined.** The Act never says which Secretary. §4 points to Commerce and §6 to State, so the designating authority is unclear and the designation is open to challenge.

6. **§2(1)(B), vagueness.** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no benchmark, human baseline, elicitation standard, or meaning for "most." With criminal penalties attached, this invites a void-for-vagueness challenge, and developers cannot know in advance whether they are covered.

7. **§2(1)(B), unbounded delegation.** The Secretary alone decides the scope of a criminal prohibition, with no process, criteria, timeline, notice, or review. That is exposed to nondelegation and APA arbitrary-and-capricious challenges, and a determination necessarily comes after the system exists.

8. **§2(1)(B), "most cognitive tasks" is too narrow.** A system that is dramatically superhuman in AI R&D, cyber offense, or bio design but not across "most" tasks falls outside this prong. The title says "Superintelligence," but the term is never defined and the operative text only approximates it.

9. **§2 and §3, missing definitions.** The bill does not define "artificial intelligence" (15 U.S.C. 9401(3) is an existing model), "train," "develop," "deploy," "advanced AI chip," "data center," "operator," "safety evaluation," or "United States" (territories). Each gap is a litigation hook, especially with criminal penalties.

10. **§2(2), "U.S. person" is too narrow.** It omits lawful permanent residents, partnerships, trusts, unincorporated associations, and entities chartered by the federal government, D.C., or territories. It may not clearly cover LLCs, and it excludes foreign-incorporated subsidiaries of U.S. firms, which is the obvious offshoring route.

11. **§2(3), covered facility.** "Advanced AI chip" is undefined and the 50,000 count is static, so chip performance gains erode it. There is no aggregation across sites or affiliates, so distributed training or splitting capacity across entities avoids registration.

12. **§2(3) versus §2(1)(A), mismatched thresholds.** By rough estimate, a 10^26 run needs on the order of 10,000–30,000 current-generation accelerators over several months to a year, well under 50,000 chips. Covered training could therefore happen at unregistered facilities. Please have a technical reviewer check my arithmetic before this goes to the Hill.

## Section 3: Prohibition

13. **§3(a), "train, develop, or deploy."** Without definitions, it is unclear whether "deploy" covers internal use, API access, or open-weight release. "Develop" is broad enough to invite First Amendment challenges as applied to research, code, and publication.

14. **§3(a), no reach to enablers.** The bill does not prohibit supplying compute, financing, hosting, distributing weights, or selling covered systems. It has no anti-circumvention or structuring provision and no express aiding-and-abetting liability for the civil penalty.

15. **§3(a), jurisdictional gaps.** A foreign entity training abroad and serving U.S. users may not be "in the United States," and foreign cloud use by non-U.S. persons is unaddressed. Given the narrow §2(2), foreign subsidiaries of U.S. firms can likely operate outside both prongs.

16. **§3(a), government and contractor activity.** The bill is silent on federal agencies, the military and intelligence community, and their contractors. The Dictionary Act's "person" does not clearly include government bodies, so the scope is ambiguous either way. Staff should decide the intended policy and say it expressly.

17. **§3(b), grandfathering is unlimited.** It exempts the "system" entirely, so a pre-enactment run can continue to any scale, and the system can be fine-tuned, scaled, redeployed, and iterated on indefinitely. There is no compute cap, no definition of when a successor becomes a new system, and no end date.

18. **§3(b), "training began" is unverifiable and gameable.** There is no baseline registry of existing systems or runs. Developers can backdate, start large runs before enactment, or initialize new runs from old weights and claim the earlier start date.

19. **§3(b) with §2(1)(B).** The Secretary's capability determination can never reach a system whose training began before enactment, even if it is clearly superhuman. That removes prong (B) from the most relevant systems, which are the ones that exist today.

20. **§3(c), safety evaluation exemption.** It is purpose-based, self-certified, undefined, and has no compute cap, licensing, oversight, or reporting. Anyone can train or deploy a covered system and call it evaluation, and the exemption does not distinguish evaluating existing systems from building new ones.

## Section 4: Registry

21. **§4(a), mechanics missing.** There is no registration deadline, no definition of "operator," no rulemaking authority, and no required content for registration. Commerce is not told what to do with registrations.

22. **§4(b), reporting is too vague and too slow.** "Computing workloads" is undefined, and reports need not identify customers, models, or compute per run. Quarterly reports mean a covered run can start and finish before anyone sees it.

23. **§4, no enforcement.** §5 penalizes only §3 violations, so failing to register, filing a late report, or filing a false one has no stated consequence. There are also no audit, inspection, subpoena, or record-retention powers, and no know-your-customer duty for compute providers.

24. **§4, no confidentiality protections.** The bill does not address trade secrets or FOIA exemptions for registry data. That invites industry resistance and legal challenge, and it leaves the information at risk of disclosure.

## Section 5: Penalties

25. **§5, no enforcing authority.** The bill does not name an agency or give the Attorney General authority to bring civil actions. It provides no administrative adjudication process, no injunctive relief to halt a run in progress, and no whistleblower or private-enforcement mechanism.

26. **§5(a), civil penalty is weak and ambiguous.** The $1M/day cap (roughly $365M a year) is small next to frontier training and deployment budgets, and "not more than" sets no floor. "Each day on which the violation occurs" does not say how a continuous training run or ongoing deployment is counted.

27. **§5(b), "willfully" and "fined under title 18."** The bill does not say what must be willful: knowledge of the compute count, of the system's capabilities, or of the designation. Under 18 U.S.C. 3571 (please have counsel verify), organizational felony fines are generally capped at $500,000 unless the alternative gain-or-loss fine is invoked, which is a trivial deterrent.

28. **§5, no individual or officer liability.** There is no provision for responsible corporate officers or for directors who direct or knowingly permit violations, so liability can stop at the corporate shell.

29. **§5, no remedies that end the violation.** There is no forfeiture, seizure, or disgorgement authority and no power to order deletion or secure custody of weights, halt a run, or restrict chips. Fines alone let a company pay and continue.

30. **§5(a) versus §8, date conflict.** §5(a) imposes penalties for violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §8 makes the Act effective 180 days later. A court could read this as creating liability before the prohibition takes effect, or as making the penalty provision inoperative in that window.

31. **§5, no whistleblower protection.** Insider reporting is the most likely way violations are detected. The bill has no anti-retaliation provision, confidential-reporting channel, or award.

32. **§3(a) and §5(b), extraterritoriality.** "U.S. person anywhere" helps, but there is no explicit statement of extraterritorial criminal application or of investigative tools abroad. The presumption against extraterritoriality gives defendants an argument.

## Sections 6–8

33. **§6, international agreement is toothless and unclear.** "Shall seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months" does not say whether the deadline applies to starting talks, concluding them, or ratifying. "Equivalent" prohibitions are undefined, and there are no reporting duties, appropriations, verification, or consequences for failure. The section also lacks tools to pressure non-participants, such as export controls on chips or cloud access.

34. **§7(a) versus §7(b), internal contradiction.** §7(a) keeps the prohibition until Congress finds that covered systems can be developed safely, but §7(b) ends the whole Act after 10 years regardless. The sunset makes (a) largely meaningless and gives industry a date to wait out. Consider deleting (b) or replacing it with a mandatory review.

35. **§7(b), no savings clause.** The bill does not preserve enforcement of pre-sunset violations, pending actions, or penalties, and the registry also lapses. "Cease to have effect" is not clearly a repeal, so whether 1 U.S.C. 109 saves penalties is uncertain.

36. **§7(a), release standard is undefined.** "Can be developed safely" has no criteria. The bill sets no NASEM deadline, funding, scope, or requirement for a favorable report, and NASEM is a private body not obligated to act. The finding is also all-or-nothing, with no way to release some systems or uses.

37. **§8, 180-day delay creates a race window.** Runs begun after enactment but before the effective date are not grandfathered, yet they are not barred until day 180, and no notice or reporting applies in the interim. The bill also does not say whether such runs must stop or may finish. This is a strong incentive to start large runs immediately.

## Cross-cutting

38. **No findings or constitutional basis.** The bill has no findings on interstate commerce, national security, or the risks that justify the prohibition. That weakens its defense against Commerce Clause and First Amendment challenges (code and research as speech). Tailoring the prohibition to conduct, with findings, would help.

39. **No severability clause.** If a court strikes down one provision, such as the §2(1)(B) delegation, the whole Act could fall with it.

40. **No judicial review, venue, or procedure for designations.** The bill sets no notice-and-comment process, no deadline for the Secretary to act, no standing or venue rules, and no relationship to state law (preemption or savings) or to existing export-control authorities.

41. **No implementing authority or funding.** The bill has no appropriations and no staffing for Commerce or any other agency. It also lacks general rulemaking authority, so the registry and enforcement may exist only on paper.

## Suggested priorities

I would lead with these, because they most directly let companies continue or make the bill easy to knock down:
- **Open loopholes:** #17–#20 (grandfathering and the safety-evaluation exemption), #1–#4 (threshold evasion), #10 and #15 (offshoring), #37 (race window).
- **Enforceability:** #25–#29, #23, and #12.
- **Legal vulnerability:** #5–#7 (undefined Secretary, vagueness, delegation), #34 (the §7 contradiction), #30 (date conflict), #38 and #39.
