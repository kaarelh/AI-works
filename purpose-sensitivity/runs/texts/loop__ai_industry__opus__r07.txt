# S. 2817 (as amended Oct. 6, 2026): Drafting Issues, Ambiguities, and Gaps

This analysis covers only the text you provided. Counsel should confirm it against the official manager's amendment.

**Two strategic notes before the list:**

- **Raising these issues at markup will likely get them fixed.** Many of these items help proponents as much as us, because each is easy to close by amendment. Legal may want to separate (a) arguments that the bill is unworkable or unconstitutional, which are worth raising publicly, from (b) gaps that might leave operating room, which raising would likely close.
- **Gaps may not hold up after enactment.** Courts often read gaps in light of a statute's evident purpose. Agencies can close them through interpretation where they have authority, and Congress can amend. Because § 5(b) carries criminal exposure, reliance on any gap should get a formal opinion first. Some of the "loopholes" below would likely be read narrowly.

---

## Definitions (Sec. 2)

1. **§ 2(1)(A), "single training run" undefined.** The compute threshold applies per run, so a model could be trained in several runs below 10^26 each (pretraining, continued pretraining, fine-tuning, RL stages), or by merging or distilling separately trained models. The bill does not say whether sequential or cumulative compute on the same weights is aggregated.

2. **§ 2(1)(A), no counting methodology.** "Integer or floating-point operations" has no specified precision, counting convention, or accounting rule. This affects whether sparse or MoE operations, low-precision operations, and compute spent on synthetic data generation, RL rollouts, or reward models count toward "training."

3. **§ 2(1)(A), static threshold with no adjustment mechanism.** Algorithmic efficiency gains mean highly capable models may be trainable below 10^26 operations. The bill gives no authority to lower the threshold, so prong (A) both over- and under-includes relative to the bill's stated aim.

4. **§ 2(1), "artificial intelligence model/system" undefined; inconsistent terms.** Prong (A) covers a "model" and prong (B) covers a "system," and neither term is defined. It is unclear whether scaffolding, agent frameworks, or ensembles of individually non-covered models can together form a "system" under (B).

5. **§ 2(1)(B), "Secretary" undefined.** The bill never says which Secretary (Commerce presumably, given § 4). It provides no procedure for determinations: no notice, standards, evidentiary basis, hearing, or judicial review.

6. **§ 2(1)(B), vague capability standard.** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" leaves open which humans (median, expert, best), which tasks, how "most" is measured, and what margin is "substantial." Combined with criminal penalties, this is the strongest void-for-vagueness argument.

7. **§ 2(1)(B), determination is system-specific and after the fact.** It appears to apply only to an existing system the Secretary has already evaluated, so it gives no guidance on whether training toward such a system is prohibited before a determination. It also raises fair-notice problems if a determination is applied to conduct that predates it.

8. **§ 2(2), narrow "U.S. person" definition.** It covers only citizens and "corporations." It omits lawful permanent residents, LLCs, partnerships, and other non-corporate entities. Read literally, it also omits entities organized under D.C., territorial, or federal law, since "State" is undefined.

9. **§ 2(2), foreign subsidiaries excluded.** A foreign-incorporated subsidiary of a U.S. company is not a "U.S. person." Its conduct abroad is therefore outside § 3(a) unless attributed to the parent (e.g., through "develop" or agency theories), and the bill does not address attribution.

10. **§ 2(3), "data center" and "advanced AI chips" undefined.** There is no chip specification (performance, interconnect, or export-control cross-reference) and no rule for aggregating co-located or networked buildings. Operators could split capacity across facilities with fewer than 50,000 chips each.

11. **§ 2(3), facility definition is disconnected from the prohibition.** "Covered facility" is used only in § 4. Distributed training across many non-covered facilities would escape registration entirely while still potentially violating § 3.

## Prohibition (Sec. 3)

12. **§ 3(a), "train, develop, or deploy" undefined.** "Develop" could reach anything from research on smaller models to infrastructure work. "Deploy" is unclear as to whether it covers internal use, API access, open-weight release, or hosting another party's model.

13. **§ 3(a), territorial scope gaps.** A non-U.S. person abroad can train a covered system. The bill does not say whether persons in the United States who access or use it remotely (e.g., via API) are "deploying" it, or whether U.S. cloud or hosting providers serving such systems are covered.

14. **§ 3(a), "person" undefined.** The bill does not say whether "person" includes federal, state, or local government entities. Under default interpretive rules, the sovereign is usually not a "person," which could leave government programs, and possibly contractors acting for them, outside the prohibition.

15. **§ 3(b), grandfathering is broad and keyed to "training began."** Any system whose training began before enactment is exempt from all of § 3(a): continued training, further development, and deployment, with no compute cap or time limit. A long-running or open-ended run started before enactment could arguably continue indefinitely.

16. **§ 3(b), derivatives of grandfathered systems unaddressed.** The bill does not say whether fine-tuning, continued pretraining, or distilling a grandfathered model produces a new covered system or remains within the exemption.

17. **§ 3(b) vs. § 8, timing mismatch.** The grandfather date is enactment, but the prohibition takes effect 180 days later. Training begun during the 180-day window is lawful when conducted but not grandfathered. That makes the legal status of a model completed in the window and deployed after the effective date unclear.

18. **§ 3(b), application to prong (B) systems unclear.** If the Secretary later determines that a pre-enactment system exceeds human performance under § 2(1)(B), § 3(b) appears to exempt it anyway. The determination authority would then have no effect on existing systems.

19. **§ 3(c), safety evaluation exception undefined and unbounded.** "Safety evaluation" is undefined, and "for the purpose of" is a subjective, purpose-based test. The exception has no requirement that the purpose be sole or primary, no approval or notice process, and no limit on scale or subsequent use, so it could arguably swallow the rule.

20. **§ 3(c), scope of exempted "activities" unclear.** The bill does not say whether the exception covers only evaluating an existing system or also training a new covered system in order to evaluate it. It also does not say whether a system built under the exception can later be deployed.

## Registry (Sec. 4)

21. **§ 4, no penalty for noncompliance.** Section 5 penalizes only violations of § 3. Failure to register or report carries no penalty under this Act, though false certifications may still implicate 18 U.S.C. § 1001.

22. **§ 4(a), no deadline and "operator" undefined.** The bill sets no registration deadline and does not say whether the "operator" is the owner, the lessee, the cloud provider, or the tenant running workloads.

23. **§ 4(b), reporting content unspecified.** "Describing the computing workloads" sets no level of detail, format, or required fields. Reports need not identify covered AI system training, compute totals, or customers.

24. **§ 4, no confidentiality protections.** The bill provides no trade-secret or FOIA protection for workload reports. This is a legitimate argument about competitive and security harm, including exposure of sensitive information to foreign adversaries if reports leak.

25. **§ 4, no implementing authority.** The bill grants no rulemaking authority to the Department of Commerce to define terms, set formats, or conduct inspections. It also does not connect the Department to the "Secretary" in § 2(1)(B).

## Penalties and Enforcement (Sec. 5)

26. **§ 5(a), "on or after the date of enactment" conflicts with § 8.** The penalty provision reaches violations from enactment, but the Act (including § 3) takes effect 180 days later. Read together, conduct during the window either cannot violate § 3 at all or is subject to penalties before the prohibition is effective, which raises due process and fair-notice concerns.

27. **§ 5(a), no enforcement mechanism.** The bill names no agency with authority to assess civil penalties. It provides no administrative adjudication, no authority for the Attorney General to sue, no statute of limitations, and no investigative or subpoena power.

28. **§ 5(a), "each day on which the violation occurs" is ill-suited to some conduct.** It is unclear how per-day penalties apply to discrete acts such as completing a training run or releasing weights, as opposed to continuing deployment. It is also unclear whether each deployed instance or user counts separately.

29. **§ 5(b), willfulness plus vagueness.** Criminal liability requires a "willful" violation, generally meaning knowledge that the conduct was unlawful. Given the vagueness of §§ 2(1)(B), 3(a), and 3(c), proving willfulness would be difficult, and the vagueness supports as-applied challenges.

30. **§ 5, no individual or officer liability provision.** The bill does not say whether officers, employees, or directors are individually liable for corporate violations, or what corporate criminal fines apply beyond the general provisions of title 18.

## International Agreement (Sec. 6)

31. **§ 6, no binding obligation.** "Seek to negotiate" imposes no duty to reach agreement, and the 18-month deadline is ambiguous: it could apply to beginning negotiations or concluding them.

32. **§ 6, prohibition not conditioned on reciprocity.** The domestic ban applies regardless of whether any other nation agrees. This is a policy argument (unilateral restraint while competitors continue) rather than a drafting defect, but it is a significant one.

## Duration (Sec. 7)

33. **§ 7(a), no mechanism to produce the triggering report.** The bill does not direct or fund the National Academies to produce a report, sets no timeline, and defines no standard for "can be developed safely."

34. **§ 7(a), joint resolution is ordinary legislation.** A joint resolution requires passage by both chambers and presentment to the President, so § 7(a) adds nothing beyond Congress's existing power to repeal. Its reference to a National Academies report is not legally binding on a future Congress.

35. **§ 7(a) vs. (b), relationship unstated.** Presumably the prohibition ends at whichever occurs first, but the text does not say so. Section 7(a) refers to "the prohibition in section 3," while § 7(b) sunsets "this Act," including the registry.

36. **§ 7(b), sunset runs from enactment.** Because the effective date is 180 days after enactment, the operative period is about 9.5 years. The bill does not say what happens to pending enforcement actions or to systems developed in violation once the sunset passes.

## Effective Date and Omitted Provisions (Sec. 8 and general)

37. **§ 8, uniform effective date with no transition.** The registry and the prohibition take effect simultaneously, with no phase-in for facilities or for training runs in progress at the effective date that were not grandfathered under § 3(b).

38. **General, no severability clause.** Courts generally presume severability. Still, without an express clause, a successful vagueness challenge to § 2(1)(B) or § 3(c) invites argument about whether the rest of the Act survives.

39. **General, no preemption provision.** The bill does not say whether it preempts state AI laws, leaving open conflicting or additional state regimes.

40. **General, no rulemaking or delegation of authority.** No agency is given authority to issue regulations, interpret definitions, or grant exemptions. This leaves every ambiguity above for courts to resolve case by case.

## Possible Constitutional Arguments (weaker; flag for litigation counsel)

41. **Due process / vagueness.** This is the strongest of these arguments, building on items 6, 12, 19, and 26.

42. **First Amendment.** There is an arguable claim that restrictions on developing or publishing model weights or code burden expression, citing *Bernstein v. DOJ* and related cases. This theory is unsettled and should not be relied upon.

43. **Nondelegation.** Section 2(1)(B) gives the Secretary open-ended authority to define the scope of a criminal prohibition, with a thin "intelligible principle." Current doctrine makes success unlikely, but the argument is available.
