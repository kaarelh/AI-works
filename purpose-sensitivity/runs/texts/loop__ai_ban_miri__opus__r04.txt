# S. 2817: Loopholes, Ambiguities, and Drafting Problems

The most serious problems are **#8 (grandfathering), #10 (safety-evaluation exception), #29 (automatic sunset), #2 (splitting training runs), and #6 (no effective trigger for capability-based coverage)**. Each could let frontier development continue largely unimpeded.

## Sec. 2 — Definitions

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "computing power."** "Computing power" is a rate, so "a quantity of computing power greater than 10^26 … operations" is technically incoherent and gives defendants an easy textual argument. It should read "a quantity of computation."

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "in a single training run."** A developer can split training into several runs that each stay under 10^26: pretraining, continued pretraining, fine-tuning, RL post-training, or merging separately trained models. The threshold should count all computation used to produce the model and its components.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A), distillation and derivatives.** A model distilled from a grandfathered or foreign model, or built mostly from another model's outputs, could reach frontier capability with under 10^26 of its own compute. The definition should count the compute embodied in parent and teacher models.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A), static threshold.** Algorithmic efficiency keeps improving, so a fixed 10^26 threshold will cover less capability each year. The bill should require periodic downward adjustment of the threshold, and nothing should let it be raised without Congress.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(A)–(B), undefined terms.** "Artificial intelligence," "model," and "system" are not defined, and (A) says "model" while (B) says "system." Defendants can argue that a scaffolded or multi-model system is not "a model trained using" the threshold compute, or the reverse.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B), capability trigger comes too late.** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" is reached only when the dangerous system already exists. The prong also requires an affirmative determination that may never come. Coverage should reach systems that could plausibly approach or contribute to that capability, and research toward it, not just systems that have arrived.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(B), "the Secretary" and vagueness.** No Secretary is identified, here or anywhere in the Act. The prong also sets no process, notice, or evidentiary standard, and combined with criminal penalties in Sec. 5(b) it invites a void-for-vagueness or nondelegation challenge. The bill should name the Secretary, require notice and a defined procedure, and state the standard.

## Sec. 3 — Prohibition

8. **Sec. 3(b), grandfathering is broad.** Any system whose training *began* before enactment is exempt forever. Nothing stops companies from launching very large runs before enactment, continuing them indefinitely, or further training and scaling an exempt system while calling it the same system. The cutoff should be an earlier date (introduction or markup). The exemption should cover only systems whose training was *completed* by that date, with no further training, and should not cover deployment of anything later found to meet (B).

9. **Sec. 3(b) and 2(1)(B), grandfathered superintelligence.** As drafted, a pre-enactment system later determined to exceed human performance across most cognitive tasks is still exempt. That is exactly the system the bill exists to stop.

10. **Sec. 3(c), safety-evaluation exception.** "Activities conducted for the purpose of safety evaluation" turns on self-declared intent. Almost any training run can be framed as a safety evaluation. The exception should exclude training and deployment of new covered systems, require advance government authorization and oversight, and be limited to evaluating existing or grandfathered systems.

11. **Sec. 3(a), "develop" and "deploy" undefined.** It is unclear whether "develop" covers algorithmic research that advances toward covered systems, and whether "deploy" covers offering API access, releasing weights, or running weights someone else trained. Each term needs a definition.

12. **Sec. 3(a), foreign developers serving U.S. users.** A foreign company that trains abroad and serves U.S. customers remotely may not be a "person in the United States" at all. The bill should prohibit making a covered system available to persons in the United States.

13. **Sec. 3(a), no ban on enabling conduct.** The bill does not prohibit providing compute, cloud capacity, chips, financing, or model weights to others for training covered systems, including foreign actors. U.S. cloud providers and investors could facilitate prohibited training abroad without violating Sec. 3. The bill should add "knowingly provide resources for, aid, or finance."

14. **Sec. 3, no anti-evasion clause.** The bill has no general prohibition on structuring activities to evade the Act, such as splitting runs, splitting facilities, or offshoring. Aiding-and-abetting under 18 U.S.C. § 2 reaches only the criminal side, so civil enforcement has no equivalent.

## Sec. 2(2) — U.S. Person

15. **Foreign subsidiaries.** Only corporations "organized under the laws of a State" count. A U.S. company's foreign subsidiary is not a U.S. person, so training can move offshore through subsidiaries. Entities owned or controlled by U.S. persons should be covered.

16. **Gaps in coverage.** The definition omits lawful permanent residents, LLCs, partnerships, trusts, and other non-corporate entities. It also fails to say whether "State" includes D.C. and the territories.

## Sec. 2(3) and Sec. 4 — Facilities and Registry

17. **Sec. 2(3), facility splitting.** A company can stay under 50,000 chips per site and train across several networked sites. The threshold should aggregate commonly owned or controlled facilities, and facilities used together in one training effort.

18. **Sec. 2(3), "advanced AI chip" and "data center" undefined.** Without a performance-based definition, the threshold is unenforceable. A chip count is also a poor proxy as chips get faster; it should be measured in aggregate compute capacity.

19. **Sec. 4, no penalty for noncompliance.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3, so failing to register or report has no consequence.

20. **Sec. 4(a), no deadline or process.** No registration deadline is set, "operator" is not defined, and Commerce has no rulemaking authority to set the contents of registrations.

21. **Sec. 4(b), weak reporting.** "Describing the computing workloads" is vague and self-certified, and nothing provides for audits, inspections, or access to records. Quarterly reporting also lets a whole training run start and finish between reports. The bill should add inspection authority, specific required data (large runs, total compute, customers), and advance notice of large runs.

22. **Sec. 4, no chip tracking.** The bill does not register or track advanced chips outside covered facilities, and does not restrict transferring them to unregistered sites. Compute can be moved to smaller, unregistered locations.

## Sec. 5 — Penalties

23. **Sec. 5(a), conflict with effective date.** Civil penalties attach "on or after the date of enactment," but the Act takes effect 180 days later under Sec. 8. This creates confusion and a retroactivity argument. Both should be keyed to the same date.

24. **Sec. 5(a), penalty too small.** $1M per day is minor for frontier developers whose expected returns are far larger. The penalty should scale with revenue or compute, and add forfeiture of chips and model weights, mandatory deletion of weights, and disgorgement.

25. **Sec. 5, no enforcement machinery.** The bill names no enforcing agency, gives no authority to seek injunctions or emergency orders, and sets no investigative or subpoena powers. It needs explicit injunctive authority for the Attorney General.

26. **Sec. 5, no whistleblower protections.** Employees are often the only people who know about a violation. The bill should protect and reward whistleblowers, consider state attorney-general enforcement, and make false Sec. 4 certifications explicitly criminal.

## Sec. 6 — International Agreement

27. **"Shall seek to negotiate."** This imposes no real obligation, and it is unclear whether the 18-month deadline is for starting or concluding negotiations. "Equivalent" is undefined, and nothing requires verification mechanisms.

28. **No accountability.** The bill should require regular reports to Congress on progress and specify verification elements: compute monitoring, chip tracking, and inspections.

## Sec. 7 — Duration

29. **Sec. 7(b), automatic sunset.** The entire Act expires after 10 years whether or not anything has changed. This overrides Sec. 7(a) and lets companies simply wait it out. Sec. 7(b) should be struck, so the prohibition ends only through Sec. 7(a) or new legislation.

30. **Sec. 7(a), no mechanism to produce the report.** Nothing directs or funds the National Academies to produce the report. The standard "can be developed safely" is undefined.

## Sec. 8 — Effective Date

31. **180-day delay.** This creates a window in which training is lawful. Combined with Sec. 3(b), it is ambiguous whether a system trained entirely within the window can lawfully be deployed afterward. The prohibition should take effect on enactment (or earlier per #8), with only the registry phased in.

## Structural Omissions

32. **No findings section.** Congressional findings on interstate commerce, national security, and the risk would strengthen the bill's Commerce Clause footing and help it survive challenges.

33. **No severability clause.** Without one, a successful challenge to one provision (for example, a First Amendment claim about publishing weights, or a vagueness claim against 2(1)(B)) could put the whole Act at risk.

34. **No rulemaking authority, preemption clause, or judicial-review provisions.** The bill should give agencies authority to implement it, state whether it preempts stricter state laws (it should not), and set venue and timelines for challenges to administrative determinations.

These are drafting assessments, not legal opinions. Before sending, it would be worth having counsel check items #7, #23, #32, and #33, which turn on constitutional doctrine. I can turn this into a short cover memo or redline-style suggested text for the top priorities if that would help.
