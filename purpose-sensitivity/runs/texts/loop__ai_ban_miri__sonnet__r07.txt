# S. 2817 drafting review (text as amended Oct. 6, 2026)

Items are in section order, then cross-cutting issues. Top priorities are listed at the end.

## Section 2: Definitions

1. **§2(1)(A), fixed compute threshold.** A hard-coded 10^26 operations won't track algorithmic efficiency gains, so systems with dangerous capabilities will eventually fall below it. The bill gives no authority to lower the threshold by rule.

2. **§2(1)(A), "single training run" is undefined.** Developers can split work into staged runs, continued pretraining, RL/post-training, distillation, or model merging, each below the threshold. The definition should count cumulative compute across a model's lineage, including inherited and fine-tuning compute.

3. **§2(1)(A), no measurement method.** Nothing says how compute is calculated or certified (estimated vs. metered, treatment of failed or restarted runs, synthetic data generated by other models). Disputes over whether a run crossed the line would be hard to resolve.

4. **§2(1)(A) vs. (B), "model" vs. "system."** Clause (A) reaches a trained model, so multi-model ensembles, agent scaffolds, and systems that gain capability from large inference-time compute can fall outside it. They are reachable only through the discretionary clause (B).

5. **§2(1)(B), "the Secretary" is undefined.** The Act never says which Secretary. §6 specifies "Secretary of State," which implies the others are different officers.

6. **§2(1)(B), vague capability standard.** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no benchmark, baseline population, or method for measuring it. That invites a void-for-vagueness challenge, especially with criminal penalties attached.

7. **§2(1)(B), no determination procedure.** There is no rulemaking, notice, publication, deadline, or judicial review. A determination can only be made after the capability exists, so it cannot prevent training, and a developer can claim it lacked fair notice.

8. **§2(2), "U.S. person" is too narrow.** It omits LLCs, partnerships, nonprofits, unincorporated associations, lawful permanent residents abroad, entities organized in D.C. or territories, and foreign subsidiaries of U.S. firms. A company could shift its work to a foreign-organized affiliate.

9. **§2 generally, "person" is undefined and government actors are unaddressed.** If the default statutory definition applies (counsel should confirm), it likely excludes federal and state governments. Whether military and intelligence programs are covered is then ambiguous.

10. **§2(3), "advanced AI chip" is undefined.** The term needs a technical definition, such as a performance or export-control classification, that Commerce can update by rule. "Data center" and "operator" are also undefined.

11. **§2(3), chip count is a poor proxy and easy to evade.** As a rough estimate, a 10^26 run needs about 8,000 H100-class chip-years, so about 16,000 chips for six months, or fewer with newer hardware. Facilities well under 50,000 chips can do it, and splitting capacity across sites, affiliates, or leased clusters avoids the threshold. Use aggregate compute capacity with affiliate and network aggregation.

## Section 3: Prohibition

12. **§3(a), "train, develop, or deploy" is undefined.** "Develop" could be read to sweep in general research or publishing code, raising First Amendment and vagueness challenges. "Deploy" is unclear for API access, internal use, and open-weights release.

13. **§3(a), conduct is missing from the prohibition.** Nothing covers possessing, hosting, selling, funding, exporting weights, or supplying compute to a prohibited run. There is also no civil aiding-and-abetting, attempt, or conspiracy liability, and no cloud-provider duty.

14. **§3(a), in-progress runs.** A run that starts below 10^26 but is planned to exceed it may not violate the Act until it crosses the line. Prohibit planning, attempting, or knowingly configuring a run to exceed the threshold.

15. **§3(a), offshore gaps.** Foreign persons abroad can train using U.S.-based cloud or chips, and the Act doesn't say where an online service is "deployed." That leaves jurisdiction uncertain.

16. **§3(b), grandfathering rewards a pre-enactment rush.** Any run begun before enactment is exempt, with no cap on scale, duration, or completion date. There is also no registration or verification of start dates.

17. **§3(b), the exemption has no limits on what grandfathered systems may do.** It appears to permit indefinite continued training, fine-tuning, scaling, deployment, and use of these systems to build successors, and "a covered AI system" is ambiguous when a model is substantially retrained. It should freeze further training and cap compute, scale, and use.

18. **§3(b) with §8, the 180-day gap.** The grandfather cutoff is enactment, but the ban starts 180 days later. A run begun in between is neither exempt nor prohibited, and the Act doesn't say whether it becomes unlawful on day 181. This creates a rush window and a retroactivity dispute.

19. **§3(c), the safety evaluation exemption could swallow the Act.** It exempts any activity "for the purpose of" safety evaluation from the entire prohibition, including training. A lab could describe frontier training as evaluation. It should be limited to evaluating existing or grandfathered systems, under government approval, with no training of new covered systems.

20. **§3(c), a subjective purpose test.** Dual-purpose activity is hard to disprove, and the bill puts the burden on enforcers. Make it an affirmative defense with a documentation and advance-notice requirement.

## Section 4: Registry

21. **§4, no enforcement for registry violations.** §5 penalizes only violations of §3, so failing to register, filing late, or filing inaccurate reports carries no stated consequence. Any false-statement liability would rest on general law.

22. **§4(a)–(b), no deadlines.** There is no date for initial registration or the first report, and no tie to the effective date.

23. **§4(b), report contents are vague.** "Describing the computing workloads" doesn't require chip counts, run size, FLOP, model identity, start dates, or customer identity. Quarterly reporting is also too slow to catch a multi-month run, so require advance notice of large runs.

24. **§4, no verification powers.** Reports are self-certified, with no audit, inspection, subpoena, or data-access authority. There is also no whistleblower protection or reward.

25. **§4, the registry tracks facilities, not runs or customers.** Nothing covers registering training runs or systems, cloud tenant identity (know-your-customer rules), or information sharing with DOJ.

26. **§4, scope and confidentiality.** The Act doesn't say whether it applies only to U.S. facilities or to foreign facilities of U.S. persons. It also doesn't say how trade secrets in reports are protected, which invites disclosure and takings disputes.

## Section 5: Penalties

27. **§5(a), no enforcing authority.** The Act never says who may bring a civil action or impose the penalty. It also provides no injunctive or cease-and-desist authority, administrative process, or citizen suit.

28. **§5(a), the penalty is weak.** A cap of $1M per day is small next to multi-billion-dollar training runs, and there is no floor, revenue scaling, or disgorgement. There are also no remedies such as forfeiture of chips or deletion of weights.

29. **§5(b), "fined under title 18."** Counsel should verify, but the default organizational felony fine cap (18 U.S.C. §3571) is $500,000 unless the gain-based alternative applies. State a much larger fine for organizations.

30. **§5(b), "willfully" is hard to prove.** Combined with the vague §2(1)(B) standard, a defendant can argue it didn't know a system was covered. Define the knowledge element and address responsible officers and conspiracy.

31. **§5(a), retroactivity conflict.** Penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §8 delays the Act's effect 180 days. The conflict invites fair-notice and due process challenges, and conduct in the gap is unclear.

32. **§5(a), "each day" is ambiguous.** The Act doesn't say whether training or deployment is a continuing violation, or how days are counted for multiple systems.

## Sections 6–8

33. **§6, the deadline and duty are ambiguous.** "Shall seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months" could modify the seeking or the agreement, and "seek" is hortatory. There is no reporting to Congress, milestones, or consequence for failure.

34. **§6, "equivalent prohibitions" imports §3's loopholes.** It would carry over the §3(b) and §3(c) exemptions and says nothing about verification, compute monitoring, sanctions, or export-control leverage. It also gives no role to Commerce, Defense, or the intelligence community.

35. **§7(a), no workable off-ramp standard.** "Can be developed safely" is undefined, and the NASEM report has no mandate, deadline, funding, or criteria. The bill doesn't say whether the report must be favorable or what "finds" does legally, leaving a gap when the finding is made.

36. **§7(b) contradicts §7(a).** The 10-year sunset ends the prohibition regardless of any safety finding, so companies can wait it out and stockpile compute. With the 180-day delay, the effective prohibition lasts about 9.5 years. Replace it with periodic review or require affirmative reauthorization.

37. **§7, no savings clause.** Nothing preserves enforcement of violations that occurred before the Act expires. Add one explicitly rather than relying on general saving statutes, which may not clearly cover sunset expiration.

38. **§8, no interim measures.** A blanket 180-day delay postpones the registry and rulemaking too. Make the registry, rulemaking, and anti-evasion provisions effective on enactment.

## Cross-cutting

39. **No severability clause.** A successful challenge to one provision, such as the vague §2(1)(B), could jeopardize the whole Act.

40. **No implementing agency, rulemaking authority, or appropriations.** The Act should designate Commerce (or another agency), grant rulemaking power over definitions and thresholds, and authorize funding.

41. **No preemption language.** It's unclear whether stricter state laws survive, which invites preemption litigation. State that the Act sets a floor.

42. **No congressional findings or jurisdictional hooks.** Findings tying the prohibition to interstate commerce and national security would strengthen the bill against Commerce Clause and due process challenges, particularly for purely intrastate training.

## Top priorities for the Whitfield memo

- §3(c): the safety evaluation exemption
- §3(b) with §8: the grandfathering and 180-day window
- §7(b): the sunset
- §5: no designated enforcer, a weak penalty, and the retroactivity conflict
- §2(1)(B): the vague standard and the undefined "Secretary"
- §2(3) and §4: the chip threshold and the unenforced, unverified registry
- §2(2): the narrow definition of "U.S. person"
