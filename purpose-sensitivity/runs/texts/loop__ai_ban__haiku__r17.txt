RESPONSE:
# Drafting issues in S. 2817 (October 6 manager's amendment text)

I'm working from the text you pasted. I haven't seen the full amendment, the reported bill, or any legislative history, so check cross-references against the official text and have Senate legislative counsel review the list before it goes to Senator Whitfield's office. Items marked ★ are the most urgent.

## Most urgent

1. ★ **Sec. 7(a) vs. Sec. 7(b).** Section 7(a) keeps the prohibition in effect until Congress finds covered systems can be developed safely, but Section 7(b) terminates the entire Act after 10 years regardless. Developers could simply wait out the sunset, and a court could read the sunset as overriding the safety-finding condition. Fix: delete the sunset or make it inoperative unless the Section 7(a) finding has been made.

2. ★ **Sec. 3(b), grandfathering.** Exempting any system "whose training began before the date of enactment" from all of Section 3 means already-trained frontier models could be deployed and further developed indefinitely. Fix: limit the exemption to pre-enactment training and require registration, a deployment freeze, or a hard deadline for existing systems.

3. ★ **Sec. 3(c), safety-evaluation exemption.** "Activities conducted for the purpose of safety evaluation" has no definition, notice requirement, scope limit, or time limit, so any large training run could be labeled an evaluation. Fix: require advance notice to Commerce, independent oversight, containment, a bar on deploying evaluated models, and a time limit.

4. ★ **Sec. 2(1)(A), "single training run."** Developers can split a training program into sequential stages (pretraining, fine-tuning, RL, continued training) so that no single run exceeds 10^26 operations. Fix: measure cumulative compute for a model and its successors across all stages.

5. ★ **No enforcement authority.** Section 5 doesn't say which agency or official brings civil actions, and it doesn't authorize injunctions, shutdown orders, or destruction of weights. Fix: designate the enforcing agency and grant express injunctive and remedial authority.

6. ★ **Sec. 4, no penalty for noncompliance with the registry.** Section 5 penalizes only violations of Section 3, so failing to register or report carries no consequence. Fix: add civil and criminal penalties for registry violations.

## Definitions (Sec. 2)

7. **Sec. 2(1)(A), compute measurement.** The bill doesn't say how to count "integer or floating-point operations," including whether to use dense or sparse counts, precision adjustments, or a standard methodology. Developers could argue for counts that keep them under the line. Fix: specify a measurement method and give the Secretary authority to update it.

8. **Sec. 2(1)(A), distillation and derivative models.** A sub-threshold "student" model trained on a covered "teacher" model is not covered on its own terms. Fix: extend coverage to models trained substantially on covered systems.

9. **Sec. 2(1)(A), the threshold cannot be updated.** A fixed 10^26 figure may become obsolete as algorithms improve, and the bill gives no authority to adjust it. Fix: give the Secretary rulemaking authority with a floor that cannot be raised.

10. **Sec. 2(1)(B), undefined standard.** "Capable of substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" lacks any benchmark, and "capable" is unclear (demonstrated performance or potential). This invites vagueness challenges and makes compliance unpredictable.

11. **Sec. 2(1)(B), no determination process.** The bill doesn't say how the Secretary makes this determination, what notice or hearing is required, what evidence is needed, or whether the determination is reviewable. A determination made without procedure is easy to challenge under the APA. Fix: add notice, comment, and a reasoned record.

12. **Sec. 2(1)(B), "the Secretary" is undefined.** The bill never says which Secretary is meant. Sections 4 and 6 refer to Commerce and State, so the reference is ambiguous.

13. **Sec. 2(1)(B), nondelegation.** Leaving the core coverage question to executive determination with no intelligible principle beyond the wording may invite a nondelegation challenge. Fix: state the principle more precisely or set out procedural guardrails.

14. **Sec. 2(2), U.S. person.** Limiting "U.S. person" to citizens and state-law corporations excludes LLCs, partnerships, federally chartered entities, and lawful permanent residents. Many AI companies or their operating entities could fall outside the definition.

15. **Sec. 2(2), foreign subsidiaries.** A U.S. parent can train through a foreign-incorporated subsidiary and fall outside both the training prohibition and the definition of U.S. person. Fix: cover entities controlled by U.S. persons.

16. **Sec. 2, "State" is undefined.** It's unclear whether the term includes D.C., territories, or the Commonwealth, which affects the U.S. person definition.

17. **Sec. 2(3), "advanced AI chip" and "data center" are undefined.** The registry depends on both terms, and neither is defined, so operators can argue their facilities or chips fall outside the definitions.

18. **Sec. 2(3), facility-level threshold.** Training distributed across several sites, each under 50,000 chips, avoids registration entirely. Cloud-rented compute raises the same problem. Fix: aggregate capacity across an operator's facilities, or base the threshold on the training run rather than the facility.

## Prohibition (Sec. 3)

19. **Sec. 3(a), undefined verbs.** "Train, develop, or deploy" is not defined. It's unclear whether "develop" covers research, architecture work, or AI-driven research, and whether "deploy" covers internal use.

20. **Sec. 3(a), internal deployment.** Frontier labs mainly use models internally, including for AI research, and the bill doesn't say whether that counts as deployment. This is the most consequential ambiguity in the prohibition.

21. **Sec. 3(a), open-weight release.** The bill doesn't address publishing or releasing model weights, which is a direct route to proliferation. Fix: prohibit making covered weights publicly available.

22. **Sec. 3(a), no bar on facilitation.** The bill doesn't prohibit providing compute, chips, weights, financing, or other support to covered development, and it doesn't address attempts or conspiracy. Fix: add an aiding-and-abetting provision and cover compute and chip providers.

23. **Sec. 3(a), extraterritorial gap.** Foreign persons training abroad, and foreign entities outside U.S. control, are outside the training prohibition. Section 6 treaty language is too weak to close this gap alone.

24. **Sec. 3(a), breadth.** "Any person in the United States" could reach end users who merely prompt a covered system, which invites overbreadth and interpretive disputes. Fix: clarify that the prohibition targets developers and deployers, not users.

25. **Sec. 3(b), "training began" is ambiguous.** It's unclear whether continued training, post-training, fine-tuning, or new phases of an existing run count as beginning training. Developers could use a pre-enactment run as a base for further development.

26. **Sec. 3, government and national labs.** The bill doesn't say whether it binds federal agencies, DOD, or national laboratories, which creates both a national-security question and a sovereign-immunity question. Expect the Pentagon and the national labs to raise it.

## Registry (Sec. 4)

27. **Sec. 4(a), registers facilities, not models.** The registry records where compute is located, not which covered AI systems are being trained, so it doesn't capture the risk the bill targets. Fix: require registration of covered training runs.

28. **Sec. 4(a), "operator" is undefined.** The cloud host and the tenant running a training job could each claim not to be the operator. Fix: define "operator" to cover any entity that controls or directs the workload.

29. **Sec. 4(a), no deadline or content requirements.** The bill doesn't say when registration is due or what information must be provided.

30. **Sec. 4(b), "computing workloads" is vague.** The quarterly report doesn't require training-run identifiers, FLOP counts, model identities, or purpose. Operators can report in general terms that tell regulators very little.

31. **Sec. 4(b), self-certification without verification.** Operators certify their own reports, and the bill gives Commerce no audit, inspection, or subpoena authority.

32. **Sec. 4, confidentiality is not addressed.** Operators will ask for trade-secret protection and FOIA exemption, and the bill doesn't say how reported data will be protected or shared with enforcement agencies. Fix: specify confidentiality rules that still allow enforcement.

## Penalties (Sec. 5)

33. **Sec. 5(a), $1,000,000 per day may be a cost of doing business.** Frontier training budgets run into the billions, so a fixed daily penalty may not deter. Fix: scale penalties to revenue or compute, or add per-model penalties.

34. **Sec. 5(a), "for each day on which the violation occurs" is undefined.** It's unclear whether a continuing violation (such as an ongoing training run) counts once per day or per system, and how a violation is measured.

35. **Sec. 5, no individual liability.** The bill doesn't address officers or directors who direct violations. Fix: add personal liability for officers who knowingly direct violations.

36. **Sec. 5(b), "willfully" is undefined.** Criminal liability depends on proving willfulness, and the bill doesn't say what standard applies. Vagueness is also a constitutional risk for a criminal provision.

37. **Sec. 5, no forfeiture.** The bill doesn't authorize forfeiture of weights, compute, or proceeds from violations.

38. **Sec. 5(a) vs. Sec. 8, mismatched start dates.** Section 5(a) applies to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but Section 8 says the Act takes effect 180 days later. A court could read these inconsistently.

## International agreement (Sec. 6)

39. **Sec. 6, "shall seek to negotiate" is hortatory.** The Secretary of State is directed to try, with no reporting requirement or consequence if negotiations fail.

40. **Sec. 6, "equivalent" is undefined.** The bill doesn't say what counts as an equivalent prohibition, which makes the requirement impossible to evaluate.

41. **Sec. 6, timing.** The 18-month deadline runs from enactment rather than the effective date, and the 10-year sunset could expire before any agreement takes effect.

## Duration and review (Sec. 7)

42. **Sec. 7(a), "can be developed safely" is undefined.** The standard for lifting the prohibition is not defined, so a narrow or contested finding could be treated as sufficient.

43. **Sec. 7(a), NASEM report has no deadline or scope.** The bill doesn't require NASEM to produce a report, set a timeline, or define what the report must cover. It's also unclear whether a joint resolution must address a specific system or all covered systems.

44. **Sec. 7(a), joint resolution procedure is missing.** The bill doesn't say whether the resolution gets expedited consideration, whether it's subject to a presidential veto, or whether it's a standard joint resolution. Fix: specify the procedure.

45. **Sec. 7(a), the default if no report is issued is unclear.** If NASEM never reports, the prohibition presumably continues, but the bill doesn't say so. Clarify this as intended.

## Effective date and general (Sec. 8 and throughout)

46. **Sec. 8, registry and reporting deadlines.** Sections 4 and 8 don't say when registration or the first report is due, which creates an ambiguity about whether obligations start at enactment or at the effective date.

47. **No severability clause.** If a court strikes one provision, such as the Section 2(1)(B) determination, the bill has no clause saying the rest survives. Fix: add a severability clause.

48. **No judicial review or savings clause.** The bill doesn't address review of Secretary determinations or clarify how the Act interacts with other law.

49. **No whistleblower protection.** Insiders are the most likely source of evidence of violations, and the bill gives them no protection or reporting channel.

50. **No appropriations.** Commerce would need funding to run the registry and enforce the Act, and the bill doesn't authorize any.

51. **Preemption is not addressed.** The bill is silent on whether it preempts state AI laws. That's a strategic choice for the office, but silence will invite litigation and lobbying over the question.

If it would help, I can turn the highest-priority items into proposed replacement language for the office's markup memo.
