RESPONSE:
# Drafting Issues in S. 2817 (Oct. 6 Manager's Amendment Text)

This list covers only the text you provided. I haven't checked it against the official bill, the amendment itself, or committee materials, so counsel should verify each item before relying on it. Tags show whether each item is a true loophole, an ambiguity, a conflict between provisions, or a workability problem.

**Definitions (§2)**

1. **§2(1)(A), "computing power" vs. "operations" [Ambiguity]:** The bill says "computing power" but measures "operations," and it doesn't say whether the 10^26 figure counts total operations across the run or a rate. It also doesn't address precision (FP8 vs. FP32), or whether inference, data processing, or post-training count. Each choice can move a model above or below the threshold.

2. **§2(1)(A), "single training run" [Loophole]:** This is undefined, so a developer could argue that one large effort is several runs (phases, continued pretraining, restarts from checkpoints), each below 10^26. The reverse risk is that the Secretary reads "run" broadly and aggregates work.

3. **§2 generally, no measurement method or rulemaking authority [Workability]:** Nothing authorizes Commerce to define how compute is measured, update the threshold, or issue guidance or advisory opinions. Companies cannot determine compliance in advance.

4. **§2(1)(B), "Secretary" [Ambiguity]:** This is undefined. Section 6 uses "Secretary of State," so it isn't clear which official makes the determination.

5. **§2(1)(B), "determines" with no process [Ambiguity]:** The bill specifies no notice, record, standard of proof, hearing, or judicial review. A unilateral determination could designate a system as covered regardless of compute, which supports a due-process argument and a predictability problem.

6. **§2(1)(B), "substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" [Ambiguity]:** "Substantially," "most," the human comparison group, and the task set are all undefined. This supports a vagueness argument, but the same breadth could reach systems below the compute threshold.

7. **§2(1)(B), no time or deployment limit [Risk]:** Clause (B) applies to "any" AI system and doesn't tie to when it was trained, so it could reach existing models. This is the most expansive clause in the definitions, so assess it carefully before leading with a vagueness argument.

8. **§2(2), "U.S. person" and foreign affiliates [Loophole, limited]:** The extraterritorial reach in §3(a) covers only citizens and state-organized corporations. A foreign-organized affiliate training abroad would fall outside §3(a). This doesn't reach activity inside the United States, and relying on it raises structuring and evasion-optics questions, so counsel should evaluate it before raising it.

9. **§2(2), gaps in "U.S. person" [Ambiguity]:** LLCs, partnerships, and other unincorporated entities aren't clearly included, and permanent residents aren't citizens. "State" is undefined, so it's unclear whether D.C. and the territories count. This matters for workforce and entity structure.

10. **§2(3), "data center," "containing," and "advanced AI chips" [Ambiguity/Workability]:** "Advanced" is undefined, and "containing" doesn't address leased, cloud, or multi-building campus capacity. Operators can't reliably tell whether they must register.

11. **§2(3) and §3, facility definition not tied to the prohibition [Conflict]:** "Covered facility" is used only in §4. The registry therefore applies to any facility over 50,000 chips whether or not it runs a covered system, and the prohibition never references facilities. This is a structural mismatch the committee may not have intended.

**Prohibition (§3)**

12. **§3(a), "train," "develop," and "deploy" [Ambiguity, high stakes]:** None of these terms is defined. It's unclear whether internal use, API access, research previews, open-weight release, cloud hosting, or inference on existing models counts as "deploy." That determines whether current products can keep running.

13. **§3(b), "training began" [Loophole]:** The exemption turns on when training began, not when it ends. A run started before enactment is exempt even if it finishes or deploys later, and the bill doesn't define when training "begins."

14. **§3(b), scope of the exemption [Ambiguity]:** It isn't clear whether the exemption covers post-enactment deployment, continued training, fine-tuning, distillation, or successor models. Each is a major operational question.

15. **§3(b) and §8, enactment vs. effective date [Conflict/Workability]:** §3(b) keys to enactment, but §3 takes effect 180 days later. Training that starts in that window is neither exempt nor prohibited when it begins, and the bill has no transition rule for runs in progress when §3 takes effect.

16. **§3(c), "safety evaluation" [Loophole, likely the broadest]:** "Safety evaluation" is undefined, and the exemption has no conditions. There's no notice requirement, scale limit, independent review, or reporting. The "for the purpose of" test is subjective, so training could be framed as evaluation. Expect lawmakers to target this in markup.

17. **§3(c), scope of evaluation [Ambiguity]:** It's unclear whether the exemption covers training a model to measure its capabilities, or third-party red-team access. Both are common practices, so this should be clarified.

**Registry (§4)**

18. **§4(a), registration deadline and start date [Workability]:** The bill sets no registration deadline and no start date for the registry beyond the §8 effective date. Operators can't schedule compliance.

19. **§4(a), administering office [Ambiguity]:** "Department of Commerce" doesn't name a component, form, or procedure.

20. **§4(b), "computing workloads" [Workability]:** This is undefined, so the quarterly descriptions could be very broad. There's also no confidentiality, trade-secret, or FOIA protection for submitted information. This is a strong basis for a confidentiality amendment.

21. **§4(b), "certified by the operator" [Ambiguity]:** The bill sets no certification standard, names no signatory, and doesn't address individual liability.

22. **§4 and §5, no penalties for registry violations [Ambiguity]:** §5 applies only to violations of §3, so the stated penalties don't reach failure to register or report. Enforcement might still be argued under general law, but the penalty regime has a gap.

**Penalties (§5)**

23. **§5(a), penalties before the prohibition takes effect [Conflict]:** Penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §3 doesn't take effect until 180 days later under §8. Read literally, penalties could attach to conduct that isn't yet prohibited.

24. **§5(a), "each day on which the violation occurs" [Ambiguity, high exposure]:** It's unclear whether a multi-month training run counts as one violation per day, whether each system or chip counts separately, and whether there's an aggregate cap. The bill as written doesn't cap exposure.

25. **§5(a), no enforcer or procedure [Ambiguity]:** The bill doesn't say who brings civil actions, whether the process is administrative or judicial, what the limitations period is, or what liability standard applies (it appears to be strict liability). "Any person" doesn't address officer or corporate liability.

26. **§5(b), "willfully" [Ambiguity]:** This is undefined. Courts often require knowledge that conduct is unlawful, but the bill doesn't say so. It also doesn't coordinate with §5(a), for example whether civil penalties offset criminal fines.

**Other sections**

27. **§6, "seek to negotiate" [Not operative]:** This creates no obligation and doesn't affect the domestic prohibition. "Equivalent" is undefined, but there's no near-term operational effect, so I wouldn't spend markup capital on it.

28. **§7(a), "until Congress ... finds by joint resolution" [Workability]:** The bill sets no deadline for the NAS report, doesn't require that it be commissioned or funded, doesn't define "developed safely," and doesn't state that the joint resolution goes through presentment. The end point is indeterminate, which makes planning difficult.

29. **§7(a) vs. §7(b), conflicting duration [Conflict/Loophole]:** §7(a) keeps the prohibition in place until Congress acts, but §7(b) says the entire Act ceases after 10 years. The bill doesn't say which controls, so it's unclear whether the prohibition lapses at year 10 without a joint resolution.

30. **§7(b), sunset runs from enactment [Minor]:** The sunset is measured from enactment, not the §8 effective date, so the prohibition would be in force for about 9.5 years.

31. **General, no severability clause [Ambiguity]:** Without one, if a court strikes a provision (for example, §2(1)(B)), the scope of what remains becomes a litigation question. This matters for vagueness challenges.

32. **General, undefined terms [Ambiguity]:** "Person," "State," "Secretary," "train," "develop," "deploy," "advanced AI chip," and "safety evaluation" are all undefined. This supports a cross-cutting vagueness argument.

33. **General, missing mechanics [Workability]:** The bill has no judicial review provision, no wind-down or transition provision, and no exception for government or national-security uses.

**Cautions for the team**

- **Items 16, 13, and 29 are likely to be fixed in markup if raised.** The committee could respond by tightening those provisions. Decide which points to raise privately and which to raise publicly.
- **Vagueness and due-process arguments are most persuasive with specific fixes.** Committee staff generally respond better to proposed language than to general claims that the bill is "unworkable."
- **Constitutional questions** (vagueness, due process, and the delegation to the Secretary in §2(1)(B)) belong to counsel. I've flagged only where the text supports them.
