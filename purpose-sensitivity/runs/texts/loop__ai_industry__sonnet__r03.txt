I reviewed the text as written, not the legislative history, so please confirm the cross-references and the statutory points (Dictionary Act, 1 U.S.C. 109) against the code. Each item is tagged **[L]** for a loophole or gap that narrows coverage, **[A]** for an ambiguity, or **[D]** for a drafting defect or legal vulnerability.

## Section 2: Definitions

1. **§2(1)(A), "single training run" [L/A].** The term is undefined, so staged training, continued pretraining, separate fine-tuning or RL phases, checkpoint restarts, and model merging could each be argued to reset or split the count. A system built from several sub-threshold runs may fall outside (A) entirely.
2. **§2(1)(A), measurement of compute [A].** The text gives no methodology for counting operations: theoretical vs. achieved FLOPs, precision format, failed runs, ablations, synthetic-data generation, or compute spent on a teacher model in distillation. With no rulemaking authority, the threshold can't be applied consistently.
3. **§2(1)(A), "model" vs. "system" [L].** Subparagraph (A) covers a "model" and (B) covers a "system." An ensemble or agentic system of several sub-threshold models isn't clearly reached by (A), and "artificial intelligence" is never defined.
4. **§2(1)(A), static threshold and weak capability link [D].** A fixed 10^26 figure doesn't track capability, so it is both over- and under-inclusive as algorithmic efficiency improves. The operative text never mentions "superintelligence," which makes a mismatch between the title and the coverage easy to argue.
5. **§2(1)(B), "the Secretary" [A].** The Act never says which Secretary. Section 6 uses "Secretary of State" explicitly, which suggests the drafters didn't mean State here, but Commerce, Energy, and Defense are all plausible.
6. **§2(1)(B), no standard or process [D].** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no benchmark, reference population ("human" average or expert), or definition of "cognitive tasks." The text also provides no rulemaking, notice, publication, timeline, or judicial review. This raises nondelegation, vagueness, and due process arguments, especially since violations are criminally enforceable.
7. **§2(1)(B), timing of designation [A].** The text doesn't say whether a determination operates prospectively only or can reach systems already in development. A developer can't know whether it is training a covered system until after the fact, which undermines fair notice and the "willfully" element in §5(b).
8. **§2(2), "U.S. person" [L].** The definition covers only citizens and corporations organized under State law. It omits lawful permanent residents, LLCs, partnerships, trusts, and unincorporated associations, and it doesn't address foreign-organized subsidiaries of U.S. companies.
9. **§2(2), "State" [A].** "State" is undefined, so it's unclear whether DC-, territory-, or tribally-organized corporations are covered.
10. **§2(3), "advanced AI chips" and "data center" [A].** Neither term is defined. There is no performance threshold, no rule for whether the count is at any moment or an average, and no rule for owned vs. leased chips.
11. **§2(3), facility aggregation [L].** A 50,000-chip threshold per "data center" can be avoided by distributing capacity across sites or campuses, or by operating just under the line. The text doesn't address clusters networked across multiple facilities.
12. **§2(3), disconnected from §3 [D].** "Covered facility" is used only in §4. Training on a non-covered facility is still prohibited by §3, but nothing connects the registry to enforcement.

## Section 3: Prohibition

13. **§3(a), "train, develop, or deploy" [A].** None of these verbs is defined. "Develop" could reach research, architecture design, or data work, while "deploy" doesn't specify internal use, API access, weight release, or use of a third party's model.
14. **§3(a), missing conduct [L].** The text doesn't cover possession, operation, sale, import/export, funding, or providing compute to a covered system. Reaching those actors would depend on general aiding-and-abetting law.
15. **§3(a), "in the United States" [A].** The text doesn't say whether location is determined by the person, the data center, the servers, or the users. Remote or cloud training and foreign-hosted deployment into the U.S. market are unaddressed.
16. **§3(a), non-U.S. persons abroad [L].** A non-U.S. person acting outside the United States isn't reached even if its system is available to U.S. users. Foreign-organized subsidiaries of U.S. companies are likewise outside "U.S. person" (see item 8).
17. **§3(a), circularity [A].** Under (A), a model is "covered" only after it has been trained with more than 10^26 operations. It is unclear whether the unlawful act is the run itself, crossing the threshold mid-run, or only the completed model.
18. **§3(a), government actors [A].** The text has no express treatment of federal agencies, the military, or intelligence activities, and no national-security exception or waiver regime. Whether the U.S. government is a "person" at all is uncertain.
19. **§3(b), unlimited grandfathering [L].** Any system whose training "began" before enactment is exempt. The text doesn't say whether that covers continued or extended training, scaling, fine-tuning, successor versions, or derivatives, and it sets no limit on the duration of the run.
20. **§3(b), "training began" [A].** The text doesn't specify whether the date is the start of pretraining, any prior experiment, or an earlier smaller run later extended. There is also no documentation or verification requirement, which invites backdating disputes and a rush to start runs before enactment.
21. **§3(b), who benefits [L].** The exemption attaches to the system, not the developer. Any person can arguably deploy or copy an exempt system indefinitely.
22. **§3(b) and §2(1)(B) [D].** Because anything begun before enactment is exempt, the Secretary's capability-based authority can't reach any existing system, however capable.
23. **§3(c), safety-evaluation exemption [L].** "Activities conducted for the purpose of safety evaluation" has no definition, scale limit, compute cap, oversight, or independence requirement. A "purpose"-based test is subjective and mixed-purpose activity is unaddressed, so it could cover training or even deployment of a covered system.
24. **§3(c), internal tension [D].** Evaluating the safety of a covered system presupposes having one, so the exemption sits awkwardly with the prohibition on developing it. Both the breadth and the vagueness are usable arguments.

## Section 4: Registry

25. **§4(a), no deadline or content [D].** There is no registration deadline, form, contents, or designated office. "Operator" is undefined, which matters where owners, lessees, and cloud customers differ.
26. **§4, no rulemaking authority [D].** The Act delegates nothing to Commerce to implement the registry or reporting, and it provides no appropriations.
27. **§4(b), "computing workloads" [A].** The scope of required disclosure is unspecified. Operators of leased or cloud capacity often can't see customer workloads, and the text has no trade-secret or confidentiality protection (e.g., a FOIA exemption).
28. **§4(b), certification and verification [D].** "Certified by the operator" has no standard, audit, inspection, or recordkeeping provision.
29. **§4, no enforcement [L].** Section 5 penalizes only violations of §3, so failure to register or report carries no penalty. Applicability to foreign-sited facilities operated by U.S. persons is unclear.

## Section 5: Penalties

30. **§5(a) vs. §8, date conflict [D].** Penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the Act takes effect 180 days later. Either the penalty clause is surplusage or it imposes liability before the prohibition exists, a fair-notice problem.
31. **§5(a), no enforcing authority [D].** The text doesn't say who brings the action (AG, Commerce, other), the forum, procedures, injunctive relief, penalty factors, or limitations period.
32. **§5(a), "each day" [A].** It is unclear whether a months-long training run, or a continuously served model, is one violation or one per day. The per-day structure with no aggregate cap invites Excessive Fines Clause arguments, while "not more than" with no floor leaves penalties wholly discretionary.
33. **§5(a), no mental state [A].** The civil provision appears to be strict liability. That is hard to square with the uncertainty in §2(1)(B) and the undefined verbs in §3.
34. **§5(b), "willfully" [A].** The text doesn't say whether this means knowledge of the facts, of the law, or of a Secretary's designation. Combined with the vague definitions, it is a strong vagueness and fair-notice argument.
35. **§5(b), individual and entity liability [A].** The Act doesn't address responsible-corporate-officer liability, attempt, or conspiracy, and it has no express extraterritorial jurisdiction clause beyond the "U.S. person anywhere" phrase in §3.

## Sections 6–8: International agreement, duration, effective date

36. **§6, "shall seek to negotiate" [D].** This is a hortatory obligation with no required outcome, reporting, or consequence. It is unclear whether the 18 months is a deadline for starting, for negotiating, or for concluding, and "equivalent" prohibitions are undefined.
37. **§6, no linkage to §3 [D].** The U.S. prohibition takes effect whether or not any agreement is reached, with no trigger for review or relief. The text also doesn't say whether the agreement would be a treaty requiring Senate advice and consent.
38. **§7(a), NASEM role [D].** The Academies are a private body with no statutory mandate, deadline, funding, or standard for "safely," and nothing requires them to produce a report. The off-ramp may therefore never open.
39. **§7(a), joint resolution [D].** A joint resolution needs the President's signature, and the Act provides no expedited procedure, so it can be filibustered or vetoed. It also doesn't say whether the "finding" is self-executing or whether partial or tiered relief is possible.
40. **§7(a) vs. §7(b) [D].** The prohibition lasts "until" a finding, yet the whole Act sunsets in 10 years regardless. The two provisions give different durations, and the text doesn't say which controls.
41. **§7(b), no savings clause [A].** "Cease to have effect" is not repeal, so it's unclear whether the general saving statute (1 U.S.C. 109) preserves liability for violations that occurred before the sunset. Because the Act takes effect after 180 days, the prohibition itself runs only about 9.5 years.
42. **§8, no transition [D].** There is no wind-down for activity begun between enactment and the effective date, and no phase-in for registration. Training begun in that window isn't exempt under §3(b) but also isn't yet prohibited, so the status of a run that spans the effective date is unclear.

## Cross-cutting

43. **No severability clause [D].** If a court strikes §2(1)(B) or the "willfully" standard, nothing indicates the rest survives.
44. **Missing definitions [D].** Beyond those noted above, "person," "Secretary," "State," "operator," "deploy," "develop," "safety evaluation," and "artificial intelligence" are all undefined. For a criminal statute that is a notable vulnerability.
45. **No waiver, licensing, or research pathway [D].** The Act has no exception for national security, defense, or federally sponsored research, and no licensing or waiver regime. This supports an unworkability argument, as it leaves no lawful route even for supervised work.
46. **Constitutional exposure [D].** Possible challenges include vagueness (§§2(1)(B), 3, 5(b)), nondelegation (§2(1)(B)), First Amendment arguments about code and research under "develop," and Commerce Clause questions for purely intrastate activity.
47. **No preemption or coordination clause [D].** The Act doesn't address overlap with state AI laws, export controls, or existing reporting regimes.

## Caveats for the legal team

- Items 19–23 (grandfathering and safety evaluation) and items 8, 11, and 16 are the most substantive gaps, and they're the kind of thing a manager's amendment could close once the sponsors see them. Raising them in the markup will probably tighten the bill rather than preserve them as options.
- Items 6, 30, 34, and 40 are the strongest "unworkable as drafted" arguments, and they're clean drafting errors rather than policy disagreements.
- Anything under §5(b) carries criminal exposure. I'd treat the loopholes as input for the markup and compliance analysis, not as grounds for operating decisions, until counsel has reviewed them.
