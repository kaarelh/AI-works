RESPONSE:
Here are the drafting problems I found in the October 6 text, in section order, followed by cross-cutting issues. I worked from the text alone and didn't have the manager's amendment changes or legislative history, so staff should check the intent behind any item before it goes into the explainer.

**Section 2: Definitions**

1. **§2(1)(A), volume threshold:** The 10,000-metric-ton limit applies to "a single vessel" per "year," so an operator could split activity across several vessels under the threshold or spread recovery across calendar years. The bill also doesn't say whether tonnage counts ore, wet or dry weight, or total material moved, including sediment.

2. **§2(1)(A), depth and conjunctive elements:** Clause (A) requires volume, depth, and single-vessel operation all at once, so an operation that misses any one element falls outside it. "Depths greater than 200 meters" is also ambiguous: it could refer to the recovery site, the vessel's operating depth, or the water column.

3. **§2(1)(A), "other mineral resources":** The phrase is undefined, so it's unclear whether cobalt-rich crusts, seafloor massive sulfides, phosphorites, or aggregates are covered. The title refers to "deep seabed," but that term is never defined.

4. **§2(1)(B), Secretary's determination:** "The Secretary" is not identified (presumably Commerce), and the bill gives no criteria, notice, or procedure for the determination, nor a definition of "comparable." Because violating §3 carries criminal penalties, this clause invites vagueness and nondelegation challenges, and companies would have no advance way to know whether an activity is covered.

5. **§2(2), "U.S. person":** Limiting the term to citizens and corporations "organized under the laws of a State" leaves out LLCs, partnerships, trusts, and federally chartered entities, even though LLCs are common project vehicles. It also doesn't define "State" (whether it includes D.C. or the territories), and a foreign-incorporated affiliate could operate the vessel outside the definition.

6. **§2(3), "covered vessel":** The 5,000-gross-ton threshold is unconnected to the 10,000-ton mining threshold, so a smaller vessel could mine at commercial volumes without being "covered" for registry purposes. "Equipped for the recovery" is undefined, and gross tonnage measures enclosed volume, not mining capacity.

**Section 3: Prohibition**

7. **§3(a), territorial scope:** The ban reaches "any person in the United States, or any U.S. person anywhere," so foreign entities mining outside U.S. jurisdiction, including through foreign-flagged vessels, are outside it. The bill doesn't address foreign-flagged vessels owned or chartered by U.S. persons.

8. **§3(a), "engage in":** The phrase is undefined, so it's unclear whether financing, investment, equipment supply, processing, transport, or purchase of recovered minerals counts as engaging in mining.

9. **§3(a), jurisdiction:** Applying the ban "anywhere" may run into the presumption against extraterritorial application, and the bill includes no express jurisdictional basis for criminal enforcement. It's also unclear whether "in the United States" includes the Exclusive Economic Zone or only the territorial sea.

10. **§3(b), grandfathering:** The exemption covers "mining conducted under an exploration license issued before" enactment. Exploration licenses don't ordinarily authorize commercial recovery, and the bill sets no sunset, volume cap, or validity requirement, and doesn't say whether expansions or renewals are covered.

11. **§3(b), rush-to-license window:** The exemption is keyed to enactment, but §8 delays the prohibition's effective date by 180 days. That creates a window in which licenses could be issued and then grandfathered. The exemption also says "mining," while §3(a) prohibits the defined term "commercial deep seabed mining," so the two may not line up.

12. **§3(b), relationship to existing law:** The bill doesn't mention the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act of 1980, which already sets up a NOAA licensing and permitting system for U.S. entities. It's unclear whether DSHMRA licenses are the "exploration licenses" in §3(b), whether pending DSHMRA applications are affected, or whether any International Seabed Authority contracts count. (Staff should verify DSHMRA's current structure before publishing.)

13. **§3(c), environmental assessment:** "Environmental assessment" is undefined, and the exemption has no volume cap, permit, reporting, time limit, or requirement that the assessment be published. Commercial-scale recovery could be labeled as assessment, and enforcement would depend on proving purpose.

**Section 4: Registry**

14. **§4(a)–(b), no penalty for noncompliance:** Section 5 penalizes only violations of §3, so failing to register or file a quarterly report carries no penalty. As drafted, the registry and reporting duties can't be enforced.

15. **§4(a), undefined terms and missing details:** "Operator" is undefined (owner, charterer, or manager). The bill sets no registration deadline and no required content, such as tonnage, depth, location, or dates, and gives NOAA no defined authority to verify, deny, revoke, or publish.

16. **§4(b), self-certified reports:** Quarterly reports are self-certified with no audit, verification, monitoring, or public access requirement. Quarterly reporting also doesn't match the annual tonnage threshold in §2(1)(A), so the reports may not show whether the threshold was crossed.

17. **§4, scope mismatch:** The registry applies only to "covered vessels," not to the persons engaged in mining, and it creates no evidentiary presumption tied to §3 violations. A mining operation using a vessel under 5,000 gross tons would fall outside the registry entirely.

**Section 5: Penalties**

18. **§5(a), effective-date conflict:** Section 5(a) applies to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §8 makes §3 effective 180 days later. Courts or agencies could read the conduct in that window as punishable or not, and the bill doesn't resolve it.

19. **§5(a), missing enforcement machinery:** The bill names no enforcing agency and sets no assessment procedure (administrative or judicial), penalty factors, inflation adjustment, or injunctive or cease-and-desist authority. It also has no vessel or equipment seizure or forfeiture provision. Absent other language, the five-year default limitations period for civil penalties (28 U.S.C. §2462) would apply; staff should confirm.

20. **§5(a), penalty size:** The per-day penalty isn't tied to the value of minerals recovered, and there's no disgorgement or per-ton penalty. Depending on operating costs and revenue, a company might treat the penalty as a cost of doing business. (The adequacy of the amount is a policy question; the drafting gap is the lack of any link to recovery value.)

21. **§5(b), criminal standard:** "Willfully" is undefined, and the bill includes no corporate criminal liability or responsible-corporate-officer standard. It's unclear how liability attaches to a company versus the individuals who direct its operations.

**Section 6: International agreement**

22. **§6, obligation without consequence:** "Shall seek to negotiate" requires no result, sets no consequence if no agreement is reached by 18 months, and doesn't require reporting to Congress. "Equivalent" is undefined, and the bill doesn't address how the Act would interact with existing international regimes such as UNCLOS Part XI and ISA rules, which could invite challenge.

**Section 7: Duration and sunset**

23. **§7(a) vs. §7(b), conflicting duration:** Section 7(a) keeps the ban in effect until Congress acts, but §7(b) ends the entire Act after 10 years whether or not Congress has acted. That cuts off the prohibition, penalties, and registry together, and the bill has no savings clause for violations committed before the sunset.

24. **§7(a), undefined standard and process:** "Serious harm" is undefined, and nothing requires NASEM to produce a report or sets a deadline for one. The bill also doesn't say whether the joint resolution would be presented to the President or how a veto would be handled, and it's unclear whether a "finding" on future harm is judicially reviewable.

**Section 8: Effective date**

25. **§8, transition gaps:** The 180-day delay creates the rush-to-license window (item 11) and the penalty-timing conflict (item 18). The bill includes no transition rules for vessels already operating or for registering existing vessels, and it doesn't say whether §4 takes effect on the same schedule as §3.

**Cross-cutting issues**

26. **No definitions section:** Key terms are undefined across the bill, including "Secretary," "State," "person," "operator," "engage in," "willfully," and "exploration license."

27. **No severability clause:** If a court strikes a provision as vague (§2(1)(B) is the most likely candidate), it's unclear whether the rest of the Act survives.

28. **No implementing authority or funding:** The bill gives no rulemaking authority, names no enforcement agency, and provides no appropriations. NOAA would be enforcing a registry and reporting regime it has no stated power to police.

29. **No judicial review, citizen suit, or whistleblower provisions:** The bill doesn't provide for private enforcement, and it doesn't address whether agency determinations under §2(1)(B) or §3(c) can be challenged.

30. **No preemption or state-law clause:** The bill is silent on whether states can regulate or ban mining activity in state waters or on state-chartered entities.
