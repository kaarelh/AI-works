RESPONSE:
Here's the issue list, organized by section. I've flagged the ones I'd treat as top priority at the end.

**Section 2 — Definitions**

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "single training run."** A developer can split training into multiple runs (e.g., pretrain to 9×10²⁵, checkpoint, then "continue" as a new run) and never cross the threshold in any one run. The term is undefined, and there is no aggregation rule for compute applied to the same model, its checkpoints, or its derivatives (fine-tuning, RL, distillation, continued pretraining).

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — compute accounting is unspecified.** The text doesn't say how to count operations (dense vs. sparse, forward-only vs. forward+backward, synthetic-data generation, RL rollouts, multiple candidate runs, or whether "integer or floating-point" means either alone or both combined). Each ambiguity is a defense in an enforcement action and a vagueness argument in a criminal case.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — fixed threshold with no update mechanism.** Algorithmic efficiency gains mean the capability reachable at 10²⁶ FLOP will rise every year, so the threshold will undercount dangerous systems over the bill's 10-year life. There is no authority to lower (or raise) the number by rule.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "model" vs. "system."** Subparagraph (A) covers a "model," (B) covers a "system," and Sec. 3 prohibits acting on a "system." A developer could argue that scaffolding, agent frameworks, or ensembles of sub-threshold models are "systems" not reached by (A), and that a bare "model" isn't a "system" under Sec. 3.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — "the Secretary" is never identified.** The bill never says which Secretary makes capability determinations. As written, prong (B) is inoperative.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — no process, standard, or timeline for determinations.** There is no notice, evidentiary standard, deadline, judicial-review provision, or rulemaking authority. The determination can also be made only after a system exists, so prong (B) cannot stop training in advance—it only catches a system after the fact.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — vagueness exposure.** "Capable of substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no measurable content, and it feeds a 10-year criminal penalty in Sec. 5(b). This invites a void-for-vagueness challenge; it should be tied to defined evaluations or to a rulemaking with published criteria.

8. **Sec. 2(2) — "U.S. person" is far too narrow.** It covers only citizens and corporations organized under State law. It omits LLCs, partnerships, trusts, nonprofits, lawful permanent residents, entities organized under D.C., territorial, tribal, or federal law, and—most importantly—foreign subsidiaries and affiliates owned or controlled by U.S. persons. A Delaware parent could run training through a Cayman or Singapore subsidiary outside the prohibition.

9. **Sec. 2(2) / Sec. 3(a) — "person" and government actors.** "Person" is undefined, and under the Dictionary Act and ordinary presumptions it generally excludes the federal government. Unless that is intended, DoD, the IC, and national labs (and their contractors acting as agents) may be outside the prohibition entirely.

10. **Sec. 2(3) — "advanced AI chips" and "data center" are undefined.** Without a definition (e.g., by reference to ECCN 3A090 or a performance threshold), the registry's scope is unknowable and operators can self-classify out of it.

11. **Sec. 2(3) — 50,000-chip threshold is easily split.** An operator can spread chips across multiple buildings, campuses, or legal entities so that no single "data center" exceeds 50,000, and geo-distributed training across sub-threshold sites is not addressed. There is no aggregation across commonly owned or networked facilities.

12. **Sec. 2(3) — no coverage of cloud compute as such.** The registry attaches to the physical facility, not to whoever controls the compute. A developer renting capacity across several providers' sub-threshold facilities, or using foreign compute, generates no reporting obligation.

**Section 3 — Prohibition**

13. **Sec. 3(a) — "train, develop, or deploy" are undefined.** "Develop" could be read narrowly (excluding, e.g., assembling a covered system from components) or so broadly that ordinary research is swept in. "Deploy" is unclear as to whether it covers internal use, API access, end-user use of a foreign system, or mere possession of weights.

14. **Sec. 3(a) — no coverage of facilitation, attempt, or conspiracy.** Cloud providers, chip suppliers, and contractors who knowingly provide the compute or services for a covered training run are not reached, and a run that is interrupted before completion may not be a "violation." Add aiding/abetting, attempt, and conspiracy language, plus a prohibition on knowingly providing compute for covered training.

15. **Sec. 3(a) — no coverage of transfer, import, or export of weights.** Nothing prohibits acquiring, importing, hosting, or exporting the weights of a covered system (e.g., one trained abroad by a non-U.S. person). "Deploy" may or may not reach this.

16. **Sec. 3(a) — partial-territorial training.** A run executed partly in the U.S. and partly abroad, or orchestrated from the U.S. on foreign hardware by non-U.S. persons, is not clearly covered. Jurisdiction should attach to any portion of training conducted in, controlled from, or using compute located in the U.S.

17. **Sec. 3(b) — grandfather clause is the bill's largest loophole.** Any run that "began" before enactment is permanently exempt, with no definition of "began." A developer can initialize a run before enactment and continue it indefinitely, or characterize every future model as continued training of a pre-enactment checkpoint. At minimum, the exemption should apply only to runs that *completed* before enactment and should cap further compute on those models.

18. **Sec. 3(b) — grandfathered systems may be deployed forever.** The exemption covers deployment as well as training, so any system that already exceeds 10²⁶ (and several likely do) remains fully usable, with no reporting, evaluation, or decommissioning requirement. That undercuts the bill's purpose where it matters most.

19. **Sec. 3(c) — "safety evaluation" exemption swallows the rule.** It is unlimited in scale, purpose-based (a state-of-mind test that is nearly impossible to disprove), and applies to "train" and "develop," so a company can build a covered system on the theory that one must build it to evaluate it. It should be limited to evaluation of existing systems, require advance authorization from a named agency, bar any training above the threshold, and bar commercial deployment of anything produced.

20. **Sec. 3(b)–(c) — exemptions have no notice or reporting requirement.** Anyone invoking either exemption should have to notify the agency, identify the system and compute used, and bear the burden of proof.

**Section 4 — Registry**

21. **Sec. 4(a) — "operator" is undefined and there is no deadline.** It's unclear whether the owner, lessor, colocation tenant, or cloud provider must register, and nothing says when. Specify the party and a date certain (e.g., 60 days after the effective date or after crossing the threshold).

22. **Sec. 4 — no penalty for failing to register or for false reports.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3. Add civil and criminal penalties for non-registration and false certification (and require certification by a named senior officer under penalty of perjury).

23. **Sec. 4(b) — report contents unspecified.** "Describing the computing workloads" could be satisfied with a one-line summary. Require, at minimum, each training run above a reporting threshold (e.g., 10²⁵), the customer, the compute used, and the model identity, and require prompt notice before any run projected to approach 10²⁶.

24. **Sec. 4 — no inspection, audit, or verification authority.** The registry is entirely self-reported. Add authority for on-site inspection, access to logs and telemetry, and third-party audits, plus subpoena power.

25. **Sec. 4 — no confidentiality or FOIA provision.** Operators will resist disclosure of customer and workload data as trade secrets; the absence of a confidentiality clause both invites litigation and weakens the reporting duty. Specify protection and the conditions for sharing with other agencies.

26. **Sec. 4 / Sec. 2(1)(B) — inconsistent agency references.** The registry sits with the Department of Commerce; capability determinations sit with an unnamed "Secretary"; enforcement is assigned to no one. Designate one lead agency throughout.

**Section 5 — Penalties**

27. **Sec. 5(a) — civil penalty is trivial.** $1M/day is a rounding error against multibillion-dollar training runs. Scale the penalty to compute cost or revenue (e.g., a percentage of global turnover or a multiple of the training expenditure) and clarify that each covered system is a separate violation.

28. **Sec. 5(a) — "on or after the date of enactment" conflicts with Sec. 8.** The Act doesn't take effect until 180 days after enactment, so no violation can occur before then; the current language invites a retroactivity challenge and confusion. Replace with "on or after the effective date."

29. **Sec. 5 — no enforcement agency, no civil action authority, no injunctive relief.** The bill never says who may bring a civil action or seek an injunction. Without authority for the Attorney General (and/or the agency) to seek injunctions, the government cannot halt an ongoing run—it can only fine afterward.

30. **Sec. 5 — no forfeiture or remedial authority.** There is no power to seize or order deletion of model weights, seize chips, or debar violators from federal contracts. A completed violation leaves the covered system intact.

31. **Sec. 5(b) — "willfully" plus vague definitions makes prosecution impractical.** Willfulness requires proof the defendant knew the conduct was unlawful; combined with issues 1–7 and 13, a defendant will almost always have a plausible good-faith reading. Consider "knowingly," and specify individual liability for officers and directors who authorize covered training.

32. **Sec. 5(b) — corporate fines default to title 18 caps.** "Fined under title 18" means organizations face the 18 U.S.C. § 3571 default (generally $500,000 or twice the gain/loss), which is inadequate for this conduct. Set an express organizational fine.

33. **Sec. 5 — no whistleblower provision.** Insider reporting is the most realistic detection route for covert training; add protections and a reward provision modeled on existing statutes.

**Section 6 — International Agreement**

34. **Sec. 6 — unenforceable and ambiguous.** "Shall seek to negotiate" imposes no obligation of result, and "not later than 18 months" doesn't say whether negotiations must begin or conclude by then. Add a reporting requirement to Congress and define "equivalent."

**Section 7 — Duration**

35. **Sec. 7(a) vs. 7(b) — internal contradiction.** (a) says the prohibition continues until Congress finds development can be done safely; (b) sunsets the entire Act—including the registry—at 10 years regardless. Companies will plan around the fixed date; the sunset should be removed, extended, or made conditional on the (a) finding.

36. **Sec. 7(a) — no mechanism for the National Academies report.** Nothing commissions, funds, or schedules the report, so the trigger may never be satisfied or may be satisfied by an industry-requested study. Specify who requests it, its scope, and its funding.

**Section 8 — Effective Date**

37. **Sec. 8 — 180-day gap with no transition rule.** Runs begun after enactment but before the effective date are neither grandfathered under 3(b) nor yet unlawful, and the bill doesn't say whether continuing such a run past the effective date is a violation. Either make the prohibition effective on enactment or add an express rule that runs in progress on the effective date must stop (or be capped).

**Cross-cutting omissions**

38. **No rulemaking authority.** No agency is authorized to issue regulations defining compute accounting, chips, training runs, evaluation procedures, or reporting formats. Nearly every ambiguity above would be curable by rule if the authority existed.

39. **No severability clause.** If a court strikes Sec. 2(1)(B) for vagueness or the extraterritorial reach of Sec. 3(a), the entire Act may fall with it.

40. **No findings or purpose section.** Findings on interstate and foreign commerce, national security, and the specific risks of covered systems strengthen the Commerce Clause basis, support rational-basis review, and help courts construe the undefined terms in line with intent.

41. **No preemption or savings clause.** The bill is silent on state AI laws, leaving it unclear whether stronger state measures survive (and giving industry an argument for implied preemption).

42. **Overbreadth as a litigation risk.** Because prong (A) is purely compute-based, the bill prohibits ordinary large models regardless of capability, while prong (B) catches only what an unnamed Secretary later identifies. Industry will use the mismatch to argue the line is arbitrary; tightening the definitions (or adding a capability-based safe harbor with evaluation) reduces that exposure.

**If the office can only take a handful of fixes to markup**, I'd prioritize: the grandfather clause (17–18), the safety-evaluation exemption (19), the "single training run" and U.S.-person definitions (1, 8), the missing enforcement agency / injunctive relief (29), the Sec. 5(a)/Sec. 8 date conflict (28), the Sec. 7 contradiction (35), and rulemaking plus severability (38–39). Those are the items most likely to either let a frontier run proceed lawfully or to get the statute enjoined.
