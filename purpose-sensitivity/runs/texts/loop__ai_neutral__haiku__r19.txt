# Loophole, Ambiguity, and Drafting Review: S. 2817 (as amended October 6, 2026)

**Definitions (§2)**

1. **§2(1)(A):** The 10^26 threshold applies to "a single training run," so a developer could split one effort across several runs, or use continued pretraining or fine-tuning of a sub-threshold model, and fall outside the definition. The bill also doesn't say how FLOPs are counted (precision, estimated vs. reported figures, which figure controls).

2. **§2(1)(B):** "The Secretary" is never identified, and the determination turns on "substantially," "most," and "cognitive tasks," none of which is defined. This is the only route to reach sub-threshold systems, so it is the most likely target of a vagueness or due-process challenge.

3. **§2(1)(B):** The bill sets no procedure for the determination (notice, hearing, written findings, or timeline) and doesn't say whether it applies to systems already deployed. Developers could face liability without advance notice of coverage.

4. **§2(2):** "U.S. person" covers only citizens and state-chartered corporations. That excludes LLCs and partnerships abroad, foreign-incorporated subsidiaries of U.S. firms, and permanent residents, so the "anywhere" reach in §3(a) is narrower than it looks. "State" is also undefined (it's unclear whether DC and territories are included).

5. **§2(3):** "Data center," "advanced AI chips," and "containing" are all undefined. The 50,000-chip threshold could be avoided by spreading chips across sites, by using cloud or colocation arrangements where the "operator" is unclear, or by arguing that certain chips aren't "advanced."

**Prohibition (§3)**

6. **§3(a):** "Train," "develop," and "deploy" are undefined and overlap. It's unclear whether releasing open weights, offering API access, distillation, or internal-only use counts as "deploy."

7. **§3(a):** Covered conduct is limited to persons in the U.S. and U.S. persons, so non-U.S. developers training abroad are reached only through §6, which is nonbinding. This is the bill's biggest structural gap.

8. **§3(a), §5(a), and §8:** The bill conflicts on when the prohibition starts. §3 has no effective date, §8 delays the Act 180 days, and §5(a) applies penalties to violations "on or after the date of enactment." It's unclear whether training may legally begin during the 180-day window.

9. **§3(b):** "Training began" is undefined, and multi-month runs may have started at many points. The clause also doesn't say whether it protects only the original run or also continued training, fine-tuning, deployment, or release, so pre-enactment projects could operate indefinitely.

10. **§3(c):** "Safety evaluation" and "for the purpose of" are undefined and purpose-based, which makes the exemption hard to prove or disprove. There are no limits on who may conduct evaluations, on system size or duration, or on disclosure of results, and the bill doesn't say whether deployment after an evaluation is covered. This may be the largest loophole in the bill.

**Registry (§4)**

11. **§4(a):** Registration covers only operators of covered facilities, not developers of covered AI systems. The registry therefore doesn't track the prohibited activity, and a covered model trained on sub-50,000-chip facilities or rented cloud capacity would never appear.

12. **§4 generally:** §5 penalizes only violations of §3, so failing to register or to file reports carries no stated consequence. The reporting obligations are effectively voluntary.

13. **§4(b):** "Describing the computing workloads" sets no content standard. It doesn't require identifying which models were trained, their compute use, or their purpose, and it sets no deadline for the first report, no public disclosure, no confidentiality rule for trade secrets, and no stated penalty for a false certification beyond general law.

**Penalties (§5)**

14. **§5(a):** "For each day on which the violation occurs" isn't defined for continuing violations. It's unclear whether a deployed model violates daily, whether a single training run is one violation, and whether penalties scale per chip. The $1 million cap may be a manageable cost for large firms, and there is no floor or disgorgement provision.

15. **§5 generally:** No agency is named to enforce civil penalties, and the bill gives no investigative subpoena power, no injunctive or cease-and-desist authority, no private right of action, and no whistleblower protections. Enforcement would likely depend on DOJ litigation, which could be slow.

16. **§5(b):** "Willfully" is undefined, and proving intent against a company with compliance structures may be difficult. The bill also doesn't address corporate liability for officers or the limitations period.

**International agreement (§6)**

17. **§6:** "Shall seek to negotiate" creates no obligation to reach an agreement, no reporting requirement to Congress, and no consequence for failure. "Equivalent prohibitions" is undefined, so progress can't be measured, and the bill doesn't say whether a resulting agreement would need Senate ratification.

**Duration (§7)**

18. **§7(a):** The "developed safely" standard is undefined. The NAS report has no deadline, scope, or required content, and ending the prohibition requires a joint resolution that both chambers pass and the President signs or that survives a veto override. Without action, the prohibition could persist indefinitely.

19. **§7(a) vs. §7(b):** §7(a) keeps the prohibition in effect "until" a joint resolution, but §7(b) ends the Act 10 years after enactment regardless. The bill doesn't say which controls, and the sunset runs from enactment rather than the §8 effective date, leaving about 9.5 years of operation.

**Cross-cutting issues**

20. **No severability clause:** If §2(1)(B) or §3(c) is struck down, the bill doesn't say whether the rest survives. Courts would decide severability on their own, which adds litigation risk.

21. **No rulemaking authority:** No agency is authorized to define terms or set compute-measurement methods, so the ambiguities in §§2 and 3 can't be resolved administratively and will likely be litigated.

22. **No preemption or savings clause:** The bill doesn't address how it interacts with state AI laws, which creates uncertainty for both compliance and enforcement.

23. **Undefined terms:** "Secretary," "person," "State," and "Department of Commerce" (as enforcer) are not defined in §2 or elsewhere.

24. **Constitutional exposure:** The most challengeable provisions are §2(1)(B) (vagueness and delegation), §3(c) (vagueness), and §5 (penalty structure and due process). Courts could strike these even if the policy goal is unaffected.
