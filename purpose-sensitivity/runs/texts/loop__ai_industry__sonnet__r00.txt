RESPONSE:
I read the bill as amended on Oct. 6. Each item is tagged **[L]** loophole (possible route to continued operation), **[A]** ambiguity, or **[D]** drafting defect or workability problem. This is an issue-spotting pass on the text as given, so counsel should verify the legal points, especially those that depend on other statutes or case law.

## Sec. 2(1): "Covered AI system"

1. **§2(1)(A), "single training run" [L/A].** The term is undefined and there is no aggregation rule, so a model built through staged pretraining, continued pretraining, RL phases, or separate runs under 10^26 may escape even if cumulative compute is far higher.
2. **§2(1)(A), compute measurement [A/D].** The bill doesn't say how operations are counted (theoretical vs. achieved, numeric precision) or whether the count includes failed or restarted runs, ablations, synthetic-data generation, or compute inherited from a teacher or base model.
3. **§2(1)(A), timing of coverage [A].** A model is covered only once it has been "trained using" more than 10^26 operations. It is unclear whether starting a run intended to cross the threshold is itself unlawful, or when an in-progress run becomes covered. This also complicates any "willfulness" showing.
4. **§2(1)(A), fixed threshold [D].** The threshold is static, with no agency authority to update it. It tracks compute rather than capability, so it is both under-inclusive (efficiency gains put capable models below it) and over-inclusive (models at the threshold need not approach "superintelligence").
5. **§2(1)(A)–(B), "model" vs. "system" [A/L].** Prong (A) covers a "model," prong (B) a "system," and neither term is defined. A system composed of several sub-threshold models, or a model plus scaffolding, may fall outside (A), and §3 prohibits training a "system."
6. **§2(1)(B), "the Secretary" [D].** No Secretary is identified. The Act mentions Commerce (§4) and the Secretary of State (§6) separately, so the officer with the key designation power is unspecified.
7. **§2(1)(B), the capability standard [A/D].** "Substantially exceeding," "most," and "cognitive tasks" are all undefined, as are the human reference population (median person, expert) and the measurement method. There is no benchmark or evidentiary standard.
8. **§2(1)(B), no procedure [D].** There is no notice, comment, findings, publication, deadline, appeal, or judicial review for a Secretary's determination. Covered status could therefore be unknowable in advance while the penalties run to $1M/day and prison.
9. **§2(1)(B), effect of a determination [A].** The bill doesn't say whether a determination operates only prospectively or reaches conduct before it, or whether it can reach systems protected by §3(b).
10. **Title vs. text [D].** "Superintelligence" is never defined and there is no findings or purpose section. Prong (A) is a pure compute test and prong (B) is closer to human-level than "vastly superhuman," so the operative text doesn't match the title or any stated purpose.

## Sec. 2(2)–(3): "U.S. person" and "covered facility"

11. **§2(2), narrow entity coverage [L].** The term includes only citizens and State-organized "corporations." It omits lawful permanent residents, LLCs, partnerships, trusts, unincorporated associations, and federally or territorially chartered entities. "State" is also undefined (D.C., territories).
12. **§2(2), foreign subsidiaries [L].** There is no ownership or control test, so a foreign-organized subsidiary of a U.S. company operating abroad is arguably not a "U.S. person." The extraterritorial reach in §3(a) then has a significant gap.
13. **§2(3), "advanced AI chips" [A].** There is no performance specification or counting rule (installed vs. operational, accelerators vs. packages), and no mechanism to update the definition.
14. **§2(3), the 50,000-chip, single-"data center" test [L/A].** "Data center" is undefined (building, campus, interconnected sites). Splitting capacity across sites, or training across multiple facilities each under 50,000 chips, avoids the registry.

## Sec. 3: Prohibition

15. **§3(a), "train, develop, or deploy" [A].** None of the verbs is defined. "Develop" could mean anything from architecture research to data curation, and "deploy" could mean internal use, API access, open-weight release, or hosting by third parties.
16. **§3(a), no use, possession, or hosting offense [L].** Only training, developing, and deploying are prohibited. Using, importing, hosting, or building on a covered system developed elsewhere isn't addressed.
17. **§3(a), territorial scope [L/A].** Foreign persons outside the U.S. are untouched even if they deploy into the U.S. market. For distributed or cloud training, it is unclear whether "in the United States" turns on the location of the chips, the staff, or the entity.
18. **§3(b), grandfathering covers all three verbs [L].** A covered system "whose training began" before enactment is exempt from the entire subsection, which arguably includes continued training, further development, scaling, and deployment, indefinitely. There is no compute cap, reporting requirement, or sunset on the exemption.
19. **§3(b), "training began" [A/L].** The term is undefined (pretraining start, any stage, checkpoint reuse). A run begun before enactment could arguably be extended, and a model not yet over the threshold at enactment might be grandfathered if it crosses later.
20. **§3(b), derivatives and successors [A].** The bill doesn't say whether fine-tunes, distillations, merges, or updated versions of a grandfathered model are the same "system" or new ones.
21. **§3(b) and §2(1)(B) [A].** It is unclear whether a post-enactment Secretary designation under (B) can reach a system whose training began earlier. If not, (B) is largely hollow for existing frontier systems.
22. **§3(b) vs. §8, enactment vs. effective date [A/D].** Grandfathering is keyed to enactment, but the prohibition starts 180 days later. A run begun in that window isn't grandfathered, but it also wasn't unlawful when started, and the bill doesn't say what happens on the effective date.
23. **§3(c), safety-evaluation exemption [L/D].** "Activities conducted for the purpose of safety evaluation" is undefined and has no limits on scale, independence, approval, or reporting. It exempts training, developing, and deploying, and the bill doesn't say whether a mixed-purpose activity qualifies or whose purpose controls.

## Sec. 4: Registry

24. **§4, no enforcement [D].** §5 penalizes only violations of §3, so nothing in the bill penalizes failing to register, failing to report, or filing a false certification.
25. **§4(a)–(b), no deadlines [D].** There is no date for initial registration, no first-report date, and no definition of "quarterly."
26. **§4, "operator" [A].** The term is undefined. For leased or multi-tenant facilities, it is unclear whether the owner, lessee, or cloud tenant is responsible and who certifies workloads the operator may not be able to see.
27. **§4(b), report content and certification [D].** "Describing the computing workloads" has no required content, granularity, or form. A report would not necessarily identify training runs, compute used, or models, so it can't be tied to §3.
28. **§4, no administrative machinery [D].** The bill gives no rulemaking authority, designated office, verification or inspection power, or confidentiality protection (trade secrets, FOIA, security) for reports.
29. **§4 vs. §2(1), registry doesn't track the prohibition [D].** Registration turns on chip count, while coverage turns on training compute. The registry can miss covered training done in smaller or distributed facilities, and the bill is silent on facilities abroad owned by U.S. persons.

## Sec. 5: Penalties

30. **§5, no enforcing authority [D].** The bill doesn't say who assesses or sues for civil penalties (AG, Commerce, other), or the procedure, venue, burden of proof, or review. It also doesn't say whether administrative adjudication is available.
31. **§5(a) vs. §8, retroactivity conflict [A/D].** Penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §3 doesn't take effect until 180 days later. This invites a due-process and fair-notice challenge, and the conflict would likely be construed narrowly.
32. **§5(a), "each day on which the violation occurs" [A].** It is unclear whether a months-long training run, or ongoing deployment, is one continuing violation or many. There are no mitigation factors, no scaling to size, and the $1M/day exposure raises a possible Excessive Fines argument.
33. **§5(a)–(b), mental state [A].** The civil penalty has no knowledge requirement and reads as strict liability. "Willfully" in (b) is undefined: it could mean knowing the system is covered or knowing the conduct is unlawful. That matters given the uncertain compute count and the (B) designation.
34. **§5, individuals vs. entities [A].** The bill doesn't address officer or employee liability, aiding and abetting, or how corporate criminal liability works. "Person" is undefined, though the Dictionary Act (1 U.S.C. §1) likely fills part of the gap.

## Sec. 6: International agreement

35. **§6, "shall seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months" [A/D].** It is unclear whether the deadline attaches to starting negotiations or concluding an agreement. "Equivalent" is undefined, and there is no reporting to Congress or consequence for failure. The U.S. prohibition continues regardless of what other countries do, which supports a competitiveness argument.

## Sec. 7: Duration

36. **§7(a) vs. §7(b) [D].** Subsection (a) says the prohibition lasts "until" Congress acts, while (b) imposes a hard 10-year sunset from enactment. The bill doesn't say which controls, and measured from the effective date the prohibition runs only about 9.5 years.
37. **§7(a), exit trigger [D].** A joint resolution needs presidential signature or a veto override. The NASEM report isn't mandated, funded, scoped, or deadlined, and NASEM, a private nonprofit, isn't obliged to produce one. "Can be developed safely" has no criteria.
38. **§7(a), all-or-nothing [D].** There is no partial relief, such as by capability class, licensing, or safe harbor. This supports an unworkability argument, since the only off-ramp is a global safety finding.
39. **§7(b), no savings clause [A].** The bill doesn't say what happens to accrued penalties or pending enforcement when the Act lapses or is lifted. Counsel should check whether 1 U.S.C. §109 applies to expiration, since abatement of pending actions is possible.

## Sec. 8 and general omissions

40. **§8, transition [D].** The 180-day delay has no transitional rules, wind-down provisions, guidance requirement, or rulemaking period. See also items 22 and 31.
41. **No severability clause [D].** If a court strikes a piece of the bill, such as prong (B) for vagueness, nothing says the rest survives.
42. **No exemptions or carve-outs [D].** There is nothing on national-security or government use, federal contractors, or non-frontier research. There is also no licensing pathway or safe harbor, and nothing on preemption of state law or interaction with other federal law.
43. **No appropriations or implementing authority [D].** No agency is funded or directed to implement the bill, apart from the unspecified "Secretary" and the Commerce registry.

## Constitutional themes for counsel

- **Vagueness and fair notice:** §2(1)(B), §3(a), and §5(b) attach criminal penalties to undefined terms.
- **Nondelegation:** §2(1)(B) gives the Secretary an unbounded designation power.
- **Retroactivity:** item 31.
- **Excessive Fines:** item 32.
- **First Amendment:** this is more contested, but the bill's reach to training code, weights, and publication may be arguable.

## Practical caution

Several of the strongest loophole items (18–23, 11–12, 14) are also the easiest for a committee amendment to close, and raising them publicly could prompt that. I'd suggest the policy team decide which items go in a markup letter, such as the vagueness, procedure, and conflict items (8, 31, 36), and which stay in privileged analysis. Also, I'd treat the grandfather and safety-evaluation readings as arguments for counsel to test rather than as a basis for operating assumptions, because the "willful" criminal exposure in §5(b) makes a wrong reading costly.
