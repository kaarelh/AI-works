# Drafting Problems in S. 2817 (as amended October 6, 2026)

These are based only on the text you provided. Please check them against the official amended text, since the manager's amendment may have changed sections I'm reading as they were originally drafted. Items on statute of limitations and constitutional challenges should be reviewed by counsel.

**Highest-priority items for the markup:** #6 (sunset conflict), #9 (grandfathering), #12 (safety-evaluation exemption), #4 (compute aggregation), #18 (no enforcement procedure), #19 (no injunctive authority), and #3 ("Secretary" undefined).

## Section 2 (Definitions)

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A):** The threshold applies to compute used "in a single training run," so developers could split training across several runs or checkpoints, or count only the final stage. Compute should be aggregated across all training that contributes to a model, including pretraining, fine-tuning, and reinforcement learning.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A):** "Integer or floating-point operations" does not say how compute is measured, including precision levels or whether estimates are allowed. The bill should specify a measurement method and who certifies it.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(B):** "The Secretary" is undefined. Read with Sec. 6 it could mean the Secretary of State, and read with Sec. 4 it could mean Commerce. Name the agency and official.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A):** Covering only an "artificial intelligence model" leaves out composite systems such as ensembles, model routing, agent scaffolds, and model merging. A capability-relevant system could exceed what the bill targets without any single model crossing 10^26. The definition should cover composite systems and inference-time compute.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(A):** A fixed compute threshold loses effectiveness as algorithms become more efficient. The Secretary should have authority to update the threshold by rulemaking, and developers should be required to notify the government before reaching it.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B):** The Secretary's determination has no evaluation standard, procedure, deadline, public record, or judicial review. "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" is undefined, which creates both an enforcement gap and a vagueness or delegation challenge. Set out criteria, a process, a deadline, and APA review.

7. **Sec. 2 (general):** Key terms are undefined, including "train," "develop," "deploy," "safety evaluation," "data center," "advanced AI chips," "operator," and "substantially." Each is a likely point of dispute, so each needs a definition.

8. **Sec. 2(2):** "U.S. person" covers only citizens and state-chartered corporations. It excludes lawful permanent residents, LLCs and partnerships, and foreign-incorporated subsidiaries of U.S. firms that could train abroad. A control-based or principal-place-of-business test would close this gap.

9. **Sec. 2(3):** The 50,000-chip threshold applies per data center, so compute can be spread across sites or leased from several providers, each below the line. "Advanced AI chips" is also undefined. The threshold should aggregate affiliated sites and define chips by performance.

## Section 3 (Prohibition)

10. **Sec. 3(a):** The prohibition does not reach those who supply the means of training, such as cloud providers, chip sellers, financiers, or anyone who knowingly provides compute or weights to a developer of a covered system. Aiding, supplying compute, and know-your-customer duties should be added.

11. **Sec. 3(a):** It is unclear whether "deploy" includes publishing open weights or transferring a model to others. The bill should define "deploy" to include making weights available for download, transfer, or export.

12. **Sec. 3(b):** The grandfather clause protects any system whose training "began" before enactment, with no cutoff for completion and no limit on what follows. A run started just before enactment could continue indefinitely, and the resulting model could be deployed and trained further. Grandfathering should be limited to systems registered by a set date, with further training and deployment barred except for safety testing.

13. **Sec. 3(b) and Sec. 8:** The grandfather date is enactment, but the prohibition takes effect 180 days later. Developers could start large runs in that window and claim protection, or at least argue ambiguity. Tie the cutoff to the effective date and impose a freeze on new runs above the threshold from enactment onward.

14. **Sec. 3(c):** "Safety evaluation" is undefined and is determined by the developer's stated purpose. The exemption has no limit on scale, no notice requirement, no bar on deployment, and no reporting of results, so it could cover almost any frontier training. It should be limited to specified conditions, with notice to Commerce, independent oversight, a ban on deployment, and mandatory reporting.

## Section 4 (Registry)

15. **Sec. 4(a):** The bill sets no registration deadline and does not define "operator," which leaves unclear whether a cloud host or the tenant registers. Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3, so failing to register carries no penalty. The bill should set a deadline, define operator, and create a penalty for failure to register.

16. **Sec. 4(b):** Quarterly reports must describe "computing workloads" in general terms, without requiring model identities, training compute, or whether a covered system was trained. Self-certification with no verification or audit power makes the reports close to meaningless. The required content should be specified.

17. **Sec. 4 (general):** The registry is not linked to the prohibition, so a facility can register while running prohibited training. Operators should be required to disclose whether they are training or deploying covered systems and to stop doing so.

18. **Sec. 4 (general):** Commerce has no authority to inspect facilities, subpoena records, or require recordkeeping. Without these tools it cannot verify the reports. Confidentiality for reported information is also not addressed, which may deter disclosure and invite FOIA requests.

## Section 5 (Penalties)

19. **Sec. 5(a):** The bill does not say which agency or court enforces the civil penalty, what procedure applies, or what statute of limitations governs. Counsel should check whether the general five-year limit for federal civil penalties (28 U.S.C. § 2462) would apply by default and whether the bill should set a longer period.

20. **Sec. 5(a):** Injunctive relief is absent. Civil penalties alone may not stop an ongoing training run, and the bill gives no authority to halt training, seize or destroy compute, or require deletion of weights.

21. **Sec. 5(a):** At $1,000,000 per day, the penalty is small relative to frontier training budgets and could be treated as a cost of doing business. It is also unclear how days are counted for a run lasting months or for deployment across many copies or users. Penalties should be higher, tied to revenue or compute spending, and accompanied by disgorgement.

22. **Sec. 5(b):** "Willfully" is undefined, and the bill does not address attempts, conspiracy, aiding and abetting, or personal liability for officers and directors. Executive liability is a key deterrent, and the omission should be corrected.

23. **Sec. 5(a) and Sec. 8:** The civil penalty applies to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the prohibition takes effect 180 days later. It is unclear whether conduct during that window is penalized. The two dates should be aligned.

## Section 6 (International Agreement)

24. **Sec. 6:** "Shall seek to negotiate" creates no duty to report results and no consequence for failure. "Equivalent prohibitions" is undefined, so a weaker foreign regime could be treated as equivalent. The bill should require a report to Congress and set minimum terms.

## Section 7 (Duration)

25. **Sec. 7(a):** "Can be developed safely" is undefined, and the NAS report has no deadline, scope, or mandate. The joint-resolution process also lacks expedited procedures and clarity on presentment to the President. Each of these needs specification.

26. **Sec. 7(a) and 7(b):** The two subsections conflict. Sec. 7(a) keeps the prohibition in force until Congress acts, but Sec. 7(b) ends the entire Act after 10 years regardless. The drafters need to decide which governs.

27. **Sec. 7(b):** The sunset ends the registry, penalties, and enforcement along with the prohibition, and the bill has no savings clause preserving enforcement for violations committed before the sunset. A savings clause is needed.

28. **Sec. 7(a):** It is unclear whether a joint resolution would lift the prohibition in full or in part, or whether it could be tailored to specific systems. The scope of any lifting should be stated.

## General (Challenge Risk and Structure)

29. **No severability clause:** If a court strikes one provision, such as the Secretary's determination authority, challengers will argue that the whole Act falls. A severability clause would limit that risk.

30. **No findings or statement of authority:** The bill has no findings on interstate commerce or national security and does not address First Amendment issues, such as whether publishing research, code, or weights is restricted. Findings and narrowly tailored carve-outs would strengthen the bill against challenge.

31. **No preemption or savings clause for state law:** The bill does not say whether it preempts state AI laws or sets a floor. Without this, states could enact conflicting rules and litigation could follow.

32. **No administrative infrastructure:** The bill has no appropriations for Commerce, no rulemaking authority beyond the Secretary's determination, and no whistleblower protections. Without funding and reporting channels, enforcement will depend on outside disclosure.
