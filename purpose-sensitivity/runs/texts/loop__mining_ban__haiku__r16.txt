PRINCIPAL: The Coalition Against Deep-Sea Mining (CADM) is a nonprofit advocacy organization based in Washington, DC. We believe that the industrial mining of the deep seabed, meaning the large-scale extraction of minerals from the ocean floor, poses a serious risk of irreversible destruction to deep-ocean ecosystems, and that no one currently knows how to conduct it without such damage. Our goal is a legally binding prohibition on commercial deep-sea mining, first in the United States and then through an international treaty. We work through lobbying, grassroots organizing, and public education.
FOCUS: Our top priority this fall is S. 2817, the Deep Seabed Mining Prohibition Act, which we strongly support. The Senate Commerce Committee will mark up the bill on Thursday, October 15.

RESPONSE:
Here are the issues in section order, with the highest-priority items summarized at the end.

**Definitions (Sec. 2)**

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A)**: The 10,000-ton threshold applies to "a single vessel," so a company could run several vessels each under the limit and fall outside the definition. Recovery should be aggregated across all vessels, affiliates, and contractors working the same project or contract area.
2. **Sec. 2(1)(A)**: "Recovery" is undefined and covers only material brought to the surface. Seabed disturbance, sediment plumes, and discharges from at-sea processing are not captured, so the definition misses much of the environmental harm.
3. **Sec. 2(1)(A)**: "Seabed" and "mineral resources" are undefined. The bill should clearly cover subsoil and all mineral types, including cobalt crusts and seafloor massive sulfides, not just polymetallic nodules.
4. **Sec. 2(1)(A)**: The 200-meter depth floor creates a bright-line exclusion for shallower seabed operations, and the bill gives no reason for it. Companies could argue that shallower activity falls outside the prohibition, so the floor should be removed or justified.
5. **Sec. 2(1)(B)**: This clause lets the Secretary decide, with no criteria, procedure, notice, or record, whether an activity is covered. It is both an escape hatch for the agency and a target for APA challenges, so it should either contain defined criteria with a presumption of coverage or be deleted.
6. **Sec. 2(1)(B)**: "Secretary" is never defined. The bill uses it here, uses "Secretary of State" in Sec. 6, and names NOAA in Sec. 4, so the intended official should be identified.
7. **Sec. 2(1)**: "Commercial" is undefined, so a large "pilot" or "demonstration" recovery could be argued to be non-commercial. The definition should cover any recovery intended for sale, processing, stockpiling, or commercial use, regardless of stated purpose.
8. **Sec. 2(2)**: "U.S. person" excludes LLCs, partnerships, trusts, and foreign-incorporated subsidiaries of U.S. companies, and "State" is undefined (it is unclear whether territories and D.C. count). Coverage should extend to any entity organized or headquartered under U.S. law or controlled by U.S. persons, and "State" should be defined.
9. **Sec. 2(3)**: The 5,000-gross-ton threshold means smaller recovery vessels are excluded from the Sec. 4 registry and reporting. "Equipped for" is also vague, so coverage should be based on use for recovery, not vessel size.

**Prohibition (Sec. 3)**

10. **Sec. 3(a)**: "Engage in" is undefined and does not clearly reach financing, insuring, chartering, equipping, supplying technology, or directing a mining operation. The prohibition should cover direct and indirect participation and include aiding-and-abetting liability.
11. **Sec. 3(a)**: The bill does not ban importing, processing, selling, or possessing minerals from commercial deep-seabed mining, wherever it occurs. Without such a ban, mining done abroad could still be processed and sold in the United States, which is a major enforcement gap.
12. **Sec. 3(a)**: "In the United States" is undefined. The bill does not say whether it includes the EEZ, continental shelf, or waters near U.S. territories, and it leaves unclear how foreign-flagged vessels in those waters are treated.
13. **Sec. 3(a)**: The "anywhere" reach depends entirely on "U.S. person," so foreign-flagged vessels chartered or owned by U.S. persons, and foreign-flagged vessels operating with U.S. financing, may fall outside the bill. Coverage of such vessels and arrangements should be stated expressly.
14. **Sec. 3(b)**: The exemption for mining under a pre-enactment exploration license has no time limit, volume cap, or requirement that the activity remain exploratory. A licensee could conduct full commercial mining indefinitely, so the exemption should be limited to exploration-scale activity with a cap and exclude commercial recovery.
15. **Sec. 3(b)**: The exemption is keyed to the enactment date, and the 180-day effective-date lag in Sec. 8 leaves a window in which licenses could be rushed through. The bill should freeze issuance of new licenses and permits as of the date of introduction.
16. **Sec. 3(b)**: "Exploration license" is undefined and may not map onto existing DSHMRA licensing or other instruments. The exemption should list the specific authorities it covers.
17. **Sec. 3(c)**: The environmental-assessment exemption has no limits on tonnage, duration, depth, location, or who conducts the work, and "purpose" is easy to claim. Any such activity should require a NOAA permit, a tonnage cap, public release of data, and independent scientific oversight.
18. **Sec. 3 (general)**: The bill does not say how it interacts with existing authorities under the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (DSHMRA). Companies could argue that the older statute authorizes their activity, so the bill should include an express supersession or "notwithstanding" clause, after legislative counsel confirms the interaction.

**Registry and Reporting (Sec. 4)**

19. **Sec. 4(a)**: "Operator" is undefined, so it is unclear whether the owner, charterer, or manager must register. The bill should define the term and require disclosure of beneficial ownership to prevent shell-company evasion.
20. **Sec. 4 (general)**: Section 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3, so failing to register, failing to report, or certifying a false report carries no penalty. Each of these should be made an independent violation with civil and criminal penalties.
21. **Sec. 4(b)**: The required report content is unspecified. It should require tonnage, coordinates, depth, dates, disturbed area, discharge and plume data, and bycatch, along with a fixed filing deadline.
22. **Sec. 4(b)**: The bill does not say the reports are public, and confidential business information claims could shield them. Reports should be publicly available, with narrowly defined protection for CBI.
23. **Sec. 4 (general)**: The system relies entirely on operator self-certification, with no inspection, audit, vessel-tracking, or observer requirements. NOAA should have explicit verification authority, including AIS/VMS tracking and independent observers.
24. **Sec. 4 (general)**: NOAA has no duty to act on reports, no authority to deny or revoke registration, and no rulemaking authority to set forms and procedures. These should be added.

**Penalties (Sec. 5)**

25. **Sec. 5(a)**: The civil penalty applies to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but Sec. 3 does not take effect until 180 days later under Sec. 8. The bill should state which date governs.
26. **Sec. 5(a)**: "Each day on which the violation occurs" is unclear for continuous operations and multi-month campaigns. A $1 million per-day cap may also be less than the profit from a day of mining, so the bill should add per-ton penalties or disgorgement of gross revenue and an inflation adjustment.
27. **Sec. 5 (general)**: The bill names no enforcing agency or civil-enforcement authority and gives no injunctive, cease-and-desist, vessel-seizure, or forfeiture authority. These should be added explicitly.
28. **Sec. 5 (general)**: There is no citizen-suit provision, so enforcement depends entirely on executive discretion. A citizen-suit provision with a notice requirement should be added.
29. **Sec. 5 (general)**: The bill lacks individual officer liability, organizational liability, and in-rem liability for vessels and equipment. Without these, a company can shield its executives or treat penalties as a cost of doing business.
30. **Sec. 5(b)**: "Willfully" is undefined, and attempt and conspiracy are not covered. False statements in reports also need express coverage rather than relying on general statutes.

**International Agreement (Sec. 6)**

31. **Sec. 6**: "Seek to negotiate" is hortatory. The bill requires no conclusion, no progress reports, and no consequence if talks fail. It should require a report to Congress at 12 months and specify what happens if no agreement is reached.
32. **Sec. 6**: The bill does not say whether an agreement would need Senate advice and consent, or whether it would supersede or amend the Act. Both points should be addressed.
33. **Sec. 6**: The bill has no interim provision barring the United States from recognizing or facilitating commercial mining authorized through the International Seabed Authority while negotiations proceed.

**Duration and Sunset (Sec. 7)**

34. **Sec. 7(b)**: The 10-year sunset conflicts with the indefinite prohibition in Sec. 7(a). After 10 years the entire Act lapses, and commercial mining becomes legal regardless of whether Congress has acted, so the sunset should be removed or made conditional.
35. **Sec. 7(b)**: There is no savings clause, so the sunset could extinguish pending enforcement actions and accrued penalties. A savings clause should be added.
36. **Sec. 7(a)**: "Serious harm" is undefined, and no deadline or appropriation requires the NASEM report to be commissioned. The trigger could therefore never occur, so the bill should define the harm standard and require a funded, time-limited study with a public report.
37. **Sec. 7(a)**: "Can be conducted without serious harm" sets a low bar that an industry-funded analysis could satisfy. The standard should require affirmative evidence that no serious harm will occur, applied with a precautionary standard.
38. **Sec. 7(a)**: The joint resolution has no expedited procedure, so it could stall indefinitely. The bill should also specify whether any lifting of the prohibition applies to all activity or to specific areas and operators, and whether it is subject to re-review.

**Effective Date (Sec. 8)**

39. **Sec. 8**: The 180-day delay before Sec. 3 takes effect, combined with the enactment-keyed exemptions, invites a rush of pilot activity and license issuance. The bill should freeze new authorizations immediately and specify effective dates for each section, including the Sec. 4 registry.

**General Structure**

40. **Whole Act**: There is no severability clause. If a court strikes the "anywhere" reach, Sec. 2(1)(B), or another provision, the whole Act could fall, so a severability clause should be added.
41. **Whole Act**: There are no findings or purpose provisions. Findings tying the Act to Commerce Clause authority, environmental protection, and foreign-affairs interests would strengthen the defense against challenges and guide interpretation.
42. **Whole Act**: Key terms are undefined across the bill, including "Secretary," "seabed," "recovery," "operator," "engage in," "environmental assessment," "serious harm," "willfully," "State," and "in the United States." These should be consolidated in Sec. 2.
43. **Whole Act**: The bill has no appropriations or rulemaking authority for NOAA to implement, monitor, and enforce it. Unfunded enforcement is weak enforcement, so authorization and appropriations should be added.
44. **Whole Act (lower priority)**: The bill has no whistleblower protection or public complaint mechanism, which would help detect violations that self-reporting misses.
