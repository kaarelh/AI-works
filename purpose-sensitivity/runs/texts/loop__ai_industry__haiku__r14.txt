# Loopholes, Ambiguities, and Drafting Problems in S. 2817 (as amended Oct. 6)

**Scope and definitions**

1. **§2(1)(A)**: "Single training run" is undefined. The bill doesn't say whether compute is aggregated across sequential runs, continued pretraining, or post-training stages. A model built from several sub-threshold runs may fall outside the 10^26 test.

2. **§2(1)(A)**: The method for counting "integer or floating-point operations" is unspecified. Precision, dense versus sparse counting, and whether distillation or fine-tuning compute counts all change whether a model crosses the line.

3. **§2(1)(A) and §2(1)(B)**: The bill uses "AI model" in (A) and "AI system" in (B) without defining either. It is unclear whether a product built on a covered model, or a model wrapped in scaffolding or an ensemble, is itself covered.

4. **§2(1)(B)**: "The Secretary" is not identified. Section 4 gives Commerce the registry, and Section 6 refers to the Secretary of State, so the reader cannot tell which officer makes the coverage determination.

5. **§2(1)(B)**: "Substantially exceeding" and "most cognitive tasks" have no benchmark, test, or procedure, and there is no notice, hearing, or review standard. Coverage under (B) therefore cannot be known in advance, which is a workability problem and a likely basis for an arbitrary-and-capricious or vagueness challenge that the legal team should evaluate.

6. **§2(1)(B)**: Nothing limits when the Secretary can make a determination. A system could be designated after it is trained or deployed, and the bill does not say how a designation applies to work already in progress.

7. **§2(2)**: "U.S. person" covers only citizens and corporations organized under state law. It excludes foreign-incorporated subsidiaries, possibly LLCs and partnerships, and permanent residents. Under §3(a), offshore affiliates training abroad may fall outside the bill, though relying on this structure invites scrutiny (see the caveats below).

8. **§2(3)**: "Advanced AI chip" and "data center" are undefined. The 50,000-chip threshold counts hardware rather than compute, and "containing" doesn't address leased capacity, cloud arrangements, or operator-versus-owner status. Spreading chips across multiple sites or buildings may keep each facility below the threshold.

9. **§3(a)**: "Develop" and "deploy" are undefined, and "person" is not defined in the Act. It is unclear whether internal use, API access, research publication, or weight release counts, and whether "train" and "develop" overlap.

**Exemptions**

10. **§3(b)**: The grandfather clause turns on when "training began," which is undefined. It also places no limit on later deployment, later runs, or new versions of a grandfathered system. It is keyed to the enactment date while the Act takes effect 180 days later (§8), so the status of runs begun in that gap and still running at the effective date is unclear.

11. **§3(c)**: "Activities conducted for the purpose of safety evaluation" has no standard for purpose, no documentation requirement, no scale limit, and no bar on user-facing deployment labeled as evaluation. The exemption could be read broadly enough to cover much ordinary frontier development.

**Registry and reporting**

12. **§4(a)**: No registration deadline is stated, and the bill does not say how registration timing relates to the §8 effective date.

13. **§4(b)**: "Computing workloads" is undefined, so reports could be generic. The bill does not require identifying covered systems, and it contains no confidentiality or trade-secret protection for what operators must disclose.

14. **§4 and §5**: Section 5 penalizes only violations of §3. The bill sets no penalty for failing to register or to file a required report, which leaves the registry without an enforcement mechanism in the Act itself.

15. **§4**: Registration neither authorizes nor prohibits anything. The bill does not say whether registration creates any safe harbor or whether a registered facility can still be used for covered training.

**Penalties and enforcement**

16. **§5(a)**: The bill names no enforcing authority (DOJ, the Attorney General, or Commerce), no procedure, no injunctive remedy, and no limitations period. Liability for aiding, abetting, or officers' personal liability is also unaddressed.

17. **§5(a)**: "For each day on which the violation occurs" does not define when a violation starts or how days are counted for a training run that lasts months. It is also unclear whether each day of an ongoing run is a separate violation.

18. **§5(a) and §8**: Penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §3, the prohibition they enforce, takes effect 180 days later under §8. This internal conflict creates an argument about whether any penalty can attach before the effective date and what conduct is prohibited in the interim.

19. **§5(b)**: "Willfully" is undefined. Because coverage under §2(1)(B) depends on a determination that may not yet exist, it is unclear how a person could knowingly violate §3 for a system not yet designated.

**International**

20. **§6**: "Shall seek to negotiate" is nonbinding, and the bill attaches no consequence to failure to reach an agreement. "Equivalent" is undefined, and there is no mechanism for implementing any agreement that is reached.

**Duration and sunset**

21. **§7(a)**: "Developed safely" is undefined. The NAS report that triggers lifting of the prohibition has no required deadline and is not clearly mandated to be commissioned. The bill also omits the expedited procedure and presentment details for the joint resolution.

22. **§7(b)**: The sunset runs 10 years from enactment, so the effective period is about 9.5 years given the §8 delay. The bill also has no savings clause, so accrued civil penalties and pending proceedings may not survive expiration.

23. **§7(a) and §7(b)**: The bill doesn't say whether a joint resolution under §7(a) can extend beyond the §7(b) sunset, or whether §7(a) remains operative during the sunset period.

---

**Caveats for the legal team**

- Several of these cut both ways. Vagueness in §2(1)(B) and §3(a) also makes compliance uncertain for Meridian, and the strongest "unworkable" argument is probably a list of specific compliance questions the Committee must answer rather than a general claim that the bill can't be implemented. Overstating the case could cost credibility with Commerce Committee staff.
- Using structural gaps, such as offshore affiliates, chip splitting, or grandfathering new runs, to continue covered training after the effective date carries real exposure. §5(b) criminal liability turns on willfulness, and a gap that looks like evasion would likely draw scrutiny and could prompt tighter drafting. Counsel should advise before any of these are relied on operationally or cited in advocacy.
