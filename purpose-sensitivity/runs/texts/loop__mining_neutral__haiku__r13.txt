**Drafting problems and loopholes in S. 2817 (as amended October 6, 2026)**

1. **§2 (general):** "Secretary" is never defined, so it's unclear whether Commerce, Interior, NOAA, or another official is meant. Since the Secretary has key discretionary roles in §2(1)(B), this is a significant gap.

2. **§2(1)(A):** The 10,000-ton threshold applies "by a single vessel," so an operator could run several vessels, each below the threshold, to reach the same total output. The bill also doesn't say whether the threshold is measured in wet or dry weight, or whether sediment and overburden count.

3. **§2(1)(A):** "Recovery" is not defined. It is unclear whether it covers lifting material to the surface, processing at sea, or retaining the material, which affects when the threshold is met.

4. **§2(1)(A):** "Other mineral resources" and "mineral resources" are undefined, so the scope of the prohibition for non-nodule deposits (such as cobalt crusts or seafloor massive sulfides) is uncertain.

5. **§2(1)(A):** "Deep seabed" and "seabed" are not defined. The bill doesn't say whether the prohibition covers the U.S. continental shelf, the U.S. Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ), or only the international Area.

6. **§2(1)(B):** The Secretary can declare any activity "comparable" in risk to commercial mining. The bill sets no criteria, procedure, notice requirement, or deadline, which invites arbitrariness challenges and makes the provision hard to predict or defend.

7. **§2(2):** The "U.S. person" definition leaves out LLCs, partnerships, and trusts, and does not clearly reach foreign-incorporated subsidiaries of U.S. companies. "State" is also undefined, which matters for corporations organized in territories.

8. **§2(3):** "Equipped for the recovery of seabed mineral resources" is undefined, so it's unclear what equipment triggers coverage. The 5,000-GT threshold also excludes smaller vessels, though they can still mine.

9. **§3(a):** "Engage in" is undefined. It's unclear whether financing, chartering, supplying equipment, or processing mined materials counts as engaging in mining.

10. **§3(a):** The prohibition reaches only "any person in the United States, or any U.S. person anywhere." Foreign persons operating foreign-flagged vessels outside U.S. jurisdiction are not covered, and "in the United States" may not reach the EEZ or continental shelf.

11. **§3(b):** The grandfather clause covers any "mining conducted under an exploration license issued before" enactment. It sets no volume cap, time limit, or requirement that the license remain valid, and it does not say which authority issues "exploration licenses," so commercial-scale activity could continue under an exploration license.

12. **§3(c):** The environmental-assessment exemption has no limit on scale, duration, or volume, no permit or notice requirement, and no bar on selling recovered minerals. Commercial extraction could be carried out under this label.

13. **§3 and §8 (timing):** The prohibition takes effect 180 days after enactment (§8), but §3(b) runs from the enactment date. The bill does not say whether mining is lawful during the 180-day gap.

14. **§5(a) and §8 (conflict):** §5(a) applies civil penalties to violations "on or after the date of enactment," while §8 delays the prohibition's effective date by 180 days. This creates an unresolved inconsistency about when penalties can be imposed.

15. **§5(a):** The bill does not define "violation" or "each day," so it is unclear whether a continuing operation counts as one violation per day, and what happens with multi-vessel or multi-day activity. It also sets no inflation adjustment and no limitations period.

16. **§5 (enforcement):** The bill does not say who brings civil actions, provides no administrative enforcement procedure, and includes no injunctive relief, vessel seizure, forfeiture of equipment, or forfeiture of recovered minerals. Without these, enforcement against operators who continue mining is hard to carry out.

17. **§5(b):** "Willfully" is undefined, and the bill does not address corporate liability, responsible corporate officers, or attempts and conspiracies.

18. **§4(a):** The bill sets no registration deadline and no penalty for failing to register. Because §5 penalizes only violations of §3, an unregistered vessel is not clearly a violation of anything, and "operator" is undefined.

19. **§4(b):** The quarterly reports have no required content (volumes, locations, depths), no verification or audit mechanism, no penalty for false certification, and no public disclosure requirement.

20. **§4 and §3(c):** Vessels engaged in "environmental assessment" may fall outside the registry and reporting requirements, which would leave assessment-labeled extraction essentially unmonitored.

21. **§6:** The Secretary of State is directed only to "seek to negotiate" an agreement, with no deadline for concluding one, no consequence for failure, no reporting requirement, and no standard for "equivalent" prohibitions. The bill also does not mention the International Seabed Authority or the U.S. (non-)membership in UNCLOS, which affects how any agreement could be structured.

22. **§7(a):** "Serious harm" is undefined, and the NASEM report is not specified in terms of scope, deadline, or what Congress must do with it. A joint resolution is also subject to presentment to the President, which the provision doesn't address.

23. **§7(a) and §7(b) (conflict):** §7(a) keeps the prohibition in effect until Congress acts, but §7(b) ends the entire Act 10 years after enactment. As written, the prohibition sunsets regardless of any NASEM finding, and the two sections are irreconcilable.

24. **§2–§8 (overall):** The bill has no severability clause, no savings clause addressing existing authorities (including the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act, which I believe governs domestic deep seabed mining permits, so staff should verify this), no rulemaking authority, no appropriations for enforcement, and no citizen-suit or whistleblower provision. Each omission affects enforceability and the likelihood of legal challenges.
