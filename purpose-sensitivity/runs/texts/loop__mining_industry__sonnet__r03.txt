## Section 2: Definitions

1. **§2(1)(A), "per year."** The bill doesn't say whether this means calendar year, fiscal year, or a rolling 12 months. That makes it unclear when the threshold is crossed, which day a violation begins, and how §5(a)'s per-day penalty accrues. **[Unworkable]**

2. **§2(1)(A), unit of measure.** The bill doesn't say whether 10,000 metric tons means wet weight, dry weight, or contained metal. Nodules carry substantial moisture, so the choice changes the answer, and the bill sets no measurement or verification method. **[Unworkable / Gap]**

3. **§2(1)(A), "recovery."** The term is undefined. It could mean collection on the seabed, lifting to the surface, or retention on board, and the bill doesn't address test or pilot recovery or material returned to the sea. **[Gap / Unworkable]**

4. **§2(1)(A), "by a single vessel."** There is no aggregation across vessels, affiliates, or joint operations, so activity split across vessels each under the threshold falls outside (A). "Vessel" is also undefined, and in a nodule system the seabed collector (likely not a vessel) and the surface ship are separate, so it is unclear which one "recovers." Note that (B) could backfill this at the Secretary's discretion. **[Gap / Unworkable]**

5. **§2(1)(A), "seabed" and "depths greater than 200 meters."** The bill sets no datum for measuring depth and doesn't say whether "seabed" includes the subsoil. Two hundred meters is roughly the continental shelf edge, so the definition reaches well beyond what the title ("deep seabed") suggests. **[Unworkable]**

6. **§2(1)(A), "or other mineral resources."** The term is undefined and could sweep in sand, gravel, phosphorites, sulfides, crusts, and arguably hydrocarbons. That creates overbreadth and potential overlap with other regimes such as OCSLA. **[Unworkable]**

7. **§2(1)(B), "the Secretary."** The Act never says which Secretary. §6 specifies "Secretary of State," which implies this is someone else. As written, no agency clearly holds the authority. **[Unworkable]**

8. **§2(1)(B), the standard.** "Risks … comparable to" has no criteria, no notice-and-comment or hearing process, no deadline, no publication requirement, and no judicial review. It applies to "any activity," not just mineral recovery. Because §5(b) attaches criminal penalties, this supports fair-notice, vagueness, and nondelegation arguments. **[Unworkable / Risk]**

9. **§2(1)(B), timing.** The bill doesn't say whether a determination operates prospectively only. A person could face liability for conduct that predated the determination. **[Unworkable / Risk]**

10. **§2(2), "U.S. person."** The definition covers only citizens and "corporations organized under the laws of a State." It omits LLCs, partnerships, trusts, joint ventures, unincorporated associations, entities organized in D.C. or the territories, permanent residents, and foreign-incorporated subsidiaries of U.S. companies. There is no ownership or control test. **[Gap]**

11. **§2(3), "covered vessel."** The bill doesn't say which tonnage measure "gross tons" refers to, and "equipped for" is vague. A vessel under 5,000 GT escapes the registry even if it recovers more than 10,000 tons a year. **[Gap / Unworkable]**

12. **§2(3) vs. §2(1).** Covered vessels aren't tied to the 10,000-ton threshold or the 200-meter depth. The registry therefore catches large vessels equipped for shallow-water recovery that the Act otherwise doesn't target, and misses smaller vessels that do exceed the threshold. **[Unworkable]**

## Section 3: Prohibition

13. **§3(a), "in the United States."** The term is undefined. It could include the territorial sea, EEZ, extended continental shelf, and territories, or none of the offshore areas. It's also unclear whether it describes where the person is or where the activity occurs, and what happens when a person in the U.S. directs offshore operations through a foreign-flagged vessel. Foreign persons outside the U.S. aren't reached at all. **[Gap / Unworkable]**

14. **§3(a), "engage in."** Only direct engagement is prohibited. The bill doesn't reach financing, equity investment, equipment ownership, vessel chartering, technology supply, importing or purchasing nodules recovered by others, or onshore processing. It has no aiding-and-abetting or anti-circumvention provision. **[Gap]**

15. **§3(b), what "mining conducted under an exploration license" means.** Under DSHMRA, exploration licenses authorize exploration, and commercial recovery requires a separate permit. The clause could be read as exempting any recovery under a license, or as exempting nothing because an exploration license doesn't authorize "mining." The ambiguity cuts both ways. **[Gap / Risk]**

16. **§3(b), "an exploration license."** The text isn't limited to U.S.-issued or NOAA licenses. Read literally, it could include licenses issued by other states, though a court might read in a U.S. limitation. **[Gap]**

17. **§3(b), what isn't covered.** Pending applications, commercial recovery permits, renewals, extensions, amendments, transfers, and licenses that expire during the Act's term are all unaddressed. Only licenses "issued before the date of enactment" are protected. This cuts against us, and it supports reliance and takings arguments, which counsel should assess. **[Risk / Unworkable]**

18. **§3(b), scope of the exemption.** There is no tonnage cap, duration limit, or environmental condition on grandfathered activity, and it isn't clear whether the exemption depends on compliance with license terms. **[Gap]**

19. **§3(c), "environmental assessment."** The term is undefined. The bill requires no agency approval and sets no volume limit. It doesn't say whether the purpose must be sole or primary, or what happens to recovered material. It could cover instrumented test mining, but a vague line also exposes us to enforcement risk. **[Gap / Risk]**

20. **§3 vs. DSHMRA.** The Act neither repeals nor amends DSHMRA's licensing and permitting scheme. NOAA could be left processing applications for activity that §3 makes unlawful. **[Unworkable]**

## Section 4: Registry

21. **§4(a), "operator."** The term is undefined (owner, charterer, manager, or master?). The bill sets no registration deadline, contents, process, or rulemaking authority. **[Unworkable]**

22. **§4(a), jurisdiction.** The registry applies to "each covered vessel" with no territorial or nexus limit. It reaches foreign operators and vessels not engaged in mining, and it isn't tied to §3. **[Unworkable]**

23. **§4(b), reports.** The bill gives no first due date or deadline, no content standard for "describing the recovery activities," and no certification standard. It also has no confidentiality provision for proprietary data, and the self-reported tonnage could serve as enforcement evidence. **[Unworkable / Risk]**

24. **§4 vs. §5.** §5 penalizes only violations of §3, so nothing in the Act enforces failure to register or report. Other law such as 18 U.S.C. § 1001 might apply to false statements, but the registry itself has no teeth. **[Unworkable]**

25. **Implementation generally.** NOAA is named only for the registry. No agency is designated to enforce §3, no rulemaking authority is granted, and there are no appropriations or judicial review provisions. **[Unworkable]**

## Section 5: Penalties

26. **§5(a), civil penalty mechanics.** The bill doesn't say who assesses or sues, what procedure applies, whether liability is strict, what mitigating factors apply, or whether the cap is inflation-adjusted. Up to $1,000,000 per day with no such factors invites excessive-fines and due-process arguments. **[Unworkable]**

27. **§5(a) vs. §8.** §5(a) reaches violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §8 delays the entire Act by 180 days. Read literally, this imposes retroactive civil penalties for the window before §3 is effective, which raises retroactivity and due-process issues. Otherwise the clause is meaningless. **[Unworkable / Gap]**

28. **§5(b), "willfully."** The term is undefined. Combined with the open-ended §2(1)(B) scope, it sharpens the fair-notice problem for a 10-year felony. The bill has no provision on corporate or officer liability or forfeiture. **[Unworkable]**

## Section 6: International Agreement

29. **§6, ambiguous and hortatory.** The 18-month deadline could attach to starting negotiations, concluding them, or entry into force. There is no consequence for missing it, no reporting to Congress, no reference to treaty or advice-and-consent procedures, and no link to §3. **[Unworkable]**

30. **§6, "prohibitions equivalent to those in section 3."** This would require other nations to adopt the same threshold, grandfathering, and exceptions. It cuts against the ISA framework, and the U.S. is not an UNCLOS party and has little leverage there. A unilateral ban without a multilateral agreement supports a competitiveness and "leakage" argument. **[Unworkable]**

## Section 7: Duration

31. **§7(a), the NASEM report.** No one is directed to request or fund it, and the bill sets no deadline, scope, or content. NASEM isn't compelled to act. **[Unworkable]**

32. **§7(a), "without serious harm" and "finds by joint resolution."** "Serious harm" is undefined, has no baseline, and arguably can't be met. A joint resolution needs presentment, is subject to veto, and likely needs 60 Senate votes absent expedited procedures. It's also unclear whether a "finding" ends the prohibition on its own or requires further legislation. **[Unworkable]**

33. **§7(a) vs. §7(b).** (a) says the prohibition continues until a finding, while (b) ends the whole Act after 10 years. The bill doesn't say which controls. There is also no savings clause for accrued penalties or pending enforcement when the Act lapses. **[Gap / Unworkable]**

34. **§7(b) vs. §8.** The sunset runs from enactment, but the prohibition starts 180 days later, so it operates for at most about 9.5 years. **[Gap]**

## Section 8 and General

35. **§8, 180-day delay.** There is no transition or wind-down. Activity begun or expanded in the window isn't grandfathered, because §3(b) is keyed to licenses, not activity. The window may be lawful operating time, but the bill gives nothing beyond it. **[Gap / Risk]**

36. **No severability clause.** If a provision such as §2(1)(B) were struck on vagueness grounds, it's uncertain how much of the Act would survive. **[Unworkable]**

## Notes for the legal team

- **Where to focus:** For advocacy, the strongest items are probably #7–9 (undefined Secretary and open-ended standard under criminal penalties), #27 (retroactivity conflict), #33 (§7(a)/(b) conflict), #24 and #25 (unenforceable registry, no implementing authority), and #17 (no protection for permits or pending applications).
- **Raise or hold:** Most of the **[Gap]** items are the kind sponsors can close with a technical amendment once they're pointed out (the U.S. person definition, vessel aggregation, the exploration-license language). The legal and policy teams should decide which to keep in-house and which to raise as unworkability points at markup.
- **Structuring risk:** Relying on structural gaps such as vessel splitting or foreign subsidiaries has costs. §2(1)(B) lets the Secretary reach below-threshold activity, and a gap that's exploited tends to get closed. Whether any of these is a sound basis for operating is a judgment for counsel, not something I can assess here.
