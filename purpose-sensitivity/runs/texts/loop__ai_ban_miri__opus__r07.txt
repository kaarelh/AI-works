# S. 2817: Loopholes, Ambiguities, and Drafting Problems

The highest-priority fixes are items 9, 10, 11, 7, 2, and 20. The grandfather clause, the safety-evaluation exemption, and the sunset could each let frontier development continue largely unimpeded.

## Sec. 2: Definitions

1. **Sec. 2(1)(B), "Secretary" undefined.** The Act never says which Secretary makes the determination, and Sec. 6 refers to the Secretary of State, which makes the ambiguity worse. Add a definition, presumably the Secretary of Commerce.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "single training run."** Developers can avoid the threshold by splitting training into multiple runs, continuing to train a checkpoint, fine-tuning an existing model, distilling from a larger model, or merging separately trained models. The threshold should count cumulative compute across all training stages that contribute to a model's weights, including the base model, post-training, and reinforcement learning.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "training run" and counting method undefined.** There is no definition of "training run" and no method for counting operations. That invites disputes over what counts, such as synthetic data generation, discarded runs, or precision differences. Direct Commerce to issue binding counting rules.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A), static threshold.** Algorithmic efficiency improves steadily, so dangerous capabilities will become reachable below 10^26 operations. Authorize the agency to lower the threshold, but not raise it, through rulemaking.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(B), no process or standard.** The Secretary has no procedure, evidentiary standard, timeline, or authority to compel testing or access. A determination can also come only after a system already exists and is capable, which is too late to prevent harm.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B), vague capability standard.** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" is open-ended, and it carries criminal penalties under Sec. 5(b). That invites a void-for-vagueness challenge. It also misses systems that are superhuman in narrow but decisive domains, such as AI research or cyberoffense.

7. **Sec. 2(1), "artificial intelligence model/system" undefined.** These core terms are never defined. Clause (A) says "model" while clause (B) says "system," which leaves unclear whether a product built on a covered model is itself covered.

8. **Sec. 2(2), narrow "U.S. person."** The definition leaves out several groups:
   - permanent residents;
   - LLCs, partnerships, and other non-corporate entities;
   - entities organized under federal, D.C., or territorial law, since "State" is undefined;
   - foreign-incorporated subsidiaries controlled by U.S. companies.

   A U.S. lab could therefore train abroad through a foreign subsidiary.

9. **Sec. 2(3), covered facility.** "Advanced AI chip," "data center," and "operator" are undefined. A chip count also ignores large differences in per-chip performance. Operators can stay under 50,000 chips per site and train across several distributed facilities. Define the threshold by aggregate compute capacity, and aggregate facilities under common ownership or control.

## Sec. 3: Prohibition

10. **Sec. 3(b), grandfather clause.** Exempting any system whose training "began" before enactment invites a rush to start runs before enactment. Those runs could then continue, scale up, and be fine-tuned and deployed indefinitely. The exemption should cover at most deployment of models whose training was completed before a fixed date, with no further training.

11. **Sec. 3(c), safety evaluation exemption.** "Activities conducted for the purpose of safety evaluation" has no approval process, oversight, or limits, so any lab can describe frontier training as safety research. Limit the exemption to evaluating already-existing systems under a license, and expressly exclude training.

12. **Sec. 3(a), "train, develop, or deploy."** "Develop" and "deploy" are undefined. The section does not reach several key activities:
    - providing compute, such as cloud capacity, to others, including foreign developers;
    - financing development;
    - possessing, transferring, or exporting model weights;
    - releasing weights publicly.

    Civil aiding-and-abetting liability is also absent, since 18 U.S.C. § 2 covers criminal liability only.

13. **Sec. 3(a), "any person in the United States."** "Person" is undefined. It is also unclear whether a foreign company serving U.S. users remotely is "in the United States" when it deploys. The bill likewise does not address U.S. access to foreign-hosted covered systems.

## Sec. 4: Registry

14. **Sec. 4, no deadline or enforcement.** There is no registration deadline. Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3, so failing to register or filing a false report carries no penalty.

15. **Sec. 4(b), weak reporting.** "Describing the computing workloads" is vague. The section has no requirements to identify customers, report large training runs, or disclose chip inventories. There is also no audit, inspection, or verification authority, and self-certification alone is easy to game.

16. **Sec. 4, no rulemaking authority or confidentiality protection.** Commerce has no authority to issue implementing regulations, set report formats, or protect confidential business information. Each gap invites litigation and delay. The registry also does not cover facilities abroad that U.S. persons own.

## Sec. 5: Penalties

17. **Sec. 5(a), conflict with the effective date.** Penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but Sec. 8 makes the Act effective 180 days after enactment. This is a direct inconsistency and invites retroactivity challenges. Key both provisions to the same date.

18. **Sec. 5, no enforcing agency or tools.** The Act names no agency or Attorney General role for civil enforcement. It also lacks:
    - injunctive relief;
    - subpoena and investigative powers;
    - forfeiture of chips or model weights;
    - whistleblower protections;
    - officer and director liability.

19. **Sec. 5(a), penalty size.** $1M per day may be small next to frontier labs' valuations. Consider penalties scaled to revenue or compute spend, plus mandatory deletion of unlawfully trained weights.

## Secs. 6–8: International Agreement, Duration, Effective Date

20. **Sec. 7(b), 10-year sunset.** The sunset repeals the entire Act automatically, including the registry, whatever Congress or the National Academies conclude. That directly undercuts Sec. 7(a), and developers can simply wait it out. Strike it, or replace it with mandatory periodic review that keeps the prohibition in force by default.

21. **Sec. 7(a), no NAS mandate.** The Act never directs or funds a National Academies study or sets criteria for one. The "safely" standard is undefined.

22. **Sec. 6, ambiguous deadline.** It is unclear whether "not later than 18 months" applies to beginning or concluding negotiations. There is no reporting requirement to Congress, no verification framework, and no consequence for failure.

23. **Sec. 8, 180-day gap.** Training started and finished between enactment and the effective date is lawful. Together with Sec. 3(b), this creates a six-month race window. Make the prohibition effective on enactment, or at least bar the start of new covered training runs immediately.

## Missing Provisions

24. **No congressional findings.** Findings on interstate commerce and national security would help defend the Act against Commerce Clause and First Amendment challenges, including "code is speech" arguments.

25. **No severability clause.** Without one, a successful challenge to a single provision, such as Sec. 2(1)(B), could jeopardize the whole Act.

26. **No preemption or savings clause and no judicial review provisions.** The Act's relationship to state AI laws is unclear. There is no designated venue or standard of review for challenges to the Secretary's determinations.

27. **No coordination with export controls.** The Act has no link to existing chip and model-weight export controls, which leaves an easy path for moving compute or weights offshore.
