Items are in section order. Constitutional and litigation-risk points are judgment calls, so have counsel confirm them before anything goes to Senator Whitfield's office. The items I'd put first are 1, 2, 7, 9, 10, 14, 16, 17, 20, 21, and 22.

**Section 2 (Definitions)**

1. **§2(1)(A), "single training run."** The term is undefined, so a developer can split training into staged or continued runs, or merge models, to stay under 10^26. The bill doesn't say whether pre-training, post-training/RL, fine-tuning, or compute inherited from a base model are aggregated.

2. **§2(1)(A), fixed compute threshold.** A static number is both under- and over-inclusive. Algorithmic efficiency gains will let dangerous systems fall below it, and it catches systems that aren't superintelligent, which invites an overbreadth challenge. There is no mechanism to adjust the threshold and no counting methodology (what counts as an "operation," synthetic-data generation, distillation from a larger model).

3. **§2(1)(A), inference-time compute.** Only training compute counts. A model trained below the threshold that gets its capability from heavy test-time compute or scaffolding is not covered unless the Secretary acts under (B).

4. **§2(1)(A), "model" versus "system."** Subparagraph (A) defines a *model*, (B) defines a *system*, and §3 prohibits training, developing, or deploying a *system*. A developer could argue that a system built from several sub-threshold models is not covered.

5. **§2(1)(B), undefined "Secretary."** The bill never says which Secretary. §6 specifies the Secretary of State, which makes the bare reference here more ambiguous.

6. **§2(1)(B), no standard or process.** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no benchmarks, evaluation method, deadline, notice, or rulemaking requirement. That invites vagueness, fair-notice, and nondelegation challenges, and companies can claim no one could know whether a system was covered.

7. **§2(1)(B), timing and effect of a determination.** The bill doesn't say whether a system becomes covered only after a determination, or whether the determination is retroactive. If it is prospective only, developers can train and deploy until the Secretary acts. If it is retroactive, the criminal "willfulness" analysis becomes murky.

8. **§2(2), "U.S. person" is too narrow.** It omits lawful permanent residents, partnerships, LLCs, unincorporated associations, and entities organized in D.C. or the territories. It also omits foreign subsidiaries and foreign-incorporated entities that US companies or investors control, so a company can move development offshore through a foreign affiliate.

9. **§2(3), "covered facility."** "Advanced AI chips" and "data center" are undefined. A 50,000-chip bright line invites operators to split capacity across sub-threshold sites or entities, rent compute across multiple facilities, or wait for per-chip performance gains to make a smaller facility equally capable. The threshold is also unrelated to the 10^26 training threshold.

**Section 3 (Prohibition)**

10. **§3(a), "train, develop, or deploy" are undefined.** "Develop" could be read to cover only the final training run and not research, data preparation, or architecture work. "Deploy" doesn't clearly cover hosting, API access, internal use, or importing and using a foreign-trained model.

11. **§3(a), no facilitation, financing, or supply-side prohibition.** Providing compute, selling chips, funding, or exporting or transferring model weights is not covered except through general aiding-and-abetting law. Cloud providers can argue they merely rent hardware.

12. **§3(a), jurisdictional reach.** A foreign company operating abroad is outside the prohibition even if it serves US customers or uses US-made chips. The "person" in "any person in the United States" is undefined, and a purely domestic activity may draw Commerce Clause challenges unless the bill includes findings.

13. **§3(b), grandfather clause has no limits.** Systems whose training "began" before enactment can continue to be trained, scaled, fine-tuned, updated, and deployed indefinitely. "Began" is undefined and there is no record-keeping requirement to verify start dates, so companies can start runs between introduction and enactment and later claim they predate it. The bill doesn't say whether a major continuation or successor model counts as a new system.

14. **§3(b), interaction with §2(1)(B).** A system the Secretary later determines to be covered under (B) is still exempt if its training began before enactment. The grandfather clause could swallow the most important category of systems, and nothing regulates the grandfathered systems at all (no caps, monitoring, or security requirements).

15. **§3(c), safety-evaluation exemption.** "Activities conducted for the purpose of safety evaluation" turns on subjective purpose. The text doesn't limit it to evaluating existing systems, so a company could argue that training a new covered system is permissible if it is meant for safety testing. There is no licensing, government approval, security standard, scope limit, or reporting requirement. Specify who bears the burden of proving the exemption applies (an affirmative defense).

**Section 4 (Registry)**

16. **§4(a), registers facilities but not developers or training runs.** There is no obligation to report planned or ongoing training runs, compute used, or model identity. Enforcement of §3 therefore depends on inferring violations from facility data. Operators of facilities below the threshold, and any cloud tenants, are not covered.

17. **§4(b), self-certification without verification.** No audit, inspection, subpoena, or data-access authority is granted, and "computing workloads" is unspecified. Quarterly reporting means violations can run for months, and there is no obligation to report chip acquisitions or changes in capacity.

18. **§4, no deadlines, rulemaking authority, or penalties.** There is no registration deadline, no authority for Commerce to issue regulations or forms, and no penalty for failing to register or filing a false report. The only fallback is general false-statements law.

19. **§4, no confidentiality or whistleblower provisions.** Companies will resist disclosure on trade-secret grounds, and the bill has no FOIA exemption or protected-data framework. There is also no whistleblower protection for employees who report violations.

**Section 5 (Penalties)**

20. **§5, no enforcing authority or procedure.** The bill doesn't say who may bring a civil action (Attorney General, Commerce, or a private party), whether administrative adjudication is available, or whether courts may grant injunctions. Criminal enforcement is similarly unassigned. A prohibition without a designated enforcer is a serious practical gap.

21. **§5(a), penalty cap is low and discretionary.** At "not more than" $1M per day, a firm spending billions on a training run may treat the penalty as a cost of doing business. Consider higher or revenue-scaled maximums, minimums, and remedies beyond fines such as cease-and-desist orders, compute seizure, or model destruction.

22. **§5(a) versus §8, conflicting dates.** Civil penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the Act takes effect 180 days after enactment. Either the penalty clause is a nullity for 180 days or a court may find it ambiguous. Either reading invites a fair-notice or retroactivity challenge.

23. **§5(b), "willfully" is undefined.** Defendants will argue the government must prove they knew the system exceeded the threshold or was covered, which is hard to prove when the (B) determination is discretionary. Specify the required mental state and what it attaches to.

24. **§5(b), no corporate or officer liability provision.** Individual executives can argue they didn't personally "violate" the section. Consider responsible-corporate-officer language and express coverage of conspiracy and structuring to evade the thresholds.

25. **§5, no anti-evasion provision.** Nothing prohibits structuring training runs, facilities, or corporate entities to avoid the definitions.

**Sections 6–8 (International, Duration, Effective Date)**

26. **§6, "shall seek to negotiate" is unenforceable.** There is no reporting to Congress, no consequence for missing the 18-month target, no verification regime, and no leverage such as export-control or sanctions consequences. "Equivalent" prohibitions is also undefined. Offshoring remains a gap until the treaty exists.

27. **§7(a), undefined "developed safely."** There is no standard, and the National Academies has no deadline or mandate to study the question. The mechanism could be gamed through lobbying for a favorable report, and a joint resolution requires presidential signature, which should be intended and stated.

28. **§7(a) versus §7(b), internal contradiction.** Subsection (a) says the prohibition remains until Congress finds otherwise, while (b) repeals the whole Act after 10 years regardless. Companies can plan to wait out the ban, and a court could read (b) as controlling, making (a) surplusage.

29. **§7(b), no savings clause.** Without one, a sunset may abate pending or future prosecutions for violations committed before the sunset. Add an express provision preserving liability for earlier violations.

30. **§8, 180-day gap creates a rush window.** Companies can start massive training runs after enactment but before the effective date. Those runs are not grandfathered under §3(b), but the text doesn't say whether continuing them past the effective date is a violation. Consider a registration or notice requirement for runs begun between introduction and the effective date.

**Missing provisions**

31. **No severability clause.** If a court strikes the (B) delegation or the extraterritorial provision, the rest of the Act could fall with it.

32. **No funding, staffing, or implementing authority.** The bill doesn't authorize appropriations or direct Commerce to build the capacity to verify compliance.

33. **No judicial review or constitutional findings.** The bill has no review provisions for Secretary determinations and no congressional findings supporting Commerce Clause authority, national security, or extinction risk. Developers may also bring First Amendment claims (code as speech) and takings claims over grandfathered or seized assets, so findings and narrow drafting matter.

34. **No chip, hardware, or weights controls.** Nothing restricts the export, sale, or transfer of advanced chips or model weights, so the supply of the key inputs is left unregulated.
