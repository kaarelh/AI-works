# Drafting Issues in S. 2817 (as amended Oct. 6)

Below is every issue I found, grouped by section. Some are clear gaps, some are ambiguities a court might resolve either way, and a few are scope choices that critics may call loopholes. Since CPR doesn't take positions, I'd describe them as "questions the text leaves open" and attribute characterizations like "loophole" to named sources.

## Sec. 2: Definitions

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "by a single vessel."** The 10,000-ton threshold applies per vessel, so an operator could split recovery across several vessels, each staying under the cap, and recover any total amount legally. This is probably the most significant gap in the bill.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A), tonnage measurement.** The bill doesn't say whether tonnage is wet or dry, or whether it is raw ore or processed material. Freshly recovered nodules hold a lot of water, so the choice could change whether an operation crosses the threshold.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "per year."** "Year" is undefined (calendar year, fiscal year, or any rolling 12 months). That affects when the threshold is crossed and when a violation begins.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "recovery."** The definition covers recovery, not seabed disturbance or collection. Collector vehicles that disturb the seafloor without lifting material, or that hold it in storage, may fall outside it. Modern systems also use a seabed collector plus a riser to a surface ship, and the text doesn't say which "vessel" is doing the recovering.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "at depths greater than 200 meters."** It's unclear whether this means water depth at the site or depth below the seabed surface. The 200-meter line is also roughly the continental shelf break, so the definition could reach shelf-edge activity (such as sand or aggregate extraction) that drafters may not intend.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "other mineral resources."** This is undefined. It could arguably reach sand, gravel, phosphorites, or even hydrocarbons, or it could be read narrowly to mean hard minerals like nodules, crusts, and sulfides.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(B), undefined "Secretary."** The bill never says which Secretary (Commerce? Interior?) makes determinations. That invites interagency disputes and challenges to any determination.

8. **Sec. 2(1)(B), open-ended agency authority tied to criminal penalties.** A Secretary can expand the definition of a crime (Sec. 5(b)) based on a vague "comparable risks" standard. The bill sets no procedure, rulemaking requirement, or criteria. This invites void-for-vagueness, nondelegation, and major-questions challenges.

9. **Sec. 2(2), "corporation."** The U.S. person definition covers only corporations. It leaves out LLCs, partnerships, trusts, and other entities that are common in resource ventures, so a company could avoid coverage by changing its legal form.

10. **Sec. 2(2), "organized under the laws of a State."** Unless "State" is read to include them, entities organized in DC or U.S. territories may be excluded. The definition also omits lawful permanent residents and federally chartered entities.

11. **Sec. 2(2), foreign subsidiaries.** A U.S. parent company's foreign-incorporated subsidiary is not a "U.S. person," so it can mine abroad. Several prominent seabed mining ventures are already organized outside the U.S.

12. **Sec. 2(3), covered vessel threshold.** Vessels under 5,000 gross tons fall outside the registry, even though they could still exceed the 10,000-ton recovery cap. "Equipped for the recovery" also lets operators avoid registration by removing or swapping equipment between voyages.

13. **Sec. 2(3) vs. Sec. 2(1), disconnected definitions.** "Covered vessel" is used only in the registry. The prohibition refers to "vessel" generally, so the two tiers don't line up.

## Sec. 3: Prohibition

14. **Sec. 3(a), "in the United States."** It's unclear whether this includes the territorial sea, the EEZ, and the continental shelf. A foreign company with a foreign-flagged vessel in the U.S. EEZ might not be covered.

15. **Sec. 3(a), no flag-state jurisdiction.** The prohibition reaches U.S. persons anywhere but not U.S.-flagged vessels as such. A U.S.-flagged vessel run by a foreign operator on the high seas may fall outside it.

16. **Sec. 3(a), "engage in."** It's unclear whether investors, financiers, charterers, equipment suppliers, sponsors, or buyers "engage in" mining. The bill also doesn't bar importing or processing seabed minerals mined by others, so U.S. companies can still source them. That last point is a scope choice, but critics may raise it.

17. **Sec. 3(b), open-ended grandfathering.** The exemption has no expiration date. It doesn't say whether renewals, extensions, modifications, or transfers to new owners keep the exemption, which could let existing license holders operate indefinitely.

18. **Sec. 3(b), "exploration license" covers "mining."** Exploration licenses typically don't authorize commercial recovery, yet the exemption covers "mining conducted under" them. This could be read to allow commercial-scale recovery by exploration licensees.

19. **Sec. 3(b), no issuing authority.** The text doesn't say who must have issued the license. It could cover licenses from NOAA under the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act, from foreign governments, or arguably International Seabed Authority exploration contracts (which are "contracts," not "licenses").

20. **Sec. 3(c), environmental assessment exemption.** "Environmental assessment" is undefined, and the exemption is purpose-based and self-declared. It has no tonnage cap, permit, approval, or oversight, so an operator could describe commercial-scale "test mining" as assessment.

## Sec. 4: Registry

21. **Sec. 4, no penalties.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3. Failing to register, failing to report, or filing false reports carries no penalty under the bill, though 18 U.S.C. § 1001 might reach knowingly false statements.

22. **Sec. 4(a), no deadline or procedure.** The bill doesn't say when registration must happen or what it must include. It also gives NOAA no rulemaking authority to fill these gaps.

23. **Sec. 4(b), no content requirements.** The quarterly reports needn't include tonnage, location, depth, or methods. Without tonnage data, NOAA can't check the Sec. 2(1)(A) threshold.

24. **Sec. 4(b), self-certification only.** Reports are certified by the operator, with no independent verification, inspection authority, vessel tracking, or observer requirement.

25. **Sec. 4, jurisdictional reach.** It's unclear whether foreign operators of foreign vessels must register. "Operator" is undefined, and the bill doesn't say whether reports are public.

26. **Sec. 4 vs. Sec. 2(1)(B), agency mismatch.** Sec. 4 names NOAA while Sec. 2(1)(B) names an unspecified "Secretary." The result could be split or unclear authority.

## Sec. 5: Penalties

27. **Sec. 5(a) vs. Sec. 8, effective-date conflict.** Civil penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the Act doesn't take effect until 180 days later. That inconsistency invites litigation over the first six months. It also raises the question of whether recovery during that window counts toward the annual tonnage threshold.

28. **Sec. 5(a), "each day on which the violation occurs."** Because the threshold is annual, it's unclear when a violation begins (the day the 10,000th ton is recovered?) and whether earlier days in the year count. This makes penalty calculations contestable.

29. **Sec. 5, no enforcement machinery.** The bill doesn't name an agency to assess or collect penalties or set any procedure. It provides no inspection, boarding, seizure, or forfeiture powers and no citizen-suit provision. Any administratively imposed penalty could also face a jury-trial challenge under *SEC v. Jarkesy* (2024).

30. **Sec. 5(b), criminal liability based on Secretary determinations.** Criminal penalties attach to conduct defined partly by Secretary determinations under Sec. 2(1)(B). This sharpens the vagueness and fair-notice problems in item 8.

## Sec. 6: International Agreement

31. **Sec. 6, "shall seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months."** It's unclear whether the deadline is for starting or finishing negotiations. "Seek to" is effectively unenforceable, and the bill has no reporting requirement or consequence for inaction.

32. **Sec. 6, "equivalent to."** "Equivalent" is undefined. The provision also doesn't address that the U.S. is not a party to UNCLOS or the ISA, the main forum for seabed mining rules.

## Sec. 7: Duration

33. **Sec. 7(a), no study is actually commissioned.** The prohibition ends based on a National Academies report, but the bill never directs or funds one. "Serious harm" is also undefined.

34. **Sec. 7(a), largely symbolic.** A joint resolution is enacted like any law, so Congress could already end the prohibition without this clause. The National Academies condition doesn't bind future Congresses.

35. **Sec. 7(a) vs. 7(b), conflicting end dates.** Sec. 7(a) suggests the ban lasts until Congress acts. Sec. 7(b) ends the whole Act after 10 years regardless. Because the clock runs from enactment, not the effective date, the ban runs only about 9.5 years.

36. **Sec. 7(b), everything expires at once.** The registry and reporting requirements sunset along with the ban, so there's no monitoring afterward. Pending penalties are likely preserved by the general savings statute (1 U.S.C. § 109), but the bill doesn't say so.

## Sec. 8: Effective Date and Overall Structure

37. **Sec. 8, 180-day window.** The delay gives operators time to start or expand operations, line up the exemptions, or restructure to avoid the "U.S. person" definition before the ban takes effect.

38. **Whole bill, no relationship to existing law.** The bill doesn't amend or repeal the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (1980) or address the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act. NOAA's existing licensing and permitting framework stays on the books alongside the ban, creating potential conflicts.

39. **Whole bill, no rulemaking authority, findings, or severability clause.** No agency can issue implementing regulations to resolve these ambiguities. There are no findings supporting the bill's reach over activity abroad, and no severability clause, though courts generally presume severability.

## Caveats for the Explainer

- **Check with legal experts and committee staff.** Some ambiguities may be resolved by background law. For example, 1 U.S.C. § 1 defines "person" broadly, so the general "any person" in Sec. 3(a) reaches more than the narrow "U.S. person" definition.
- **Some items are policy choices, not drafting errors.** Items 5, 16, and 37 fit here, and supporters may defend them as intentional.
- **Watch for further changes.** More amendments may be offered at the Oct. 15 markup, so it's worth confirming the final text before publishing.
