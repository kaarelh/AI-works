**Drafting issues in S. 2817 (text as amended October 6, 2026)**

These are observations on the text, not legal opinions. Before anything is published, the explainer should be checked against the official text and any CRS or committee analysis, which I can't access from here.

**Section 2(1)(A): 10^26 compute threshold**

1. "Training run" is undefined, and the Act doesn't say how FLOPs are counted. It's unclear whether counting uses dense or sparse operations, which numeric precision applies (an FP8 operation and an FP32 operation count very differently), whether post-training such as RL or fine-tuning counts, and who certifies the number.
2. Nothing prevents a developer from splitting one training effort into several sequential "runs" or restarts, each below 10^26, with no aggregation rule.
3. "Trained using" doesn't clearly address incremental training that pushes a model past the threshold cumulatively, such as continued pretraining of a sub-threshold or grandfathered model.

**Section 2(1)(B): Secretary's determination**

4. The Secretary can designate a system as covered based on capability, but "substantially exceeding human performance" and "most cognitive tasks" have no benchmark, process, notice requirement, or evidentiary record. Developers would have no way to know in advance whether they are covered, which invites vagueness challenges.
5. "Secretary" is never identified (Commerce is implied), and there are no deadlines for determinations or procedures for petitioning for one.
6. Subparagraph (B) applies to a "system," not a "model." It's unclear whether an agent or scaffold built on a sub-threshold model counts, and the Act doesn't say whether the determination reaches deployed systems already in use.

**Section 2(2): "U.S. person"**

7. The definition covers citizens and corporations organized under state law only. It excludes LLCs, partnerships, federally or D.C.-chartered entities, and foreign-organized subsidiaries of U.S. companies, many of which are common vehicles for AI development. This leaves real gaps in the extraterritorial reach of Section 3(a).
8. The definition makes individual U.S. citizens liable "anywhere" but not U.S.-organized companies operating abroad through non-corporate forms. That mismatch looks unintended and should be noted.

**Section 2(3): "Covered facility"**

9. "Data center," "advanced AI chips," and "containing" are undefined. The Act doesn't say whether leased chips count, how multi-site operations are treated, or when the count is measured.
10. The registry is keyed to chip count, but the prohibition is keyed to compute. A training run above 10^26 could be carried out at facilities under 50,000 chips, or split across several facilities that each stay below the threshold, and fall outside the registry entirely.

**Section 3(a): Prohibition**

11. "Develop" and "deploy" are undefined. The Act doesn't say whether releasing model weights, offering API access, or internal use counts as deployment, which leaves open-weight release a possible gap.
12. Territorial scope is unclear. Section 3(a) reaches "any person in the United States" and "any U.S. person anywhere," so a foreign company that trains abroad and serves U.S. users from abroad may fall outside both, and the Act doesn't say where training legally occurs when compute is cloud-hosted.
13. The prohibition doesn't address people who supply compute, chips, financing, or assistance. Cloud providers, chip suppliers, and investors are not mentioned, and it's unclear whether general aiding-and-abetting principles would apply.

**Section 3(b): Existing systems**

14. The grandfather clause doesn't define when training "began," sets no compute or time cap, and requires no registration. Because it exempts the system itself, the exemption could also cover ongoing deployment indefinitely.
15. The grandfather date is tied to enactment, but Section 3 doesn't take effect until 180 days later (Section 8). A training run started in that interval is neither grandfathered nor yet prohibited, and the intended treatment isn't stated.

**Section 3(c): Safety evaluation exemption**

16. "Safety evaluation" is undefined, and the exemption has no limit on scale, no reporting requirement, no requirement that evaluators be independent or government-approved, and no bar on public deployment. A developer could label a deployment as an evaluation and claim the exemption.
17. The exemption doesn't say whether it covers training for evaluation purposes or only testing of models that already exist, which makes it broader than it may have been intended to be.

**Section 4: Registry**

18. The registration obligation has no deadline, no content requirements, and no penalty for failure to register. Section 5 penalizes only violations of Section 3.
19. Quarterly reports describe "computing workloads" but don't require identifying models, per-run compute, or whether a run crossed 10^26. The registry therefore can't verify compliance with the threshold.
20. The Act gives Commerce no audit or inspection authority, no confidentiality protections for commercially sensitive data, and no penalty for a false certification.
21. Facility operators may be cloud providers who don't know what their tenants are training, while the entities actually training have no reporting duty under Section 4.

**Section 5: Penalties**

22. No agency is named to enforce the civil penalty, and the Act doesn't say whether enforcement is by agency action, DOJ suit, or private action. It also provides no injunctive authority, no power to halt a run or seize weights or compute, and no limitations period.
23. The civil penalty applies to violations "occurring on or after the date of enactment," but Section 3 doesn't take effect until 180 days later (Section 8). No violation of Section 3 can occur before that date, so this phrase likely should refer to the effective date.
24. The penalty is "for each day," and it's unclear whether a continuing training run or ongoing deployment counts as one violation per day or many. A $1 million daily maximum may also be modest compared with the cost of a frontier training run, which the explainer may want to check against current figures.
25. "Willfully" is undefined, and the Act has no provision for individual liability of officers or directors. The criminal penalty also doesn't cover the Section 4 reporting duties.

**Section 6: International agreement**

26. "Shall seek to negotiate" creates no obligation to reach an agreement, no consequence if the State Department fails, and no reporting requirement. "Equivalent" is undefined, and the Act doesn't say how an agreement would interact with the domestic prohibition.

**Section 7: Duration**

27. Section 7(a) keeps the prohibition in force until a joint resolution finds covered systems can be developed safely, but Section 7(b) sunsets the entire Act after 10 years. The Act doesn't say which controls.
28. "Safely" is undefined, and Section 7(a) refers to a National Academies report that the Act never requires NAS to produce, with no deadline. The trigger could therefore never occur.
29. The sunset in Section 7(b) ends the registry and penalties along with the prohibition, and there is no savings clause for violations committed before sunset or for pending enforcement actions.

**Section 8: Effective date**

30. The 180-day effective date and the 10-year sunset both run from enactment, so the operative period is unclear (about 9.5 years). The Act also has no transition rules for facilities already operating when the registry takes effect.

**General drafting and litigation risk**

31. The Act has no definitions section covering "person," "training," "deploy," or "develop," and no rulemaking authority, so Commerce couldn't clarify ambiguities through regulation.
32. There is no severability clause, and no provision on judicial review or the standard a court would apply to a Secretary's determination. If one provision is struck, the whole Act could be challenged.
33. Several provisions could face litigation: vagueness challenges to Sections 2(1)(B), 3(c), and 5(b); nondelegation concerns about the open-ended determination power in Section 2(1)(B); and possible First Amendment arguments about restrictions on publishing weights or code. Each is a risk to flag for the explainer, and it should be confirmed with legal counsel rather than stated as settled.

**Most consequential for the explainer:** items 3, 10, 12, 14, 16, 18–19, 22, 27–28. Together they show how a developer could evade the prohibition through compute splitting, facility thresholds, territorial scope, the grandfather clause, or a self-labeled safety evaluation, and how the bill's enforcement and sunset mechanics leave gaps.
