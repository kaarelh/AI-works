# S. 2817 (as amended Oct. 6, 2026): Loopholes, Ambiguities, and Drafting Problems

## Sec. 2: Definitions

1. **§2(1)(A), "artificial intelligence model."** Neither "artificial intelligence" nor "model" is defined. This creates uncertainty about whether ensembles, agentic scaffolds, or multi-model systems count, and about which component's training compute is measured.

2. **§2(1)(A), "computing power … operations."** The bill mislabels a quantity of operations as "computing power," which in engineering usage is a rate, not a total. This is a minor drafting error, but it feeds the vagueness argument.

3. **§2(1)(A), counting methodology.** The bill gives no method for counting operations. It doesn't address numeric precision, whether sparse or mixture-of-experts computation is counted in full, or whether failed or discarded runs count. Two good-faith calculations for the same run could land on opposite sides of 10^26.

4. **§2(1)(A), "single training run."** The term is undefined. Developers could split training into multiple sub-threshold runs through staged pretraining, continued pretraining, separate fine-tuning phases, or merging separately trained models, so that no single run exceeds 10^26.

5. **§2(1)(A), excluded upstream compute.** Only compute "in a single training run" counts. Compute spent on synthetic data generation, reward models, teacher models for distillation, or search and inference-time scaling is excluded, even though it can be large and capability-relevant.

6. **§2(1)(A), distillation and derivatives.** A smaller model distilled from a covered or grandfathered model, using under 10^26 operations, is not covered under prong (A), even if it matches the parent's capability.

7. **§2(1)(A), static threshold.** No one has authority to update the threshold. Algorithmic efficiency gains mean highly capable systems will increasingly fall below 10^26, while the line will capture progressively less frontier work. This is both a loophole and an argument that the bill is arbitrary and quickly obsolete.

8. **§2(1)(A), "greater than."** A run of exactly 10^26 operations is not covered. This is trivial but technically true.

9. **§2(1)(B), "Secretary" undefined.** The Act never says which Secretary. Commerce is implied only by §4, and §6 separately names the Secretary of State. Prong (B) arguably cannot operate until this is resolved.

10. **§2(1)(B), vague standard.** "Substantially exceeding human performance," "most cognitive tasks," and "capable" are undefined. The bill specifies no benchmark, no comparison population (average human or expert), and no evidentiary standard. This supports void-for-vagueness challenges, which are especially strong given the criminal penalties in §5(b).

11. **§2(1)(B), no procedure.** There is no notice, comment, hearing, deadline, judicial review provision, or rulemaking authority for the Secretary's determination. This raises due process and nondelegation (intelligible principle) arguments.

12. **§2(1)(B), timing problem.** A system's capabilities usually can't be determined until after it is trained. Prong (B) therefore can't, as a practical matter, prohibit the training that produced the system. It reaches only post-determination development and deployment, and nothing is covered until the Secretary acts.

13. **§2(1)(A) vs. (B), "model" vs. "system."** Prong (A) covers a "model" and prong (B) a "system." This leaves open whether wrapping a sub-threshold model in tools or agents (a "system") changes coverage, and whether a determination about a system attaches to its underlying model.

14. **§2(2), narrow "U.S. person."** The definition covers only citizens and corporations "organized under the laws of a State." It omits:
    - lawful permanent residents;
    - LLCs, partnerships, and other non-corporate entities;
    - federally chartered entities;
    - arguably, entities organized in D.C. or the territories, since "State" is undefined.

    For conduct abroad, only listed U.S. persons are reached.

15. **§2(2), foreign subsidiaries.** A foreign-incorporated subsidiary of a U.S. company is not a U.S. person. The Act has no "owned or controlled by" language and no parent-liability provision, so training conducted abroad by a foreign subsidiary is not facially prohibited.

16. **§2(3), "covered facility."** "Data center," "advanced AI chips," and "containing" are undefined. Compute can be split across multiple sites under 50,000 chips each. Chips are also not equivalent units across generations, so the threshold measures the wrong thing.

17. **§2(3), disconnected from §3.** The facility definition is used only for the registry. It plays no role in defining or enforcing the prohibition, so prohibited training at a non-covered facility goes unregistered.

## Sec. 3: Prohibition

18. **§3(a), "train, develop, or deploy" undefined.** "Develop" could sweep in research, tooling, and data work far upstream of any covered model, which supports overbreadth and vagueness arguments. "Deploy" is unclear as to internal use, API access, and open-weight release. **Use** of a covered system is not prohibited at all.

19. **§3(a), territorial ambiguity.** For persons acting remotely, the bill doesn't say where training "occurs": where the operators sit, or where the hardware sits. A foreign entity training on foreign hardware, with some personnel in the U.S., presents an unresolved question.

20. **§3(a), foreign-hosted access.** U.S. users or companies accessing a covered model hosted abroad by a non-U.S. person may not be "deploying" it. The bill also contains no import or access restriction.

21. **§3(a), individual liability.** "Any person" reaches individual employees (under 1 U.S.C. §1, "person" includes individuals and corporations). Engineers would face personal criminal exposure under definitions they cannot apply with confidence. This is an unworkability and chilling-effect argument.

22. **§3(b), grandfather keyed to enactment, not the effective date.** Training that begins during the 180-day window between enactment and the effective date (§8) is not grandfathered. It would become unlawful mid-run on the effective date. This is an internal inconsistency with §8.

23. **§3(b), scope of grandfathered activity.** The exemption applies to the "covered AI system" as a whole. This arguably permits continued development, further training, and deployment of a grandfathered system indefinitely. The bill doesn't address when continued training or modification produces a *new* system.

24. **§3(b), "training began."** The term is undefined. It creates an incentive to formally start a long-running training run before enactment and continue it. The bill has no cap on duration or total compute for grandfathered runs.

25. **§3(b), grandfathers prong (B) systems.** A system the Secretary later determines to be superhuman is fully exempt if its training began before enactment. This undercuts the bill's stated purpose and is an internal-coherence argument.

26. **§3(c), "safety evaluation" exception.** The exception is undefined, purpose-based, and unbounded. It lacks a "sole" or "primary" purpose requirement, an approval process, qualified-evaluator criteria, limits on deployment, and recordkeeping. It also doesn't say whether training a model in order to evaluate it qualifies.

## Sec. 4: Registry

27. **§4, "operator" undefined.** The bill doesn't specify whether the registrant is the facility owner, the colocation lessor, the cloud provider, or the customer running workloads.

28. **§4(a), no deadline or content.** The bill sets no registration deadline, requires no registration contents, and gives no authority to prescribe forms.

29. **§4(b), "describing the computing workloads."** The content and level of detail are unspecified. The bill also lacks confidentiality or trade-secret protections, which supports unworkability and takings/FOIA concerns.

30. **§4, no penalty.** §5 penalizes only violations of §3. Failure to register or report carries no penalty under the Act. False certified statements could still trigger 18 U.S.C. §1001, so this should not be treated as a free pass.

31. **§4, no geographic limit.** The bill doesn't say whether foreign data centers operated by U.S. entities must register. The answer is ambiguous either way.

## Sec. 5: Penalties

32. **§5(a), "on or after the date of enactment."** This conflicts with the effective date in §8. It suggests penalties for conduct before the Act takes effect, which is incoherent. As applied to §5(b), it raises ex post facto problems.

33. **§5(a), "each day on which the violation occurs."** For a discrete act like "training," the unit of violation is unclear: the day the run began, every day of the run, or each deployment day.

34. **§5, no enforcement mechanism.** The bill designates no enforcing agency, gives no authority to assess civil penalties, provides no administrative process or venue, and grants no investigative, subpoena, or inspection authority. Civil enforcement would presumably require DOJ to sue in district court.

35. **§5(b), "willfully."** In federal criminal law, this generally requires proof that the defendant knew the conduct was unlawful (Bryan v. United States; Ratzlaf v. United States). Combined with the vague definitions, this is a high bar to meet.

36. **§5, rule of lenity spillover.** The same definitions carry both civil and criminal consequences. Under Leocal v. Ashcroft and Clark v. Martinez, a single statute is generally interpreted consistently across contexts, so ambiguities may be construed narrowly even in civil enforcement.

## Sec. 6: International Agreement

37. **§6, ambiguous deadline.** "Seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months" could mean begin seeking by 18 months or conclude by 18 months. Either way, the provision has no consequence, reporting requirement, or link to §3.

38. **§6, no reciprocity trigger.** The domestic prohibition takes effect regardless of whether any other country agrees. This supports the competitiveness and national-security argument. The bill also doesn't address whether a resulting agreement would be a treaty requiring Senate advice and consent.

## Sec. 7: Duration

39. **§7(a), illusory mechanism.** Congress can repeal the Act at any time, so the joint-resolution provision is legally surplus. The provision also:
    - directs no one to commission the National Academies report;
    - sets no timeline and provides no funding;
    - leaves "can be developed safely" undefined;
    - terminates only §3, leaving §4 running.

40. **§7(b), sunset measured from enactment.** Because the prohibition starts 180 days after enactment, the effective prohibition period is about 9.5 years. The bill has no savings clause for pending enforcement. The general savings statute (1 U.S.C. §109) likely preserves liability, but its application to an expiring (versus repealed) statute is less settled.

## Sec. 8 and Structural Omissions

41. **§8, effective date mismatches.** The 180-day effective date is inconsistent with §3(b) and §5(a), both keyed to enactment. It is also unclear whether the Secretary may make §2(1)(B) determinations before the effective date.

42. **Structural: no rulemaking authority.** No agency is given authority to issue regulations clarifying compute accounting, "training run," "safety evaluation," or other terms. The ambiguities above therefore can't be cured administratively.

43. **Structural: no anti-evasion clause.** The bill has no provision reaching structuring, splitting, or arrangements designed to avoid the thresholds. This makes items 4, 15, and 16 more consequential.

44. **Structural: no preemption clause.** The bill doesn't address state AI laws, leaving room for a patchwork of state regimes.

45. **Structural: no severability clause.** Courts presume severability (Barr v. AAPC, 2020), so this matters less than it might appear. Still, a successful vagueness challenge to §2(1)(B) or §3(c) would invite litigation over what survives.

## Practical Notes for Legal

- **Two categories, different uses.** Items 9–12, 22, 32, 34, 39, and 41 are the strongest "unworkable as drafted" arguments. Items 4–6, 14–15, 23–24, and 26 are the main "operate under it" gaps. Raising the latter at markup will likely prompt the committee to close them, and staff counsel will probably spot many of them regardless.
- **Exposure risk.** Operating in reliance on aggressive readings is risky. This applies especially to the §3(c) safety-evaluation exception, offshore subsidiary structures, and run-splitting. The bill carries a 10-year criminal penalty, there is §1001 exposure on certified reports, and courts may read evasion as evidence of willfulness. These readings should get a formal opinion before anyone relies on them.
- **Verify before filing.** Please have counsel confirm the case citations and the savings-statute point before anything goes in a filing or letter.
