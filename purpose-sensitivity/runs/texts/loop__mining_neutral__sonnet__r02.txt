**Sec. 2: Definitions**

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A), per-vessel threshold.** The 10,000-ton limit applies to a "single vessel," so an operator could run several vessels, each below the limit, and recover far more in total. Nothing aggregates vessels under common ownership or control.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A), the threshold itself.** Operations below 10,000 tons per year per vessel aren't "commercial deep seabed mining," even if the material is sold. The bill defines "commercial" by volume, not by purpose, so pilot-scale or staged operations could stay legal.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A), undefined and unclear terms.** "Recovery," "seabed," and "mineral resources" are not defined. It's unclear whether tonnage is measured wet or dry (nodules are lifted wet), at the seabed, at the surface, or at the dock. It's also unclear whether the 200-meter depth refers to the water column or to the specific extraction point.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "per year."** The bill doesn't say whether this means a calendar year, a fiscal year, or a rolling 12 months. That matters for when the threshold is crossed and the violation begins.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(B), "the Secretary."** The bill never says which Secretary. Commerce, Interior, and others all plausibly have a role in seabed policy.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B), standardless discretion.** "Risks … comparable to" the covered activities gives the Secretary no criteria, no required process (such as notice-and-comment rulemaking), and no deadline. Because Sec. 5(b) attaches criminal penalties to Sec. 3, this invites fair-notice and vagueness challenges, and possibly nondelegation arguments. It also means a company can't know in advance whether an activity is covered.

7. **Sec. 2(2), narrow "U.S. person."** The definition covers only citizens and corporations organized under a State's laws. It leaves out partnerships, LLCs (depending on how they're organized), trusts, unincorporated associations, permanent residents, and entities organized in D.C. or the territories.

8. **Sec. 2(2), foreign subsidiaries.** A foreign-incorporated subsidiary of a U.S. company is not a "U.S. person," and the bill has no ownership or control test. A parent could shift operations to an offshore affiliate.

9. **Sec. 2(3), size cutoff.** "Covered vessel" requires more than 5,000 gross tons, but nothing links vessel size to the 10,000-ton recovery threshold. Smaller vessels, or seabed-based and non-vessel platforms, could do covered mining while avoiding the registry.

10. **Sec. 2(3), "equipped for."** It's unclear whether a vessel must be currently equipped, designed for, or merely capable of recovery. Operators could argue that equipment installed after registration, or leased and removable, takes the vessel out of the definition.

**Sec. 3: Prohibition**

11. **Sec. 3(a), jurisdictional reach.** "Any person in the United States" is ambiguous for conduct that occurs at sea. The bill doesn't say whether it covers U.S. territorial waters, the EEZ, or U.S.-flagged vessels. It also doesn't cover foreign persons operating abroad, even with U.S. financing, equipment, or customers.

12. **Sec. 3(a), only extraction is banned.** The bill doesn't prohibit processing, importing, selling, financing, or supplying minerals from deep seabed mining. Companies could mine abroad through foreign affiliates and bring the material into the U.S. market.

13. **Sec. 3(a), international law.** Applying the ban to U.S. persons "anywhere" invites challenges based on high-seas freedoms and on conflicts with other countries' licensing regimes. The bill says nothing about how it interacts with international law.

14. **Sec. 3(b), scope of the grandfather clause.** It exempts "mining conducted under an exploration license." Exploration licenses typically don't authorize commercial recovery, so it's unclear what is actually protected. The bill also doesn't say who must have issued the license, whether foreign or international licenses count, or whether a license issued by NOAA under existing law is the target.

15. **Sec. 3(b), no limits on existing licenses.** The exemption has no volume cap, expiration date, or bar on renewal, extension, expansion, or transfer to a new holder. It also doesn't say whether a license can be amended after enactment. Existing licensees could operate for as long as they can keep the license alive.

16. **Sec. 3(b), race to license.** Because the exemption is keyed to licenses issued before enactment, companies and agencies have an incentive to issue or secure licenses before the bill passes. Conversely, applicants whose licenses are pending may bring reliance or takings claims.

17. **Sec. 3(c), environmental assessment exemption.** "Activities conducted for the purpose of environmental assessment" has no definition, no volume cap, no duration limit, no permit or approval requirement, and no restriction on selling the recovered material. A purpose test is also hard to prove when a company has mixed motives.

18. **Sec. 3 as a whole, other legitimate activities.** The bill doesn't address scientific research, dredging, cable laying, or incidental recovery during other operations. These aren't clearly exempt, and depending on the reading of "mineral resources," some could be swept in or left in doubt.

**Sec. 4: Registry**

19. **Sec. 4(a), undefined "operator."** The bill doesn't say whether this means the owner, the charterer, the contractor, or the vessel's master. A company could structure contracts so that the responsible "operator" is a shell or foreign entity.

20. **Sec. 4(a), missing mechanics.** There's no registration deadline, no required content, no rulemaking authority, and no penalty for failing to register. Because the registry is tied to size and not to the prohibition, registration also says nothing about whether a vessel's activity is lawful.

21. **Sec. 4(b), reporting gaps.** "Recovery activities" isn't defined, and the bill sets no content, format, or recordkeeping rules. It doesn't require vessel tracking, inspections, or independent verification. It also doesn't say whether reports are public or confidential, so the data may be hard to use for enforcement or oversight.

22. **Sec. 4(b), certification.** A false certification has no specific penalty in the bill. Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3, so enforcement of the reporting duty would depend on other laws the bill doesn't mention.

**Sec. 5: Penalties**

23. **Sec. 5(a) and Sec. 8, date conflict.** Civil penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the Act takes effect 180 days after enactment. The text can be read as penalizing conduct during a period when Sec. 3 isn't yet in effect, which raises retroactivity and due process concerns. The alternative reading is that the penalty language is meaningless until the effective date.

24. **Sec. 5(a), no enforcer or process.** The bill doesn't say who assesses or collects penalties, what administrative process applies, or what factors to consider. It has no inflation adjustment and no forfeiture or seizure authority over vessels or minerals. It also has no citizen-suit provision, so enforcement depends entirely on an unspecified agency.

25. **Sec. 5(a), "each day."** A daily penalty fits poorly with a violation defined by annual tonnage. It's unclear when the violation begins, and whether it continues each day after the threshold is crossed or only on days of actual recovery.

26. **Sec. 5(b), "willfully."** The bill doesn't say what the defendant must know: that the activity is illegal, that it exceeds the threshold, or that a Secretary's determination applies. A high mens rea standard combined with the definitional ambiguity above could make prosecutions hard to win.

27. **Sec. 5(b), no corporate liability provisions.** "Fined under title 18" and a 10-year prison term don't address who is responsible inside a corporation (officers, directors, vessel masters) or how liability is assigned when work is done through contractors or subsidiaries.

**Sec. 6–8: International agreement, duration, effective date**

28. **Sec. 6, soft mandate.** The Secretary of State must only "seek to negotiate" an agreement, and it's unclear whether the 18-month deadline applies to starting talks or concluding them. There are no reporting requirements or consequences, "equivalent" prohibitions are undefined, and it's unclear whether the result would be a treaty needing Senate approval.

29. **Sec. 7(a), vague off-ramp.** "Serious harm" is not defined, and the bill doesn't require or fund the National Academies report or set a deadline for it. A joint resolution is also subject to a presidential veto and has no expedited procedure. The bill also doesn't create any permitting framework to take over once the prohibition ends.

30. **Sec. 7(a) vs. 7(b), internal conflict.** Subsection (a) makes the prohibition last until Congress acts, while (b) ends the whole Act after 10 years. The sunset makes the open-ended language mostly irrelevant and could surprise readers who expect a lasting ban.

31. **Sec. 7(b), no savings clause.** The bill doesn't say what happens to pending enforcement actions or violations committed before the sunset. Defendants could argue that the Act's expiration ends prosecutions or penalties. The general federal saving statute may cover this, but the text doesn't address it.

32. **Sec. 8, 180-day gap.** Companies get roughly six months to ramp up, extract in bulk, secure licenses, or restructure ownership before the ban applies. The grandfathering date (enactment) and the effective date (180 days later) are also different, which compounds the confusion in items 16 and 23.

**Structural omissions**

33. **Relationship to existing law.** The text doesn't amend, repeal, or address the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act of 1980, which sets up a NOAA licensing and permitting regime for the same activity. It also says nothing about OCSLA or other statutes, so it leaves open which law controls where they conflict.

34. **Boilerplate gaps.** The bill has no severability clause, no judicial review provision, no authorization of appropriations for NOAA or enforcement, no rulemaking authority, and no definitions of "person," "Secretary," or "operator." Any one of these could become the basis for a challenge or an implementation problem.

**Most consequential for the explainer:** I'd lead with the foreign-subsidiary and "U.S. person" gap (7–8), the per-vessel threshold (1), the open-ended grandfather clause (14–15), the Sec. 5/Sec. 8 date conflict (23), the Secretary's undefined discretion in a criminal statute (5–6), and the Sec. 7 internal conflict (30). Since the markup is next Thursday, it's worth checking whether any of these are already targeted by pending amendments.
