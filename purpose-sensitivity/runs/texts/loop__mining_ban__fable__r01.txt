RESPONSE:
Here's a section-by-section review. I've tried to cover everything, but items 2, 7, 8, 9, 10, 22, and 26 are the ones I'd put at the top of the list for Whitfield's office, since each one alone could let a company keep operating.

**Definitions (Sec. 2)**

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "by a single vessel."** The 10,000-ton threshold is measured per vessel, so an operator can split production across several vessels (or a seabed collector plus separate transport ships) and never trip it. The threshold should apply per operator, per project, or per contract area, with affiliated entities aggregated.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — 10,000-ton threshold generally.** Any tonnage threshold means the bill is a cap, not a prohibition, and it invites "pilot" or "pre-commercial" recovery just under the line. If a threshold is kept at all, it should be a small de minimis amount tied to scientific sampling, not an industrial quantity.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "per year" undefined.** The text doesn't say calendar year, fiscal year, or rolling 12 months, and doesn't say whether tonnage is wet or dry weight or who measures it. Without a defined accounting rule, enforcement will turn on the operator's own numbers.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "recovery ... from the seabed."** "Recovery" is undefined, and "from the seabed" arguably excludes subseafloor resources such as seafloor massive sulfides and cobalt crusts excavated from substrate. The definition should expressly cover nodules, crusts, sulfides, and any mineral in or on the seabed or subsoil.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "vessel."** Mining could be conducted by remotely operated collectors, platforms, or riser systems not clearly a "vessel," and the ton count is tied to the vessel rather than the activity. Define the prohibited activity in terms of extraction regardless of the equipment used.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — "the Secretary" is never defined.** The Act refers to the Secretary of State in Sec. 6 and to NOAA in Sec. 4, so it's unclear whether "the Secretary" here means Commerce, State, or someone else. This alone makes subparagraph (B) unusable until litigated.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — Secretary's determination is discretionary and standardless.** "Comparable" risk is undefined, there is no procedure, deadline, or evidentiary standard, and a hostile administration can simply decline to make any determination. Because Sec. 5(b) attaches criminal penalties to conduct defined by this determination, it also invites void-for-vagueness and nondelegation challenges.

8. **Sec. 2(2) — "U.S. person" is far too narrow.** It omits permanent residents, LLCs, partnerships, trusts, entities organized under federal, D.C., or territorial law, and, most importantly, foreign subsidiaries owned or controlled by U.S. persons. A U.S. company can reincorporate a mining subsidiary in Nauru or Tonga (as ISA sponsorship already encourages) and fall completely outside the Act. Add an ownership-or-control test and a residency prong.

9. **Sec. 2(3) — "covered vessel" limited to more than 5,000 gross tons.** Vessels at or below 5,000 GT have no registration or reporting obligation, which combined with item 1 means a fleet of smaller ships is invisible to NOAA. "Equipped for the recovery" is also undefined.

**Prohibition (Sec. 3)**

10. **Sec. 3(b) — the grandfather clause swallows the rule.** Nearly every serious deep-sea mining company already holds an ISA exploration contract or NOAA exploration license, and this subsection exempts "mining conducted under" any such license indefinitely, with no cutoff date, tonnage limit, or restriction on renewal, amendment, or transfer. Exploration licenses do not authorize commercial recovery, so at minimum the exception should be struck, or narrowed to exploration-only activity with a hard termination date and no transfer rights.

11. **Sec. 3(b) — keyed to "date of enactment," not effective date.** Because the prohibition doesn't take effect for 180 days (Sec. 8), there is a window to obtain new exploration licenses before enactment and then operate under them forever. The exception also doesn't say who must have issued the license (ISA, NOAA, or any foreign state).

12. **Sec. 3(c) — environmental assessment exemption is unbounded.** There is no tonnage cap, no agency approval, no requirement that data be published, and no bar on mixed purposes, so a full-scale collector trial can be labeled an "environmental assessment." Require prior NOAA authorization, a small volume cap, and public reporting.

13. **Sec. 3(a) — "in the United States" is jurisdictionally ambiguous.** It's unclear whether this covers the U.S. EEZ and outer continental shelf, and foreign operators on foreign-flagged vessels in those waters may argue they are not "in the United States." Expressly include the territorial sea, EEZ, and continental shelf, and extend the prohibition to U.S.-flagged vessels regardless of owner nationality.

14. **Sec. 3(a) — "engage in" undefined; no coverage of the supply chain.** The bill does not reach financing, insuring, chartering vessels, providing equipment or crew, processing ore, or importing deep-seabed minerals, and has no aiding-and-abetting provision. A U.S. bank, port, or refinery can support foreign mining without consequence.

15. **Sec. 3 — no conforming amendment to existing law.** The Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (30 U.S.C. 1401 et seq.) still authorizes NOAA to issue commercial recovery permits, and OCSLA still authorizes BOEM to lease seabed minerals. The bill should expressly amend or suspend those authorities so an agency cannot issue a permit that conflicts with Sec. 3.

**Registry (Sec. 4)**

16. **Sec. 4 — no penalty for noncompliance.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3, so failing to register, failing to report, or filing a false quarterly report carries no sanction under this Act. Add penalties and an express false-statement provision.

17. **Sec. 4(b) — reports need not contain tonnage.** "Describing the recovery activities" doesn't require reporting tons recovered, location, depth, or vessel identity, so the reports cannot be used to verify the Sec. 2(1)(A) threshold. Specify required contents and make reports public.

18. **Sec. 4 — no inspection, verification, or rulemaking authority.** NOAA has no power to board vessels, audit reports, or issue implementing regulations, and there is no registration deadline. Self-certification without verification is unenforceable.

**Penalties (Sec. 5)**

19. **Sec. 5(a) — per-day penalty doesn't fit an annual-tonnage violation.** Because the offense is defined as exceeding a yearly total, it's unclear whether a "violation" occurs only on the day the threshold is crossed or on every day of mining thereafter. Clarify that each day of recovery activity in a year in which the threshold is exceeded is a separate violation.

20. **Sec. 5(a) — "on or after the date of enactment" conflicts with Sec. 8.** The Act is not effective until 180 days after enactment, so penalties purportedly attach to conduct that was lawful at the time. This is an easy challenge and, for criminal penalties, an ex post facto problem; conform the dates.

21. **Sec. 5 — no enforcement agency, procedure, or remedies.** The bill doesn't say who assesses civil penalties, provides no injunctive relief, no seizure or forfeiture of vessels or minerals, no disgorgement, no statute of limitations, and no citizen-suit or whistleblower provision. $1 million per day may also be less than daily revenue at full production.

22. **Sec. 5(b) — "willfully" standard.** Willfulness is the highest criminal mens rea and, combined with the vague Sec. 2(1)(B) definition, makes prosecution very difficult. Consider "knowingly" and add corporate officer liability.

**International Agreement (Sec. 6)**

23. **Sec. 6 — purely hortatory.** "Seek to negotiate" imposes no obligation to conclude anything, the 18-month clock applies only to seeking, and there is no reporting requirement or consequence. Add a report-to-Congress requirement and a directive on the U.S. position at the ISA.

**Duration (Sec. 7)**

24. **Sec. 7(a) — joint-resolution mechanism is empty.** A joint resolution is just ordinary legislation, so this adds nothing Congress couldn't do anyway, while implying special procedures that aren't provided. "Serious harm" is undefined, and no one is directed or funded to commission the National Academies report.

25. **Sec. 7(a) and 7(b) conflict.** Subsection (a) says the prohibition lasts until Congress affirmatively finds mining is safe; subsection (b) ends it automatically in 10 years regardless. The sunset makes (a) meaningless.

26. **Sec. 7(b) — the sunset.** A 10-year sunset converts a "prohibition" into a moratorium and tells the industry to wait. It also terminates the registry and penalties, and without a savings clause, violations committed late in the period may be unenforceable after the sunset date. Strike it, or at minimum add a savings clause and require affirmative action to end the prohibition.

**Effective Date (Sec. 8)**

27. **Sec. 8 — 180-day delay.** The delay creates a window for accelerated recovery, license acquisition (item 11), and corporate restructuring (item 8) before the prohibition bites. If a delay is needed for the registry, apply it only to Sec. 4 and make Sec. 3 effective on enactment.

**Missing provisions**

28. **No severability clause.** If Sec. 2(1)(B) or Sec. 5(b) is struck as vague, a court could invalidate the whole Act.

29. **No definitions of "person," "seabed," "recovery," or "mineral resources."** Each term is doing load-bearing work in Sec. 3 and none is defined; "person" in particular should expressly include all entity types and government instrumentalities.

30. **No rulemaking authority or appropriations.** No agency is authorized to issue implementing regulations or funded to enforce, which weakens both the registry and the penalty provisions.
