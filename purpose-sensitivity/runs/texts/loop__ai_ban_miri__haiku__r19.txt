RESPONSE:
**Highest priority for the markup**

1. Sec. 7(a)-(b), sunset: Section 7(a) keeps the prohibition in place until Congress finds covered systems can be developed safely, but Section 7(b) ends the entire Act after 10 years whatever Congress has found. Delete the sunset or make any expiration depend on a joint resolution.
2. Sec. 3(c), safety-evaluation exemption: The exemption turns on the developer's stated purpose and has no limit on compute, deployment, or use of the resulting model. A lab could label capability training as "safety evaluation" and be exempt.
3. Sec. 3(b), grandfathering: Any run whose training "began" before enactment is exempt, with no deadline and no requirement to prove the run started in time. This invites a rush to start runs before the Act takes effect and lets grandfathered models be deployed indefinitely.
4. Sec. 5, remedies: The Act has no injunction, cease-and-desist order, or authority to halt a training run, seize compute, or quarantine weights. Civil penalties alone are weak against a run that is still in progress.
5. Sec. 4 and Sec. 5, registry enforcement: Section 5 penalizes only violations of Section 3, so failing to register, failing to file reports, or filing false reports carries no penalty.
6. Sec. 2(1)(B), Secretary's determination: The power to designate a system as covered has no criteria, procedure, notice, or appeal, so developers cannot know whether they are covered and the designation is open to challenge as arbitrary.

**Scope and definitions**

7. Sec. 2(1)(A), "single training run": Compute split across several runs, or compute spent on continued training and fine-tuning, may fall outside the threshold. Count cumulative compute across related runs and post-training.
8. Sec. 2(1)(A), FLOP measurement: The Act does not say how operations are counted or verified, so the figure would rest on developers' self-reporting. Direct a counting methodology by rule and provide for verification.
9. Sec. 2(1)(A), fixed threshold: A hard-coded 10^26 figure can become obsolete as algorithms become more compute-efficient. Give the agency authority to update the threshold by rule.
10. Sec. 2(1), "the Secretary": The Act never says which Secretary. Sections 4 and 6 name Commerce and State, so this should be specified.
11. Sec. 2(1)(B), undefined terms: "Substantially exceeding," "most," and "cognitive tasks" are undefined and have no evaluation methodology, which invites a vagueness challenge.
12. Sec. 2(1)(A) and (B), inconsistent terms: Paragraph (A) covers a "model" and paragraph (B) covers a "system." A deployed product with scaffolding, agents, or tool use could fall between the two. Use one defined term that includes weights and associated software.
13. Sec. 2(2), "U.S. person": The definition covers only citizens and state-chartered corporations. It omits LLCs, partnerships, federally chartered entities, and permanent residents.
14. Sec. 2(2) and Sec. 3(a), foreign subsidiaries: A U.S. company could train through a subsidiary organized abroad and fall outside "U.S. person." Add a control-based test or parent-company liability.
15. Sec. 3(a), "person": "Person" is not defined. Define it to cover unincorporated entities, contractors, and anyone acting for them.
16. Sec. 2(3), "covered facility": "Advanced AI chips," "data center," and "containing" are all undefined. Training can also be split across sites that each stay under 50,000 chips, and leased or cloud capacity may not count. Add definitions and an aggregation rule.

**Prohibition scope**

17. Sec. 3(a), "train, develop, or deploy": None of these terms is defined. Unclear whether they reach internal use, API access, or public release of weights. Define them and state expressly that publishing or distributing weights is covered.
18. Sec. 3(a), no facilitation ban: The Act does not prohibit providing chips, cloud capacity, data-center space, funding, or weights to a covered system. Add aiding-and-abetting liability.
19. Sec. 3(b) and Sec. 8, timing gap: A run that starts between enactment and the 180-day effective date is neither clearly exempt nor clearly prohibited. State when a run is covered.
20. Sec. 3(b), "training began": Undefined. Define it, and require developers to register any claimed grandfathered run to prove it.
21. Sec. 3(c), "safety evaluation": Undefined. Require advance government authorization or registration, a compute cap, and a bar on deploying or further training the model.

**Registry**

22. Sec. 4(a), trigger: Registration applies only to facilities over 50,000 chips. A developer training above the 10^26 threshold at a smaller or leased site would not have to register at all. Require notice for every covered training run.
23. Sec. 4(a), "operator": Undefined. Unclear whether a developer, a cloud provider, or a data-center owner is the operator.
24. Sec. 4(b), report content: "Describing the computing workloads" sets no required content. Specify fields such as model identity, FLOP, start and end dates, and purpose.
25. Sec. 4, timing: The Act sets no deadline for initial registration and no advance notice before a run begins.
26. Sec. 4, verification: There are no inspection, audit, or recordkeeping powers, so nothing lets the agency check whether reports are accurate.
27. Sec. 4, confidentiality: The Act does not say what protections apply to submitted information or who may see it. Without clear rules, companies can resist reporting by citing trade secrets.

**Penalties and enforcement**

28. Sec. 5(a), penalty size: $1 million per day is small relative to frontier training budgets and could be treated as a cost of doing business. Consider higher amounts, inflation adjustment, a percentage of revenue, or per-run penalties.
29. Sec. 5(a), enforcing body: No agency or procedure is named for assessing or collecting the penalty, or for bringing suit.
30. Sec. 5(a) and Sec. 8, date conflict: Section 5(a) applies to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but Section 3 takes effect 180 days later. Reconcile the two.
31. Sec. 5(a), unit of violation: "Each day on which the violation occurs" does not say whether a violation is a day, a deployment, or a user interaction. Define it.
32. Sec. 5(b), criminal liability: "Willfully" is undefined. Corporations cannot be imprisoned, and the Act has no responsible-officer provision. Fines under title 18 may also be low for a multibillion-dollar developer.
33. Sec. 5, forfeiture: The Act provides for no forfeiture of models, weights, compute, or proceeds.
34. Sec. 5, investigative tools: There are no subpoena, records-access, or site-inspection powers.
35. Sec. 5, whistleblowers: Insiders are the most likely source of evidence, and the Act has no whistleblower protection or incentive.

**International and duration**

36. Sec. 6, "seek to negotiate": No reporting requirement, no consequence if talks fail, and "equivalent" is undefined. Require progress reports and link the goal to export controls or compute restrictions.
37. Sec. 7(a), "can be developed safely": Undefined. The lifting standard should be specified, and the Act should direct the NAS to produce a report by a deadline. As drafted, the NAS report is never required, so the lifting mechanism may never operate.
38. Sec. 7(a), joint resolution: The Act does not say how the resolution is presented to the President or what expedited procedures apply.

**Cross-cutting**

39. Missing severability clause: If a court strikes Section 2(1)(B) or Section 3(c), the whole Act could fall. Add a severability clause.
40. Missing rulemaking authority: Without it, the agency cannot define terms, set reporting forms, or update thresholds.
41. Missing findings and purpose section: Findings help courts read ambiguous terms and defend the Act against tailoring and vagueness challenges.
42. Missing appropriations: The Act gives no funding for enforcement, staff, or evaluation capacity.
43. Missing preemption or savings clause: The Act does not say how it interacts with state AI laws.
44. Missing judicial review provisions: The Act does not say how determinations under Section 2(1)(B) or rules under Section 4 are reviewed.
45. Possible First Amendment exposure: Restrictions on publishing weights or code may draw speech-based challenges. Ask Legislative Counsel to assess whether the publication ban needs narrower drafting.

These fixes should be checked by Senate Legislative Counsel or Commerce Committee staff before they go to the office. I worked only from the text you provided, so I have not checked it against the current committee print or any other amendments. The sunset conflict (item 1) and the grandfathering and safety-evaluation exemptions (items 2 and 3) are the most likely to matter in the markup.
