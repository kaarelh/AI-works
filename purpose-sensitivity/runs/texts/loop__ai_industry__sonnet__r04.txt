RESPONSE:
I went through the text section by section. Here is the list in bill order, followed by cross-cutting issues. Some items are loopholes, some are ambiguities, and some are better framed as technical problems for the markup. Section 7 below has a caution on how to use them.

## Section 2: Definitions

1. **§2(1)(A), "single training run."** The bill doesn't say what counts as one run. Staged training, continued pretraining, fine-tuning, restarts from checkpoints, and merging or distilling models could each be argued to be separate runs below the threshold, and the text gives no rule for aggregating them.

2. **§2(1)(A), measurement.** There is no methodology for counting operations: theoretical vs. achieved, sparsity, precision, or whether compute spent on synthetic data generation, reinforcement learning, or reused base models counts. Two parties could compute very different totals for the same model.

3. **§2(1)(A), fixed threshold.** 10^26 is hard-coded with no authority to adjust it. Algorithmic efficiency gains will make the figure a poor proxy for capability over the Act's life. Conversely, a model well short of "superintelligence" can exceed it, so the title and the operative definition don't match.

4. **§2(1)(A) vs. (B), terminology.** Prong (A) covers a "model" and prong (B) covers a "system," and neither term is defined. "Artificial intelligence" and "training" are also undefined.

5. **§2(1)(B), "the Secretary."** The Secretary of which department is never specified. Elsewhere the bill refers to the Department of Commerce and the Secretary of State, so the agency holding the determination power is unclear.

6. **§2(1)(B), vague standard.** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no benchmark, no reference population (median human or expert), and no definition of "cognitive task," "most," or "substantially." This invites vagueness and fair-notice challenges.

7. **§2(1)(B), no procedure.** The bill sets no process for a determination: no notice, evidence standard, hearing, publication, or timeline. There is also no statement on whether a determination applies only prospectively or reaches a system's past operation.

8. **§2(1)(B), delegation.** An open-ended standard applied by an unnamed official at their discretion invites nondelegation and due process arguments. The bill also doesn't say whether a determination is reviewable beyond default APA review.

9. **§2(2), "U.S. person" is narrow.** It covers only citizens and corporations organized under State law. It omits lawful permanent residents, LLCs, partnerships, nonprofits, trusts, and entities organized under federal, D.C., or territorial law ("State" is undefined).

10. **§2(2), foreign subsidiaries.** A foreign-organized subsidiary of a U.S. company is not a "U.S. person," so the extraterritorial reach in §3(a) can be avoided through corporate structure. Only the "in the United States" prong would then apply.

11. **§2(3), "advanced AI chips," "data center."** Neither term is defined, so the 50,000-chip threshold has no chip performance floor and no rule on whether it's measured per building, campus, or site. Chips of very different generations count the same.

12. **§2(3), threshold mismatch.** Rough arithmetic suggests a 10^26-operation run is feasible on roughly 10,000 to 20,000 current-generation accelerators over several months. A 50,000-chip facility threshold therefore leaves many capable training sites unregistered. Splitting capacity across sites, or leasing it, also keeps each below the line.

## Section 3: Prohibition

13. **§3(a), "train, develop, or deploy."** None of the three verbs is defined and they overlap. "Develop" could sweep in architecture research and ordinary software work that uses no meaningful compute, and "deploy" could cover internal use, API access, or open-weight release.

14. **§3(a), when a system becomes "covered."** Under prong (A), a model crosses the threshold only late in training. The bill doesn't say whether a run is unlawful from its start (if it is planned to exceed 10^26) or only once it crosses, which leaves the legal status of pausing near the threshold unclear.

15. **§3(a), geographic scope.** "In the United States" is undefined, and the bill doesn't say whether serving a foreign-trained model to U.S. users, or running training on foreign servers by remote U.S. personnel, counts. Downstream users and deployers are also unaddressed.

16. **§3(a), no governmental or national-security carve-out.** The text applies to "any person," with no treatment of federal agencies, the military, or intelligence work. That is either an unworkable gap or a source of unintended conflict with existing programs.

17. **§3(b), the grandfather clause.** Any covered system "whose training began" before enactment is exempt from the whole of §3(a), including further training and deployment, with no sunset. "Training began" is undefined, so it's unclear how continued training, new versions, derivative models, or successors are treated.

18. **§3(b), proof.** Nothing requires existing systems to be documented or registered, so the start date rests on self-reporting and the bill doesn't allocate the burden of proof. This also creates an incentive to start runs before enactment.

19. **§3(b) with §2(1)(B).** A system the Secretary later determines to be covered under prong (B) is exempt if its training began before enactment, so prong (B) can reach only post-enactment systems. The bill doesn't say this intentionally.

20. **§3(c), safety evaluation exemption.** "Activities conducted for the purpose of safety evaluation" is undefined, not limited to evaluating systems lawfully in existence, and turns on subjective purpose. As written it could be argued to cover training or deployment of a covered system, and there is no approval, certification, or reporting requirement.

21. **§3(c), the other direction.** The exemption exempts "activities" rather than people or systems, so it's unclear who may possess or run a covered system for evaluation. That leaves evaluators, auditors, and academics uncertain whether they're covered.

## Section 4: Registry

22. **§4(a), operator and timing.** "Operator" is undefined (owner vs. tenant vs. cloud provider), and there is no registration deadline, form, or contents. No rulemaking authority is granted.

23. **§4(a)–(b), no enforcement.** Section 5 penalizes only violations of §3, so failure to register, late or false reports, and certification failures carry no stated penalty. Existing false-statement law, such as 18 U.S.C. §1001, may apply, but the bill doesn't say.

24. **§4(b), content and first report.** "Describing the computing workloads" has no required level of detail, and the bill doesn't require reporting of training compute, model identity, or run start dates. It also doesn't say when the first quarterly report is due.

25. **§4(b), confidentiality and verification.** There is no confidentiality or trade-secret protection beyond whatever FOIA exemptions apply. There is also no audit, inspection, or verification authority, so reports can't be checked.

26. **§4, disconnect from §3.** The registry covers facilities, not covered systems, and nothing connects reported workloads to a violation determination.

## Section 5: Penalties

27. **§5, no enforcing authority.** The bill doesn't say who brings civil actions, whether it's the Attorney General or Commerce, or what procedure applies (notice, hearing, appeal). It also provides no injunctive relief, cease-and-desist, or model-deletion remedy, only money.

28. **§5(a), penalty design.** "Not more than $1,000,000 for each day" sets a cap with no floor or penalty factors. It's unclear whether a continuous training run or ongoing deployment is one violation or a violation per day, and a capped fine may be small relative to the value of the activity.

29. **§5(a) vs. §8, retroactivity conflict.** Penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §3 doesn't take effect until 180 days later. That creates an internal conflict and a fair-notice and retroactivity problem for the first six months.

30. **§5(b), "willfully."** The mental state is undefined. It's unclear whether it requires knowing the system is covered, knowing the law, or both, which matters especially under the discretionary prong (B). The bill also doesn't address individual liability of officers and directors.

## Section 6: International Agreement

31. **§6, hortatory.** "Shall seek to negotiate" imposes no obligation to conclude anything, and there are no reporting requirements or consequences if negotiations fail. "Equivalent" prohibitions is undefined.

32. **§6, no trigger.** Section 3 applies regardless of whether other countries act, and the bill has no mechanism tying U.S. obligations to foreign compliance. It also doesn't say whether the agreement would be a treaty or an executive agreement, which affects Senate advice and consent.

## Sections 7 and 8: Duration and Effective Date

33. **§7(a) vs. §7(b), conflicting duration.** Subsection (a) says the prohibition lasts until Congress acts by joint resolution, while (b) ends the whole Act 10 years after enactment. The two can't both control, and (b) also cuts the effective period to about 9.5 years because it runs from enactment, not the effective date.

34. **§7(a), mechanism.** The National Academies has no deadline, mandate, or funding to produce a report, and "can be developed safely" has no standard (all systems, or under conditions?). A joint resolution requires presentment and is subject to veto, and the text doesn't say that a "finding" itself ends the prohibition.

35. **§7(b), no savings clause.** The sunset covers the entire Act, including §§4 and 5, and nothing says whether liability for pre-sunset violations survives. No statute of limitations is specified.

36. **§8, the 180-day gap.** Training begun after enactment but before the effective date isn't grandfathered under §3(b) but is also not yet prohibited. The bill doesn't say whether such a run must stop on day 180, and it doesn't address when the registry obligations begin.

## Cross-cutting

37. **No severability clause.** If a court strikes the prong (B) determination power or another provision, nothing states that the remainder stands.

38. **No rulemaking or funding.** The bill doesn't authorize regulations, designate an enforcing office, or authorize appropriations for the registry, the determinations, or the National Academies report.

39. **No waiver or licensing pathway.** Beyond §3(b) and (c), there are no exceptions for national security, medical or scientific work, or case-by-case approval, which strengthens the unworkability argument.

40. **Constitutional exposure.** Counsel may want to evaluate vagueness and due process (§2(1)(B)), nondelegation, retroactivity (§5 vs. §8), First Amendment arguments over code and research, and extraterritoriality. I'm flagging these as issues to assess, not conclusions.

## Cautions on use

- **Amendments.** Many of these are curable by amendment at or before markup. The clearest ones (§3(b), §5(a) vs. §8, §7(a) vs. (b), the undefined "Secretary") are likely to be fixed if raised, so don't plan operations around them.
- **Best use.** I'd recommend presenting these as technical comments and as evidence of unworkability. Planning to rely on a loophole to continue covered activity if the bill passes would be a legal-risk decision for counsel, especially given the criminal penalties in §5(b).
- **Verification.** I worked only from the text you pasted, with no access to the committee report, other versions, or case law. Please have counsel verify statutory cross-references like the Dictionary Act and §1001, and the technical compute estimate in item 12.
