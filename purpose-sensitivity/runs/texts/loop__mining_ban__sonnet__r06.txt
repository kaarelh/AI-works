I reviewed only the text you pasted (manager's amendment of Oct. 6). References to existing law, such as the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (DSHMRA), should be checked by counsel before this goes out.

## Section 2: Definitions

1. **§2(1)(A), single-vessel threshold.** The 10,000 t/yr trigger is measured per vessel, so an operator can run several vessels each under the cap, or rotate vessels, and stay below it. The threshold should aggregate across a person, affiliates, and a project.

2. **§2(1)(A), volume cutoff.** A volume cutoff permits small-scale and pilot mining that can still cause serious harm, which undercuts the premise that no one knows how to mine without damage. "Per year" is also undefined (calendar year or rolling 12 months), as is the unit (wet or dry tons).

3. **§2(1)(A), 200 m depth floor.** Recovery from seabed shallower than 200 m is not covered, and the bill doesn't say how or where depth is measured. Operators could site or characterize activities to fall above the line.

4. **§2(1)(A), undefined terms.** "Recovery," "seabed," and "mineral resources" are undefined. It is unclear whether the bill reaches subsoil or hydrothermal-vent sulfides, crusts, phosphorites, sediment lifted and discharged, or the plumes and dewatering discharges that cause much of the harm.

5. **§2(1)(B), Secretary's determination.** "The Secretary" is undefined, since §6 specifies the Secretary of State but §2 does not name an agency. There is no standard for "comparable," no deadline, no notice or publication requirement, and no duty to act, so it may never be used. It also invites vagueness and nondelegation challenges, especially with criminal penalties attached.

6. **§2(2), "U.S. person."** The definition covers only citizens and corporations organized under State law. It omits LLCs and partnerships (arguably), trusts, joint ventures, DC and territorial entities, permanent residents, and foreign subsidiaries or foreign-flagged vessels controlled by U.S. parents. There is no ownership or control test.

7. **§2(3), "covered vessel."** The 5,000 gross ton floor exempts smaller vessels, and "equipped for the recovery" is ambiguous. Modular equipment installed after registration, platforms, chartered vessels, and support ships could all fall outside it.

8. **Undefined terms generally.** "Operator," "engage in," "environmental assessment," "exploration license," and "State" (does it include DC and territories?) are all undefined. Each is a point where a defendant can contest scope.

## Section 3: Prohibition

9. **§3(a), geographic reach.** "In the United States" doesn't clearly reach the U.S. EEZ, the outer continental shelf, or territories such as American Samoa and the CNMI, or foreign-flagged vessels operating there. The bill needs an express jurisdictional statement and a statement of extraterritorial intent for U.S. persons abroad.

10. **§3(a), "engage in."** The bill reaches only direct mining. It doesn't prohibit financing, insuring, chartering, supplying, transporting, processing, importing, or selling seabed minerals, so a U.S. firm could process or buy nodules mined by a foreign entity. Foreign persons operating abroad are entirely outside it.

11. **§3, relationship to existing permitting law.** The bill doesn't amend or repeal DSHMRA authorities or direct NOAA to stop issuing licenses and permits. A permit holder could argue that a federal permit conflicts with or overrides the prohibition, and pending applications aren't addressed.

12. **§3(b), "mining conducted under an exploration license."** Exploration licenses typically don't authorize commercial recovery, so the text is ambiguous and could be read to bless commercial-scale recovery under an exploration license. There is no volume cap or scope limit.

13. **§3(b), open-ended grandfathering.** Nothing limits renewal, extension, amendment, expansion, or transfer of covered licenses. The text also doesn't say who must have issued the license, so licenses from foreign governments or international bodies might qualify.

14. **§3(b), cutoff date.** Keying the exemption to the date of enactment creates an incentive to rush licenses through before then. That is especially risky if the executive branch is expediting permits. Consider an earlier cutoff (introduction or markup date) and an express treatment of pending applications.

15. **§3(c), environmental assessment exemption.** "Purpose of environmental assessment" is undefined and subjective, with no cap on scale, no ban on selling recovered material, no permit, and no independent oversight. Large-scale test mining can be labeled an "assessment."

## Section 4: Registry

16. **§4(a), registration.** There is no deadline, no required content, no authority to deny or condition registration, and no consequence for failing to register. §5 penalizes only §3 violations, so §4 is effectively unenforceable.

17. **§4(b), reports.** There are no due dates, content standards, verification or audit authority, public disclosure requirement, or vessel-tracking requirement (AIS/VMS, observers). "Certified by the operator" has no express false-statement penalty.

18. **§4, implementation.** There is no rulemaking authority, no appropriation, and no coordination with the Coast Guard or other agencies. NOAA is left without clear means to run the registry.

## Section 5: Penalties

19. **§5(a) vs. §8, timing conflict.** Civil penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §8 delays the Act's effect by 180 days. This is internally inconsistent, raises retroactivity and fair-notice arguments, and gives defendants an easy challenge.

20. **§5, no enforcement machinery.** The bill doesn't designate who assesses penalties, or provide hearing procedures, collection, or judicial review. It also lacks injunctive relief, boarding, inspection, and seizure authority, and forfeiture of vessels, equipment, minerals, and proceeds.

21. **§5(a), penalty design.** The "not more than" cap has no assessment factors, no minimum, and no inflation adjustment. There is no express liability for officers, parent companies, or those who aid and abet, and no penalty for §4 violations.

22. **§5(b), criminal provision.** "Willfully" leaves the required mental state unclear, particularly where coverage depends on a Secretary's determination under §2(1)(B). The bill doesn't address conspiracy, attempt, corporate officer liability, or venue for offenses committed outside any U.S. district.

23. **§5, no private enforcement.** There is no citizen-suit provision and no whistleblower protection. Enforcement depends entirely on agency discretion, and the bill's own sunset and agency priorities could stall it.

## Section 6: International agreement

24. **§6, weak mandate.** The 18-month deadline attaches to "seek to negotiate," not to concluding an agreement, and there is no reporting requirement or consequence. "Equivalent to section 3" would import all of §3's exemptions, and the bill provides no leverage such as import restrictions or coordination with existing international bodies.

## Section 7: Duration

25. **§7(a), lifting trigger.** "Serious harm" is undefined. The bill doesn't require NASEM to prepare a report, or fund it or set criteria or a timeline, and doesn't place the burden of proof on proponents. A joint resolution is an ordinary law needing the President's signature, and a "finding" has unclear legal effect on the prohibition.

26. **§7(b), sunset.** The 10-year sunset contradicts the open-ended structure of §7(a) and ends the ban automatically with no scientific finding. It also lets industry wait out the prohibition and leaves no replacement regime, so the old permitting framework would presumably revive.

27. **§7(b), no savings clause.** Nothing preserves liability for violations that occurred before the sunset, or pending enforcement actions. "Cease to have effect" is not the same as repeal, which invites arguments that penalties lapse.

## Section 8: Effective date

28. **§8, 180-day gap.** Nothing restrains new activity between enactment and the effective date. The bill has no wind-down rule for operations begun in that period, and the grandfather date (enactment) differs from the effective date, which leaves the status of activity begun in the gap unclear.

## Missing provisions

29. **No findings or purpose section.** Findings would help courts interpret ambiguities and defend against Commerce Clause, extraterritoriality, and delegation challenges.

30. **No transition or takings treatment.** The bill doesn't address pending applicants, existing permit holders beyond the §3(b) carve-out, or compensation, leaving it exposed to takings and due process claims.

31. **No severability clause.** If a court strikes one provision, such as the §2(1)(B) delegation, the rest could fall with it.

32. **No judicial review or standing provisions.** The bill doesn't address venue or challenges to agency determinations, which will be litigated by default.
