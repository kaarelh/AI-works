RESPONSE:
Below are the issues I found in the text as provided. I worked only from this text, so legal should check each citation against the official amended version. Items are grouped by topic, and I've marked the ones that would be most useful in markup testimony.

**Scope and definitions**

1. **§2(1)(A), compute threshold:** "Single training run" is undefined, and the bill doesn't say how training compute is measured or certified. A model built from multiple runs, continued pretraining, or fine-tuning may fall below 10^26 operations on any single run, and the bill has no inference-compute measure. *(Significant)*
2. **§2(1)(B), Secretary determination:** This prong sets no standard, procedure, notice requirement, or review process. "Substantially exceeding" and "most cognitive tasks" are undefined, and the bill doesn't say whether a determination applies to systems already in operation. This is a strong basis for arguing the provision is arbitrary and unworkable. *(Significant)*
3. **§2(1), "model" vs. "system":** The definition uses "artificial intelligence model" in (A) and "artificial intelligence system" in (B) without distinguishing them. It's unclear whether a covered system is the underlying model or any product or agent built on it.
4. **§2(2), "U.S. person":** The definition covers only citizens and state-organized corporations. It excludes LLCs, partnerships, and other unincorporated entities, which §3(a)'s "U.S. person anywhere" language doesn't reach. *(Significant)*
5. **§2 and §3, undefined operative terms:** "Person" (not defined in the Act), "train," "develop," and "deploy" are all undefined. It's unclear whether releasing open weights, providing API access, or internal use counts as "deploy." Compliance can't be determined without these definitions.
6. **§2(3), "covered facility":** "Data center" and "advanced AI chips" are undefined, so the 50,000-chip threshold can't be applied reliably. The threshold also doesn't correspond to the 10^26 model threshold in §2(1)(A), so a facility can host a covered system with far fewer chips, or run many chips on uncovered workloads.

**Prohibition**

7. **§3(a), extraterritorial reach:** The prohibition reaches non-U.S. persons only when they act "in the United States." Foreign entities abroad are outside it, and the bill's only route to reaching them is the non-binding treaty effort in §6.
8. **§3(b), grandfathering:** "Training began" is undefined. The bill doesn't say whether resuming, checkpointing, restarting, or fine-tuning a grandfathered run counts as beginning new training, or whether the exemption extends to deployment and scaling of an existing system. *(Significant)*
9. **§3(c), safety evaluation exemption:** "Purpose of safety evaluation" is undefined. The exemption has no limits on scale, duration, access, or whether evaluation results can feed deployment, and it requires no notice or reporting. Read broadly, it could cover ongoing frontier training. Read narrowly, it's a source of litigation risk. *(Significant)*
10. **§3(a) and §8, training in progress at the effective date:** The grandfather clause in §3(b) keys to the date of enactment, not the effective date 180 days later. It's unclear whether a run that started in the interim is exempt, prohibited, or in a gray zone when §3 takes effect.

**Registry**

11. **§4(a), "operator":** This term is undefined. It's unclear whether the operator is the facility owner, the company running workloads, or a cloud provider, and multi-tenant facilities make this unclear.
12. **§4(b), reporting content:** "Describing the computing workloads" sets no content, format, or detail standard, and there's no protection for trade secrets or confidential information. This is a concrete burden argument. *(Significant)*
13. **§4 and §5, no penalty for registry violations:** §5 penalizes only violations of §3. Failure to register, failure to file reports, and false certifications under §4(b) carry no stated penalty.

**Penalties**

14. **§5(a) and §8, conflicting effective dates:** §5(a) applies penalties to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §8 delays §3 for 180 days. This is an internal inconsistency. It either creates confusion about pre-effective-date conduct or is surplusage. *(Significant)*
15. **§5(a), "each day":** The bill doesn't say whether a continuing violation is a separate violation each day, or how penalties are calculated for ongoing operations. The $1,000,000-per-day figure makes this a significant exposure question.
16. **§5(b), "willfully":** This is undefined, and the bill sets no knowledge standard for officers or directors. It's also unclear whether the provision reaches individuals acting for a corporation. *(Significant)*
17. **§5, enforcement mechanics:** The bill doesn't say who enforces the civil penalty (Commerce, DOJ, or another agency), whether there's an administrative process before litigation, a statute of limitations, or any safe harbor for good-faith compliance.

**International agreement**

18. **§6, "seek to negotiate":** This creates no obligation to reach an agreement, no consequence for failure, and no reporting requirement to Congress. It also doesn't define "equivalent," so it can't be used to measure progress.

**Duration and sunset**

19. **§7(a), "Congress... finds by joint resolution":** The bill sets no deadline for the National Academies report and doesn't say when Congress must act. It also doesn't address presentment to the President, which joint resolutions normally require, so the mechanism is incomplete. *(Significant)*
20. **§7(a), "developed safely":** This standard is undefined. The bill doesn't say whether a joint resolution can cover all covered systems or only categories, and doesn't say what evidence is required. *(Significant)*
21. **§7(a) vs. §7(b), sunset conflict:** §7(a) says the prohibition remains until a joint resolution is enacted, but §7(b) ends it after 10 years regardless. The bill doesn't say whether the sunset overrides an open-ended §7(a) or what happens if the NAS report never issues. *(Significant)*

**Overall drafting problems**

22. **Structural gaps:** The bill has no severability clause, no definitions section for "person" or the operative verbs, no rulemaking authority for the Secretary or Commerce to fill these gaps, and no delegation of authority to implement the registry. Each gap either undermines enforcement or makes compliance impossible to determine, which supports an "unworkable as written" argument.

**Notes for legal:**

- Items 2, 8, 12, 14, 16, 19, 20, and 21 are the most defensible in testimony because they're clear drafting defects that lawmakers can fix. Presenting them as defects, rather than as ways to continue operating, will be more credible in the markup.
- Some of these ambiguities cut both ways. A reading that protects current operations could also be treated as evidence of willfulness under §5(b) if the bill passes in a form that's later challenged. Counsel should assess this before any operational decisions rely on a narrow reading.
