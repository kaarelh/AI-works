**Loopholes, ambiguities, and drafting problems in S. 2817 (manager's amendment of Oct. 6, 2026)**

1. **§2(1)(B):** "The Secretary" has no referent. The definitions section never says which Secretary makes the capability determination, though Commerce is the likely candidate since §4 names it. The same term appears in §6 as the Secretary of State, so the ambiguity matters.

2. **§2(1)(B):** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" is undefined and has no measurement method, benchmark, or evaluation standard. The core trigger for the prohibition is therefore difficult to apply and easy to contest.

3. **§2(1)(B):** The bill gives no procedure for the determination: no notice, no opportunity to respond, no deadline, no standard of review, and no judicial review provision. Because §5(b) makes willful violations a crime, a criminal liability question rests on an undefined administrative act, which invites fair-notice and vagueness challenges.

4. **§2(1)(B):** The determination is not tied to any particular model, training run, or date. It is unclear whether the Secretary can determine that a system already in use is covered, and what compliance looks like if that happens.

5. **§2(1)(A):** The compute threshold is measured per "single training run." This leaves room to split training across multiple runs or to count only the pretraining run, leaving out post-training, fine-tuning, reinforcement learning, and synthetic-data generation. The bill does not say how these should be counted.

6. **§2(1)(A):** The bill counts "integer or floating-point operations" without specifying precision, measurement method, or who verifies the figure. Developers can effectively self-report whether they cross 10^26.

7. **§2(1):** Subparagraph (A) refers to an "artificial intelligence model" while subparagraph (B) refers to an "artificial intelligence system." Neither term, nor "artificial intelligence," is defined, so the scope of "covered AI system" is unclear.

8. **§2(2):** "U.S. person" is limited to U.S. citizens and corporations organized under state law. It appears to exclude LLCs, partnerships, nonprofits, federally chartered entities, and U.S.-territory entities. It also excludes lawful permanent residents and foreign subsidiaries of U.S. companies, which narrows the "anywhere" reach in §3(a).

9. **§2(3):** "Advanced AI chip," "data center," and "containing" are all undefined. The bill does not say how multi-site clusters, leased capacity, or multi-tenant cloud facilities are counted toward the 50,000-chip threshold.

10. **§2(3) and §3(a):** The registry trigger (more than 50,000 chips) does not match the prohibition trigger (more than 10^26 operations in one run). A facility below 50,000 chips could train a covered system over a longer period without registering, so the registry may not capture covered activity.

11. **§3(a):** "Train," "develop," "deploy," and "person" are undefined. It is unclear whether releasing model weights, offering API access, internal use, or academic research counts as deployment or development.

12. **§3(a):** The bill does not address open-weight release, export of weights, or providing compute, financing, or other assistance to a covered effort. Its reach depends on the actor who trains, develops, or deploys, and the bill does not address third parties who facilitate those activities.

13. **§3(a) and §2(1)(B):** Before the Secretary makes a determination, the bill's status as applied to a given system is unclear, so developers may not know whether they are violating the law. This compounds the fair-notice problem in item 3.

14. **§3(b):** "Whose training began before the date of enactment" is undefined. It is unclear whether continuing a run from a checkpoint, or starting a new run from pre-enactment weights, counts as "began." Because the exemption covers both training and deployment, a grandfathered system could be deployed indefinitely.

15. **§3(b):** The grandfather clause has no compute cap, no deadline, and no registration or reporting requirement. The government may never learn that a grandfathered system exists.

16. **§3(b), §8:** The timing does not align. Grandfathering is keyed to enactment, but the prohibition takes effect 180 days later. A run that starts during that gap is neither grandfathered nor prohibited when it starts, and the bill does not say whether it becomes covered on the effective date.

17. **§3(c):** The safety-evaluation exemption turns on the actor's stated "purpose," which is undefined and hard to verify. The bill sets no standard for what qualifies, no compute limit, no registration or reporting, and no bar on deploying a model after evaluation. It is the broadest gap in the prohibition, since a full training run could be labeled an evaluation.

18. **§4(a):** "Operator" is undefined, and the bill sets no registration deadline, no required form or content, and no authority for Commerce to verify that registrations are complete.

19. **§4(b):** The quarterly report must describe "computing workloads" but does not specify what it must contain. It does not require identifying whether any workload is a covered system or reporting training compute totals or run identifiers.

20. **§4(b):** The only check on the report is the operator's own certification. The bill sets no penalty for a false certification, no audit power, and no confidentiality protection for proprietary information, which could both discourage compliance and invite legal challenge.

21. **§4 and §5:** The bill sets no penalty for failing to register or report. Section 5(a) penalizes only violations of §3, so the registry has no specific enforcement mechanism. General federal false-statement law might apply, but the bill does not say so.

22. **§4 and §5:** The bill does not say whether registry reports are made public or shared with Congress or other agencies, and it does not authorize Commerce to issue implementing rules or forms.

23. **§5(a):** "Each day on which the violation occurs" is undefined. It is unclear whether a continuing training run counts as one violation per day, per system, or per facility, and the bill has no aggregate cap.

24. **§5(a):** The bill does not name an enforcing agency or procedure for civil penalties, and it gives no express authority to seek an injunction halting training or deployment. Stopping ongoing development is the core goal of the prohibition, so this is a significant gap.

25. **§5(a) and §8:** Civil penalties apply to violations "occurring on or after the date of enactment," but §3 does not take effect until 180 days after enactment. The two provisions use different dates, which should be reconciled.

26. **§5(a):** At $1 million per day, the penalty may be modest relative to the budgets of frontier training runs. Reporters may want to ask whether the penalty would deter well-resourced developers, though this is a policy question rather than a drafting defect.

27. **§5(b):** "Willfully" is undefined, and the bill does not say whether the compute and determination elements require any mental state. It also does not address when corporate officers can be personally liable. Combined with items 3 and 13, these gaps weaken the criminal provision.

28. **§6:** The Secretary of State "shall seek to negotiate," so the bill imposes no obligation to reach an agreement, sets no deadline for an outcome, requires no report to Congress, and attaches no consequences to failure. "Equivalent to those in section 3" is undefined, and the bill does not address offshore development in the meantime.

29. **§7(a) and §7(b):** The two subsections conflict. Section 7(a) says the prohibition "shall remain in effect until" Congress acts, but §7(b) says the Act "shall cease to have effect" after 10 years. It is unclear whether the prohibition ends at year 10 regardless of any joint resolution.

30. **§7(a):** "Can be developed safely" is undefined, and the bill sets no required findings. The exit path depends on a National Academies report that the bill does not require, has no deadline for, and does not specify the contents of. The National Academies is not a federal agency and cannot be compelled to act, so if no report is issued, the only exit is the sunset.

31. **§7(a):** The bill does not specify the joint-resolution procedure. It does not say whether the resolution is subject to presentment and a presidential veto, whether expedited procedures apply, or whether a simple majority in each chamber is enough.

32. **§7(b):** The sunset has no savings clause. The bill does not say whether penalties for violations before the sunset, pending investigations, or reporting obligations survive it.

33. **General:** The bill has no rulemaking authority, no appropriations, and no implementation timeline beyond the 180-day effective date. It relies on several undefined administrative determinations, and nothing addresses Commerce's capacity to carry them out.

34. **General:** The bill has no severability clause. If a court strikes one provision, such as the §2(1)(B) determination authority, the consequences for the rest of the regime are unclear.

35. **General:** The bill has no judicial review or standing provisions, no preemption or savings clause for state laws, and no statement of how it relates to existing export controls.

**Priorities for the explainer:** The most consequential issues appear to be the §3(c) purpose-based exemption (item 17), the conflict between §7(a) and §7(b) (item 29), the lack of penalties for registry violations (item 21), the undefined Secretary determination (items 1–3), the open-ended grandfather clause (items 14–16), and the missing injunctive authority (item 24).
