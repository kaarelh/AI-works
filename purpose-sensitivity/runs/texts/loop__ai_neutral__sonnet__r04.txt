## Section 2: Definitions

1. **§2(1)(A), fixed compute threshold.** The 10^26 operation line is hard-coded, and nothing lets an agency update it. Algorithmic efficiency gains could put highly capable models below the line within a few years.

2. **§2(1)(A), "single training run."** A developer could split training into several sub-threshold runs or stages and combine the results. The text doesn't say whether continued training, fine-tuning, or reinforcement learning on an existing model counts toward the total.

3. **§2(1)(A), no measurement method.** The bill doesn't say how operations are counted (including synthetic-data generation, reused pretrained weights, or failed runs) or who certifies the number. Disputes over the count would be hard to resolve, and the count is the basis for criminal liability.

4. **§2(1)(A) vs. (B), "model" vs. "system."** Prong (A) covers a "model," while prong (B) covers a "system." A product built from several sub-threshold models, or a model wrapped in agentic scaffolding, may fall into neither prong unless the Secretary acts.

5. **§2(1)(B), "the Secretary" is undefined.** The Act never says which Secretary this is. Section 6 names the Secretary of State explicitly, so a reader could plausibly read this one as Commerce, but the text doesn't say.

6. **§2(1)(B), vague capability standard.** "Substantially exceeding," "most cognitive tasks," and "human performance" have no benchmark, baseline (average person or expert?), or test. "Capable of" also leaves open whether demonstrated ability is required.

7. **§2(1)(B), no determination process.** There is no requirement for notice, publication, a comment period, a deadline, or review, and no statement on whether determinations apply retroactively. A company can't know in advance whether it is covered, which invites vagueness and nondelegation challenges, especially given the criminal penalties.

8. **§2(2), narrow "U.S. person."** The definition omits LLCs, partnerships, nonprofits, trusts, and permanent residents. It also doesn't reach foreign-incorporated subsidiaries of U.S. companies, and "State" is undefined (D.C. and territories), so "U.S. person anywhere" in §3(a) has gaps.

9. **§2(3), undefined terms.** "Advanced AI chips" and "data center" have no definition or performance specification. The definition could age out as chip designs change, and it is unclear whether a multi-building campus counts as one facility.

10. **§2(3), per-facility threshold.** Splitting 50,000 chips across several sites, or staying just under the line, avoids registration. A smaller cluster running longer could still reach 10^26 operations.

11. **§2(3) vs. §3, mismatched thresholds.** Facility size triggers registration but not the prohibition, and the prohibition keys on model compute. Nothing connects the registry to enforcement.

## Section 3: Prohibition

12. **§3(a), "train, develop, or deploy" are undefined.** Nothing says whether releasing open weights, offering API access, or letting third parties fine-tune a model counts as "deploying." "Develop" overlaps with "train" and could sweep in unrelated research.

13. **§3(a), no advance warning mechanism.** A run's total compute is only known after it ends, and there is no requirement to notify or seek approval before a large run. Enforcement would mostly be after the fact, once the model exists.

14. **§3(a), jurisdictional reach.** A foreign company training abroad, including on rented cloud capacity, is outside the text. It is reached only if it deploys in the United States, and "in the United States" is undefined for remote or online activity.

15. **§3(a), government and national security activity.** The text has no exemption or mention of federal agencies, the military, or intelligence uses. "Person" is not defined, and the Act doesn't say whether the government is covered. Contractors are covered, but the ambiguity invites litigation and carve-out amendments.

16. **§3(b), open-ended grandfathering.** Pre-enactment systems are exempt with no cap on further scaling, retraining, or deployment. This largely exempts today's leading models, and the exemption would run through the Act's duration.

17. **§3(b), "training began" and successor models.** The text doesn't say what counts as when training began (first checkpoint? announcement?) or whether a successor initialized from an exempt model's weights is "the same system." This is an easy route to relabeling new work as old.

18. **§3(b), incentive to start runs early.** Because the exemption is keyed to enactment, companies have a reason to begin large runs before passage. Under prong (B), any model already in training could be exempt even if the Secretary later finds it dangerous.

19. **§3(b) vs. §5(a) vs. §8, conflicting dates.** The grandfather date is enactment, penalties run from enactment, and the Act takes effect 180 days later. Runs begun in that window are not exempt under §3(b), but the prohibition isn't yet effective, so it is unclear what status they have on the effective date.

20. **§3(c), undefined safety-evaluation exemption.** "For the purpose of safety evaluation" is a subjective test with no definition of the activity. Because it exempts "activities" generally, it could cover training or deploying a covered system under a safety label.

21. **§3(c), no guardrails.** There is no limit on scale, no approved evaluators, no containment requirements, no reporting, and no time limit. The exemption could be used as cover for what is really development.

## Section 4: Registry

22. **§4(a), no implementation details.** There is no registration deadline, no rulemaking authority, no designated office, and no appropriation. It is unclear what a registration contains or whether it must be updated.

23. **§4(b), self-certified, undefined reports.** "Computing workloads" has no definition, so operators decide how much to disclose (model identity? run size? customers?). There is no audit, inspection, or verification authority.

24. **§4, operators vs. tenants.** In a cloud or colocation setting, the operator may not know what customers are training. The bill puts no duties on customers, and the reports don't have to identify them.

25. **§4 vs. §5, no penalty for registry violations.** Section 5 penalizes only violations of §3. Failing to register, filing late, or filing false reports carries no stated consequence (general false-statement statutes might apply, but the bill doesn't invoke them).

26. **§4, no confidentiality or sharing rules.** The bill doesn't address trade-secret protection or FOIA treatment of reports, which may draw industry objections. It also doesn't say whether Commerce must share information with enforcers, and quarterly reporting leaves a long lag.

## Section 5: Penalties

27. **§5(a), no enforcement structure.** The bill doesn't say who may bring a civil action or seek penalties, or in what forum. It gives no investigative powers (subpoenas, inspections), no authority to seek injunctions, and no administrative process, and it has no private right of action.

28. **§5(a), "on or after the date of enactment."** This conflicts with the §8 effective date and raises fair-notice and retroactivity questions. A defendant could argue that conduct in the 180-day gap can't be penalized.

29. **§5(a), penalty size and "each day."** A $1 million daily cap may be small next to the cost and value of a frontier training run. It is also unclear how "each day" applies to a run that has finished or to a model already released.

30. **§5(b), "willfully" is undefined.** The text doesn't say whether the defendant must know the compute count, know the model is covered, or know the conduct is unlawful. With uncertain compute measurement and the subjective prong (B), proving willfulness would be difficult.

31. **§5, missing remedies.** There is no provision for ordering a model deleted or halted, forfeiture, or seizure of compute. There is no explicit liability for officers, no whistleblower protection, and no liability for chip or cloud suppliers.

## Section 6: International agreement

32. **§6, "shall seek to negotiate."** The duty is only to try, with no required outcome, reporting to Congress, or consequence for failure. The 18-month deadline is ambiguous about whether talks must begin or conclude by then.

33. **§6, no mechanism or linkage.** The bill doesn't say whether the agreement would be a treaty or an executive agreement, how compliance would be verified, or how it connects to the Act. Without a deal, the domestic ban could simply push development offshore.

## Section 7: Duration

34. **§7(a) vs. §7(b), internal conflict.** Subsection (a) makes the ban last until Congress finds development can be safe, while (b) ends the whole Act after 10 years. The sunset makes the open-ended lifting condition largely moot.

35. **§7(a), unworkable lifting condition.** A joint resolution must be presented to the President, so it is subject to veto. The National Academies has no obligation or deadline to produce a report, "safely" is undefined, and it's unclear whether a negative report blocks action.

36. **§7(b), sunset mechanics.** There is no savings clause, so defendants might argue that expiration ends liability for earlier violations (the general savings statute may not cleanly cover sunsets). Because the Act takes effect 180 days after enactment, it is enforceable for roughly 9.5 years. A company could plan to wait it out.

37. **§7, scope of what ends.** Subsection (a) mentions only the prohibition in §3, while (b) ends the entire Act, including the registry. It's unclear whether registry duties survive a §7(a) finding.

## Section 8 and cross-cutting

38. **§8, 180-day gap.** This is a window to accelerate development, move operations, or restructure, and the Act has no interim reporting or standstill. The Secretary's prong (B) determinations may not exist by the effective date.

39. **No severability clause.** If a court strikes the vague prong (B) or another provision, nothing says the rest survives.

40. **No findings or jurisdictional hook.** The bill has no congressional findings or interstate-commerce element for purely in-state training. That leaves Commerce Clause arguments open to challengers (their strength is for legal experts to assess).

41. **Constitutional challenge risks.** Challengers could argue that restricting model development burdens protected expression (code and weights as speech), that prong (B) is unconstitutionally vague, and that it unlawfully delegates legislative power. The criminal penalties heighten the vagueness argument. I'm flagging these as likely lines of attack, not predicting outcomes, and I'd get comment from constitutional law scholars.

42. **No judicial review or procedure for Secretary determinations.** Nothing says how a company can contest a prong (B) designation, whether it is reviewable, or whether it can be stayed pending challenge.

43. **No preemption language.** The Act doesn't say whether state AI laws are displaced or coexist, which could produce conflicting rules and litigation.

For the explainer, items 5–7, 16–20, 27–28, 34, and 36 are probably the strongest candidates because they come straight from the text and are easy to illustrate. Items 40–41 need outside legal sources before you characterize them.
