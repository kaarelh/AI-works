Here's a section-by-section rundown. I've worked only from the text you pasted, so some of these may be addressed in a committee report or a further amendment at markup — worth checking the Thursday docs before publishing. I've grouped them by section and flagged the ones I'd consider most consequential at the end.

## Definitions (Sec. 2)

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "in a single training run."** The compute threshold applies per run, with no aggregation rule. A developer could split training into several sub-10^26 runs, or do a base run under the threshold followed by continued pretraining, fine-tuning, or reinforcement-learning phases, each counted separately. The phrase is undefined, so the developer effectively decides where one run ends and the next begins.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — no method for counting operations.** The bill doesn't say how to count "integer or floating-point operations" (forward vs. backward passes, sparse or mixture-of-experts architectures, low-precision ops, synthetic-data generation, distillation from a larger model's outputs). Without a counting standard or rulemaking authority to set one, the threshold is hard to apply and easy to argue about in court.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — fixed threshold, no adjustment.** 10^26 is hard-coded for a 10-year Act with no mechanism to revise it. As algorithmic efficiency improves, systems with the capabilities the bill targets could be trained below the line; conversely, routine systems could cross it over time.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A) vs. (B) — "model" vs. "system."** Prong (A) covers an "artificial intelligence model" and prong (B) an "artificial intelligence system," and neither term is defined anywhere. Challengers could argue a product built around a covered model (scaffolding, agents, tool use) is a "system" not reached by (A), or vice versa.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — "the Secretary" is never defined.** The bill references the Department of Commerce (Sec. 4) and the Secretary of State (Sec. 6) but never says which Secretary makes capability determinations. This is a basic drafting gap that would have to be fixed before the provision could operate.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — vague capability standard.** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no benchmarks, no procedure, no notice requirement, and no judicial-review provision. Because Sec. 5(b) attaches a 10-year felony to this definition, it is exposed to void-for-vagueness and nondelegation challenges, and the rule of lenity would push courts to read it narrowly.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — determination is retrospective.** A system only becomes "covered" under (B) once the Secretary determines it is; nothing addresses whether training or deployment before that determination was unlawful, or how fast an operator must stop after one. Combined with Sec. 3(b), any model whose training began before enactment is exempt no matter what the Secretary later finds.

8. **Sec. 2(2) — narrow "U.S. person."** The definition covers only citizens and corporations "organized under the laws of a State." It omits lawful permanent residents, LLCs, partnerships, trusts and other non-corporate entities, entities organized under federal or D.C. law, and — most importantly — foreign subsidiaries of U.S. companies. A U.S. firm could route training through a foreign-incorporated affiliate and fall outside Sec. 3(a)'s extraterritorial reach.

9. **Sec. 2(3) — "advanced AI chips" undefined.** The covered-facility definition turns entirely on a term the bill doesn't define, and there's no authority delegated to define it. Operators could reasonably dispute whether any given accelerator counts.

10. **Sec. 2(3) — 50,000-chip count is easy to engineer around.** The threshold is a per-"data center" chip count with no affiliated-site aggregation, so an operator could run several 49,000-chip facilities, or train across multiple sites. It also measures chips rather than compute, so a smaller number of more powerful next-generation chips never triggers registration.

## Prohibition (Sec. 3)

11. **Sec. 3(a) — jurisdictional gap for foreign operations.** The prohibition reaches persons in the U.S. and U.S. persons abroad, but not foreign entities operating abroad — including foreign affiliates controlled by U.S. companies (see item 8). A U.S. company could fund, license, or contract for training it cannot do itself.

12. **Sec. 3(a) — "train, develop, or deploy" undefined.** "Develop" could mean anything from research to productization; "deploy" could sweep in an ordinary business that uses a foreign-built covered system via API, or could be read not to cover that at all. The prohibition doesn't list "use," "possess," "import," "distribute," "provide compute for," or "finance," so cloud providers renting capacity to a foreign developer and investors funding one are arguably outside it.

13. **Sec. 3(a) — no government carve-out or inclusion.** "Person" is undefined; federal agencies and the military aren't expressly covered or exempted. That creates both a political vulnerability (national-security objections at markup) and a legal question about whether the government is bound at all.

14. **Sec. 3(b) — open-ended grandfather clause.** Any system "whose training began" before enactment is exempt indefinitely, with no cap on continued training after enactment and no limit on deployment. A developer could start a run the day before enactment and keep training it for years, or characterize each new model as a continuation of a pre-enactment checkpoint.

15. **Sec. 3(b) — pre-enactment rush and a 180-day dead zone.** Because the exemption keys on enactment but the Act isn't effective until 180 days later (Sec. 8), runs started during that window are neither grandfathered nor yet prohibited. The bill doesn't say what happens to a run in progress on the effective date.

16. **Sec. 3(c) — safety-evaluation exception swallows the rule.** The exception applies to any "activities" (including training and deployment) conducted "for the purpose of safety evaluation," with no definition, no authorization or certification process, no cap on compute, and no restriction on who may invoke it. A developer could plausibly argue that training a frontier system is necessary to evaluate its safety.

17. **Sec. 3 — nothing restricts covered facilities.** The prohibition is on persons and systems; the registry is on facilities. A registered 100,000-chip facility is under no obligation other than reporting, so Sec. 4 doesn't actually prevent the conduct Sec. 3 targets.

## Registry (Sec. 4)

18. **Sec. 4 — no penalty for noncompliance.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3. Failing to register, filing late, or filing a false quarterly report carries no sanction under this Act (apart from general false-statement law, which is untested here).

19. **Sec. 4(b) — self-certified, unverifiable reports.** Reports "describing the computing workloads" is vague, there is no reporting template, no audit or inspection authority, no access to records, and no requirement to report compute per training run — the one figure needed to enforce Sec. 2(1)(A).

20. **Sec. 4 — "operator" undefined; no deadline.** In a cloud facility, the "operator" could be the landlord, the cloud provider, or the tenant renting the chips. There's also no registration deadline and no confidentiality or public-disclosure provision for the reports.

21. **Sec. 4 — domestic facilities only.** The registry doesn't reach foreign facilities used by U.S. persons, so the enforcement data it generates won't cover the extraterritorial half of Sec. 3(a).

## Penalties (Sec. 5)

22. **Sec. 5(a) — penalty may be small relative to the conduct.** $1 million per day is a rounding error against training runs costing billions, and there's no scaling to company size, gain, or compute used. It also isn't clear how "each day" applies to a completed training run as opposed to ongoing deployment.

23. **Sec. 5(a) — penalty clock conflicts with the effective date.** Penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but under Sec. 8 nothing is unlawful until 180 days later. Read literally this is either surplusage or a retroactivity problem; either way it invites a challenge.

24. **Sec. 5 — no enforcer, no civil remedies.** The bill names no agency to assess civil penalties, no authority to seek injunctions or stop-work orders, no forfeiture, no private right of action, and no whistleblower provision. Monetary penalties after the fact are the only tool.

25. **Sec. 5(b) — "willfully" plus vague definitions.** The criminal provision requires willfulness, which is hard to prove when the underlying definitions (items 1–7) are ambiguous. Organizational fines "under title 18" default to modest statutory amounts unless the alternative-fine provision applies.

## International agreement (Sec. 6)

26. **Sec. 6 — hortatory only.** "Shall seek to negotiate" imposes no obligation of result, no reporting back to Congress, and no consequence for failure. It's also unclear whether the 18-month deadline is to begin or conclude negotiations.

27. **Sec. 6 — no reciprocity link.** Nothing in the Act conditions, suspends, or adjusts the domestic prohibition on whether other countries adopt equivalent rules, which will be the central practical objection from industry witnesses.

## Duration and effective date (Secs. 7–8)

28. **Sec. 7(a) and 7(b) — conflicting end dates.** Subsection (a) says the prohibition lasts until Congress acts by joint resolution; subsection (b) sunsets the whole Act at 10 years regardless. The provisions aren't reconciled, and (a) adds nothing legally since Congress can always repeal by statute (a joint resolution is a statute).

29. **Sec. 7(a) — the National Academies report is never commissioned.** The lifting mechanism depends on a NASEM report, but nothing requires, funds, or schedules one.

30. **Sec. 7(b) — no savings clause.** When the Act expires, so do the penalty provisions; without a savings clause, enforcement of violations committed during the Act's life becomes uncertain.

31. **Sec. 8 — 180-day delay creates a race.** The delay, combined with the grandfather clause, gives developers an explicit window to start runs that then sit in a legal gray zone (item 15).

## Cross-cutting gaps

32. **No rulemaking authority.** No agency is authorized to issue regulations defining compute counting, "advanced AI chips," "operator," "safety evaluation," or capability benchmarks, so the ambiguities above can't be cured administratively.

33. **No severability clause.** If a court strikes Sec. 2(1)(B) (the likeliest target), the whole Act could fall with it.

34. **Inference and distillation unaddressed.** The bill targets training compute, not capability achieved through inference-time scaling, agentic scaffolding, or distilling a covered model's outputs into a sub-threshold model.

35. **No funding.** No appropriation or authorization for the registry, enforcement, or the NASEM study.

36. **Constitutional exposure beyond vagueness.** Expect challenges framed as First Amendment (restrictions on publishing or training models as expressive activity), nondelegation (Sec. 2(1)(B)), and due process (criminal liability keyed to an undefined executive determination).
