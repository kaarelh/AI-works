RESPONSE:
Here are the issues I found in the October 6 text. I worked from the text alone, so I couldn't check the underlying bill or confirm whether the markup already addresses any of these. Items marked (conflict) are internal inconsistencies, not just gaps.

1. **§2(1)(A):** "Single training run" is undefined. A developer could split one large training effort into several runs, or continue training a model after an initial run, and argue that no single run crossed 10^26 operations.

2. **§2(1)(A):** The text doesn't say how operations are counted (precision, sparsity, reinforcement learning, synthetic data generation, or inference-time compute), and no agency is assigned to verify the figure. The threshold therefore depends largely on developer self-reporting.

3. **§2(1)(B):** "The Secretary" is never identified. §4 points to the Department of Commerce, but the bill doesn't say so, and it gives no authority, procedure, notice, or evidentiary standard for making determinations.

4. **§2(1)(B):** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no benchmark, measurement method, or timeline. A determination could be challenged as arbitrary and capricious, and it's unclear whether developers could know in advance that a system is covered.

5. **§2(1):** "Artificial intelligence model" (A) and "artificial intelligence system" (B) are used without definition. It's unclear whether a product built on a below-threshold model is covered under (B).

6. **§2(2):** "U.S. person" covers only citizens and state-organized corporations. It excludes LLCs, partnerships, D.C. and territorial entities, and permanent residents, and it doesn't reach foreign subsidiaries of U.S. companies, which weakens the "any U.S. person anywhere" clause in §3(a).

7. **§2(3):** "Advanced AI chips" is undefined. The bill has no chip list, performance threshold, or cross-reference to existing export-control classifications.

8. **§2(3):** "Data center" and "containing" don't address distributed training across sites or leased cloud capacity, so a developer could spread chips across facilities to stay under 50,000 per facility. The facility threshold also isn't linked to the model threshold: a 10^26 run could happen in a smaller facility, and a large facility isn't barred from sub-threshold work.

9. **§3(a):** "Train," "develop," and "deploy" are undefined. It's unclear whether fine-tuning, evaluation, inference-as-a-service, or open-weight release counts. "In the United States" is also undefined for cloud training, including remote access to compute located abroad.

10. **§3(a):** The clause reaches "any person in the United States, or any U.S. person anywhere." A non-U.S. company training abroad, including through a foreign subsidiary of a U.S. firm, may fall outside both prongs.

11. **§3(b):** The grandfather clause protects systems whose training "began" before enactment, but doesn't say whether that protection covers later deployment, continued training, fine-tuning, or new runs built on a grandfathered checkpoint. It also doesn't require grandfathered systems to be registered or disclosed.

12. **§3(b) and §8 (conflict):** The grandfather clause is keyed to enactment, but the prohibition takes effect 180 days later. The bill doesn't say whether training started in that gap is lawful, or whether it becomes a violation on the effective date.

13. **§3(c):** "Safety evaluation" is undefined. The bill doesn't require the evaluator to be qualified or independent, or require notice, a time limit, or a size limit, so a developer could label a full training run as an evaluation. The "purpose" test is also subjective, and it doesn't say how to treat mixed-purpose activities.

14. **§4(a):** "Operator" is undefined, and there's no registration deadline. Because §5 penalizes only violations of §3, failing to register carries no penalty.

15. **§4(b):** "Computing workloads" isn't defined, and the bill doesn't specify required content (such as model identity, parameter count, compute totals, or dates). "Certified by the operator" doesn't identify which officer must sign or what liability follows a false certification.

16. **§4 (general):** The bill doesn't make reports public, doesn't authorize Commerce to share them with enforcement agencies, and doesn't provide for verification. The registry therefore may not reveal §3 violations to anyone able to act on them.

17. **§5(a):** No enforcing agency is named, and the bill doesn't say whether civil penalties are assessed administratively or by a court. It also gives no investigative authority, such as subpoena power, which makes the penalty hard to collect.

18. **§5 (remedies):** The bill provides no injunctive or emergency relief, no authority to halt or seize compute, and no whistleblower protections. The main enforcement tool is therefore a monetary penalty that may not stop a run in progress.

19. **§5(a):** "Each day on which the violation occurs" is ambiguous for a training run that lasts months. The bill doesn't say whether that counts as one violation, one per day, or one per model, and it has no inflation adjustment.

20. **§5 and §8 (conflict):** §5(a) applies to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §8 delays the Act's effect by 180 days. Penalties could therefore attach to conduct during the window when §3 isn't yet in force.

21. **§5(b):** "Willfully" is undefined, and the bill doesn't address corporate criminal liability, responsible-officer liability, attempt, or conspiracy. It also doesn't state a limitations period, so the general federal default would likely apply; counsel should confirm that.

22. **§6:** "Shall seek to negotiate" creates no deliverable, such as a report to Congress, and no consequence if negotiations fail. "Equivalent prohibitions" is undefined, and the 18-month deadline runs past the 180-day effective date without addressing that gap.

23. **§7(a) and §7(b) (conflict):** §7(a) says the prohibition lasts until Congress acts, but §7(b) ends the entire Act after 10 years. The bill doesn't say which controls, so the prohibition could lapse even without a joint resolution.

24. **§7(a):** The bill doesn't set a deadline for the National Academies report, doesn't require that the report be produced, and doesn't define "developed safely." The prohibition could last indefinitely if no report is issued.

25. **§7(a):** The bill doesn't say whether a joint resolution goes to the President. If it does, a veto would require a two-thirds override, and the bill includes no expedited procedures for considering the resolution.

26. **§7(b) and §8:** The 10-year sunset runs from enactment, not the effective date, so the prohibition would be in force for about nine and a half years. The bill also has no savings clause, so it doesn't say whether penalties for violations before the sunset remain enforceable.

27. **Bill-wide (severability):** There's no severability clause. If a court strikes the delegated determination in §2(1)(B) or the safety-evaluation exemption in §3(c), it's unclear whether the rest of the Act survives.

28. **Bill-wide (definitions and authority):** The bill doesn't define "artificial intelligence," "train," "deploy," "operator," "safety evaluation," or "advanced AI chips," and it grants the Secretary no rulemaking authority. Terms like "substantially exceeding human performance" could also face a vagueness challenge in the criminal context of §5(b).

29. **Bill-wide (preemption, overlap, and funding):** The bill doesn't address preemption of state AI laws, its relationship to existing export controls, judicial review of determinations, or appropriations for enforcement.
