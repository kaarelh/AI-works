# Loopholes, Ambiguities, and Drafting Problems in S. 2817 (as amended Oct. 6, 2026)

The five most serious problems are #19 (sunset), #12 (environmental assessment exception), #11 (grandfather clause), #5 (U.S. person definition), and #24 (conflicts with existing licensing laws). Each of these could let commercial mining continue largely unchanged.

### Sec. 2(1): Definition of "commercial deep seabed mining"

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "by a single vessel."** The 10,000-ton threshold applies per vessel, so an operator can split production across several vessels, each under the cap, and recover unlimited total tonnage. The threshold should apply per person, including affiliates and all vessels they control.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A), 10,000 metric tons per year.** This allows meaningful pilot-scale and test mining, which is itself destructive and is how companies prove commercial systems. The bill also doesn't say whether tonnage is wet or dry weight or how the "year" is measured. Consider a zero threshold, or tie the threshold to a clearly defined research permit.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "recovery."** Only lifting material counts. Collector tests that disturb the seabed, leave sediment plumes, or discard nodules without bringing them aboard arguably fall outside the definition. The definition should cover disturbance of the seabed and subsoil, not just recovery.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(B), "the Secretary."** The bill never defines "Secretary," and it gives no standard for "comparable" risks, no rulemaking procedure, and no judicial review. Because criminal liability (Sec. 5(b)) can rest on this open-ended determination, it invites vagueness and nondelegation challenges that could put the whole definition at risk.

### Sec. 2(2): Definition of "U.S. person"

5. **Foreign subsidiaries and other entity types are excluded.** The definition covers only citizens and corporations organized under State law. That leaves out lawful permanent residents, LLCs, partnerships, trusts, and, most importantly, foreign-incorporated subsidiaries controlled by U.S. companies. A U.S. company could mine anywhere outside the U.S. through a foreign affiliate. The definition should cover any entity organized under U.S. law and any foreign entity owned or controlled by a U.S. person.

6. **"State" is undefined.** It is unclear whether entities organized in D.C., Puerto Rico, or other territories, or federally chartered entities, are covered.

### Sec. 2(3): Definition of "covered vessel"

7. **The 5,000-gross-ton threshold.** Smaller vessels, including ROV support ships, escape the registry entirely. The definition also doesn't say which tonnage measurement system applies.

8. **"Equipped for the recovery."** Operators can remove or swap equipment between voyages to move in and out of coverage. The definition should cover vessels capable of being used, or intended to be used, for recovery.

9. **"Covered vessel" doesn't match the prohibition.** The term is used only in Sec. 4, while Sec. 2(1)(A) refers to any "single vessel." Mining from a vessel under 5,000 tons is prohibited but never registered or reported, which makes enforcement much harder.

### Sec. 3: Prohibition

10. **Sec. 3(a), narrow scope.**
    - "United States" is undefined, so it is unclear whether a foreign company mining in the U.S. EEZ or on the continental shelf is "in the United States."
    - The prohibition doesn't reach U.S.-flagged vessels operated by foreign persons.
    - It covers only those who "engage in" mining. It doesn't reach financing, sponsoring, insuring, chartering vessels for, or processing, transporting, or importing deep-seabed minerals.
    - An import and processing ban is the most effective way to reach foreign operators.

11. **Sec. 3(b), grandfather clause.**
    - It exempts "mining" under any exploration license issued before enactment. It doesn't say who issued the license (NOAA, the ISA, or a foreign sponsoring state), and it covers renewals, extensions, transfers, and expanded scope.
    - It perversely authorizes *mining* under licenses that only authorized *exploration*.
    - The cutoff is the enactment date, so applicants will rush to get licenses issued during markup and floor consideration.
    - This exception should be struck, or limited to non-extractive exploration that ends on a fixed date.

12. **Sec. 3(c), environmental assessment exception.** "Environmental assessment" is undefined, and the exception has no tonnage cap, permit requirement, agency oversight, or limit on who qualifies. Any operator could relabel test mining or commercial mining as "assessment." The exception should be limited to permitted, non-commercial scientific research with strict scale limits, a ban on selling recovered material, and public disclosure.

### Sec. 4: Registry

13. **No penalties for registry violations.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3, so failing to register, failing to report, or filing false reports carries no penalty under the Act.

14. **No deadline, and a self-defeating reporting duty.** Sec. 4(a) sets no deadline for registration. Sec. 4(b) applies only to "registered" operators, so an operator that never registers has no reporting obligation.

15. **Weak, unverifiable reports.** Self-certified quarterly reports with unspecified content ("recovery activities") can't be checked. The section should require tonnage, location, depth, and dates, plus vessel tracking, independent observers, inspection authority, and public availability.

16. **Unclear jurisdiction and agency.** Sec. 4 doesn't say which vessels it reaches (U.S.-flagged, foreign vessels in U.S. waters, or U.S.-owned vessels anywhere). It also assigns duties to NOAA while Sec. 2 refers to an undefined "Secretary," and no agency is given rulemaking authority.

### Sec. 5: Penalties

17. **Sec. 5(a), no enforcement machinery.**
    - The bill doesn't say who assesses civil penalties, under what procedure, or in which court.
    - It provides no investigative, boarding, or inspection authority (for example, for the Coast Guard).
    - There is no injunctive relief, no forfeiture of vessels or recovered minerals, and no citizen-suit provision.

18. **Sec. 5(a), "on or after the date of enactment."** This conflicts with Sec. 8, which delays the effective date by 180 days. It invites litigation over whether conduct during that window is penalized and raises fair-notice objections.

### Sec. 6: International agreement

19. **"Shall seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months."** It is ambiguous whether the Secretary must begin or conclude negotiations within 18 months, and nothing is enforceable either way, since the section requires no reporting to Congress. "Equivalent to those in section 3" also exports all of Sec. 3's loopholes into the treaty goal.

### Sec. 7: Duration

20. **Sec. 7(a) vs. 7(b), internal contradiction.** Sec. 7(a) keeps the prohibition in place until Congress acts, but Sec. 7(b) terminates the entire Act after 10 years regardless. The sunset makes the prohibition temporary and effectively about 9.5 years long, given the 180-day delayed effective date. Sec. 7(b) should be struck.

21. **Sec. 7(a), no study and no standard.** Nothing directs the National Academies to produce the report, sets a timeline, or specifies its scope. "Serious harm" is undefined. A clearer, higher standard (for example, "without significant or irreversible harm to marine ecosystems") would strengthen the provision.

### Sec. 8: Effective date

22. **180-day delay.** This opens a six-month window to scale up operations, obtain licenses (see #11), and recover material before the prohibition applies. The delay should be eliminated or shortened, or the bill should freeze new licenses and permits as of introduction.

23. **Mismatched trigger dates.** The bill uses three different dates: enactment (Secs. 3(b) and 5(a)), 180 days after enactment (Sec. 8), and 10 years after enactment (Sec. 7(b)). These should be harmonized.

### Missing provisions

24. **No conforming amendments to existing licensing laws.** The bill doesn't repeal or amend the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (NOAA licenses and permits) or address mineral leasing under the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act. Agencies could keep issuing authorizations, and operators could argue those statutes control. The bill should expressly bar new licenses, permits, and leases and revoke pending applications.

25. **No severability clause.** A successful challenge to one provision, such as Sec. 2(1)(B) under #4, could take down more of the Act than necessary.

26. **No general definitions or regulatory authority.** "Person," "United States," "Secretary," "seabed," and "mineral resources" are all undefined, and no agency has general authority to issue regulations implementing the Act.

27. **No savings clause.** The bill should state that it doesn't preempt stricter state laws (several states already ban seabed mining in their waters) or limit other federal environmental laws.

I'd recommend having counsel double-check items #18 and #24 against current statutory and regulatory text. Those depend on how the bill interacts with existing law, which I can't verify from here.
