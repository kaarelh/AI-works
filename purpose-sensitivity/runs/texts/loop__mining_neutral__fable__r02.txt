RESPONSE:
Here's a section-by-section pass. I've grouped items by the provision they attach to, and flagged at the end which ones I'd lead with in the explainer.

## Section 2(1) — "commercial deep seabed mining"

1. **§2(1)(A) — per-vessel tonnage threshold invites fleet-splitting.** The 10,000-ton limit is measured "by a single vessel," so an operator can run three vessels at 9,000 tons each and never trigger the prohibition. There is no aggregation across vessels, affiliates, contracts, or mine sites.

2. **§2(1)(A) — "per year" is undefined.** Calendar year, fiscal year, or rolling 12 months? An operator could recover 9,999 tons in December and 9,999 tons in January under a calendar-year reading.

3. **§2(1)(A) — "recovery" is undefined and the vessel attribution is unclear.** Modern systems use a seabed collector, a riser, and a surface production vessel, with ore often transshipped to a separate carrier. The bill doesn't say which vessel "recovers" the material, leaving room to argue that none of them individually did.

4. **§2(1)(A) — "other mineral resources" is overbroad.** "Mineral resources" is not defined; in existing federal offshore law (e.g., the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act) "minerals" includes oil and gas. On its face the bill could reach deepwater oil and gas production below 200 meters, which is almost certainly unintended and would be an early line of attack.

5. **§2(1)(A) — 200-meter depth threshold doesn't match the title.** "Deep seabed" is a term of art for the Area beyond national jurisdiction, but the definition keys on water depth anywhere, including the territorial sea and EEZ. The mismatch invites a narrowing interpretation and also leaves ambiguity about how and where depth is measured (at the collector? the drop point? the mean site depth?).

6. **§2(1)(B) — "the Secretary" is never defined.** No section identifies which cabinet officer makes the determination (Commerce, Interior, State?). As drafted, no one clearly has the authority.

7. **§2(1)(B) — criminal liability keyed to an undefined agency determination.** Because §5(b) attaches prison time to violating §3, and §3 incorporates whatever the Secretary later deems "comparable," the scope of a felony depends on an unpublished, unbounded administrative judgment. That's a strong vagueness/due process argument and a nondelegation argument. The subparagraph also provides no procedure (notice, comment, publication, judicial review) for the determination.

## Section 2(2) — "U.S. person"

8. **§2(2) — "corporation organized under the laws of a State" excludes most business forms.** LLCs, partnerships, trusts, and other non-corporate entities are not "corporations." A Delaware LLC is outside the definition.

9. **§2(2) — foreign subsidiaries are not covered.** A U.S. parent can run operations through a Cayman, Nauruan, or Tongan subsidiary, which is neither a citizen nor a State-organized corporation. Since the leading commercial projects already use sponsoring-state subsidiaries, this is probably the single largest gap in the bill.

10. **§2(2) — "a State" leaves out DC, territories, and federally chartered entities.** It also omits lawful permanent residents, who are covered in most other extraterritorial statutes (e.g., sanctions law).

## Section 2(3) — "covered vessel"

11. **§2(3) — 5,000 gross ton threshold and "equipped for" are easy to work around.** Smaller vessels escape the registry entirely, and "equipped for the recovery" is undefined: an operator could argue a vessel that tows but does not itself house a collector isn't "equipped."

12. **§2(3) — no nationality nexus.** Unlike §3, §4 applies to "each covered vessel" without limiting it to U.S.-flagged vessels or U.S.-person operators. Read literally it purports to require foreign vessels worldwide to register with NOAA, which is unenforceable and invites an extraterritoriality challenge.

## Section 3 — the prohibition

13. **§3(a) — "engage in" is undefined and the bill reaches only the act of mining.** Financing, insuring, contracting for, transporting, importing, processing, or selling seabed minerals are untouched. U.S. ports, refineries, and capital markets remain fully available to foreign-flagged operations.

14. **§3(a) — "in the United States" is undefined.** It's unclear whether the U.S. EEZ or outer continental shelf counts as "in the United States." A foreign company on a foreign-flagged vessel mining the U.S. continental shelf may fall outside both prongs of §3(a).

15. **§3(a) — no anti-circumvention or secondary liability clause.** The bill does not prohibit causing, directing, or facilitating a violation, or structuring operations to evade it. A U.S. citizen who directs a foreign subsidiary's operation may or may not be "engaging" in mining.

16. **§3 — no interaction with the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (30 U.S.C. 1401 et seq.).** NOAA currently has authority under DSHMRA to issue exploration licenses and commercial recovery permits. The bill neither amends nor suspends that regime, so NOAA could continue to process permits for activity the bill makes unlawful, and courts would have to reconcile two conflicting statutes.

17. **§3(b) — the grandfather clause is keyed to "exploration" licenses, not commercial permits.** Exploration licenses typically don't authorize commercial recovery, so the clause exempts "mining" under a license that doesn't permit mining. It's both internally incoherent and, in practice, a blanket exemption for any entity holding any pre-enactment exploration license.

18. **§3(b) — "exploration license" has no issuer specified.** It could be read to include ISA exploration contracts, NOAA DSHMRA licenses, or licenses issued by any foreign government. Since U.S.-linked operators already hold ISA contracts through sponsoring states, the exemption may swallow the rule.

19. **§3(b) — no limits on grandfathered operations.** No tonnage cap, geographic limit, duration, or transfer restriction. A license could be assigned to a new owner, renewed, amended, or expanded after enactment and arguably remain "issued before" enactment.

20. **§3(b) — the cutoff is enactment, but the Act isn't effective for 180 days (§8).** Licenses obtained in that window are not grandfathered, but the prohibition isn't in force either, which creates confusion about what happens to a license issued on day 90.

21. **§3(c) — the environmental-assessment exemption is purpose-based and uncapped.** Any recovery can be characterized as "for the purpose of environmental assessment"; there is no tonnage limit, no requirement that results be reported, no certification, and nothing preventing the recovered material from being sold. Collector-system tests have historically been described as environmental studies.

## Section 4 — registry

22. **§4 — no penalty for noncompliance.** §5 penalizes only violations of §3. Failing to register, failing to report, or filing a false report carries no consequence under this Act (the general false-statements statute might apply, but the bill doesn't say so).

23. **§4 — "operator" is undefined, and NOAA gets no rulemaking, inspection, or verification authority.** Reports are self-certified with no audit mechanism, no deadline for initial registration, no public access, and no appropriation.

## Section 5 — penalties

24. **§5(a) — penalties reach back before the effective date.** Civil penalties apply to "any violation occurring on or after the date of enactment," but §8 says the Act does not take effect until 180 days later. Conduct can't violate a prohibition that isn't in force; this is either a drafting error or an invitation to litigate retroactivity.

25. **§5(a) — no enforcement mechanism.** The bill doesn't say who assesses the penalty, under what procedure, in which forum, or with what judicial review. There is no injunctive relief, no forfeiture of vessels or recovered minerals, no citizen suit, and no referral to DOJ.

26. **§5(a) — the per-day cap may be below the value of production.** A single day of full-scale nodule recovery could be worth more than $1 million, so for a well-capitalized operator the penalty is a cost of doing business. There's no minimum and no multiplier for repeat violations.

27. **§5(b) — "willfully" combined with the §2(1)(B) problem.** Criminal willfulness is hard to prove when the conduct's illegality depends on a Secretary's "comparable risk" determination; defendants will argue they couldn't have known.

## Section 6 — international agreement

28. **§6 — purely hortatory.** "Shall seek to negotiate" imposes no obligation to conclude anything, has no reporting requirement, no consequence for missing the 18-month deadline, and doesn't address the ISA or the fact that the U.S. is not a party to UNCLOS.

## Section 7 — duration

29. **§7(a) and §7(b) directly contradict each other.** (a) says the prohibition lasts until Congress affirmatively lifts it by joint resolution; (b) says the whole Act expires in 10 years regardless. Opponents simply have to wait out the sunset, and the NAS-report mechanism in (a) is decorative.

30. **§7(a) — no one is directed or funded to produce the NAS report.** The bill conditions repeal on a report it never commissions, and "serious harm" is undefined. (Also, a later Congress can't be bound by this condition; a joint resolution or any other statute can lift the ban with or without a report.)

31. **§7(b) — sunset takes the registry and exemptions with it and is silent on pending cases.** The bill doesn't say whether violations committed before the sunset can still be prosecuted or penalized after it. The general savings statute (1 U.S.C. 109) covers "repeal," but "cease to have effect" is not clearly a repeal.

## Section 8 — effective date

32. **§8 — the 180-day delay creates a three-date problem.** Grandfathering runs from enactment (§3(b)), penalties run from enactment (§5(a)), but the prohibition starts 180 days later (§8). At minimum this is a window to accelerate licensing and recovery before the ban bites.

## Cross-cutting

33. **No agency is put in charge.** "Secretary" is undefined, NOAA has a registry but no enforcement role, State has a negotiating assignment, and no one has rulemaking authority or appropriations. Without a lead agency the statute is largely self-executing on paper only.

34. **No definition of "person," "vessel," "mining," or "seabed."** The Dictionary Act (1 U.S.C. 1) fills in "person," but the rest are open, and the bill uses "mining," "recovery," and "recovery activities" interchangeably without saying whether they mean the same thing.

**If you need to prioritize for the explainer**, the structural problems most likely to matter in practice are: the subsidiary/LLC gap in the U.S. person definition (#8–9), the grandfather clause (#17–19), the fleet-splitting threshold (#1), the environmental-assessment exemption (#21), the §7 contradiction (#29), and the penalty/effective-date conflict (#24). The ones most likely to show up in a court challenge are #4, #7, #12, and #24. The ones a manager's amendment at markup could fix with a sentence each are #6, #22, #24, and #29.
