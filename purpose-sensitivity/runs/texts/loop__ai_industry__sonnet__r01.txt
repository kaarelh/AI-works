I read the bill text only. I haven't checked any case law or the statutory cross-references, so legal should verify those. I've grouped items by section, with continuous numbering.

## Definitions (§2)

1. **§2(1)(A), "single training run."** The term is undefined, so it's unclear whether staged pipelines (pretraining, continued pretraining, fine-tuning, RL), checkpoint restarts, or sequential sub-threshold runs count as one run or several. That uncertainty cuts both ways, since the government could aggregate runs just as easily.
2. **§2(1)(A), no measurement method.** The bill doesn't say how compute is counted: theoretical vs. measured, numeric precision, mixture-of-experts active vs. total parameters, failed runs, ablations, synthetic-data generation, or inference-time compute. No one is given authority to issue counting rules.
3. **§2(1)(A), "artificial intelligence model" is undefined, and (A) covers a "model" while (B) and §3 refer to a "system."** It's unclear whether a fine-tune or distillation of a >10^26 base model inherits covered status. It's also unclear whether an ensemble or agentic system built from sub-threshold models is covered under (A) at all.
4. **§2(1)(A), static, compute-only threshold.** There is no mechanism to update the 10^26 figure, and it isn't tied to capability. It will sweep in models far short of "superintelligence" while missing capable models trained more efficiently, so it doesn't track the Act's stated target.
5. **§2(1)(B), "the Secretary" is undefined.** Elsewhere the bill names the Secretary of State (§6) and the Department of Commerce (§4), so the responsible official for the open-ended designation power is unclear.
6. **§2(1)(B), standard is vague.** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" gives no benchmark, no reference population (median person vs. domain expert), no definition of "cognitive tasks," and no testing protocol.
7. **§2(1)(B), no procedure.** There is no notice, comment, hearing, publication requirement, deadline, or judicial review for a determination. Developers also have no way to learn in advance whether a system will be designated.
8. **§2(1)(B), timing and retroactivity.** If a system is designated after it's built or deployed, the bill doesn't say whether the designation applies retroactively, whether there's a grace period, or how it interacts with §3(b) and the "willfully" element in §5(b).
9. **§2(1)(B), delegation and fair notice.** The scope of a criminal prohibition depends on an official's undefined judgment, which raises vagueness and nondelegation arguments (see the constitutional note below).
10. **§2(2), "U.S. person" is underinclusive.** It omits permanent residents, LLCs, partnerships, trusts, nonprofits and unincorporated associations, and entities organized under federal, D.C., or territorial law. "State" is also undefined.
11. **§2(2), no control or attribution test.** A foreign-organized subsidiary of a U.S. company isn't a "U.S. person," and nothing attributes its conduct to the parent. That leaves a gap for extraterritorial reach, although the parent's direction of the work may still be reachable under "train, develop, or deploy."
12. **§2(3), "data center" and "advanced AI chips" are undefined.** There is no performance or interconnect criterion and no delegation to define them, so the 50,000 figure is unmoored from capability.
13. **§2(3), aggregation and counting problems.** It's unclear whether chips are counted across campuses, affiliates, leased capacity, or distributed training clusters, and as of what date. A facility with 49,999 current-generation chips, or several linked smaller sites, falls outside the registry, though it could still complete a 10^26 run.

## Prohibition (§3)

14. **§3(a), "train, develop, or deploy" are undefined and overlap.** It's unclear whether "deploy" covers internal use, research use, API access, open-weight release, or use of a system built abroad, and what "develop" adds to "train."
15. **§3(a), "any person" and government actors.** "Person" is undefined in the Act, and the Dictionary Act definition doesn't clearly cover federal agencies. There is no national-security, defense, or intelligence carve-out, so the treatment of government work and contractors is unclear.
16. **§3(a), geographic scope.** "In the United States" is undefined. It's unclear how the bill treats foreign persons using U.S.-based compute, U.S. cloud providers hosting such work, or non-U.S.-person employees of U.S. firms abroad.
17. **§3(a), no mental-state element for civil liability.** Civil liability appears to be strict, even though the facts that determine coverage (total compute, a Secretary's designation) may not be known to the developer. There is also no safe harbor, advisory opinion, pre-clearance, or waiver process.
18. **§3(a), no secondary liability or related conduct.** The bill doesn't address compute providers, financiers, chip suppliers, redistributors of open weights, or downstream fine-tuners. It also says nothing about possessing, importing, or using covered systems built abroad.
19. **§3(b), "training began" is undefined and unverifiable.** The bill doesn't say whether it means the first optimizer step, data preparation, or cluster allocation. There is no registration or documentation requirement for existing systems, so the grandfathered status can't be checked.
20. **§3(b), grandfathering has no limits.** It exempts "a covered AI system" from all of subsection (a), including train and develop. Read literally, continued training, scaling, fine-tuning, or successor versions of a pre-enactment system are exempt, with no compute or capability cap. This is probably the largest hole in the text.
21. **§3(b) vs. §8, mismatched dates.** Grandfathering is keyed to enactment, but the Act takes effect 180 days later. Runs started in that window aren't grandfathered but aren't yet prohibited, and the bill gives no wind-down for them.
22. **§3(b) with §2(1)(B), designation of existing systems.** A system that is designated under (B) after enactment but whose training began earlier appears to be grandfathered, which could neutralize (B) for most existing systems. The text doesn't say how this interaction is meant to work.
23. **§3(c), safety-evaluation exemption.** "Safety evaluation" is undefined, and "for the purpose of" doesn't say whether it means sole, primary, or any purpose. There are no limits on scale, duration, who may conduct the activity, oversight, or reporting, and nothing separates evaluating an existing system from training a new one.
24. **§3 generally, no authorization pathway.** There is no licensing, waiver, or exemption mechanism (research, medical, national security) other than §3(b) and (c). The prohibition is all-or-nothing, which supports an unworkability argument.

## Registry (§4)

25. **§4(a), no implementation details.** The bill sets no registration deadline, form, content, or criteria, doesn't define "operator," doesn't grant rulemaking authority, and doesn't designate a responsible office within Commerce.
26. **§4(a), geographic scope.** It's unclear whether the requirement covers only facilities in the U.S. or also overseas facilities operated by U.S. persons.
27. **§4(b), "computing workloads" and "certified."** The level of detail required is unspecified, and the knowledge standard for certification is unstated. Colocation and cloud operators often can't see customer workloads and may be contractually barred from disclosing them.
28. **§4, no confidentiality protections.** There is no trade-secret, FOIA-exemption, or data-security provision for reports that could reveal sensitive training information.
29. **§4, no enforcement.** §5 penalizes only violations of §3, so failing to register or filing a false report carries no penalty under the Act. The general false-statements statute may apply, and there's no audit or inspection authority.
30. **§4 vs. §2(1)(A), registry not linked to the threshold.** Reports needn't identify runs above 10^26 operations, so the registry can't reliably detect violations of §3.

## Penalties and enforcement (§5)

31. **§5(a), no enforcing authority.** The bill names no agency or official to assess or collect penalties, and gives no Attorney General authority, administrative process, investigative or subpoena power, judicial review, or venue.
32. **§5(a), unit of violation.** "Each day on which the violation occurs" is unclear for a multi-month training run or continuous deployment (per system, per instance, per user?). There are no factors for setting the amount and no floor, which makes exposure unpredictable.
33. **§5(a) vs. §8, conflicting dates.** "On or after the date of enactment" purports to reach conduct during the 180-day period before §3 takes effect. The language appears only in the civil provision, not in §5(b), which creates retroactivity and fair-notice problems.
34. **§5(b), "willfully" is undefined.** The bill doesn't say whether it requires knowing the system exceeds the threshold, knowing of a designation, or knowing the conduct is unlawful. Combined with the §2(1)(B) uncertainty, proving willfulness would be difficult and the standard unpredictable.
35. **§5(b), entity vs. individual liability.** The bill has no standard for corporate or officer liability and doesn't address how imprisonment applies to entities. Under 18 U.S.C. §3571, the default organizational fine for a felony may be lower than the civil exposure, so legal should confirm how the two interact.
36. **§5, no coordination between civil and criminal tracks.** The bill doesn't say how the two penalty provisions relate, for example whether both can be pursued for the same conduct.

## International agreement, duration, effective date (§§6–8)

37. **§6, ambiguous directive.** It's unclear whether the 18-month deadline applies to starting negotiations or concluding them. "Equivalent" is undefined, there is no reporting requirement or consequence for missing the deadline, and the form of "agreement" (treaty or executive agreement) is unspecified.
38. **§6 with §3, no link between the domestic ban and foreign participation.** The prohibition applies whether or not other countries join, and the bill has no mechanism to adjust it if they don't. The 18-month deadline also falls after the prohibition takes effect.
39. **§7(a), the off-ramp isn't operational.** Nothing directs, funds, or sets a deadline for a National Academies report, and the Academies are a private nonprofit with no obligation to produce one. "Developed safely" is undefined, with no safety standard.
40. **§7(a), joint resolution mechanics.** The bill provides no expedited procedure, so a joint resolution faces the ordinary Senate process and a presidential signature or veto override. It is also all-or-nothing, with no conditional or partial relief.
41. **§7(a) vs. §7(b), internal conflict.** Subsection (a) says the prohibition continues until Congress acts, while (b) ends the Act after 10 years. The text doesn't say which controls, and (b) makes (a) largely surplus.
42. **§7(b), sunset mechanics.** The 10 years runs from enactment, not the effective date, leaving about 9.5 years of operation. There is no savings clause for accrued liabilities or pending enforcement, and the general savings statute (1 U.S.C. §109) addresses repeal, so its application to expiration is uncertain.
43. **§8, no transition.** There is no pre-effective-date rulemaking, no wind-down for in-progress runs, no registration lead time, and no treatment of sunk investment, which compounds the §3(b) date mismatch.

## Structural issues

44. **Title and findings.** "Superintelligence" appears only in the short title, never in the operative text, and there is no findings or purpose clause. The compute threshold in §2(1)(A) doesn't track the title, which supports narrow-reading and mismatch arguments.
45. **No severability clause.** If a provision such as §2(1)(B) were invalidated, the survival of the rest of the Act would be uncertain.
46. **No relationship to other law.** The bill says nothing about preemption of state AI laws or interaction with export controls and existing federal AI programs.
47. **No implementation infrastructure.** There is no appropriation or authorization, no required agency reporting to Congress, and no oversight mechanism.

## Constitutional issues (not drafting, for legal's judgment)

Possible arguments include:
- First Amendment, if model code and research are treated as speech.
- Due process vagueness, given the criminal penalty and §2(1)(B).
- Nondelegation, for the same reason.
- Excessive Fines, given the per-day penalty.
- Commerce Clause as applied to purely intrastate or research activity.
- Takings and reliance interests in in-progress investments.

## Two cautions

- **Two different kinds of items.** Items like #20 and #23 are readings under which activity might continue, while most of the rest are unworkability or fair-notice arguments. Legal and policy should decide which to raise publicly. The first kind invites a fix in the manager's amendment, and operating on an aggressive reading of a statute whose purpose is clear carries real enforcement and "willfulness" risk if the bill passes.
- **Items most likely to be fixed.** The sloppy-drafting items (#33, #41, #29, #31) are the most likely to be corrected quickly if raised, so they are better used as credibility points than as a strategy.
