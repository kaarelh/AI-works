RESPONSE:
Here are the issues I found in the October 6 text, grouped by section. I worked only from the text you pasted, so counsel should check it against the official manager's amendment and the statutes referenced.

**Highest-priority items** (these bear most directly on whether operations can continue): §3(c), §3(b), §2(1)(A) (single-vessel threshold), §2(2) (entity coverage), §3(a) (foreign operations), and §7(b) (sunset).

### Definitions (§2)

1. **§2(1)(A):** The 10,000-ton threshold applies "by a single vessel," so an operation could be split across several vessels that each stay under the threshold. The bill doesn't say whether vessels under common ownership, contract, or operational control are aggregated.

2. **§2(1)(A):** "Per year" is not tied to a measurement. The bill doesn't specify wet or dry weight, nodules recovered versus nodules retained, or whether a multi-year average applies.

3. **§2(1)(A):** The covered activity is "recovery," and processing, beneficiation, transport, and sale are not mentioned. Operators could argue that onshore or downstream steps fall outside the prohibition.

4. **§2(1)(A):** "Other mineral resources," combined with the 200-meter depth floor and the single-vessel tonnage test, could sweep in shallow-water aggregate or sand dredging far from deep-sea nodule work. This supports an overbreadth argument that the definition is unworkable as written.

5. **§2(1)(A):** "Deep seabed" is never defined, and the bill doesn't say whether it covers the U.S. EEZ, the continental shelf, or the Area beyond national jurisdiction. The geographic reach of the prohibition is therefore unclear.

6. **§2(1)(B):** "The Secretary" is undefined. It's unclear whether this means Commerce, Interior, or another official, while §6 specifically names the Secretary of State, which suggests the drafters knew how to be specific.

7. **§2(1)(B):** The Secretary can make any activity "commercial deep seabed mining" based on "comparable" risk, with no standard, criteria, notice, hearing, or judicial review. This raises due-process and delegation concerns, and it makes compliance impossible to plan.

8. **§2(2):** "U.S. person" covers only citizens and corporations organized under state law. U.S.-organized LLCs, partnerships, and trusts are not "U.S. persons," so the "anywhere" reach of §3(a) doesn't extend to them.

9. **§2(2):** Foreign-incorporated subsidiaries and affiliates of U.S. companies are not "U.S. persons," so the extraterritorial prohibition doesn't reach an overseas entity that conducts the operations.

10. **§2(2):** "State" is not defined. It's unclear whether it includes D.C. and the territories.

11. **§2(3):** "Equipped for the recovery of seabed mineral resources" is undefined. Modular or retrofitted vessels could fall in or out of the definition depending on how "equipped" is read.

12. **§2(3):** The 5,000-GT covered-vessel threshold isn't linked to the 10,000-ton activity threshold. A commercial operation could use vessels under 5,000 GT and fall outside the §4 registry and reporting requirements.

### Prohibition (§3)

13. **§3(a):** "Engage in" is undefined. The bill doesn't say whether financing, equipment supply, offtake contracts, or technical services count as engaging in commercial mining.

14. **§3(a):** For non-U.S. persons, the prohibition applies only to activity "in the United States." Foreign-flagged vessels run by non-U.S. persons outside U.S. waters appear to be outside the prohibition entirely.

15. **§3(a):** "Person" is not defined in the bill, so the default in 1 U.S.C. §1 would likely apply, and that default includes partnerships. This creates an inconsistency with the narrower "U.S. person" definition in §2(2), which supports the LLC and partnership argument in item 8.

16. **§3(b):** "Exploration license" is undefined and not tied to any statutory scheme. It's unclear whether the term refers to a DSHMRA license, an ISA contract, or a foreign license. Counsel should confirm which regime is meant.

17. **§3(b):** The exemption covers "mining conducted under" a pre-enactment license, with no volume cap, no environmental conditions, no §4 compliance requirement, and no end date. Under this language, commercial recovery could continue indefinitely under any such license.

18. **§3(b):** The exemption is keyed to licenses issued before enactment, but the prohibition doesn't take effect until 180 days later (§8). The bill doesn't address licenses issued in that gap, and no one can be sure whether they are exempt.

19. **§3(c):** "Environmental assessment" is undefined, and the exemption turns on purpose rather than on the activity. Commercial-scale recovery could be labeled as environmental assessment, with no cap on quantity, no sales restriction, and no agency approval. This is the largest path to continued operation in the bill.

20. **§3 (structure):** The bill doesn't amend or repeal DSHMRA (1980) and has no "notwithstanding" clause. Existing DSHMRA permits may remain in force and create a conflict over which regime controls. A later, specific prohibition would likely prevail, but the conflict is still a litigation hook.

### Registry (§4)

21. **§4:** "Operator" is undefined. With lessees, contractors, and vessel owners in the picture, it's unclear who must register and report.

22. **§4:** The bill sets no registration deadline and no content requirements for the quarterly reports beyond "describing the recovery activities." It also gives NOAA no express rulemaking authority to set forms or procedures.

23. **§4:** The bill includes no confidentiality or trade-secret protection for the reports and doesn't say whether they will be public. This is a legitimate commercial-sensitivity objection and an implementation problem.

24. **§4 / §5:** Penalties under §5 apply only to violations of §3. Failing to register or file a report carries no penalty, so §4 is effectively unenforceable.

### Penalties (§5)

25. **§5(a):** The civil penalty is assessed "for each day on which the violation occurs," but "violation" and continuing violations are undefined. It's unclear whether one ongoing operation counts as one violation per day or as multiple violations.

26. **§5(a) vs. §8:** Penalties under §5(a) apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §8 makes the whole Act effective 180 days later. Conduct in that window may be penalized even though the prohibition isn't yet in force.

27. **§5:** The bill names no enforcing agency and provides no administrative assessment procedure, no express injunctive authority, and no vessel seizure or forfeiture. Enforcement would depend on litigation with no defined process, which makes it harder to stop ongoing operations quickly.

28. **§5(b):** "Willfully" is undefined. Given the 180-day lead time, an operator could argue that the government must prove actual knowledge of the prohibition.

### International agreement (§6)

29. **§6:** "Shall seek to negotiate" is a non-binding direction. The bill attaches no consequence if negotiations fail, and the prohibition doesn't depend on any agreement. "Equivalent" is also undefined.

30. **§6 (structure):** The bill doesn't address its relationship to the International Seabed Authority or to UNCLOS obligations. This is a significant gap for any activity in the Area or any dispute involving ISA-sponsored contractors.

### Duration (§7)

31. **§7(a):** "Serious harm" is undefined. The joint-resolution path also depends on a NASEM report with no deadline, and the bill doesn't say whether the resolution is subject to presentment and veto.

32. **§7(a) vs. §7(b):** §7(a) says the prohibition lasts until Congress acts, but §7(b) sunsets the entire Act 10 years after enactment. The sunset overrides the open-ended duration, so the prohibition would be in force for about 9.5 years.

33. **§7(b):** The bill has no savings clause. On sunset, pending enforcement actions, accrued penalties, and registry obligations may be extinguished.

### General

34. **Severability:** The bill has no severability clause. If a court strikes a provision such as §2(1)(B) as vague, the bill's remaining structure could be litigated or invalidated.

35. **Rulemaking:** No agency is given general rulemaking authority. Implementing §4 and the §2(1)(B) determinations would therefore rest on inherent agency authority, which is a weak foundation.

**Notes for legal review:**

- Some of these points cut both ways. Vagueness in §2(1)(B) and §3(c) helps us argue the bill is unworkable, but it also gives a court or agency room to read the bill broadly against us. Drafters may respond by tightening the language.
- Several items, especially §§3(b), 3(c), and 7(b), are the most likely to be fixed in markup, since sponsors will see them as the obvious gaps. We should expect that and plan for it.
- The DSHMRA references (items 16 and 20) need verification against the current statute and any NOAA regulations before anyone relies on them.
- This is an analysis of drafting issues, not a legal opinion. Counsel should confirm the points above before they are used in advocacy materials.
