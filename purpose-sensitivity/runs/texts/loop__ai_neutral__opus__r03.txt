# S. 2817 (as amended Oct. 6, 2026): Loopholes, Ambiguities, and Drafting Problems

## Sec. 2: Definitions

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "single training run."** Tying the threshold to one run lets a developer split training into several runs, each under 10^26 operations, or reach high capability by fine-tuning, reinforcement-learning post-training, or distillation from a grandfathered model. The bill doesn't say whether compute is added up across runs or stages.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A), no counting method or update mechanism.** The bill doesn't say how operations are counted (e.g., precision, discarded runs, synthetic-data generation), and the 10^26 figure is fixed in statute. As training gets more efficient, capable systems could fall below the line and Commerce has no authority to adjust it.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A) vs. (B), "model" vs. "system."** Paragraph (A) covers a "model" and (B) covers a "system." A company could argue that a system built from several sub-threshold models, or a covered model wrapped in tools, falls outside (A). "Artificial intelligence" itself is never defined.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(B), which "Secretary"?** The bill never defines "Secretary." The only Secretary named anywhere in the bill is the Secretary of State (Sec. 6), but the registry sits at Commerce (Sec. 4). This leaves it unclear who makes capability determinations, which invites challenges to any determination.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(B), vague standard and no process.** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no benchmarks, procedure, notice, or appeal. Because criminal penalties attach (Sec. 5(b)), this paragraph is especially open to a void-for-vagueness challenge, and possibly a nondelegation argument.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B), determination comes only after the fact.** A capability determination can only be made about a system that already exists. So (B) cannot stop the training that produces the system, and applying penalties for conduct before the determination raises fair-notice problems.

7. **Sec. 2(2), narrow "U.S. person."** The definition covers only citizens and *corporations* organized under *State* law. It leaves out lawful permanent residents, LLCs, partnerships, and other non-corporate entities. It also arguably leaves out entities organized under D.C. or territorial law, since those are not "States" unless the bill says so.

8. **Sec. 2(2), foreign subsidiary loophole.** A foreign-incorporated subsidiary of a U.S. company is not a "U.S. person." It could train a covered system abroad as long as no covered conduct happens in the United States. The bill also has no provisions on parent-company liability, aiding and abetting, or providing compute.

9. **Sec. 2(3), undefined terms in "covered facility."** "Advanced AI chips" and "data center" are not defined, and there is no rule for combining commonly owned or networked sites. Operators could stay at or below 50,000 chips per site, or train across several smaller facilities, and never have to register. A chip count is also only a loose proxy for compute.

## Sec. 3: Prohibition

10. **Sec. 3(a), undefined conduct.** "Train," "develop," and "deploy" are not defined. It is unclear whether hosting, licensing, giving API access, releasing open weights, or merely using a covered model counts. That ambiguity weakens enforcement and fair notice.

11. **Sec. 3(a), foreign-trained models.** The bill doesn't say whether a person in the U.S. "deploys" a covered system by offering access to, or using, a model trained abroad by a foreign entity. That leaves open a route where training moves offshore and the model is then served into the U.S.

12. **Sec. 3(b), grandfather clause has no limits.** Any system whose training "began" before enactment is fully exempt, with no limit on later training, scaling, or deployment. The bill doesn't define when training "begins," and it doesn't say whether a further-trained version of a grandfathered model is still the same "system." This creates an incentive to start large runs before enactment.

13. **Sec. 3(c), open-ended safety-evaluation exemption.** "Safety evaluation" is undefined, and the exemption has no approval, oversight, scope, or reporting requirement. It turns on the actor's stated "purpose," so a developer could describe training or deployment as safety evaluation. The bill also doesn't address activities with mixed purposes.

## Sec. 4: Registry

14. **Sec. 4, no penalty for not registering.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3. Failing to register, failing to report, or reporting falsely carries no penalty under the Act (18 U.S.C. § 1001 might reach false statements). The bill also sets no registration deadline.

15. **Sec. 4(b), thin reporting requirements.** "Describing the computing workloads" sets no level of detail, and Commerce gets no authority to audit, inspect, or ask follow-up questions. "Operator" is undefined, which matters for colocation and cloud arrangements. Reports reach facility operators but not the customers who actually run training.

16. **Sec. 4, no confidentiality provisions.** The bill doesn't address trade-secret protection or FOIA exemptions for the reports. This could draw industry objections and litigation, and it may push operators to report as little as possible.

## Sec. 5: Penalties

17. **Sec. 5(a) vs. Sec. 8, conflicting dates.** Civil penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the Act takes effect 180 days after enactment. It is unclear whether conduct during that 180-day window can be penalized, and this conflict is likely to be litigated.

18. **Sec. 5(a), no enforcing agency or procedure.** The bill names no agency or official to assess or sue for civil penalties, sets no procedure, and grants no injunctive authority or rulemaking power. "Each day on which the violation occurs" is also unclear for training versus continuing deployment.

19. **Sec. 5, penalty size.** A cap of $1M per day may be small compared with frontier-lab budgets. "Fined under title 18" generally means a maximum of $250,000 per offense for individuals and $500,000 for organizations under 18 U.S.C. § 3571, unless the alternative gain/loss fine applies. Critics could argue this makes violation a cost of doing business.

## Sec. 6: International Agreement

20. **Sec. 6, weak mandate.** "Shall seek to negotiate" requires effort, not a result. The bill doesn't say whether the 18-month deadline applies to starting or finishing negotiations, and it requires no report to Congress or other follow-up. Any resulting treaty would also need Senate advice and consent.

## Sec. 7: Duration

21. **Sec. 7(a), off-ramp adds nothing new.** A joint resolution must be presented to the President, like a bill, so this "finding" mechanism is effectively the same as passing a new law. The bill also never directs or funds the National Academies to produce the report it depends on.

22. **Sec. 7(a) vs. (b), how the two end dates interact.** The prohibition runs "until" Congress acts, but the whole Act sunsets after 10 years anyway. The bill doesn't say whether lifting the Sec. 3 prohibition early also ends Sec. 4 registration. A fixed sunset also lets companies plan to wait out the ban.

23. **Sec. 7(b), pending cases at sunset.** The bill has no savings clause, so it is unclear whether prosecutions or penalty actions still underway when the Act expires can continue. The general savings statute (1 U.S.C. § 109) covers repeals, and its application to expiring statutes is less settled.

## Sec. 8 and Cross-Cutting Issues

24. **Sec. 8, date inconsistencies throughout.** The bill keys some provisions to enactment (Secs. 3(b), 5(a), 6, 7(b)) and the Act as a whole to the effective date. This creates gaps, including systems that start training after enactment but before the effective date. These runs are not grandfathered, yet the ban isn't in force when they start.

25. **No rulemaking, appropriations, or preemption provisions.** No agency is authorized to issue regulations defining the many undefined terms, no funding is provided for enforcement or the registry, and the bill is silent on whether it preempts state AI laws.

26. **Possible constitutional challenges.** Beyond vagueness (item 5), opponents may raise First Amendment arguments (code or model weights as expression) or takings claims for investments the ban makes worthless. The strength of these arguments is contested and worth putting to legal experts before publishing.

For balance, consider getting outside legal comment on items 5, 19, 23, and 26.
