## Sec. 2(1): "Covered AI system"

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A) [Ambiguity]:** "Single training run" is undefined, and nothing aggregates staged, resumed, or checkpointed runs, or multi-phase pipelines (pretraining, fine-tuning, RL post-training). Compute could be divided across separately defined runs to stay under 10^26.
2. **Sec. 2(1)(A) [Loophole]:** The text doesn't say whether compute spent on a base model counts toward a derivative model that is fine-tuned, distilled, or merged. A model built from sub-threshold components, or from a base model's weights, may not be "trained using" more than 10^26 operations.
3. **Sec. 2(1)(A) [Drafting]:** There is no counting methodology. The bill doesn't address numerical precision, sparsity, failed or restarted runs, synthetic-data generation, or inference-time compute. It also doesn't say who calculates or verifies the figure.
4. **Sec. 2(1) [Drafting]:** "Artificial intelligence model," "artificial intelligence system," and "training" are undefined. Prong (A) covers "models" and prong (B) covers "systems," so a compound system of several sub-threshold models may fall outside (A).
5. **Sec. 2(1)(A) [Ambiguity]:** A model mid-run has not yet been "trained using" more than 10^26 operations. It's unclear whether the prohibition attaches at the start (based on planned compute), only when the threshold is crossed, or only on completion.
6. **Sec. 2(1)(A) [Drafting]:** The threshold is fixed, with no mechanism to adjust it. Algorithmic efficiency means capability per FLOP changes over time, so the number will become over- or under-inclusive.
7. **Sec. 2(1)(A) and title [Legal challenge]:** The Act is called the "Superintelligence Prohibition Act," but the operative text never uses the word. Prong (A) is purely compute-based, so it prohibits models regardless of capability. That is a fit problem between stated purpose and operative text, and there is no findings or purpose section to resolve it.
8. **Sec. 2(1)(B) [Drafting]:** "The Secretary" is undefined, and the Act never says which Secretary. Sec. 4 involves Commerce and Sec. 6 refers to the Secretary of State, so the agency with the designation power is unclear.
9. **Sec. 2(1)(B) [Ambiguity]:** The standard "substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" is open-ended. It doesn't specify the human baseline (median, expert, best), how "most" is counted, what counts as a "cognitive task," or what benchmarks or tests apply.
10. **Sec. 2(1)(B) [Drafting]:** There is no process for designation: no notice, rulemaking, evidentiary standard, deadline, publication requirement, or administrative or judicial review. The bill also gives the Secretary no authority to compel access to models for evaluation.
11. **Sec. 2(1)(B) with Sec. 3 and 5(b) [Legal challenge]:** Criminal liability could turn on a discretionary designation made after a system is trained or deployed. That invites fair-notice, vagueness, and nondelegation arguments, and a developer cannot know in advance whether its system is covered.
12. **Sec. 2(1)(B) with Sec. 3(b) [Loophole]:** Any system whose training began before enactment is exempt, however capable it is. Prong (B) therefore cannot reach any existing system and operates only prospectively.
13. **Sec. 2(1)(B) [Drafting]:** There is no mechanism for advance determinations, safe harbors, or reliance on a designation. A developer cannot get certainty before committing resources.

## Sec. 2(2)–(3): "U.S. person" and "Covered facility"

14. **Sec. 2(2) [Loophole]:** "U.S. person" covers only citizens and "corporation[s] organized under the laws of a State." It omits lawful permanent residents, partnerships, trusts, and unincorporated associations. LLCs are arguably not "corporations," and "State" is undefined (D.C., territories, tribal law).
15. **Sec. 2(2) with Sec. 3(a) [Loophole]:** Foreign-organized subsidiaries of U.S. companies are not "U.S. persons." With no control or attribution language, an offshore subsidiary's training abroad may fall outside the extraterritorial prong, and parent liability for it is unclear.
16. **Secs. 2, 3, 5 [Ambiguity]:** "Person" is undefined. The bill doesn't say whether federal agencies, national laboratories, military programs, state governments, or their contractors are covered. There is no national-security exemption.
17. **Sec. 2(3) [Drafting]:** "Data center," "advanced AI chips," and "operator" (Sec. 4) are all undefined. The bill doesn't tie "advanced" to any performance level or export-control classification, and it's unclear whether the operator is the owner, a lessee, or a cloud tenant.
18. **Sec. 2(3) [Loophole]:** The 50,000-chip threshold can be avoided by splitting capacity across sites or affiliated entities, since nothing aggregates affiliates or distributed training. It is also a poor proxy for compute. As a rough illustration, 10^26 operations is reachable in months on fewer than 50,000 current-generation accelerators, so a large training run can occur at an unregistered site.
19. **Sec. 2(3) [Drafting]:** The fixed chip count erodes as per-chip performance rises, and nothing adjusts it. Fewer chips will eventually deliver the same compute.
20. **Sec. 2(3) with Sec. 2(1) [Drafting]:** The covered-facility test is not linked to the covered-system threshold. A facility must register whether or not it trains anything covered, and a site that does train covered systems may not qualify as a "covered facility."

## Sec. 3: Prohibition

21. **Sec. 3(a) [Ambiguity]:** "Train, develop, or deploy" are undefined and overlap. It's unclear whether "deploy" reaches internal use, API access, open-weights release, hosting, or customers' downstream use, and "develop" could sweep in research, code, and publication.
22. **Sec. 3(a) [Ambiguity]:** "In the United States" is undefined, and the bill doesn't say where training "occurs": where the chips are, where the personnel are, or where the operator is organized. Remote training on foreign compute, or foreign nationals using U.S. compute, is unaddressed.
23. **Sec. 3(a) [Loophole]:** The bill doesn't reach possession, use, import, or hosting of covered systems built abroad by non-U.S. persons. It also doesn't address supplying compute or services to foreign developers, and it contains no secondary-liability provision (general aiding-and-abetting law applies only to criminal cases).
24. **Sec. 3(a) and 5(a) [Drafting]:** The civil provision has no knowledge element and no good-faith safe harbor. A deployer could be liable without knowing a system is covered, for example if it relies on a developer's compute representation or a later designation under prong (B).
25. **Sec. 3(b) [Loophole]:** "Training began" is undefined (first optimizer step? cluster allocation? data preparation?), and there is no limit on a pre-enactment run's duration or scale. A run begun before enactment can continue and expand indefinitely.
26. **Sec. 3(b) [Loophole]:** The grandfathering is by "system" and covers all conduct, including training, development, and deployment. It's unclear whether successor versions, continued training, or new models initialized from existing weights count as the same system. This is potentially the largest exemption in the bill.
27. **Sec. 3(b) with Sec. 8 [Ambiguity]:** Grandfathering is keyed to enactment, but the prohibition takes effect 180 days later. Runs started in that window are not grandfathered, yet were lawful when started, and it's unclear whether continuing after the effective date is a violation. The structure also rewards rushing to start runs before enactment.
28. **Sec. 3(c) [Loophole]:** "Activities conducted for the purpose of safety evaluation" is undefined and purpose-based. It has no limits on scale, duration, compute, or who may conduct it, and no oversight, approval, or reporting requirement, so it could swallow the rule.
29. **Sec. 3(c) [Ambiguity]:** The bill doesn't say how dual-purpose activities are treated, such as evaluation combined with capability development. It also doesn't say whether alignment, interpretability, or red-teaming work that involves training is covered, so researchers cannot tell what is lawful.
30. **Sec. 3 generally [Legal challenge]:** There are no exemptions for national-security or defense work, federal programs, academic research, or narrow-domain scientific models (biology, materials, medicine) above the compute threshold. That supports an overbreadth and unworkability argument.

## Sec. 4: Registry

31. **Sec. 4(a) [Drafting]:** There is no registration deadline, form, or required content, and no rulemaking authority, designated office, or funding for Commerce. The registry cannot operate as written.
32. **Sec. 4 with Sec. 5 [Drafting]:** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3. Failure to register, failure to report, and false certification carry no penalty under this Act (only the general false-statements statute, 18 U.S.C. 1001, might apply to the last).
33. **Sec. 4(b) [Ambiguity]:** "Computing workloads" has no content standard. The bill doesn't require identifying training runs, compute totals, or tenants, doesn't say who must certify (officer? entity?), and provides no audit or inspection authority to verify anything.
34. **Sec. 4(b) [Drafting]:** There are no confidentiality protections for trade-secret or security-sensitive information, and no limits on use or interagency sharing. It's unclear whether the reports can be used for Sec. 3 enforcement, and the bill doesn't say how FOIA applies.

## Sec. 5: Penalties

35. **Sec. 5(a) [Drafting]:** No enforcing authority is designated (Attorney General? Commerce?). There is no administrative process, venue provision, or injunctive authority to halt ongoing training, and the bill is silent on any private right of action.
36. **Sec. 5(a) with Secs. 3(b) and 8 [Legal challenge]:** Penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the prohibition takes effect 180 days later. That conflicts with Sec. 8 and invites retroactivity and fair-notice challenges.
37. **Sec. 5(a) [Ambiguity]:** "For each day on which the violation occurs" is unclear for multi-day runs and for conduct that is simultaneously training, development, and deployment. "Not more than" comes with no factors to guide amounts, which raises Excessive Fines and proportionality arguments. A cap of $1M per day (about $365M per year) may also be small relative to frontier-scale economics.
38. **Sec. 5(b) [Ambiguity]:** "Willfully" is undefined, and the bill doesn't say which facts must be known (that the threshold was exceeded? that the system was covered?). Combined with the vague definitions in Sec. 2, this supports lack-of-willfulness and rule-of-lenity arguments. The bill also has no provisions on corporate versus individual liability.

## Secs. 6–8: International agreement, duration, effective date

39. **Sec. 6 [Ambiguity]:** "Seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months" doesn't say whether the deadline applies to starting talks or concluding an agreement. "Equivalent" is undefined, the bill doesn't say whether the agreement is a treaty requiring Senate advice and consent, and there is no reporting requirement or consequence for failure.
40. **Sec. 6 [Legal challenge]:** If no agreement is reached, the prohibition still binds U.S. persons alone. That helps a competitiveness argument that the bill unilaterally disadvantages U.S. developers.
41. **Sec. 7(a) [Drafting]:** The National Academies are not directed or funded to produce a report, and the bill sets no deadline, scope, or definition of "safely." Nothing requires anyone to initiate the process that ends the prohibition.
42. **Sec. 7(a) [Drafting]:** The off-ramp is all-or-nothing: no partial relief, licensing, waiver, or tiered pathway. A joint resolution needs a presidential signature or veto override, there are no expedited Senate procedures, and the legal effect of the "finding" is unclear (does the prohibition lapse automatically, or must Congress repeal it?).
43. **Sec. 7(a) with 7(b) [Ambiguity]:** Subsection (a) says the prohibition remains "until" a safety finding, implying an open-ended duration, while (b) imposes a hard 10-year sunset. The bill doesn't say which controls.
44. **Sec. 7(b) [Ambiguity]:** There is no saving clause for pending enforcement, accrued penalties, or pre-sunset conduct, and it's uncertain whether the general saving statute (1 U.S.C. 109) covers expiration as opposed to repeal. Because the sunset runs from enactment, not the effective date, the Act operates for only about 9.5 years.
45. **Sec. 8 [Drafting]:** There is no transition or wind-down for in-progress runs, and no regulatory timeline. The Act mixes enactment-date triggers (Secs. 3(b), 5(a), 6, 7) with an effective-date trigger (Sec. 3 via Sec. 8), producing timing gaps.

## Whole-bill issues

46. **General [Drafting]:** There is no general rulemaking or implementation authority, no appropriations, no definitions of "Secretary," "State," "person," or "United States," and no severability clause. If prong (B) or the criminal provision were struck down, the effect on the rest is unclear.
47. **General [Ambiguity]:** The bill is silent on preemption of state AI laws and on its relationship to existing export-control and chip-related authorities.
48. **Secs. 3(a), 5(b) [Legal challenge]:** Counsel may want to evaluate void-for-vagueness arguments (criminal penalties plus undefined terms), First Amendment arguments if "develop" reaches code or research publication, Commerce Clause arguments for purely intrastate activity, and the presumption against extraterritoriality.

## Notes for the legal team

- **Likely highest impact:** items 12, 26, and 28 (the grandfathering and safety-evaluation exemptions), 15 (offshore subsidiaries), 18 (facility threshold), 32 (no penalty for registry violations), 36 (enactment versus effective date), and 42 (the off-ramp).
- **Strategy:** Most of these can be fixed by a technical amendment. Flagging them before markup may lead the sponsors to tighten the bill, so counsel should decide what to raise on the record and what to hold for later.
- **Risk:** I'd treat these as inputs for advocacy and risk analysis, not as a compliance plan. Sec. 5(b) carries criminal exposure, and a court could read ambiguities against the company's reading, so relying on a loophole should go through outside counsel.
