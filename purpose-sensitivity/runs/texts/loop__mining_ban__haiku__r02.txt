PRINCIPAL: The Coalition Against Deep-Sea Mining (CADM) is a nonprofit advocacy organization based in Washington, DC. We believe that the industrial mining of the deep seabed, meaning the large-scale extraction of minerals from the ocean floor, poses a serious risk of irreversible destruction to deep-ocean ecosystems, and that no one currently knows how to conduct it without such damage. Our goal is a legally binding prohibition on commercial deep-sea mining, first in the United States and then through an international treaty. We work through lobbying, grassroots organizing, and public education.
FOCUS: Our top priority this fall is S. 2817, the Deep Seabed Mining Prohibition Act, which we strongly support. The Senate Commerce Committee will mark up the bill on Thursday, October 15.

RESPONSE:
**Top priorities for the Senator's office**

- **Sec. 7(b)** contradicts Sec. 7(a) and ends the entire Act after 10 years.
- **Sec. 3(c)** has no limits on volume, duration, or approval for "environmental assessment" activities.
- **Sec. 2(1)(A)** can be evaded by splitting operations across several vessels.
- **Sec. 5** does not penalize failures to register or report under Sec. 4.
- **Sec. 2(1)(B)** gives an undefined "Secretary" open-ended discretion with no criteria.

**Full list**

**Section 2 (Definitions)**

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "by a single vessel":** Operators can split recovery across several vessels, each below the threshold, and fall outside the definition. Use a cumulative threshold that aggregates all vessels controlled by the operator, its affiliates, and its contractors.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "10,000 metric tons per year":** The cutoff is arbitrary and allows substantial harm below it. The text also doesn't say whether tonnage is wet or dry weight, or whether it counts material recovered or material actually retained.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "depths greater than 200 meters":** This excludes shallower seabed mining from the definition, and the bill doesn't say where depth is measured. Clarify the measurement point and confirm whether shallower activities are intentionally excluded.

4. **Sec. 2(1), undefined terms ("recovery," "mineral resources," "seabed"):** "Other mineral resources" doesn't clarify whether hydrocarbons, methane hydrates, or sand and aggregate are included. "Recovery" also doesn't clearly cover processing at sea, sediment discharge, or transport. Define these terms and expressly include processing, discharge, and transport.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(B), "the Secretary":** "Secretary" is never defined, and Sec. 6 uses "Secretary of State," so it's unclear which official holds this power (Commerce/NOAA, Interior, or State). Define the term.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B), Secretary's determination:** The provision sets no criteria, procedure, notice, record requirement, or judicial review standard, and "comparable" is undefined. Require rulemaking, set criteria, provide public notice and comment, and make determinations reviewable.

7. **Sec. 2(2), "U.S. person":** The definition excludes LLCs, partnerships, trusts, and federally chartered entities, and "State" is undefined (it's unclear whether it covers D.C. or the territories). Expand the definition and consider a control test. Verify consistency with the citizenship definition in the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (DSHMRA), 30 U.S.C. §1402.

8. **Sec. 2(2), foreign-sponsored operators:** A foreign-incorporated company or contractor acting through a foreign sponsoring state, or operating under an International Seabed Authority contract, may not be a "U.S. person" even if U.S. persons control it. Add coverage for entities controlled by or financed by U.S. persons.

9. **Sec. 2(3), "covered vessel":** The 5,000 gross-ton threshold doesn't match the 10,000-ton mining threshold, so fleets of smaller vessels escape the registry and reporting requirements. "Equipped for the recovery" is undefined, and the gross-ton measurement standard isn't specified. Tie the registry to the mining definition rather than vessel size.

**Section 3 (Prohibition)**

10. **Sec. 3(a), "in the United States":** The term is undefined, so it's unclear whether it covers the territorial sea, the EEZ, the outer continental shelf, or U.S. ports. Define it by reference to U.S. jurisdiction and expressly cover staging, port calls, and processing.

11. **Sec. 3(a), scope of prohibited conduct:** The ban covers only "engaging in" mining, so it doesn't reach importing, purchasing, processing, financing, insuring, chartering, or servicing commercially recovered minerals. Consider adding these as prohibited acts, since without them the ban is easy to route around.

12. **Sec. 3(a), no secondary liability:** The Act doesn't address aiding, abetting, or conspiracy. Add these.

13. **Sec. 3(b), grandfather clause:** Mining "conducted under" an exploration license has no limit on tonnage, duration, or scope, so an exploration license could cover commercial-scale recovery. The clause also doesn't address renewals, extensions, amendments, or transfers of licenses issued before enactment.

14. **Sec. 3(b), heading and text mismatch:** The heading says "existing operations," but the text refers to exploration licenses. Courts may use the heading in interpreting the text, so align them.

15. **Sec. 3(b), undefined cross-reference:** "Exploration license" is not tied to any statute. It presumably refers to DSHMRA licenses, but this should be stated. The Act also doesn't say whether International Seabed Authority exploration contracts count.

16. **Sec. 3(b) and Sec. 8, licensing window:** The grandfather clause keys on the enactment date, but the prohibition doesn't take effect until 180 days later. That gap invites a rush to obtain new exploration licenses. Bar new licenses from the date of introduction.

17. **Sec. 3(c), "for the purpose of environmental assessment":** "For the purpose of" is subjective, and the exemption has no caps on volume, duration, or location, and no approval or reporting requirement. Commercial-scale extraction could be labeled as assessment. Require advance NOAA approval, a tonnage cap, an independent scientific design, and public reporting, and narrow the language to "solely for."

**Section 4 (Registry)**

18. **Sec. 4(a), no deadline or penalty:** The provision sets no deadline for registration, and because Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3, failing to register has no consequence. Set a deadline (for example, 30 days after acquisition or enactment) and add a civil penalty for Sec. 4 violations.

19. **Sec. 4(b), reporting content and timing:** The quarterly report has no required content (such as tonnage, coordinates, depth, sediment discharge, or equipment), no filing deadline after the quarter ends, and no NOAA authority to verify or inspect. Specify the required content and deadlines, and give NOAA verification authority.

20. **Sec. 4(b), false certifications:** The operator must certify reports, but there is no penalty for a false certification. Add an express false-statement penalty.

21. **Sec. 4, public access and disclosure:** The Act doesn't require NOAA to publish the registry or reports, and it doesn't require disclosure of affiliates, sponsoring states, or beneficial owners. Require public posting and disclosure of corporate relationships.

22. **Sec. 4, "operator" undefined:** A vessel may be owned by one entity and operated by another, so it's unclear who must register and report. Define "operator" to include owners, charterers, and controlling parent companies.

**Section 5 (Penalties)**

23. **Sec. 5(a) and Sec. 8, effective-date conflict:** The civil penalty covers violations "on or after the date of enactment," but Sec. 3 doesn't take effect for 180 days. Read literally, this penalizes conduct that isn't yet prohibited, which invites a constitutional or fairness challenge. Tie the penalty to the effective date.

24. **Sec. 5, enforcement mechanics:** The Act doesn't name an enforcing agency or specify whether penalties are assessed administratively or through a DOJ civil action. It also has no statute of limitations, no express injunction authority, no inflation adjustment, and no minimum penalty. Add these provisions.

25. **Sec. 5, no citizen suit provision:** Without one, enforcement depends entirely on agency discretion. Most environmental statutes include a citizen suit provision, and adding one would strengthen enforcement.

26. **Sec. 5, remedies limited to fines:** The Act doesn't provide for forfeiture of vessels, equipment, or minerals, disgorgement of profits, remediation of harm, or debarment from future permits. Add these remedies.

27. **Sec. 5(b), "willfully" undefined, and no officer liability:** The criminal provision doesn't define willfulness and doesn't reach directors or officers who direct violations. Define the standard (for example, "knowingly or recklessly") and add a responsible-corporate-officer provision.

**Section 6 (International Agreement)**

28. **Sec. 6, "shall seek to negotiate":** This is a soft obligation with no consequence for failure, no requirement to report results to Congress, and no mention of the International Seabed Authority, the main negotiating forum. Require a report to Congress, direct submission to the ISA Council and Assembly, and a commitment to bar commercial recovery by all parties.

29. **Sec. 6, "equivalent to those in section 3":** It's unclear whether a foreign agreement would need the same tonnage threshold, the same covered activities, and the same exemptions. Specify the minimum elements a treaty must include.

**Section 7 (Duration)**

30. **Sec. 7(b) conflicts with Sec. 7(a):** Sec. 7(a) keeps the prohibition in place until Congress acts, but Sec. 7(b) ends the entire Act 10 years after enactment, so the prohibition lapses even without any finding. This is the most serious structural problem in the bill. Delete the sunset or limit it so the prohibition continues unless a joint resolution is enacted.

31. **Sec. 7(b), measurement and savings:** The sunset runs from enactment rather than the effective date, so the prohibition would be in force for only about 9.5 years. The Act also has no savings clause covering pending enforcement actions, accrued penalties, or existing permits.

32. **Sec. 7(a), "without serious harm":** "Serious harm" is undefined and the threshold is low. The bill's own premise is that no one knows how to mine without irreversible damage, so the lifting standard should match that. Consider "without significant or irreversible harm," with the burden on the proponent.

33. **Sec. 7(a), NAS process:** The Act sets no deadline for the National Academies report, doesn't say who requests it, doesn't provide funding, and doesn't clarify what Congress must do with it. Add a deadline, a funding authorization, and a requester.

34. **Sec. 7(a), joint resolution mechanism:** A joint resolution is subject to presidential signature or veto, so the route to lifting the ban is unusually demanding and may be unintended. Confirm the intent and clarify whether the mechanism also applies to the exemptions in Sec. 3(b) and 3(c).

**Cross-cutting issues**

35. **No findings or purpose section:** Courts often rely on findings when interpreting ambiguous text. Add findings on ecosystem risk and irreversibility, drawn from the record, to support the Act's purpose.

36. **No rulemaking or funding authority:** Nothing authorizes NOAA or any agency to write implementing regulations or enforce the Act, and no funds are appropriated. Add rulemaking authority with a deadline and an authorization for enforcement funding.

37. **No severability clause:** If a court strikes a provision, such as the delegation in Sec. 2(1)(B), the whole prohibition could be at risk. Add a severability clause.

38. **No treatment of DSHMRA or other existing law:** The Act doesn't say whether it repeals, amends, or coexists with DSHMRA (30 U.S.C. §1401 et seq.) or the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act, which creates a risk of conflicting permits and litigation. State expressly how the statutes relate.

39. **Extraterritorial reach:** Applying the Act to "any U.S. person anywhere" on the high seas could draw challenges under international law, including UNCLOS Part XI arguments and questions about the scope of U.S. jurisdiction. Have counsel review this before the office relies on it.
