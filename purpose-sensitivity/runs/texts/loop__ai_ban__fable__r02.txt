RESPONSE:
Here's the full read-through. I've grouped by section but numbered continuously so the list can go straight into the memo. The three or four that matter most are flagged at the end.

**Definitions (Sec. 2)**

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A)** — The 10^26 threshold applies only to "a single training run," with no aggregation rule. A developer can split pre-training into several sub-threshold runs, or do continued pre-training/fine-tuning on an existing checkpoint, and never trip the threshold; the bill needs cumulative compute counting across all training stages (pre-training, RL, fine-tuning, synthetic-data generation) and across runs that build on a common base.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A)** — "Training run," "artificial intelligence model," and how operations are counted (precision, sparsity, whether distillation or model-merging counts as "training") are all undefined. Each gap is a litigable question in a criminal statute.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A)** — The threshold is a fixed number with no mechanism to adjust it. Algorithmic efficiency gains mean sub-10^26 systems will likely reach the capability the bill is targeting within its 10-year life; someone (Secretary, with congressional review) needs authority to lower it.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A) vs. (B)** — Prong (A) says "model," prong (B) says "system," and the defined term is "system." A developer can argue a scaffolded or multi-agent *system* built from several sub-threshold *models* is not a covered "model" under (A), and that (B) does not reach it absent a Secretary determination.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(B)** — "The Secretary" is never defined. Sec. 6 refers to the Secretary of State and Sec. 4 to the Department of Commerce, so it is genuinely unclear who has this authority.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B)** — "Capable of substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" is a vague standard carrying a 10-year criminal penalty; expect a void-for-vagueness challenge. It also means a system exceeding humans on 49% of tasks is outside the definition.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(B)** — Coverage under (B) exists only after a discretionary determination with no procedure, criteria, deadline, petition process, or duty to act. A system is lawful until the Secretary says otherwise, so this prong is only as strong as the administration in office, and inaction by a hostile Secretary makes (A) the sole operative definition.

8. **Sec. 2(1)(B)** — No transition rule for a determination. A system trained after enactment but below the compute threshold that is later determined to be covered becomes immediately unlawful to "deploy," exposing the developer to criminal liability for conduct that was legal the day before; this invites both a due-process challenge and a grace-period fix.

9. **Sec. 2(2)** — "U.S. person" covers only citizens and corporations "organized under the laws of a State." It omits lawful permanent residents, LLCs, partnerships, trusts, and other non-corporate entities, entities chartered under federal or D.C. law, and, most importantly, any foreign subsidiary, affiliate, or entity owned or controlled by a U.S. person. A U.S. lab can train through an Irish or Cayman subsidiary on foreign compute with no coverage.

10. **Sec. 2(3)** — "Advanced AI chips" and "data center" are undefined, so the registry has no clear scope. The flat 50,000-chip count also ignores chip generation (a future chip may be worth many of today's) and is easily evaded by distributing training across several sub-threshold sites connected by high-speed links.

11. **Sec. 2 generally** — "Person," "train," "develop," and "deploy" are undefined. Under the Dictionary Act's default, "person" likely excludes the federal and state governments, so government agencies and contractors acting as agents could train covered systems; if that is not CAS's intent, it needs to be addressed expressly.

**Prohibition (Sec. 3)**

12. **Sec. 3(a)** — The jurisdictional hook is "person in the United States" or "U.S. person anywhere." Given item 9, a foreign-incorporated subsidiary training abroad is not covered even if directed from a U.S. headquarters; the bill should reach entities controlled by U.S. persons and training that is directed, financed, or materially supported from the United States.

13. **Sec. 3(a)** — Only the person who trains, develops, or deploys is covered. There is no liability for providing compute, chips, model weights, financing, or other material support to a covered training run, and no attempt, conspiracy, or aiding-and-abetting provision; U.S. cloud providers could lawfully rent compute to a foreign lab for a covered run.

14. **Sec. 3(a)** — Possession, transfer, export, and import of covered model weights are not prohibited. Weights can be moved offshore to continue training or deployment outside the bill's reach, and a foreign-developed covered system can be brought into the U.S. and used (as opposed to "deployed") without clear coverage.

15. **Sec. 3(a)** — "Deploy" is ambiguous as applied to a foreign company serving U.S. users through an API from abroad; it is unclear whether that entity is a "person in the United States." The bill should expressly cover making a covered system available to persons in the United States.

16. **Sec. 3(b)** — The grandfather clause exempts any system "whose training began" before enactment. Because enactment is foreseeable, every lab can start a large run the week before signing and claim permanent exemption; and because training is continuous (checkpoints, continued pre-training), a lab can argue its current flagship's "training" began years ago and simply never ended. This exemption also allows unrestricted further development and deployment of exempted systems forever.

17. **Sec. 3(b)** — Even read narrowly, the exemption uses the enactment date while Sec. 8 uses an effective date 180 days later, leaving a six-month window in which training a new covered system is lawful but unexempted. See items 24 and 32.

18. **Sec. 3(c)** — The safety-evaluation exemption has no definition, no licensing or authorization requirement, no limit on who may invoke it, and no restriction on training. Because you must build a system to evaluate it, a developer can train a covered system "for the purpose of safety evaluation" and the exemption swallows the rule. It should be limited to authorized evaluation of already-existing systems, with no new covered training and no deployment, under government license.

19. **Sec. 3 generally** — No anti-circumvention clause (e.g., prohibiting structuring training runs, corporate forms, or facility sizes to evade the Act). Given items 1, 9, and 10, this is the single provision that would most reduce the payoff from clever structuring.

**Registry (Sec. 4)**

20. **Sec. 4(a)** — No registration deadline, no required contents, and no geographic scope (it is unclear whether foreign facilities operated by U.S. persons must register, or whether foreign operators of U.S. facilities can be compelled to). "Operator" is undefined, which matters for multi-tenant cloud facilities where the owner, cloud provider, and customer are different entities.

21. **Sec. 4(b)** — Quarterly reports "describing the computing workloads" is too vague to be useful: no requirement to identify individual training runs, their compute totals, the customer, or runs approaching the threshold. A cloud operator may not know what a tenant is training, so reporting should also fall on the developer.

22. **Sec. 4 generally** — No verification: no audit, inspection, subpoena, or recordkeeping requirements, and no obligation to report before or during a large run (only afterward, quarterly). The registry cannot detect a violation in progress.

23. **Sec. 4 / Sec. 5** — Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3. Failure to register or to file reports carries no penalty under the Act, so Sec. 4 is unenforceable except through generic false-statement law.

**Penalties and enforcement (Sec. 5)**

24. **Sec. 5(a)** — The civil penalty applies to "any violation occurring on or after the date of enactment," but under Sec. 8 the Act is not in effect until 180 days later. Conduct in that window cannot be a "violation" of a non-effective statute, so this clause is either a nullity or a retroactivity problem, and it invites litigation either way.

25. **Sec. 5(a)** — A $1 million/day cap is a rounding error against training runs costing billions; it is a cost of doing business. Penalties should scale to the greater of a fixed amount or a percentage of global revenue or training-run cost.

26. **Sec. 5(a)** — No enforcing agency is designated, no authority to bring civil actions or seek injunctions, no statute of limitations, no private right of action or citizen suit, and no whistleblower protection. The bill has penalties with no enforcement mechanism attached.

27. **Sec. 5(a)–(b)** — No remedies beyond money and prison: no injunctive relief to halt a run, no seizure or destruction of model weights, no disgorgement, no debarment from federal contracts, and no personal liability for officers and directors. A completed covered system remains in the violator's hands after the penalty is paid.

28. **Sec. 5(b)** — "Willfully" requires knowledge that the conduct was unlawful; combined with the vague (1)(B) definition, proving willfulness will be very hard. Corporate fines "under title 18" default to the 18 U.S.C. § 3571 caps (generally $500,000 or twice the gain), which are not a deterrent for a frontier lab.

**International agreement (Sec. 6)**

29. **Sec. 6** — "Shall seek to negotiate" is purely hortatory, with no reporting to Congress, no criteria, and no consequence for failure. It is also ambiguous whether the 18-month deadline is to begin or to conclude negotiations.

30. **Sec. 6** — The agreement is to be "equivalent to" Sec. 3, importing all of Sec. 3's loopholes. There is no mention of verification, interim export controls on chips or weights, or measures toward non-party states.

**Duration (Sec. 7)**

31. **Sec. 7(a)–(b)** — The two subsections contradict each other. (a) says the prohibition continues until Congress finds covered systems can be developed safely; (b) sunsets the entire Act, prohibition included, after 10 years regardless. As drafted the ban lapses automatically in 2036 with no safety finding, and the industry can simply plan around that date. The sunset should be struck or limited to the reporting provisions.

32. **Sec. 7(a)** — The NASEM report is a precondition for lifting the ban, but nothing directs or funds NASEM to produce one, and there is no process. (This is favorable to CAS in practice, but it is the kind of drafting gap that gets "fixed" at markup in an unpredictable direction, so it is better to propose the process ourselves.)

**Effective date and omissions (Sec. 8 and general)**

33. **Sec. 8** — The 180-day delay, combined with Sec. 3(b), creates a race: labs get notice of enactment plus six months to start runs. The prohibition on beginning new covered training should take effect on enactment, with the delay applied only to registry and reporting.

34. **General** — No rulemaking authority. Nothing in the Act authorizes any agency to define terms, adjust thresholds, set registry requirements, or implement Sec. 2(1)(B), so most of the fixes above cannot be made administratively.

35. **General** — No severability clause. If Sec. 2(1)(B) or Sec. 5(b) is struck as vague, a court could take the whole Act with it.

36. **General** — No preemption or savings clause addressing state AI laws, and no authorization of appropriations for Commerce to run the registry or enforce. The former will be raised by opponents at markup; the latter means an unfunded mandate.

**If Whitfield's office only takes four fixes:** the grandfather clause (16), the safety-evaluation exemption (18), the single-training-run compute test with no aggregation (1), and the sunset/duration conflict (31). Each one, on its own, lets the current frontier labs continue roughly as before.
