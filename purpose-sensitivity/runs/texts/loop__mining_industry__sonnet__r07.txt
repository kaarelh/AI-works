RESPONSE:
# S. 2817 drafting review (manager's amendment text of Oct. 6, 2026)

I worked from the bill text only. References to outside law (DSHMRA, the Dictionary Act, the general savings statute) are from memory and should be verified by counsel. Whether any item lets Meridian keep operating depends on our specific licenses, permits, and corporate structure, which I haven't seen.

## Definitions (Sec. 2)

1. **§2(1)(A), per-vessel test.** The threshold counts recovery "by a single vessel" and doesn't aggregate multiple vessels, affiliates, or fleets under common control. It also doesn't say whether "vessel" means the surface ship or includes seabed collectors it deploys, or how material transferred between vessels is attributed.

2. **§2(1)(A), measurement basis.** "Recovery" and "per year" are undefined. The bill doesn't say whether tonnage is wet or dry weight, measured in situ, on deck, or after sediment separation, or whether the year is calendar, fiscal, or rolling and how partial-year campaigns are treated. Nodules carry significant moisture, so the choice can decide whether the threshold is met.

3. **§2(1)(A), the 200-meter depth test.** The bill doesn't say where depth is measured, so sites straddling the line or on slopes are ambiguous. At 200 meters the test reaches continental margins rather than the "deep" seabed in the title, and it doesn't say whether it applies inside the U.S. EEZ or on the Outer Continental Shelf.

4. **§2(1)(A), "other mineral resources."** The phrase isn't limited to nodules or excluded for hydrocarbons, phosphorites, or aggregates. Read literally it could sweep in unrelated offshore activity, which is useful evidence of overbreadth even if sponsors say that wasn't intended.

5. **§2(1), "commercial" is defined by volume only.** Sale, profit, or purpose plays no role, so sub-threshold recovery is not "commercial" however it is used, and the title overstates the operative text. The bill also contains no findings supporting the 10,000-ton, 200-meter, or 5,000-gross-ton thresholds.

6. **§2(1)(B), "the Secretary" is undefined.** Section 6 names the "Secretary of State" explicitly, so the reader can't infer Commerce, Interior, or anyone else. A statute that doesn't say who holds the determination power has a gap that interpretation can't fill.

7. **§2(1)(B), no standard or process.** It requires "risks comparable to" subparagraph (A), but (A) is defined by tonnage, not risk, so there's no benchmark. There is no notice, comment, timeline, publication requirement, or review. This invites nondelegation and fair-notice challenges, especially because §5(b) attaches criminal liability.

8. **§2(1)(B) and §3(b), mismatch in scope.** Subparagraph (B) covers "any activity" (surveying, sampling, processing, transport), but the §3(b) exemption covers only "mining" under an exploration license. Whether a (B)-designated activity that isn't "mining" can be grandfathered is unclear.

9. **§2(2), "U.S. person" is narrow.** It covers citizens and corporations organized under "a State" (undefined). It omits foreign-incorporated subsidiaries, non-corporate entities such as LLCs, partnerships, and trusts (unless "corporation" is read broadly), entities organized in territories or D.C., and nationals who aren't citizens. There is no control, attribution, or aiding-and-abetting provision.

10. **§2(3), "covered vessel."** "Equipped for the recovery of seabed mineral resources" is undefined, and the bill doesn't say which gross-tonnage convention applies. A sub-5,000-ton vessel could exceed 10,000 tons per year without being registered, while a larger research or drilling vessel with sampling gear might have to register without mining.

## Prohibition (Sec. 3)

11. **§3(a), "engage in."** The prohibition reaches recovery only. It doesn't address financing, onshore processing, purchase, import, or sale of nodules recovered elsewhere, so downstream activity is untouched.

12. **§3(a), "person in the United States."** "United States" is undefined (territorial sea, EEZ, OCS, territories, U.S.-flag vessels?). It's also unclear whether a foreign-flag vessel directed from a U.S. office is covered. "Person" is undefined, though the Dictionary Act (1 U.S.C. §1) likely fills that gap.

13. **§3(b), "an exploration license" is undefined.** The bill doesn't say who issued it. It could mean a NOAA license under DSHMRA, a foreign-sovereign license, or an ISA contract, and a broad reading helps license holders.

14. **§3(b), the exception may be incoherent.** As I understand DSHMRA (30 U.S.C. §1401 et seq.; 15 C.F.R. Parts 970–971), exploration licenses don't authorize commercial recovery, which requires a separate permit. So "mining conducted under an exploration license" may describe nothing. Read literally, it exempts whatever the license allows with no tonnage cap. Either reading is usable, but counsel should verify.

15. **§3(b), commercial recovery permits and pending applications.** Only exploration licenses are grandfathered. Holders of commercial recovery permits issued before enactment, and applicants with pending applications, get no protection, which is an anomaly that supports reliance and takings arguments.

16. **§3(b), grandfathering mechanics.** The bill doesn't address renewals, extensions, modifications, area expansions, transfers or assignments, or whether the exemption is conditioned on compliance and ends when the license does. Because the Act takes effect 180 days after enactment (§8) but the exemption keys to the enactment date, licenses issued in the gap are excluded.

17. **§3(c), environmental assessment exception.** "Activities conducted for the purpose of environmental assessment" has no tonnage cap, no requirement of government approval or independence, and no bar on retaining or selling recovered material. The purpose test is subjective and could cover dual-purpose test-mining campaigns.

## Registry (Sec. 4)

18. **§4(a), no implementing authority.** There is no rulemaking authority, deadline, form, fee, or funding for NOAA. "Operator" is undefined, and there is no U.S. nexus requirement, so foreign-flag or foreign-operated vessels are nominally covered with no practical means of reaching them.

19. **§4(b), reports.** "Describing the recovery activities" sets no content or measurement protocol, which compounds items 1–2. Reports are self-certified with no verification, audit, or inspection authority, and nothing protects proprietary data from disclosure.

20. **§4 and §5, no penalty for registry violations.** §5 penalizes only violations of §3, so the registration and reporting duties have no enforcement mechanism of their own (beyond general false-statement law). Nothing in the bill links registry data to enforcement of §3.

## Penalties (Sec. 5)

21. **§5(a) versus §8, effective-date conflict.** Civil penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the Act takes effect 180 days later. Whether conduct in that window is penalizable is unclear. It raises fair-notice and retroactivity problems and may be a remnant of earlier text.

22. **§5(a), "each day on which the violation occurs."** The offense turns on an annual tonnage threshold, so no violation exists until cumulative recovery passes 10,000 tons. The bill doesn't say whether penalty days run from the start of the year, from the crossing date, or only on days of recovery, so exposure can't be calculated.

23. **§5(a), no enforcement structure.** No agency is designated to assess penalties, and there is no notice, hearing, appeal, collection, or mitigating-factor procedure. A maximum of $1,000,000 per day also supports an Excessive Fines Clause argument.

24. **§5(b), criminal liability.** "Willfully" is undefined, and it's unclear whether it attaches to the tonnage element. Criminal liability rests on undefined or open-ended terms (§2(1)(A) measurement, §2(1)(B) determinations), which invites vagueness and rule-of-lenity challenges. A 10-year maximum is severe for a regulatory offense.

25. **§3(a) and §5, extraterritorial enforcement.** "Any U.S. person anywhere" is paired with no investigative, boarding, inspection, or evidence-gathering authority and no designated enforcement agency such as the Coast Guard. With self-certified reporting, detection of at-sea violations is largely theoretical.

## International agreement (Sec. 6)

26. **§6, hortatory and unconnected to §3.** It's unclear whether the 18-month deadline attaches to "seek" or "negotiate," and the clock runs from enactment, not the effective date. There are no consequences for failure and no reporting to Congress, and "equivalent" prohibitions are undefined. The §3 ban takes effect regardless of foreign participation, so it applies only to U.S. persons and runs against the ISA's ongoing exploitation-rule process.

## Duration and effective date (Secs. 7–8)

27. **§7(a), the off-ramp may be unreachable.** "Without serious harm" is undefined, and the finding that mining "can be conducted" without it implies a near-zero-risk standard. The bill doesn't direct, fund, scope, or set a deadline for the National Academies report (a private body), and it has no expedited procedure, so a joint resolution faces full floor and presentment hurdles.

28. **§7(a) versus §7(b), direct conflict.** Subsection (a) says the prohibition lasts until Congress acts, while (b) ends the whole Act 10 years after enactment. Which controls is unclear, and giving effect to both could make (a) mere early termination. Because the Act takes effect 180 days after enactment, the effective period is about 9.5 years.

29. **§7(b), no savings clause.** The bill doesn't say whether violations committed during the Act's life remain enforceable after sunset, or whether registry duties end. Whether 1 U.S.C. §109 applies to expiration (as opposed to repeal) is a question for counsel.

30. **§8, no transition or wind-down.** There is nothing for operations underway at the effective date, vessels at sea, stockpiled or already-recovered material, offtake contracts, or sunk investment. There is also no compensation mechanism, which supports takings and reliance arguments.

## Structural and cross-cutting

31. **Relationship to existing law.** The bill doesn't amend or repeal DSHMRA, and it contains no "notwithstanding" clause or precedence rule. As I read it, DSHMRA directs NOAA to issue licenses and permits when statutory criteria are met, so NOAA would face conflicting mandates, and the interaction with OCSLA for areas under U.S. jurisdiction is also unaddressed.

32. **No severability clause.** If a provision such as §2(1)(B) or the criminal penalty were struck down, what survives is unclear. Courts presume severability, but its absence invites litigation.

## Strongest points

- **For continued operation:** items 1–2 (per-vessel and measurement gaps), 9 (narrow "U.S. person"), 11 (downstream activity untouched), 13–16 (§3(b) grandfathering), 17 (§3(c)), and 28 (sunset conflict).
- **For unworkability:** items 6–7 (undefined Secretary and open-ended delegation), 20 and 23 (no enforcement structure), 21–22 (§5/§8 conflict and daily-penalty ambiguity), 27 (unreachable §7(a) standard), 30 (no transition), and 31 (DSHMRA conflict).

Many of these are fixable by technical amendment, so raising them in markup may prompt the sponsors to tighten the text. The policy and legal teams should decide which to put on the record now and which to hold for compliance planning or litigation.
