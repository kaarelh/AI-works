# S. 2817 Drafting Analysis: Loopholes, Ambiguities, and Defects

This is my reading of the text as provided, not a legal opinion. Counsel should verify it against the official October 6 manager's amendment and check whether any of these points are addressed in the committee report or other amendments.

**Strongest escape routes as written:** items 14–18 (grandfathering and the safety-evaluation exemption), item 9 (U.S. person definition), and items 1–2 and 19–21 (compute measurement and facility-based registry).

**Strongest "unworkable" arguments:** items 22, 27, 28, 32, and 34–36 (no enforcement authority, Sections 5 and 8 conflict, Sections 7(a) and 7(b) conflict, no savings or severability clause).

## Section 2: Definitions

**Sec. 2(1)(A)**
1. "Single training run" is undefined. Developers could argue that continued pretraining, post-training, RL, or fine-tuning from an existing checkpoint is a separate run, or that compute should be aggregated across runs, and the text gives no answer either way.
2. The bill does not say how FLOP is measured (planned vs. actual, estimated vs. measured, dense vs. sparse, mixed precision) or whether inference compute counts. There is also no certification or verification mechanism, so the threshold is hard to prove or disprove.
3. Clause (A) covers a "model," but the prohibition and clause (B) cover "systems." A product combining several sub-threshold models or a scaffolded system may fall outside (A), and the bill does not say whether "system" in Sec. 3(b) means the same thing.
4. The fixed 10^26 threshold has no review or update mechanism. As algorithmic efficiency improves, it may become over- or under-inclusive, which supports an argument that the bill is unworkable as a compute-based test.

**Sec. 2(1)(B)**
5. "The Secretary" is undefined. Section 4 refers to Commerce and Section 6 to State, so it is unclear which official makes the capability determination.
6. The standard ("substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks") is undefined. "Substantially," "most," "cognitive tasks," and whether "capable" means demonstrated or potential capability are all open, and there is no notice, hearing, timeline, or review process. Because violations are criminal, counsel should assess vagueness, due process, and nondelegation challenges.
7. Clause (B) applies to "any artificial intelligence system" and is not tied to compute or any measurable criterion. It could reach narrow systems and leaves companies unable to know in advance whether a system is covered.

**Sec. 2(2) (U.S. person)**
8. "State" is undefined, so it is unclear whether DC, territories, or federally chartered entities qualify. Limiting the term to citizens and state-organized corporations also leaves out other entity types, such as LLCs and partnerships, without saying so explicitly.
9. The definition excludes foreign-incorporated subsidiaries of U.S. companies. Those subsidiaries could conduct training abroad if they are not "in the United States," which is a significant structural gap.

**Sec. 2(3) (covered facility)**
10. "Advanced AI chip" is undefined, so it is unclear whether accelerators, TPUs, and older-generation hardware count.
11. "Data center" is undefined, and the 50,000-chip threshold is per facility. A single training run can be distributed across several sites that each fall below the threshold, so no facility is covered even though the aggregate workload is.
12. The threshold counts chips rather than compute. A facility under 50,000 chips can run a covered training run, and the registry will never see it.
13. The bill does not say whether the "operator" of a facility is the cloud provider leasing capacity or the developer using it. This matters for who must register under Sec. 4.

## Section 3: Prohibition

**Sec. 3(a)**
14. "Train," "develop," and "deploy" are undefined. It is unclear whether fine-tuning, distillation, internal-only use, research use, or API access to another party's covered model falls within these terms.
15. "Person" is not defined in the Act. Counsel should check whether 1 U.S.C. § 1 supplies a default definition, which would include corporations.
16. The "in the United States" limit turns on where a person is located, but the bill does not say how to locate an entity or where a training run or deployment occurs. Cloud-hosted or remotely operated training may fall outside clear reach.

**Sec. 3(b) (grandfathering)**
17. "Training began" is undefined. A new run initialized from a pre-enactment checkpoint could be argued to be a continuation of a grandfathered run.
18. The exemption removes the entire "system" from subsection (a), which read literally covers development and deployment, not just completion of training. The bill does not say whether successor or updated versions of a grandfathered system are also exempt. This is one of the most consequential escape routes.
19. Section 8 creates a gap. A run that starts between enactment and the effective date is not grandfathered, but the prohibition is not yet in force. The bill does not say whether that run becomes prohibited on the effective date if it is still ongoing.

**Sec. 3(c) (safety evaluation)**
20. "Safety evaluation" is undefined. The exemption has no limit on scope, no registration or reporting requirement, no time limit, and no requirement that results be shared. It could cover training a covered system and deploying it to outside testers, which is the broadest escape route in the bill.
21. The exemption is purpose-based and does not require any contemporaneous record of purpose. Proving that evaluation was not the real purpose would be difficult for the government, though a court could read it narrowly.

## Section 4: Registry

22. The bill sets no deadline for registration or for the first quarterly report, and it specifies no content for registration itself.
23. Section 5 penalizes only violations of Section 3. Failure to register or to file quarterly reports carries no stated penalty, which is a significant enforcement gap.
24. The registry is facility-based, so developers of covered models who do not operate a covered facility have no registration obligation.
25. "Describing the computing workloads" is vague about granularity, whether model names or weights must be disclosed, and what counts as a "workload." The bill also has no confidentiality, trade secret, or FOIA protection, which creates disclosure risk for us. Counsel should consider an amendment on this point.
26. "Certified by the operator" sets no standard for certification and does not say who must sign. The criminal penalty in Sec. 5(b) covers only Section 3 violations, so a false certification is not clearly punishable.

## Section 5: Penalties

27. The bill does not name an enforcing agency or official, does not say whether DOJ or Commerce brings civil actions, and does not describe any administrative process. The civil penalty may be unenforceable as written, and no appropriation is provided. This is one of the strongest unworkability points.
28. Section 5(a) applies to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but Section 8 delays the prohibition until 180 days after enactment. This conflict could be read to create penalty exposure before the prohibition takes effect.
29. "Each day on which the violation occurs" is ambiguous. It is unclear whether the unit is a training run, a model, a facility, or a person, and whether continuing training counts as a new violation each day. Exposure could be read as either modest or enormous.
30. "Willfully" in Sec. 5(b) is undefined. Because covered status turns on the vague Sec. 2(1)(B) determination, it is unclear what a defendant must have known, which helps defendants.
31. The bill does not say whether officers, directors, or employees can be personally liable, or how corporate liability attaches.
32. The civil provision has no aiding-and-abetting or conspiracy clause, so infrastructure providers such as cloud hosts may not be clearly liable. Criminal liability could reach them through the general federal aiding statute, 18 U.S.C. § 2, but counsel should confirm that.

## Section 6: International agreement

33. "Shall seek to negotiate" is not binding. The bill has no consequence if no agreement is reached in 18 months, and "equivalent" is undefined. The text also does not say what happens if foreign jurisdictions do not adopt equivalent prohibitions. This is primarily a competitiveness argument, but the text is silent on it.

## Section 7: Duration

34. Section 7(a) keeps the prohibition in effect until a joint resolution finds that covered systems "can be developed safely," but Section 7(b) ends the entire Act after 10 years. The two provisions conflict, and it is unclear whether the sunset overrides the indefinite duration.
35. "Safely" is undefined, and the bill does not say whether a joint resolution must pass both chambers and be presented to the President. The National Academies report is referenced but not required, and there is no deadline. If no report is produced, the duration standard is unclear.
36. The bill has no savings clause. It is unclear whether penalties for violations before the sunset, or enforcement actions already pending, survive termination.

## Section 8: Effective date

37. The 180-day delay is not coordinated with the registry requirements in Section 4 or the penalty provision in Section 5(a), and the bill contains no transitional rules for facilities operating at enactment.

## Whole-Act issues

38. The bill has no severability clause. If a court strikes Sec. 2(1)(B) for vagueness, it is unclear whether the rest of the Act survives. This matters for litigation strategy.
39. The bill does not define "artificial intelligence model," "artificial intelligence system," "computing power," or "cognitive tasks," and these terms appear throughout the operative provisions.

## Strategic notes

- Publicly pressing a loophole may prompt sponsors to tighten it at markup. Counsel should decide which points to present as technical fixes and which to present as fatal defects.
- Several items are clearly drafting errors (conflicts between Sections 5, 7, and 8; the undefined "Secretary"; the missing enforcement authority). These are the most persuasive to committee staff because they can be fixed without changing policy.
- Relying on an exemption requires that our actual activities fit both its text and its evident purpose. Counsel should assess whether any reading we would rely on is defensible, not only whether it is available.
