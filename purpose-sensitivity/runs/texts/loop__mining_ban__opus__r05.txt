RESPONSE:
# S. 2817 (as amended Oct. 6): Loopholes, Ambiguities, and Drafting Problems

## Sec. 2: Definitions

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A), annual tonnage threshold.** Defining the banned activity as recovering *more than 10,000 metric tons per year* means any operation below that level is legal. This permits pilot mining, test mining, and slow commercial extraction. A flat prohibition on commercial recovery, or a much lower threshold for test activity, would close this gap.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "by a single vessel."** The per-vessel cap lets an operator split production across a fleet, with each ship staying under 10,000 tons. The threshold should apply per operator, project, or affiliated group, with an anti-evasion (aggregation) clause.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A), what counts as recovery "by a vessel."** Modern systems use a seabed collector, a riser, a production vessel, and separate transport ships, so it is unclear which vessel does the "recovery." Activity not conducted from a vessel at all, such as fixed platforms or seabed-based systems, may fall outside the definition entirely.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "recovery" undefined.** It is unclear whether collector trials that disturb or discharge sediment without lifting ore to the surface count. Since the seabed disturbance is the core ecological harm, the definition should cover collection, disturbance, and extraction, not just recovery to the surface.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "per year" and "depths greater than 200 meters" undefined.** "Year" could mean a calendar year, fiscal year, or rolling 12 months. "Depth" could mean water depth or depth below the seafloor. Both give defendants room to argue they fall outside the definition.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "other mineral resources" is overbroad.** Under related statutes such as OCSLA, "minerals" includes oil and gas. Read literally, this could ban deepwater oil and gas production and possibly methane hydrate or sand recovery. That would draw powerful opposition and invite a narrowing interpretation that weakens the bill. The term should be defined to mean hard minerals, such as nodules, crusts, and sulfides, and should expressly exclude hydrocarbons if that is the intent.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "seabed" doesn't clearly include subsoil.** Seafloor massive sulfide deposits are mined by cutting below the surface. The definition should say "seabed and subsoil."

8. **Sec. 2(1)(B), "the Secretary" is undefined.** The Act never says whether this means the Secretary of Commerce, Interior, or another official. This creates uncertainty about who has authority under subparagraph (B).

9. **Sec. 2(1)(B), open-ended secretarial determination.** Criminal liability under Sec. 5(b) would turn on a Secretary's discretionary finding of "comparable" risk. This invites vagueness and nondelegation challenges, provides no procedure such as notice-and-comment rulemaking, and gives a hostile administration no obligation to act. Covered activities should be stated in the statute, with any expansion done by rulemaking that operates only prospectively.

10. **Sec. 2(2), narrow "U.S. person."** The definition covers only citizens and *corporations*. It omits LLCs, partnerships, trusts, and other entities, as well as lawful permanent residents and U.S. nationals. Mining ventures commonly use LLC and partnership structures.

11. **Sec. 2(2), "organized under the laws of a State."** "State" is undefined, so entities organized in D.C., Puerto Rico, Guam, the CNMI, American Samoa, or under federal law may be excluded. That gap is notable because the CNMI and American Samoa are areas of active nodule interest.

12. **Sec. 2(2), foreign subsidiaries.** A U.S. company can conduct mining through a foreign-incorporated subsidiary it owns or controls without violating the Act. The definition should reach entities owned or controlled by U.S. persons.

13. **Sec. 2(3), covered vessel threshold and mismatch.** Vessels of 5,000 gross tons or less escape registration, and "equipped for the recovery" is unclear for multipurpose or easily refitted ships. The registry definition (Sec. 2(3)) also doesn't line up with the prohibition definition (Sec. 2(1)), so some vessels can mine without ever registering.

## Sec. 3: Prohibition

14. **Sec. 3(a), "in the United States" undefined geographically.** It is unclear whether this reaches the territorial sea, the EEZ, and the continental shelf. Without that, a foreign-flagged vessel operated by a non-U.S. company in the U.S. EEZ may not be covered. The bill should expressly cover all areas under U.S. jurisdiction and all U.S.-flagged vessels.

15. **Sec. 3(a), no ban on facilitation, sponsorship, financing, or imports.** The Act bans only "engag[ing]" in mining. U.S. persons could still finance, insure, or supply foreign operations, and the U.S. could import, process, or sell deep-sea minerals. The bill should add prohibitions on aiding and abetting, financing, and importing or trading minerals from commercial deep seabed mining.

16. **Sec. 3(a), no bar on federal permitting.** The bill neither repeals nor amends the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (DSHMRA, 30 U.S.C. 1401 et seq.) or OCSLA marine-minerals leasing. NOAA and BOEM therefore retain statutory authority to issue licenses, permits, and leases for the very activity this Act bans. Courts disfavor implied repeal, so the bill should expressly prohibit agencies from issuing new authorizations and terminate pending applications.

17. **Sec. 3(b), the existing-operations exemption is the largest loophole.** It exempts any "mining conducted under an exploration license" issued before enactment.
   - It has no time limit and does not address renewals, extensions, or transfers.
   - "An exploration license" is not limited to U.S.-issued licenses, so it arguably covers ISA exploration contracts or licenses from other states.
   - It converts exploration authorizations, which do not permit commercial recovery, into a shield for commercial mining.
   - It encourages a rush to obtain licenses before enactment.

   The fix is to strike it, or to limit it to non-extractive exploration under U.S. licenses, with a firm wind-down date.

18. **Sec. 3(c), "environmental assessment" exemption is undefined and self-declared.** It has no definition, no permit or approval requirement, no volume or area limits, and no designation of who decides an activity's "purpose." Pilot or test mining could easily be labeled environmental assessment. The exemption should be limited to permitted scientific research with strict caps on area and volume.

## Sec. 4: Registry

19. **Sec. 4(a), no scope, deadline, or "operator" definition.** The section doesn't say which vessels must register (U.S.-flagged, in U.S. waters, or operated by U.S. persons) or by when. "Operator" is also undefined.

20. **Sec. 4, no penalty for noncompliance.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3, so failing to register or report, or filing a false report, carries no penalty under this Act.

21. **Sec. 4(b), self-certification without verification.** Quarterly reports are certified only by the operator. The bill provides no content requirements, vessel monitoring or AIS mandates, inspection or boarding authority, recordkeeping, or public disclosure. As written, the registry is unlikely to generate evidence usable in enforcement.

## Sec. 5: Penalties

22. **Sec. 5(a) vs. Sec. 8, effective-date conflict.** Penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the Act takes effect 180 days after enactment. Defendants would exploit this inconsistency, and it creates retroactivity arguments. Conform both provisions to the effective date.

23. **Sec. 5(a), per-day penalty doesn't fit an annual threshold.** A violation exists only once 10,000 tons have been recovered in a year, so it is unclear which "days" a violation "occurs" on. The penalty could be limited to the days after the threshold is crossed, or be uncomputable until year's end.

24. **Sec. 5, no enforcement machinery.** The bill doesn't specify:
   - which agency enforces it;
   - whether civil penalties are assessed administratively or in court;
   - the procedures and judicial review that apply;
   - investigative, inspection, or subpoena powers;
   - injunctive relief, seizure, or forfeiture of vessels and minerals;
   - citizen suits.

   On the penalty process: after *SEC v. Jarkesy* (2024), administratively imposed civil penalties are more vulnerable to Seventh Amendment challenges. Specifying district-court enforcement is the safer option.

25. **Sec. 5(b), "willfully" standard.** Willfulness generally requires proof that the defendant knew the conduct was unlawful, which is hard to prove. Consider a "knowingly" tier, possibly with a lower maximum sentence.

## Sec. 6: International Agreement

26. **Sec. 6, ambiguous and unenforceable mandate.** "Shall seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months" could mean merely starting talks within 18 months, and it imposes no obligation to report or to achieve any result. The section also doesn't mention the International Seabed Authority. Because it requires prohibitions "equivalent to those in section 3," it would export every loophole above. Add reporting requirements to Congress and direct U.S. support for a moratorium at the ISA.

## Sec. 7: Duration

27. **Sec. 7(a) vs. 7(b), direct internal conflict.** Subsection (a) keeps the ban until Congress acts on a National Academies report. Subsection (b) ends the entire Act after 10 years regardless. The sunset effectively overrides (a), and the conflict will generate litigation about which governs. Striking (b) fully resolves this.

28. **Sec. 7(a), no study is actually commissioned and the standard is undefined.** Nothing directs or funds a National Academies report, and "serious harm" is undefined. Also, any joint resolution is simply a new law, so (a) adds little legal force. If kept, (a) should commission the study and set a scientific standard.

29. **Sec. 7(b), sunset runs from enactment, not effectiveness.** Combined with Sec. 8, the ban is effective for only about 9.5 years.

## Sec. 8: Effective Date

30. **Sec. 8, 180-day window.** Commercial mining remains legal for six months after enactment, inviting a rush to begin or expand operations. Make the prohibition effective on enactment, keeping the delay only for registry provisions if NOAA needs setup time.

## Missing Provisions

31. **No severability clause.** Given the litigation risks around Sec. 2(1)(B) and extraterritorial application, a severability clause would help keep the rest of the Act intact if one provision is struck.

32. **No rulemaking authority or anti-evasion clause.** No agency is authorized to issue implementing regulations. The bill also has no general prohibition on structuring activities to evade the Act, such as splitting operations across vessels or entities.

**Highest-priority fixes for the Whitfield memo:** #17 (grandfathering of existing licenses), #16 (DSHMRA/OCSLA permitting left intact), #27 (sunset conflict), #1–2 (tonnage and per-vessel threshold), #18 (environmental assessment exemption), #12 and #15 (subsidiaries and financing/imports), and #22 (effective-date conflict). #6 (oil and gas overbreadth) is also worth flagging early, since opponents are likely to use it against the bill.

I'd recommend having counsel review the legal points (especially #6, #16, #24) before the memo goes out.
