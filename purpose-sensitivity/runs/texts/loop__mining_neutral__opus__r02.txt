# S. 2817 Drafting Analysis: Loopholes, Ambiguities, and Enforcement Gaps

This review covers only the text you pasted (the manager's amendment of October 6, 2026). Some of these gaps may be intentional policy choices rather than oversights, so it's worth asking the sponsors' and committee offices before characterizing them in print.

---

### Sec. 2: Definitions

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "by a single vessel."** The 10,000-ton threshold applies per vessel, so an operator could split recovery across several vessels, each staying under the cap, and mine an unlimited total. Nothing aggregates tonnage by operator, company, project, or site.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A), relay operations.** "Recovery... by a single vessel" doesn't say how tonnage is attributed when one vessel's seabed collector gathers material and a second vessel lifts or stores it. Operators could structure handoffs so no single vessel is credited with crossing the threshold.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A), non-vessel methods.** The definition reaches only recovery "by a vessel." Recovery from fixed platforms, seabed installations, or shore-controlled systems falls outside subparagraph (A) entirely.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "per year."** The text doesn't say whether this means a calendar year, fiscal year, or rolling 12-month period. The choice affects when a violation occurs and lets operators time recovery to straddle periods.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(A), how tonnage is measured.** Polymetallic nodules hold substantial water, and the bill doesn't specify wet or dry tonnage, or whether sediment brought up with the ore counts. That ambiguity could easily decide whether an operation is over or under the threshold.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "recovery" is undefined.** It's unclear whether recovery means lifting material to the surface or also includes collecting, dislodging, or stockpiling it on the seabed. Operators could disturb large areas while arguing nothing was "recovered."

7. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "other mineral resources."** This undefined phrase could be read narrowly (hard minerals such as nodules, crusts, and sulfides) or broadly enough to cover sand, gravel, or even hydrocarbons, which some statutes treat as minerals. That creates overbreadth arguments and uncertainty for other offshore industries.

8. **Sec. 2(1)(A), no geographic scope.** The definition doesn't say whether it covers the U.S. exclusive economic zone and continental shelf, areas beyond national jurisdiction, foreign EEZs, or all three. Activity in shallower waters (200 meters or less) is excluded regardless of scale.

9. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "commercial" isn't an element.** The defined term says "commercial," but the definition contains no commercial-purpose requirement. That mismatch invites litigation over whether a commercial purpose must also be shown, especially for research or pilot operations.

10. **Sec. 2(1)(B), "the Secretary" is undefined.** The Act never says which Secretary (Commerce, Interior, or another) makes the determination. Meanwhile Sec. 4 assigns the registry to NOAA, so authority within the bill is internally inconsistent.

11. **Sec. 2(1)(B), delegation and vagueness.** Criminal liability under Sec. 5(b) can attach to activities the Secretary "determines" pose "comparable" risks. There is no standard, procedure, notice-and-comment requirement, or publication requirement. That invites nondelegation and void-for-vagueness challenges, and the bill grants no general rulemaking authority to implement the provision.

12. **Sec. 2(2), entity coverage.** "U.S. person" covers only citizens and "corporation[s] organized under the laws of a State." It omits LLCs, partnerships, trusts, and other non-corporate entities, as well as lawful permanent residents. Since many mining ventures are organized as LLCs, this gap matters.

13. **Sec. 2(2), foreign subsidiaries.** With no ownership or control test, a U.S. company can conduct mining through a foreign-incorporated subsidiary, which is neither a "U.S. person" nor necessarily "in the United States." This is likely the single largest structural loophole.

14. **Sec. 2(2), "State" is undefined.** The definitions don't say whether corporations organized in the District of Columbia or U.S. territories count. Without a definition, entities organized there could argue they fall outside the term.

15. **Sec. 2(3), threshold mismatch.** A "covered vessel" must exceed 5,000 gross tons, but the prohibition isn't tied to vessel size. Vessels of 5,000 tons or less escape the registry and reporting requirements even if they mine at prohibited levels.

16. **Sec. 2(3), "equipped for."** Operators could avoid covered-vessel status by installing recovery equipment only temporarily or using modular systems. The bill also doesn't say whether a covered vessel must be U.S.-flagged or have any U.S. connection.

### Sec. 3: Prohibition

17. **Sec. 3(a), "person" and "in the United States" are undefined.** "Person" is never defined. "In the United States" doesn't specify whether it includes the territorial sea, the EEZ, or the continental shelf, which determines whether foreign operators in U.S. waters are covered.

18. **Sec. 3(a), only direct conduct is prohibited.** The section bars "engag[ing] in" mining but says nothing about financing, sponsoring, contracting for, supplying equipment to, or buying minerals from prohibited operations. U.S. firms could invest in or offtake from foreign-flagged operations without violating the Act. There is also no import restriction on deep seabed minerals.

19. **Sec. 3(b), the grandfather clause.** Exempting "mining conducted under an exploration license" mixes two categories. Exploration and commercial recovery are distinct permit stages under existing law, so the clause could be read to allow full-scale commercial mining by anyone holding a pre-enactment exploration license.

20. **Sec. 3(b), licensing authority is unspecified.** The text doesn't say whether the exploration license must come from NOAA, the International Seabed Authority, or a foreign sponsoring state. A license from any jurisdiction could arguably qualify.

21. **Sec. 3(b), no time limit or transfer rule.** The exemption has no end date and doesn't address renewals, extensions, modifications, area expansions, or transfers to new owners. Grandfathered licenses could become indefinite and tradable.

22. **Sec. 3(c), environmental assessment exception.** The exemption depends only on stated "purpose." It has no definition, volume cap, approval process, oversight, or limit on later sale of the recovered material, so an operator could frame commercial-scale recovery as environmental assessment, and a purpose-based test is hard to disprove.

23. **Sec. 3, relationship to existing law.** The bill doesn't amend or reference the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act of 1980 or the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act. Agencies could still have authority to issue recovery permits under those laws, and courts disfavor finding implied repeals.

### Sec. 4: Registry

24. **Sec. 4, no penalties for noncompliance.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3, so failing to register, failing to report, or filing false reports carries no penalty under this Act. A general federal false-statements statute might apply, but the bill doesn't say so.

25. **Sec. 4(a), no deadline.** The registration requirement has no deadline and doesn't say whether it applies to foreign operators, foreign-flagged vessels, or vessels operating outside U.S. waters.

26. **Sec. 4(b), reports don't need to include tonnage.** Reports need only "describ[e] the recovery activities." They aren't required to state tonnage, location, depth, or the vessels involved, which means the reports can't verify compliance with the 10,000-ton threshold.

27. **Sec. 4(b), self-certification only.** The bill provides no independent verification, audits, observers, vessel tracking, or public disclosure. It also gives NOAA no authority to inspect, board vessels, or demand records.

### Sec. 5: Penalties

28. **Sec. 5(a), conflict with the effective date.** Civil penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but Sec. 8 says the Act takes effect 180 days after enactment. That leaves it unclear whether conduct during the 180-day window can be penalized, and the conflict invites challenge.

29. **Sec. 5(a), per-day penalty vs. annual threshold.** A violation exists only once a vessel exceeds 10,000 tons in a year, but penalties accrue "for each day." The bill doesn't say whether the violation starts on day one, on the day the threshold is crossed, or only for days after that, which makes penalty calculation contestable.

30. **Sec. 5, no enforcement machinery.** No agency is designated to enforce the Act. The bill doesn't say whether civil penalties are assessed administratively or require a lawsuit by the Justice Department. It includes no authority for investigations, subpoenas, boarding, or seizure or forfeiture of vessels and minerals, and no citizen-suit provision.

31. **Sec. 5(b), extraterritorial criminal enforcement.** The criminal provision applies to U.S. persons "anywhere" but doesn't address venue, evidence-gathering abroad, or the vagueness problems from Sec. 2(1)(B) (item 11). A "willfulness" standard combined with an open-ended agency definition makes prosecutions harder to win.

### Sec. 6: International Agreement

32. **Sec. 6, unclear deadline.** It's ambiguous whether the 18-month deadline applies to beginning negotiations or concluding them. In either case, "shall seek" creates no enforceable obligation and requires no report to Congress.

33. **Sec. 6, "equivalent prohibitions."** The bill doesn't address how an agreement would interact with the International Seabed Authority framework or the fact that the U.S. hasn't ratified UNCLOS. It also doesn't say whether any resulting agreement would be submitted as a treaty.

### Sec. 7: Duration

34. **Sec. 7(a) vs. 7(b), internal contradiction.** Subsection (a) says the prohibition lasts until Congress acts by joint resolution. Subsection (b) ends the entire Act after 10 years regardless. Which provision controls is unclear, and if (b) controls, the ban expires automatically even without a finding that mining is safe.

35. **Sec. 7(a), no operative mechanism.** The section doesn't commission or fund a National Academies report, and doesn't say what happens legally once a joint resolution passes (automatic termination, or something else). "Serious harm" is undefined. Since a joint resolution is itself legislation, the provision adds little beyond Congress's existing power to amend the law.

36. **Sec. 7(b), the sunset takes everything with it.** The registry, reporting, and penalty provisions all expire at 10 years. There is no savings clause for pending enforcement actions or violations committed before the sunset. The general federal savings statute addresses repeals, and its application to a self-executing expiration is less settled.

### Sec. 8: Effective Date

37. **Sec. 8, the 180-day window.** The delay lets companies accelerate operations or restructure (for example, moving activity into foreign subsidiaries or splitting fleets) before the ban takes effect. Meanwhile other provisions key off the enactment date (Sec. 3(b), 5(a), 6, 7(b)), creating the timing inconsistencies noted above.

### General

38. **No severability clause.** Courts generally presume severability, but given the constitutional challenges likely against Sec. 2(1)(B) and Sec. 5(b), the bill's silence leaves room to argue that invalid provisions should bring down more of the Act.

---

**Suggested framing for the explainer:** Items 1, 13, 19, 22, and 34 are probably the most consequential, since each could independently allow large-scale mining to continue or the ban to lapse. For balance, you may want to ask the sponsors whether any were deliberate, such as narrowing the scope to win votes or the grandfather clause protecting existing investments, and whether more amendments are expected at markup.
