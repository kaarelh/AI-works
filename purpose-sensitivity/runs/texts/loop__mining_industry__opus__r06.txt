# S. 2817: Loopholes, Ambiguities, and Drafting Problems

This analysis is based only on the text you provided (as amended October 6, 2026). It does not account for committee report language, other statutes beyond those noted, or later amendments.

## Section 2: Definitions

1. **§2(1)(A), "by a single vessel."** The 10,000-ton threshold applies per vessel, so an operation split across several vessels, each recovering under 10,000 tons, is arguably not "commercial deep seabed mining." Nothing aggregates tonnage by operator, project, or affiliated entities.

2. **§2(1)(A), wet vs. dry tonnage.** The bill doesn't say whether "metric tons" means wet or dry weight. Freshly recovered nodules carry substantial water, so the choice could move an operation above or below the threshold.

3. **§2(1)(A), "per year."** The bill doesn't specify a calendar year, fiscal year, or rolling 12 months. Recovery could be timed around a fixed-year boundary to stay under the cap in each period.

4. **§2(1)(A), "recovery" undefined.** It's unclear whether recovery happens when material is collected on the seabed, lifted to the surface, or landed ashore. It's also unclear whether a seabed collector or riser system counts as part of the "vessel."

5. **§2(1)(A), "at depths greater than 200 meters."** The text doesn't say whether depth means water depth at the site or depth of the resource. It also doesn't say how depth is measured, for example at what tide datum.

6. **§2(1)(A), "other mineral resources" undefined.** This phrase is potentially overbroad. Read literally, it could reach sand and gravel, hydrocarbons, or scientific sampling at depth. That supports an unworkability or overbreadth argument, though a court would likely read it in context.

7. **§2(1)(B), "the Secretary" undefined.** The bill never says which Secretary (Commerce, Interior, or another). That leaves the catch-all authority without a clear holder and creates administrative uncertainty.

8. **§2(1)(B), vague standard plus criminal penalties.** "Risks … comparable to" mining is an open-ended standard. It lets an agency define conduct that §5(b) punishes with up to 10 years in prison, which supports due-process vagueness and nondelegation arguments. Note that Touby v. United States permits delegation of crime definition under an "intelligible principle," so the nondelegation argument is weaker than the vagueness argument. This provision also cuts against you, because it lets the government expand the prohibition beyond the tonnage threshold.

9. **§2(1)(B), no procedure.** The bill provides no rulemaking requirement, notice, or process for the Secretary's determination. It's unclear whether a determination applies prospectively or to a single activity, and how it can be challenged.

10. **§2(2), "corporation organized under the laws of a State."** This omits LLCs, partnerships, and other non-corporate entities. It also omits entities organized in D.C. or the territories, unless "State" is defined elsewhere, which it isn't here. Foreign subsidiaries of U.S. companies are not "U.S. persons."

11. **§2(2), lawful permanent residents excluded.** Only citizens qualify among individuals. Green-card holders operating abroad fall outside the extraterritorial reach.

12. **§2(3), "covered vessel" thresholds.** Vessels of 5,000 gross tons or less are exempt from the registry. "Equipped for the recovery" is undefined, so it's unclear whether removable or modular gear counts. Note that "covered vessel" is used only in §4, not in the §3 prohibition.

## Section 3: Prohibition

13. **§3(a), "person" and "United States" undefined.** The bill doesn't say whether "in the United States" includes the territorial sea, the EEZ, or the continental shelf. A foreign-organized entity operating outside that area is not reached at all.

14. **§3(a), "engage in" undefined; no ancillary prohibitions.** The bill doesn't prohibit financing, sponsoring, chartering vessels to, processing, or importing nodules from non-U.S. operators. Criminal aiding-and-abetting liability under 18 U.S.C. §2 could still reach some conduct under §5(b).

15. **§3(b), "mining" under an "exploration license."** This exemption appears to allow full mining, with no tonnage limit, as long as it occurs "under" a pre-enactment exploration license. That is likely the largest loophole in the bill. However, a court may hold that mining exceeding the license's scope isn't "conducted under" it.

16. **§3(b), license issuer and source unspecified.** The bill doesn't say whether the license must be a NOAA license under the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (DSHMRA), an International Seabed Authority (ISA) contract, or a foreign-sponsored license. Read broadly, any pre-enactment exploration authorization worldwide could qualify.

17. **§3(b), no limit on renewals, transfers, or amendments.** The bill is silent on whether a license renewed, extended, transferred, or modified after enactment keeps the exemption. Read literally, the exemption has no time limit apart from the §7(b) sunset.

18. **§3(b), commercial recovery permits not addressed.** The exemption covers exploration licenses but not DSHMRA commercial recovery permits. That creates an odd result where holders of the more advanced authorization get less protection. Watch for a fix in markup.

19. **§3(b) and §8, enactment-vs.-effective-date window.** The exemption keys off the date of enactment, but the prohibition takes effect 180 days later. Activity in that window is lawful. Licenses issued during the window are not grandfathered, which is worth clarifying in your favor if pending applications exist.

20. **§3(c), "environmental assessment" undefined.** There is no tonnage cap, permit requirement, or oversight, and the exemption turns on subjective "purpose." Large-scale test mining could be characterized as assessment. Expect enforcement to challenge purposes that look pretextual.

21. **§3 overall, no conforming amendment to DSHMRA.** The bill doesn't amend or repeal 30 U.S.C. §1401 et seq., which still directs NOAA to process licenses and permits. That creates a statutory conflict. Courts disfavor implied repeal but would likely give the later, specific prohibition effect.

## Section 4: Registry

22. **§4(a), no deadline, procedure, or penalty.** No registration deadline is set. §5 penalizes only §3 violations, so failing to register or report has no penalty under the Act. False certifications could still trigger 18 U.S.C. §1001.

23. **§4(a), "operator" undefined, jurisdictional scope unclear.** The bill doesn't say whether the operator is the owner, charterer, or license holder. It also doesn't say whether the registry applies to foreign operators with no U.S. nexus.

24. **§4(b), report content unspecified, no rulemaking authority.** The bill doesn't define what "describing the recovery activities" requires (for example tonnage, location, or depth). It also gives NOAA no authority to issue regulations, set formats, or verify reports.

## Section 5: Penalties

25. **§5(a) vs. §8, effective-date conflict.** The civil penalty applies to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the Act takes effect 180 days after enactment. Because the prohibition isn't in force until then, the better reading is that no violation can occur earlier. Still, the text is internally inconsistent.

26. **§5(a), no enforcing agency or procedure.** The bill doesn't say who assesses penalties, whether there is an administrative hearing, how penalties are collected, or what the limitations period is. There are no inspection, subpoena, or seizure powers. This supports an unworkability argument, but Congress can easily fix it.

27. **§5(b), mens rea and vagueness.** "Willfully" requires knowledge that the conduct was unlawful. Combined with the ambiguities above (tonnage, depth, "comparable" activities, exemption scope), that could make criminal prosecution difficult.

## Section 6: International Agreement

28. **§6, hortatory and ambiguous deadline.** "Shall seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months" could mean begin seeking or conclude within 18 months. Either way there is no consequence for failure, and any treaty would need separate Senate advice and consent. The bill also doesn't address its relationship to ISA processes.

## Section 7: Duration

29. **§7(a) vs. §7(b), internal conflict.** Subsection (a) says the prohibition lasts until Congress acts, while (b) sunsets the entire Act after 10 years regardless. Subsection (b) likely controls, which makes (a)'s mechanism largely redundant.

30. **§7(a), no mechanism, undefined standard.** Nothing directs or funds a National Academies report, and "serious harm" is undefined. A joint resolution requires presidential signature, so it is functionally the same as ordinary repeal. The provision is legally redundant.

## Structural Gaps

31. **No severability clause.** Without one, a successful challenge to one provision (for example §2(1)(B)) raises a question of whether other provisions survive. Courts usually presume severability, though.

32. **No rulemaking authority anywhere in the Act.** No agency can issue implementing regulations to resolve the ambiguities above. That supports the argument that the bill is unworkable as drafted.

## Caveats for the Legal Team

- **Strongest points for continued operations:** items 1, 15–17, and 20 (vessel-splitting, the exploration-license exemption, and the environmental-assessment exemption). These depend heavily on how a court reads "conducted under" and "purpose," and aggressive positions may draw enforcement scrutiny.
- **Raising these at markup may get them fixed.** Several items (7, 18, 25, 26, 29) are easy technical corrections. Publicly highlighting the loopholes could lead the committee to close them. Decide which to argue as "unworkable" and which to stay quiet about.
- **Some items cut against you.** §2(1)(B) and the breadth of "other mineral resources" could expand the prohibition's reach.
- This is issue-spotting, not a legal opinion. Counsel should verify against the official text and related statutes, particularly DSHMRA and any existing licenses you hold.
