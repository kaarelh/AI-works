# Drafting Issues and Gaps in S. 2817 (Manager's Amendment, Oct. 6, 2026)

This list is based only on the text you provided. I haven't seen the prior version of the bill or the statutes it touches, so counsel should verify the cross-references, especially the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (DSHMRA).

**Definitions (§2)**

1. **§2(1)(A):** The 10,000-metric-ton threshold applies to "a single vessel," so an operation split across several vessels that each recover less may fall outside subparagraph (A). "Recovery" and "per year" are also undefined (wet or dry weight, nodules or overburden, and how the annual period is measured).
2. **§2(1)(A):** "Seabed" and "other mineral resources" are undefined. The term could be read narrowly to exclude materials such as cobalt-rich crusts or seafloor massive sulfides, or byproduct recovery.
3. **§2(1)(A):** The depth test applies only to recovery "at depths greater than 200 meters," so activity in shallower waters falls outside the definition on its face.
4. **§2(1)(B):** The Secretary's determination has no criteria, procedure, notice requirement, deadline, or stated standard of review. "Comparable" is undefined, and the cross-reference to subparagraph (A) measures risk against a per-vessel tonnage metric that may not fit non-vessel activities.
5. **Throughout:** "The Secretary" is never identified. The bill doesn't say whether it means Commerce, Interior, or State, which affects who makes determinations and who enforces.
6. **§2(2):** The "U.S. person" definition covers citizens and corporations organized under state law, but it doesn't mention LLCs, partnerships, or trusts, and it has no ownership or control test for foreign-controlled entities. Counsel should assess how the definition applies to our corporate structure.
7. **§2(3):** "Gross tons" is not defined by reference to a measurement standard, and "equipped for the recovery of seabed mineral resources" is vague about what equipment qualifies.
8. **§2(3) vs. §2(1)(A):** A vessel under 5,000 gross tons can still exceed the 10,000-ton annual threshold in §2(1)(A), but it isn't a "covered vessel," so the §4 registry and reporting requirements wouldn't reach it. The definitions don't line up.
9. **§3(b) and general:** "Exploration license" is never defined or tied to any licensing regime. It's unclear whether it covers DSHMRA licenses and permits, International Seabed Authority contracts, or foreign licenses.

**Prohibition (§3)**

10. **§3(a):** "Engage in" is undefined and doesn't clearly reach financing, contracting, equipment supply, processing, or sale of recovered minerals.
11. **§3(a):** "In the United States" is undefined. It may not extend to the Area beyond national jurisdiction, the EEZ, or the extended continental shelf, so non-U.S. persons operating in those areas may fall outside the prohibition.
12. **§3(a):** The bill contains no import or sale prohibition, so minerals recovered abroad by foreign operators could enter U.S. commerce.
13. **§3(b):** This is the broadest exemption. It exempts "mining conducted under" a pre-enactment exploration license without limiting volume, scale, duration, license area, or whether commercial-scale recovery is allowed under it. Read literally, a grandfathered exploration license could support full commercial operations.
14. **§3(b) and §8:** The grandfather clause is keyed to the date of enactment, but the prohibition takes effect 180 days later. Licenses issued during that gap are neither clearly grandfathered nor clearly subject to the prohibition. The section also doesn't require that a license stay valid, unexpired, or untransferred.
15. **§3(c):** The environmental assessment exemption doesn't define "purpose," set a cap on tonnage, require agency approval or reporting, or bar sale of recovered material. Commercial-scale collection could be characterized as assessment.

**Registry and reporting (§4)**

16. **§4(a):** "Operator" is undefined, and the section sets no registration deadline, content requirements, or fee.
17. **§4(b):** Quarterly reports have no required content (tonnage, location, depth), no verification or confidentiality rules, and no stated purpose. "Recovery activities" is undefined.
18. **§4 and §5(a):** Section 5 penalizes only violations of §3. Failing to register, failing to file reports, or filing a false certification carries no penalty under the Act itself, though general false-statement law may apply.

**Penalties (§5)**

19. **§5 (general):** No agency is designated to enforce the Act. The bill gives no investigation, subpoena, injunction, or vessel and equipment forfeiture authority, and it creates no citizen-suit mechanism. This is the most significant enforceability gap.
20. **§5(a) and §8:** The civil penalty applies to violations "occurring on or after the date of enactment," but §3 doesn't take effect until 180 days later. The two provisions conflict on when liability begins.
21. **§5(a):** "Each day" is undefined, and the section doesn't say how continuing violations are counted, whether there is an aggregate cap, whether the amount is inflation-adjusted, what factors govern assessment, or what the limitations period is.
22. **§5(b):** "Willfully" is undefined, and the section doesn't address responsible corporate officers or good-faith reliance on a grandfathered license. Both bear directly on criminal exposure.

**Duration and sunset (§7)**

23. **§7(a) vs. §7(b):** Section 7(a) keeps the prohibition in force until Congress passes a joint resolution, but §7(b) ends the entire Act 10 years after enactment. It's unclear whether the prohibition lapses at 10 years regardless of congressional action.
24. **§7(b) and §8:** The sunset runs from enactment, but the operative prohibition starts 180 days later, so the Act would have about 9.5 years of effect. The bill also has no savings clause, so pending enforcement actions may not survive the sunset.
25. **§7(a):** "Serious harm" is undefined, no deadline requires the National Academies report to be produced, and the joint resolution procedure (presentment and veto) isn't specified. This is a risk to us, since it makes the prohibition harder to lift, not an opening.

**Other provisions and omissions**

26. **§6:** "Shall seek to negotiate" creates no obligation to reach agreement, no reporting requirement, and no consequence for failure. The 18-month deadline also falls after the 180-day effective date, so the two timelines aren't coordinated.
27. **General:** The bill has no severability clause and no savings clause for existing permits. It doesn't address its relationship to DSHMRA (30 U.S.C. §1401 et seq.), international obligations such as UNCLOS and ISA rules, or whether it displaces existing federal permits.
28. **General:** The bill doesn't address foreign-flagged vessels in U.S. waters or in transit.

**Notes for the legal team**

- **Strongest markup arguments:** Items 13 (grandfather clause), 15 (assessment exemption), 19 (no enforcer), 20–21 (penalty timing and per-day counting), 23–24 (sunset conflict), and 18 (no penalty for registry violations). These are drafting defects that make the bill hard to administer.
- **Framing:** Opponents will likely argue we're exploiting ambiguity, so presenting these as clarity problems that need fixing, rather than as loopholes we intend to use, is probably the stronger position publicly.
- **Legal exposure:** Relying on any exemption in practice carries risk. Willful violations carry up to 10 years in prison, and per-day civil penalties could accumulate quickly. Counsel should confirm the reading of any exemption before operations depend on it.
