## Section 2: Definitions

1. **§2(1)(A), "single vessel" and aggregation.** The threshold applies per vessel, so activity spread across several vessels, each under 10,000 t/yr, falls outside (A). Nothing aggregates vessels under common ownership, control, or project.

2. **§2(1)(A), attribution in multi-component systems.** A nodule system typically has a seafloor collector, a riser, a surface production vessel, and shuttle vessels. The text doesn't say which "vessel" is doing the "recovery," so tonnage could be attributed to one vessel, split across several, or to a collector that may not be a "vessel" at all. Systems using no vessel (fixed or autonomous) aren't addressed.

3. **§2(1)(A), "recovery" and the tonnage measure are undefined.** The bill doesn't say whether tonnage is wet or dry weight (nodules carry substantial moisture), lifted or retained after at-sea processing, or ore or contained metal. It also doesn't say how "per year" is measured (calendar year, rolling 12 months, prorated for short campaigns), which matters for pilot and test campaigns.

4. **§2(1)(A), "other mineral resources" and the 200 m depth.** The terms are undefined and the geographic scope is unstated, so the text could reach the EEZ, extended continental shelf, and high seas alike. At 200 m, the line is shallow for something titled "deep seabed" and could arguably capture other offshore activity (sand and gravel, and possibly hydrocarbons, given OCSLA's broad "minerals" definition). That is a useful unworkability argument and could widen the coalition of opposition.

5. **§2(1)(B), "the Secretary" is never defined.** Section 2 doesn't say which Secretary (Commerce? Interior? State?), and the only other uses are §6 (explicitly State) and §4 (NOAA). Who makes the determination, and under what authority, is open.

6. **§2(1)(B), standardless delegation.** "Risks … comparable" has no metric, no required findings, no notice, comment, or hearing, and no judicial review provision. Because §3 incorporates it and §5(b) attaches criminal penalties, it raises fair-notice, vagueness, rule-of-lenity, and nondelegation arguments.

7. **§2(1)(B), caution on items 1–3.** This clause lets the Secretary sweep in below-threshold activity, which weakens any loophole that depends on the (A) threshold. Counsel should weigh that before relying on those items.

8. **§2(2), foreign subsidiaries.** "U.S. person" covers only citizens and corporations organized under state law, with no ownership or control test. A foreign-incorporated subsidiary or joint venture is outside the definition, which is the most obvious structural gap.

9. **§2(2), entity types and jurisdictions.** The definition omits partnerships, LLCs (arguably not "corporations"), trusts, and associations. It also omits entities organized under federal, D.C., or territorial law, and "State" isn't defined, so it's unclear whether D.C. and the territories count. It also omits lawful permanent residents.

10. **§2(3), "covered vessel" threshold.** The 5,000 "gross tons" cutoff doesn't track the 10,000 t/yr recovery threshold, so a smaller vessel can be a prohibited miner but not a registrant. "Gross tons" is also imprecise (gross tonnage is a volumetric measure under the 1969 Tonnage Convention).

11. **§2(3), "equipped for the recovery of seabed mineral resources."** The phrase doesn't say whether temporary or modular equipment counts. Read literally, it could also pull in dredgers and other non-mining vessels.

## Section 3: Prohibition

12. **§3(a), "in the United States" is undefined.** It's unclear whether this includes the territorial sea, EEZ, or OCS, and a vessel at sea isn't obviously "in the United States." It's also unclear whether a Houston-based parent directing a foreign operation is "engag[ing]" in the United States.

13. **§3(a), "engage in" is narrow.** The bill doesn't prohibit financing, equipment supply, vessel leasing, offtake, onshore processing, or import of nodules mined by non-U.S. persons abroad. U.S. processing of foreign-mined material appears untouched.

14. **§3(a), no reconciliation with existing law.** The bill doesn't amend or reference DSHMRA (30 U.S.C. §1401 et seq.) or OCSLA, which already govern exploration licenses and commercial recovery permits, so conflicting regimes would coexist. Applying the ban to U.S. persons "anywhere" also invites freedom-of-the-high-seas objections.

15. **§3(b), "mining conducted under an exploration license."** Exploration licenses typically don't authorize commercial recovery, and the clause doesn't mention commercial recovery permits at all. The text is ambiguous about what is grandfathered, and holders of pre-enactment permits who aren't clearly covered would have strong takings and reliance arguments.

16. **§3(b), "an exploration license" has no stated issuer.** Read literally, a license issued by a foreign sponsoring state could qualify, and a U.S. person holding one might be exempt.

17. **§3(b), transitions are unaddressed.** There is no treatment of renewals, extensions, amendments to the licensed area, transfers or assignments, or applications pending at enactment. The hard cutoff at "date of enactment" invites a rush of issuance and disputes over timing.

18. **§3(b), no conditions or limits.** The exemption has no requirement of compliance with license terms, no scale cap, and no end date other than the §7(b) sunset.

19. **§3(c), the environmental assessment exemption is purpose-based and unbounded.** There is no definition of "environmental assessment," no volume or duration limit, no agency approval, and no reporting requirement. Dual-purpose test mining could plausibly be labeled assessment, and the subjective-purpose test makes it hard to enforce.

20. **§3 generally, no exemptions or transition rules.** The bill has no carve-out for scientific research, cable laying, or incidental recovery, and no wind-down period for operations or vessels in progress.

## Section 4: Registry

21. **§4, no enforcement.** §5 penalizes only violations of §3, so failing to register or report carries no stated penalty.

22. **§4(a), no implementing authority.** It sets no registration deadline, procedure, or fee, grants no rulemaking power, and doesn't define "operator" or say what NOAA does with a registration.

23. **§4(a), no jurisdictional nexus.** The mandate applies to "each covered vessel" regardless of flag or location, which is unenforceable against foreign-flag vessels outside U.S. waters.

24. **§4(b), reporting gaps.** "Recovery activities" is undefined, the first due date is unspecified, there is no standard for the operator's certification, and nothing protects proprietary data from disclosure.

## Section 5: Penalties

25. **§5(a) conflicts with §8.** Civil penalties run for violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the Act doesn't take effect until 180 days later. The result is a retroactivity and fair-notice problem, and an internal contradiction about conduct in the gap.

26. **§5(a), no enforcement mechanism.** No agency is designated to assess or collect penalties, and there is no notice, hearing, or judicial enforcement procedure, no assessment factors, and no inflation adjustment or limitations period (default is 28 U.S.C. §2462).

27. **§5(a), $1,000,000 per day.** With no gradation, cap, or mitigation factors, it is open to an Eighth Amendment excessive-fines challenge, particularly for continuing operations.

28. **§5(b), "willfully" is undefined.** It's unclear whether it requires knowledge of the unlawfulness or only of the act. That is especially problematic when "commercial deep seabed mining" can turn on an after-the-fact §2(1)(B) determination.

29. **§5(b), inconsistent with §5(a) and disproportionate.** The criminal provision lacks the "on or after enactment" language, so the two penalty tracks begin at different times. Up to 10 years' imprisonment for what is essentially a regulatory violation also invites proportionality criticism.

## Section 6: International Agreement

30. **§6, "shall seek to negotiate" has no consequence.** There is no consequence if talks fail by 18 months, and "equivalent" prohibitions is vague (including whether the §3(b)–(c) exemptions must be mirrored). The text doesn't say whether the agreement is a treaty or executive agreement, or how it relates to UNCLOS and the ISA regime, to which the U.S. is not a party.

31. **§6 vs. §7.** Negotiating a permanent international prohibition makes little sense for a domestic law that sunsets in 10 years.

## Section 7: Duration

32. **§7(a) conflicts with §7(b).** Subsection (a) makes the ban indefinite until Congress acts, while (b) ends the whole Act 10 years after enactment. The two provisions can't both govern.

33. **§7(a), the release condition is practically unreachable.** There is no requirement or deadline for the NASEM report, no funding or scope, and no expedited procedure, so a joint resolution faces the full filibuster and veto process. "Serious harm" is undefined, and "without serious harm" reads as a near-zero-risk standard.

34. **§7(b), no savings clause.** The text doesn't say whether pending or past violations can be enforced after sunset. The sunset is also measured from enactment, not the effective date, so the ban operates for about 9.5 years.

## Section 8 and General

35. **§8, the 180-day gap is unaddressed.** The text doesn't say whether agencies may issue or process permits during the gap, how pending applications are treated, or what happens to activities started before the effective date. Together with items 17 and 25, this is the most fertile area for timing disputes.

36. **General, no severability clause.** Courts presume severability absent one, so this helps little, but it's worth noting if §2(1)(B) is challenged.

## Triage and strategy

- **Strongest operational points** (subject to counsel's view): §3(b) scope and issuer (15–18), §3(c) (19), §2(2) (8–9), §2(1)(A) aggregation and attribution (1–3), and the §3(a) gaps (12–13).
- **Strongest "unworkable" arguments:** §7(a)/(b) conflict (32), §5(a)/§8 conflict (25), §2(1)(B) (5–6), no enforcing agency (26), toothless §4 (21), and the sweep into other offshore industries (4).
- **Caution on strategy:** Raising loopholes publicly at markup may prompt the sponsors to close them, whereas the drafting inconsistencies are safer to highlight. Relying on structuring around the text (foreign subsidiaries, assessment-labeled activity) also carries enforcement and reputational risk, so I'd have counsel decide which items go to the Hill and which stay internal.
