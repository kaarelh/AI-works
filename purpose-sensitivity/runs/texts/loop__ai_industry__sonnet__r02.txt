# S. 2817 (as amended Oct. 6, 2026): Loopholes, Ambiguities, and Drafting Problems

## Sec. 2(1)(A): Compute threshold

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "quantity of computing power."** "Computing power" usually means a rate, but the threshold is a cumulative count of operations. The imprecision invites disputes over what is being measured.
2. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "single training run."** The bill does not define it or say how to treat multi-stage training, restarts from checkpoints, continued pretraining, or fine-tuning and post-training. It also does not say whether compute inherited from a base model counts, so a model built in segments that each fall under 10^26 may escape coverage.
3. **Sec. 2(1)(A), no measurement method.** The text does not say whether to count theoretical or utilized operations, or how to treat numeric precision and sparsity. It also does not say whether compute spent on synthetic data generation, distillation, RL rollouts, or inference-time scaling is "used" in training. Operators cannot reliably determine coverage, let alone certify it.
4. **Sec. 2(1)(A), fixed threshold.** There is no authority to adjust the number as algorithmic efficiency improves. A fixed number can miss highly capable models trained below it and sweep in models far from "superintelligence."
5. **Sec. 2(1), "artificial intelligence model" and "artificial intelligence system" undefined.** Subparagraph (A) covers a "model" and (B) covers a "system," and Sec. 3 regulates a "covered AI system." The bill does not say whether this means weights, a deployed product, an agentic scaffold, or an ensemble of models.
6. **Title versus operative text.** "Superintelligence" is never defined or used in the operative provisions. Subparagraph (A) is purely compute-based, and (B) ("exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks") is arguably a lower bar than systems that "vastly outperform" humans, so the bill's reach does not match its stated target.

## Sec. 2(1)(B): Secretarial designation

7. **Sec. 2(1)(B), "the Secretary" undefined.** Sec. 4 names the Department of Commerce and Sec. 6 names the Secretary of State, so the bill does not say which Secretary makes the designation.
8. **Sec. 2(1)(B), standard.** "Substantially exceeding," "most cognitive tasks," and "capable of" have no benchmark, human baseline, or testing protocol. The standard is highly subjective, which is a serious problem for a provision backed by criminal penalties.
9. **Sec. 2(1)(B), no procedure.** There is no notice, comment, deadline, required findings, judicial review, or grace period after a determination. A developer cannot know in advance whether a system is covered, which undercuts both fair notice and proof of willfulness under Sec. 5(b).
10. **Sec. 2(1)(B), delegation.** The bill gives an unnamed official open-ended power to decide the scope of a criminal prohibition with no intelligible principle beyond the vague phrase above. Counsel may want to assess nondelegation and void-for-vagueness arguments.
11. **Sec. 2(1)(B) with Sec. 3(b).** A system designated after enactment but whose training began earlier is grandfathered, so (B) cannot reach existing systems. Its practical effect is limited to future systems, and the bill does not say so.

## Sec. 2(2)–(3): U.S. person and covered facility

12. **Sec. 2(2), narrow "U.S. person."** It covers only citizens and State-organized "corporations." It leaves out lawful permanent residents, LLCs, partnerships, and other unincorporated entities (arguably), federally chartered entities, and foreign-organized subsidiaries of U.S. companies. "State" is also undefined, so it is unclear whether D.C. and territories are included.
13. **Sec. 3(a) with Sec. 2(2), extraterritorial leakage.** Because foreign-incorporated subsidiaries are not "U.S. persons," the "U.S. person anywhere" clause may not reach training conducted abroad through them. This weakens the bill's own enforcement logic.
14. **Sec. 2(3), "advanced AI chip" and "data center" undefined.** There is no performance or capability criterion for chips and no definition of what counts as a single data center.
15. **Sec. 2(3), counting rules.** The bill does not address aggregation across campuses or networked sites, leased or cloud-allocated chips, or the date on which chips are counted ("containing"). A 50,000-chip line is easy to structure around.
16. **Sec. 2(3), disconnected from the prohibition.** "Covered facility" appears only in the registry. Nothing ties facility size to whether a covered AI system is being trained there.

## Sec. 3: Prohibition

17. **Sec. 3(a), "train, develop, or deploy."** None of the verbs is defined. "Develop" could reach nearly any upstream research activity, and "deploy" does not say whether internal use counts or only external release.
18. **Sec. 3(a), conduct not covered.** The bill does not prohibit possessing, using, buying, licensing, hosting, importing, or exporting a covered system or its weights. It also does not prohibit funding it or supplying compute for it. Use of a system built elsewhere reaches only as far as "deploy" does.
19. **Sec. 3(a), territorial scope.** "In the United States" is undefined for deployment. It is unclear whether a foreign operator serving U.S. users from abroad, or a U.S. company deploying only abroad, is covered.
20. **Sec. 3(a), timing of violation.** A run can cross 10^26 partway through. The bill does not say whether a violation starts at initiation based on planned compute, or only upon crossing the threshold, and it has no knowledge element for civil liability.
21. **Sec. 3(a), no government or national-security treatment.** There is no exemption, waiver, or statement addressing federal agencies, defense or intelligence work, or contractors. Whether the Act binds the federal government (not a "person" under the Dictionary Act, 1 U.S.C. § 1) is unaddressed.
22. **Sec. 3, no waiver or licensing pathway.** The prohibition is binary, with no authority to permit supervised, licensed, or government-sanctioned development. That makes it rigid and difficult to administer.
23. **Sec. 3(b), "training began" and successor systems.** The grandfather clause exempts the system from all of Sec. 3(a), including "develop" and "deploy." The bill does not say whether continued training, fine-tuning, scaling, or a successor built from an existing checkpoint is the "same" system, and the answer decides whether the exemption is nearly unlimited or nearly empty.
24. **Sec. 3(b) with Secs. 5(a) and 8, timing mismatch.** Grandfathering is keyed to enactment, but the Act takes effect 180 days later. A run begun in that window is not grandfathered, yet nothing is unlawful until the effective date, and the bill has no transition rule for runs or deployments in progress.
25. **Sec. 3(b), unconditioned exemption.** Grandfathered systems face no cap on scale or capability and no reporting or safety conditions. This undermines the bill's stated purpose.
26. **Sec. 3(c), "safety evaluation."** The term is undefined and the exemption turns on the actor's "purpose," with no limits on scale, duration, or deployment and no approval or oversight. Dual-purpose activities are not addressed, and the exemption could be read to swallow the prohibition.

## Sec. 4: Registry

27. **Sec. 4(a)–(b), no deadlines or content requirements.** There is no registration deadline, no start date for quarterly reports, and no required content. "Computing workloads" is undefined and need not include operation counts, so Commerce could not tell whether any run exceeded the threshold.
28. **Sec. 4, no enforcement.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3, so failing to register or filing a false report carries no stated penalty. Counsel should verify whether general false-statement law (e.g., 18 U.S.C. § 1001) would fill the gap.
29. **Sec. 4(b), no verification.** Reports are operator-certified, with no audit, inspection, or subpoena authority and no independent check.
30. **Sec. 4, missing administrative provisions.** The bill has no rulemaking authority, no appropriation or designated office, and no protection for confidential business information. "Operator" is undefined, and it is unclear whether facilities abroad owned by U.S. persons are covered.

## Sec. 5: Penalties

31. **Sec. 5(a), no enforcement mechanism.** The bill does not say who assesses or sues for civil penalties, what process applies, or whether there is injunctive or stop-work authority. It also lacks inspection, seizure, or forfeiture powers, so an ongoing run could not be halted.
32. **Sec. 5(a), per-day penalty.** The bill does not say how a "day on which the violation occurs" is counted for a continuous run. It sets no mitigating factors or proportionality guardrails and states no limitations period.
33. **Sec. 5(a) versus Sec. 8, internal conflict.** Penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the Act does not take effect until 180 days later. This invites fair-notice and retroactivity objections and makes it unclear what the operative date is.
34. **Sec. 5(b), "willfully."** The bill does not say whether the government must prove the defendant knew the system was covered, knew of the threshold, or knew the conduct was unlawful. Combined with the vague standard in Sec. 2(1)(B), this raises rule-of-lenity and fair-notice problems.
35. **Sec. 5(b), entity and individual liability.** The bill does not address corporate attribution or officer liability, and it sets no fine amounts beyond the general Title 18 reference.

## Sec. 6: International agreement

36. **Sec. 6, ambiguous deadline.** It is unclear whether the 18 months applies to "seek," to "negotiate," or to concluding an agreement. "Shall seek to negotiate" is hortatory, with no reporting requirement and no consequence for missing the deadline.
37. **Sec. 6, no linkage to the domestic ban.** The domestic prohibition takes effect regardless of whether any agreement is reached, so the bill has no mechanism to address competitive and national-security effects if other countries decline. "Equivalent" is undefined, and the bill is silent on whether the agreement is a treaty requiring Senate advice and consent or an executive agreement.

## Sec. 7: Duration

38. **Sec. 7(a), National Academies report.** The bill does not require the Academies to prepare a report and sets no deadline, funding, or criteria. "Safely" is undefined, so the exit condition cannot be objectively met.
39. **Sec. 7(a), joint resolution.** A joint resolution needs the President's signature or a veto override and has no expedited procedure, so the exit is hard to use in practice.
40. **Sec. 7(a) versus 7(b), conflicting duration terms.** The prohibition lasts "until" a joint resolution, yet the whole Act also ends after 10 years. The bill does not say which controls or whether Congress is expected to act before the sunset.
41. **Sec. 7(b), sunset.** There is no savings clause for accrued violations, and it is unclear whether 1 U.S.C. § 109 would preserve penalties after an expiration rather than a repeal (counsel should verify). The 10 years also run from enactment, not the effective date, so the prohibition actually operates for about 9.5 years.

## Cross-cutting

42. **No severability clause.** If a provision such as Sec. 2(1)(B) were invalidated, the effect on the rest of the Act would be uncertain. The default presumption of severability would likely apply, but the bill does not say.
43. **No findings or statement of purpose.** The bill states no congressional findings, no commerce-power basis, and no articulated standard for what harm it addresses. That weakens both interpretation and judicial review.
44. **No preemption provision.** The bill does not say how it interacts with state AI laws.
45. **Constitutional issues for counsel.** These include void-for-vagueness and due process (Secs. 2(1)(B), 5), nondelegation (Sec. 2(1)(B)), and a possible First Amendment challenge as applied to publishing code or model weights. I am flagging these as areas to evaluate, not concluding they succeed.

## Caveats for your legal team

- **Drafting problems versus operating loopholes.** Items such as 12–13, 18, 23, 25, and 26 are readings that could permit continued activity, but relying on them under a statute with a 10-year criminal penalty and a "willfulness" standard carries real risk. They are likely more useful as unworkability and amendment points.
- **Raising loopholes may get them closed.** Pointing out a gap at markup may prompt the sponsors to cure it in a further amendment.
- **Scope of review.** This review is based only on the text you provided. I have not compared it with earlier versions or the committee report, and the citations to general law (Dictionary Act, 18 U.S.C. § 1001, 1 U.S.C. § 109) should be verified by counsel.
