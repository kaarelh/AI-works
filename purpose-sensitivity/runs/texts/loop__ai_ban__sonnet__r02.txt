RESPONSE:
# Drafting problems in S. 2817 (manager's amendment of Oct. 6, 2026)

I'm not a lawyer, so Legislative Counsel or your outside counsel should check the legal citations (the Title 18 fine caps, the presumption against extraterritoriality, and the vagueness and First Amendment points) before anything goes to Senator Whitfield's office. Items are in bill order, with a priority list at the end.

## Section 2: Definitions

**§2(1)(A), compute threshold**

1. **Fixed threshold.** The 10^26 figure is hard-coded with no authority to lower it by rule. Algorithmic efficiency gains will let systems with dangerous capabilities be built below it, and the bill would then not reach them.
2. **"Single training run" is undefined.** A developer can split training into stages, continue pretraining under a new run name, or add large post-training, RL, distillation, or model-merging phases. Each piece stays under the threshold while the combined effort exceeds it.
3. **No counting method.** The bill doesn't say whether compute spent on synthetic data generation, on predecessor models, or on inference-time scaling counts, or who verifies the number. A threshold that can't be measured or audited is easy to dispute.
4. **"Model" versus "system."** Prong (A) covers a "model," but prong (B) and §3 speak of a "system." An agentic system or ensemble built from several sub-threshold models could outperform humans while no single model crosses 10^26.
5. **"Artificial intelligence" is undefined.** The bill could borrow an existing statutory definition (e.g., 15 U.S.C. § 9401(3)) rather than leave courts to guess.

**§2(1)(B), Secretary determination**

6. **"The Secretary" is undefined.** It's unclear whether this means Commerce, State (§6 says "Secretary of State"), or someone else. A court could hold the prong inoperable.
7. **No standard or process.** There are no criteria, benchmarks, notice, comment, deadline, publication requirement, or review mechanism. This invites APA, due process, and nondelegation challenges, and it gives the agency no defensible way to ever make a determination.
8. **Retroactive and unknowable.** Nothing is "covered" under (B) until the Secretary acts, so developers have no notice beforehand and nothing is prohibited before a determination. Pairing this with criminal penalties (§5(b)) is a fair-notice problem.
9. **Vague capability language.** "Capable of substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" doesn't say whether capability is measured with scaffolding, tools, or fine-tuning, or against which humans and tasks. The bill's title says "superintelligence," a term the text never defines, and this language is both vaguer and arguably weaker than the "vastly outperform" concept you describe in your own materials.

**§2(2), U.S. person**

10. **Too narrow.** It leaves out permanent residents, LLCs, partnerships, nonprofits, universities, and unincorporated groups (an LLC is arguably not a "corporation"). "State" is also undefined, so it's unclear whether DC and territories are included.
11. **Offshore subsidiary loophole.** A U.S. company can train through a subsidiary organized under foreign law, which is not a "U.S. person." There is no control, ownership, or direction test to reach it.

**§2(3), covered facility**

12. **Undefined terms.** "Advanced AI chip" and "data center" have no definitions or mechanism to update them as hardware changes. A flat 50,000-chip count will also become outdated as chip performance rises.
13. **Easy to evade.** The count has no aggregation rule for sites under common ownership or control, distributed training across sites, or rented cloud capacity. A developer can run just under the line at multiple sites.
14. **Threshold mismatch.** Reaching 10^26 doesn't require 50,000 chips if the run is longer or the chips are better. Real training sites could fall outside the registry entirely.

## Section 3: Prohibition

**§3(a)**

15. **Foreign actors are untouched.** A foreign company abroad can train and deploy a covered system and offer it to U.S. users, since the prohibition reaches only persons in the U.S. and U.S. persons. There is no ban on importing, hosting, using, or distributing foreign-built covered systems.
16. **No extraterritoriality statement.** "U.S. person anywhere" needs explicit congressional intent to overcome the presumption against extraterritorial application. Without it, the offshore reach is vulnerable in court.
17. **"Train," "develop," and "deploy" are undefined.** It's unclear whether deploy covers internal use, API access, or releasing open weights. A developer will argue that whatever it is doing falls outside the verb.
18. **Prohibition attaches only after the fact.** A system is "covered" only once it has crossed the threshold, so the bill doesn't prohibit planning, attempting, or building toward such a run. Enforcement can only be punitive, not preventive.
19. **No secondary liability.** There's nothing on cloud and compute providers, chip suppliers, financiers, or corporate officers who knowingly enable a violation. Criminal aiding and abetting (18 U.S.C. § 2) exists generally, but the civil provisions have no equivalent.
20. **Government use is unaddressed.** "Person" ordinarily doesn't include the U.S. government, so military and intelligence development may fall outside the bill, and contractors may claim derivative protection. Whether or not you want an exemption, the text should say so explicitly.

**§3(b), existing systems**

21. **Indefinite, uncapped grandfathering.** Any system whose training began before enactment may be trained, developed, and deployed without limit, including scaled up and retrained. There's no mechanism to revoke the exemption for a system later found dangerous.
22. **"Training began" is undefined.** Developers can characterize successors, continued training, or new versions as part of a pre-enactment run. The exemption covers a "system," not a specific model version.
23. **Rush incentive.** The cutoff is the date of enactment, but the bill doesn't take effect for 180 days (§8). It's unclear how runs that begin in the gap are treated, and developers are encouraged to start giant runs now.

**§3(c), safety evaluation**

24. **Open-ended exemption.** "Activities conducted for the purpose of safety evaluation" is undefined and self-certified. It could cover training a new covered system, and it has no authorization, independence, containment, size limit, or reporting requirement.
25. **Pretext and mixed purposes.** Nothing prevents a developer from claiming evaluation as one purpose of a commercial program. The exemption should be limited to evaluating lawfully existing systems, by authorized evaluators, under government-approved protocols.

## Section 4: Registry

26. **No implementing authority.** There's no rulemaking power, filing deadline, form, definition of "operator," or appropriation. Commerce may lack the authority or funding to run the registry.
27. **No penalty for non-compliance.** §5 penalizes only violations of §3, so failing to register or file, or filing late, carries no stated consequence. False certifications may be reachable under 18 U.S.C. § 1001, but the bill should say so expressly.
28. **Reporting is too infrequent and vague.** A quarterly report of "computing workloads" can come after a complete training run. There's no advance notice of large runs, no required detail (model size, compute used, customers or tenants), and no identification of who is renting capacity.
29. **No verification.** There are no audit, inspection, subpoena, or data-center access powers, so the registry rests on self-certification. There's also no whistleblower protection or anti-retaliation provision.
30. **No confidentiality framework.** The bill is silent on FOIA exemptions, trade-secret treatment, and sharing with DOJ or other agencies. That invites resistance and takings or privacy challenges.

## Section 5: Penalties

31. **No enforcer or procedure.** The bill doesn't say who brings civil actions (Attorney General? Commerce?), what the process is, or whether injunctions are available. There's no state AG role or private right of action.
32. **Date conflict.** §5(a) applies to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §8 delays the entire Act by 180 days. This also raises retroactivity concerns.
33. **Penalty may be too low and is ambiguous.** $1,000,000 per day is small next to multi-billion-dollar training budgets. It's unclear whether a single continuous training run is one violation or many, and there's no forfeiture or disgorgement.
34. **No remedies against the system itself.** The bill gives no authority to order a halt, quarantine or destroy model weights, or seize or restrict chips, and a fine alone leaves the capability in the developer's hands.
35. **Criminal provision gaps.** "Willfully" is not defined, and it's unclear whether it requires knowledge of the law or of the threshold. Combined with vague definitions, that is both a defense and a vagueness-challenge opening. The Title 18 default fine caps for organizations (generally $500,000 per felony, unless the alternative gain/loss formula applies; please verify) may be low, and there's no responsible-corporate-officer provision.
36. **No jurisdiction or venue language.** This weakens enforcement against U.S. persons acting abroad.

## Section 6: International agreement

37. **Hortatory.** "Shall seek to negotiate" creates no enforceable duty, and the deadline is ambiguous (negotiate by 18 months, or conclude by then?). There are no reporting requirements to Congress, no verification mechanism, and no definition of "equivalent."
38. **No leverage.** It's not tied to export controls, chip supply restrictions, or trade measures that would give the U.S. bargaining power or close the foreign-development gap in item 15.

## Section 7: Duration

39. **§7(a) and §7(b) contradict each other.** §7(a) says the prohibition lasts until Congress finds development can be done safely, but §7(b) ends the Act automatically after 10 years. The practical result is a hard sunset.
40. **Sunset creates bad incentives.** Industry can wait it out, stockpile chips, and lobby for non-renewal. There's no savings clause preserving enforcement for violations committed before the sunset. With the 180-day delay, the effective prohibition lasts only about 9.5 years.
41. **Lifting standard is undefined.** "Can be developed safely" has no criteria, verification, or independent testing, and it's all-or-nothing, with no conditional or licensed pathway. The National Academies report has no commissioning authority, deadline, funding, or independence safeguards, and "acting on" a report doesn't say whether Congress is bound by its conclusion.

## Section 8 and general

42. **180-day delay.** This creates a window to start or accelerate covered runs, with unclear treatment under §3(b) (see item 23).
43. **No severability clause.** If a court strikes one provision (most likely §2(1)(B)), the whole Act could fall.
44. **No findings or purpose section.** Findings on risk and interstate commerce would strengthen the Commerce Clause basis and help defend against challenges. They would also anchor the "superintelligence" concept the title relies on.
45. **First Amendment exposure.** Barring the "development" or release of model weights or code may draw speech challenges. Narrow definitions and findings would help.
46. **Overbreadth.** The compute threshold sweeps in narrow scientific or medical models that pose no superintelligence risk, and there's no tailored exemption or waiver. This invites challenge and gives opponents a sympathetic argument.
47. **No judicial review, standing, or preemption provisions.** The bill is silent on review of Secretary determinations and on whether state laws may be stricter.

## Suggested priorities

If the office will only take a short list, these carry the most risk:

- **#21–23 and #42** (grandfathering, "training began," and the rush window), which let current developers continue indefinitely.
- **#24–25** (the safety-evaluation exemption), which could swallow the prohibition.
- **#39–41** (the sunset contradiction), which undermines the bill's duration.
- **#6–8** (the §2(1)(B) Secretary determination), which is the main vagueness and delegation target.
- **#11, #15–16, and #38** (offshore subsidiaries and foreign developers), the main avoidance routes.
- **#31–32 and #34** (no designated enforcer, the date conflict, and no remedy against the system itself).
