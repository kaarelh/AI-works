# S. 2817 Issues for Senator Whitfield's Office

Issues are listed in section order. Items marked **[High]** are the ones most likely to let frontier development continue or to sink the bill in court. A short priority list is at the end.

---

### Sec. 2: Definitions

1. **[High] Sec. 2(1)(A): "single training run" is easy to evade.** A developer could split training into several runs below 10^26, each continuing from the previous checkpoint, or add compute through fine-tuning, continued pretraining, RL post-training, or distillation from a larger model. The threshold should count cumulative compute across all training that contributes to a model's weights, including predecessor models it was derived from.

2. **[High] Sec. 2(1)(A): a fixed compute threshold weakens over time.** Algorithmic efficiency gains steadily reduce the compute needed to reach a given capability, so dangerous systems may eventually be trained below 10^26. The bill should give an agency authority to lower the threshold by rule, with no authority to raise it.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A): no counting method.** The bill does not say how operations are counted. Examples include mixed-precision or sparse operations, synthetic-data generation, and compute used by other models in the training loop. Without a method, compute estimates will be disputed in every enforcement action. Commerce should be directed to issue a binding counting methodology.

4. **[High] Sec. 2(1)(B): "the Secretary" is never identified.** No section defines which Secretary makes determinations, which creates an obvious ambiguity and an easy litigation argument. Specify the Secretary of Commerce, or whichever official is intended, and define the term in Sec. 2.

5. **[High] Sec. 2(1)(B): the capability standard is vague and invites a vagueness challenge.** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no metric. Because Sec. 5(b) attaches up to 10 years' imprisonment, defendants will argue void-for-vagueness. The prong needs either objective criteria or a rulemaking process that produces them.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B): the capability prong only triggers after the fact.** A system becomes covered only once the Secretary has "determined" its capabilities, which may happen only after it has been built. There is also no procedure for making determinations, giving notice, or reviewing them, which invites APA and due-process challenges. Consider covering systems "reasonably expected" to meet the standard, and setting out a determination procedure.

7. **Sec. 2(1): "model" in (A) does not match "system" in (B), and "artificial intelligence" is undefined.** A developer could argue that a scaffolded or multi-model system built from sub-threshold components is not a "model trained using" more than 10^26 operations. Define "artificial intelligence system" and make clear that systems combining models are covered.

8. **[High] Sec. 2(2): "U.S. person" is too narrow.** The definition omits lawful permanent residents, LLCs, partnerships, and entities organized under federal, D.C., or territorial law. It also omits foreign subsidiaries controlled by U.S. companies. A U.S. lab could therefore do prohibited work abroad through a foreign affiliate, or through green-card-holding staff working overseas. Consider a definition modeled on the export-control or sanctions regimes (e.g., 50 U.S.C. 4801 or the OFAC definitions), plus foreign entities owned or controlled by U.S. persons.

9. **Sec. 2(3): "advanced AI chips" and "data center" are undefined.** Without technical specifications, operators can dispute whether their hardware counts.

10. **[High] Sec. 2(3): a chip-count threshold can be avoided.** Spreading hardware across several sites with fewer than 50,000 chips each avoids registration, even if the sites are networked for distributed training. A raw chip count also ignores large differences in per-chip performance. Set the threshold by aggregate compute capacity, and aggregate facilities under common ownership or control or that are networked together.

### Sec. 3: Prohibition

11. **Sec. 3(a): "develop" and "deploy" are undefined.** "Develop" could be read so broadly that it reaches ordinary research, which creates First Amendment and vagueness exposure. It could also be read so narrowly that it adds nothing to "train." It is also unclear whether offering an API to U.S. users from servers abroad counts as deploying "in the United States."

12. **[High] Sec. 3(a): enabling conduct is not prohibited.** The bill does not bar a U.S. cloud provider from supplying compute to a foreign entity training a covered system abroad, since the provider is not itself "training." It also does not bar transferring, publishing, or possessing covered model weights, or knowingly providing chips, funding, or personnel to prohibited training. Add prohibitions on providing compute or material support, plus an anti-structuring or anti-circumvention clause.

13. **[High] Sec. 3(b): the grandfather clause lets existing systems keep improving.** Exempting any system "whose training began before" enactment covers all current frontier models, apparently permanently. It may also cover continued training of those models indefinitely, because the run "began" earlier. At a minimum, bar further training of grandfathered systems. Better, also restrict new deployment of them or subject them to review.

14. **[High] Sec. 3(b): the cutoff creates a race to start training runs.** Keying the exemption to enactment rewards labs that launch large runs while the bill is pending, possibly runs that are only nominally "begun." Use the date of introduction or a fixed past date, and define when training "begins."

15. **[High] Sec. 3(c): the safety-evaluation exception has no limits.** Anyone can claim a safety-evaluation "purpose." Nothing limits the exception to evaluating existing systems as opposed to training new ones, and nothing requires approval, oversight, or a scale cap. As written it could swallow the prohibition. Limit it to evaluating, not training, systems that are already lawful, under a license or with notice to the agency.

### Sec. 4: Registry

16. **Sec. 4(a): there is no registration deadline, and "operator" is undefined.** It is unclear whether "operator" means the owner, the lessee, or the cloud tenant. Set a deadline, such as 30 days after the effective date or after the facility first meets the threshold.

17. **[High] Secs. 4 and 5: failing to register or report carries no penalty.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3, so the registry is unenforceable. Extend penalties to Sec. 4 and expressly apply false-statement liability to certifications.

18. **Sec. 4(b): the reporting requirement has no content standard.** "Describing the computing workloads" could be satisfied by a single generic sentence. Require specific items, such as large training runs, total compute per customer, customer identity (know-your-customer), and model identifiers. Also give Commerce rulemaking authority to set the format.

19. **Sec. 4: there is no verification authority.** Commerce has no inspection, audit, records-retention, or subpoena power, so it cannot check the self-certified reports.

20. **Sec. 4: there is no confidentiality provision.** Operators will resist reporting on proprietary or national-security grounds. Add FOIA-exempt treatment so they cannot cite disclosure risk to justify thin reports.

### Sec. 5: Penalties

21. **[High] Sec. 5(a) vs. Sec. 8: the effective dates conflict.** Civil penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the Act takes effect 180 days later. This creates confusion over the interim period and, for Sec. 5(b), possible fair-notice and ex post facto arguments. Align both provisions to the effective date.

22. **Sec. 5: no enforcement authority is assigned.** The bill does not say who assesses civil penalties or brings actions, such as Commerce or the Attorney General. It also provides no assessment procedure and no injunctive relief, so there is no clear mechanism to halt an ongoing training run.

23. **Sec. 5(a): the penalty is too small for the largest developers.** $1,000,000 per day is small compared with the valuations of frontier labs, so it may be treated as a cost of doing business. Consider penalties tied to revenue, disgorgement, forfeiture of hardware and model weights, and debarment.

24. **Sec. 5: there is no clear secondary liability.** Officers, directors, and people who direct or knowingly assist violations are not clearly covered on the civil side. The criminal side relies only on 18 U.S.C. 2, which may be contested.

### Sec. 6: International Agreement

25. **Sec. 6: the deadline is ambiguous and has no consequences.** It is unclear whether the 18 months applies to beginning negotiations or concluding them. There is no reporting requirement to Congress and no consequence for inaction. Require periodic reports to the relevant committees.

26. **Sec. 6: "equivalent" prohibitions is undefined.** The bill also does not address verification or monitoring provisions, which are central to whether any agreement would work.

### Sec. 7: Duration

27. **[High] Sec. 7(b): the 10-year sunset conflicts with 7(a) and lets developers wait out the ban.** Sec. 7(a) says the prohibition lasts until Congress finds safe development is possible, but 7(b) ends the whole Act after 10 years regardless. Developers can plan around a known end date. Strike the sunset, or replace it with mandatory reauthorization review that leaves the prohibition in place by default.

28. **Sec. 7(a): no NAS study is commissioned, and there is no standard.** The provision relies on a National Academies report that nothing in the bill requires, funds, or scopes. "Developed safely" has no criteria. If 7(a) stays, specify what the report must address.

29. **Sec. 7(a) is legally superfluous.** Congress can always amend or repeal the Act by ordinary legislation. A joint resolution is subject to presentment, so it is effectively a statute anyway. The provision may also signal an expected off-ramp. Consider whether it serves a purpose.

### Sec. 8 and Omissions

30. **Sec. 8: the 180-day delay is a window for activity.** Combined with Sec. 3(b), it gives developers months of lead time. Any delay needed to set up the registry should not delay the prohibition itself.

31. **[High] The bill has no severability clause.** It will face Commerce Clause, First Amendment (code and model weights as speech), extraterritoriality, and vagueness challenges. Without severability, one invalid provision risks the whole Act.

32. **The bill grants no rulemaking authority.** Commerce or the designated agency needs explicit authority to define terms, update thresholds, and implement Secs. 2 and 4.

33. **The bill has no whistleblower protections.** Employees are often the best source of information about covered training runs. Add anti-retaliation protections and a reporting channel.

34. **The bill has no preemption or savings clause.** Without one, a court could read the Act as preempting stronger state laws, or industry could argue for that reading. Add a clause preserving more protective state laws.

35. **The bill is not coordinated with export controls.** Nothing links it to chip and model-weight export controls under ECRA, which are a key lever against offshore circumvention.

### Top Priorities

If the office can only take up a few fixes, these matter most:

* The grandfather and cutoff problems in Sec. 3(b) (items 13–14)
* The safety-evaluation exception in Sec. 3(c) (item 15)
* The 10-year sunset in Sec. 7(b) (item 27)
* Evasion of the "single training run" threshold (item 1)
* The narrow U.S. person definition and the lack of a ban on supplying compute (items 8 and 12)
* The undefined "Secretary" and the vague capability standard (items 4–5)
* No penalties for the registry, and the conflicting effective dates (items 17 and 21)
* The missing severability clause (item 31)
