**Definitions (Sec. 2)**

1. **§2(1)(A), per-vessel threshold.** The 10,000-ton limit applies to "a single vessel," and nothing aggregates affiliated companies or fleets. An operator could spread production across several vessels, each under the limit, or rotate vessels, and stay outside the definition.

2. **§2(1)(A), measurement.** "Recovery," "per year" (calendar or rolling), wet versus dry weight, at-sea versus landed tonnage, and transfers between vessels are all undefined. That makes the threshold hard to prove and easy to contest.

3. **§2(1)(A), below-threshold and start-of-violation problems.** Anything at or under the threshold is lawful, including pilot or test mining. It's also unclear when a violation begins for a vessel that crosses 10,000 tons midyear, which affects how daily penalties would be counted.

4. **§2(1)(A), "other mineral resources" and "seabed."** Neither term is defined. It's unclear whether the bill covers sulfides, cobalt crusts, or drilling into subsoil deposits. It might also sweep in oil, gas, or sand and gravel extraction below 200 meters, depending on how "mineral resources" is read.

5. **§2(1)(A), geography and "deep."** The text never says where the definition applies (U.S. EEZ, extended continental shelf, or high seas). The 200-meter cutoff also doesn't match the "deep seabed" label in the title.

6. **§2(1)(B), "the Secretary" is undefined.** The bill doesn't say which department. §6 names the Secretary of State and §4 names NOAA, so the omission looks like a drafting gap.

7. **§2(1)(B), standardless catch-all.** "Risks… comparable to" is undefined, and there's no rulemaking, notice, or publication requirement. Because violations carry criminal penalties under §5(b), this invites fair-notice (vagueness) and improper-delegation challenges. It also lets a future administration widen or narrow the prohibition.

8. **§2(2), "U.S. person" is narrow.** It omits foreign-incorporated subsidiaries of U.S. companies, U.S.-controlled foreign entities, and permanent residents. It also covers only "corporations," not partnerships, LLCs, or trusts, and "State" is undefined, so D.C. and territorial entities may fall outside it.

9. **§2(3), "covered vessel."** Vessels of 5,000 gross tons or less can mine, even above the §2(1)(A) threshold, without registering. "Equipped for the recovery" is vague (temporary or modular equipment, support vessels), and the bill doesn't say whether foreign-flag vessels are covered.

**Prohibition (Sec. 3)**

10. **§3(a), reach.** The prohibition covers people "in the United States" and U.S. persons, so foreign companies mining abroad are untouched. "In the United States" is undefined (territorial sea, EEZ, territories?), and "person" is undefined.

11. **§3(a), only "engaging in" mining.** The text doesn't reach processing, importing, purchasing, transporting, financing, or supplying vessels for minerals mined by others abroad. Beyond general criminal aiding-and-abetting law, there's no secondary liability. The U.S. could remain a processing hub for nodules mined elsewhere.

12. **§3(b), what the grandfather clause covers.** It exempts "mining conducted under an exploration license," but exploration licenses ordinarily authorize exploration, not commercial recovery. Under existing law (the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act), as I understand it, NOAA issues separate exploration licenses and commercial recovery permits. So the exemption is either broader than intended or leaves permit holders in limbo. Please verify this against the statute.

13. **§3(b), no limits on the exemption.** There's no cap on volume or area, no expiration, and no bar on renewing, amending, expanding, or transferring licenses. Nothing conditions the exemption on compliance or allows revocation, so it could be effectively open-ended until the sunset.

14. **§3(b), which licenses count.** "An exploration license" doesn't say it must be a U.S.-issued license, so licenses from other countries or international bodies might qualify. Companies with pending applications get no exemption, which sets up reliance or takings claims.

15. **§3(b), timing.** The cutoff is the date of enactment, so licenses issued, amended, or expanded before then are protected. That gives license-holders and agencies an incentive to act while the bill is pending.

16. **§3(c), environmental assessment exemption.** "Purpose of environmental assessment" is undefined and depends on the actor's stated intent. There's no cap on volume or duration, no approval or notice requirement, and no bar on keeping or selling recovered material. This could shelter de facto pilot mining, and intent is hard to disprove.

**Registry (Sec. 4)**

17. **§4(a), mechanics missing.** "Operator" is undefined, and there's no registration deadline, required content, rulemaking authority, or fee. The bill also doesn't say whether foreign-flag vessels must register.

18. **§4 and §5, registry has no enforcement.** §5 penalizes only violations of §3, so failing to register, failing to report, or filing an inaccurate report carries no penalty in this bill. General false-statement laws might reach a false certification, but the bill doesn't say so.

19. **§4(b), self-certified reports.** Reports must "describe" recovery activities, with no required content such as tonnage, location, depth, or dates. There's no audit, inspection, vessel-tracking, recordkeeping, or independent-observer authority, and no rules on public disclosure or confidentiality. Without those, NOAA can't verify the §2(1)(A) threshold.

20. **§4 versus §3(b)–(c), registry doesn't separate exempt from unlawful activity.** Operators claiming a license or assessment exemption aren't required to identify the license or purpose in their filings. NOAA would struggle to tell exempt operations from violations.

**Penalties (Sec. 5)**

21. **§5(a), civil enforcement mechanics.** The bill names no enforcing agency or Attorney General authority, no administrative process, no penalty factors, no inflation adjustment, no injunctive relief, and no limitations period. "Each day on which the violation occurs" is also unclear for continuous operations.

22. **§5(a), deterrence.** Depending on scale, a $1 million daily cap could be less than a large operation's daily revenue, making it a cost of doing business. The bill has no forfeiture or seizure of vessels, minerals, or proceeds, and no disgorgement.

23. **§5(a) versus §8, retroactivity conflict.** Penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §8 delays the whole Act by 180 days, so §3 isn't in effect at enactment. The conflict is a ready ground for a fair-notice challenge.

24. **§5(b), criminal standard and venue.** "Willfully" is unspecified (knowledge of the conduct or of its illegality?), and it's layered on vague terms in §2(1)(B) and §3(c). There's no corporate-officer liability provision and no venue provision for offenses committed abroad (a general venue statute may fill the gap).

**International and duration provisions (Secs. 6–7)**

25. **§6, no binding outcome.** The Secretary of State need only "seek to negotiate." There's no reporting requirement, consequence for missing the deadline, instrument type (treaty or executive agreement), or definition of "equivalent." It's also unclear whether the 18-month deadline governs the effort or the agreement, and the section doesn't address the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea or the International Seabed Authority (the U.S. isn't a party to the Convention).

26. **§7(a), exit condition.** The bill doesn't require the National Academies to produce a report or set a deadline or criteria for one. "Serious harm" is undefined, and "acting on a report" doesn't say whether a report is a prerequisite. A joint resolution is subject to veto and has no expedited procedure.

27. **§7(a) versus §7(b), contradictory duration.** Subsection (a) keeps the prohibition until Congress makes a finding, but (b) ends the entire Act after 10 years regardless. The finding requirement is therefore meaningless as a safeguard, and the sunset runs from enactment, so effective coverage is about 9.5 years.

28. **§7(b), no savings clause.** The bill doesn't say what happens to pending enforcement actions, penalties for pre-sunset violations, or registry obligations when the Act lapses. The general federal savings statute may help, but it isn't clearly written for a sunset.

**Effective date and general drafting (Sec. 8 and overall)**

29. **§8, 180-day gap.** Companies can begin or ramp up operations, or stockpile, before the prohibition applies. The bill says nothing about operations underway on the effective date (exempt, wind-down, or violation?), which sets up reliance or takings claims.

30. **Whole bill, relationship to existing law.** The bill doesn't amend, repeal, or reconcile with the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act or other existing seabed authorities. NOAA could have conflicting licensing authority and no clear instruction on which controls.

31. **Whole bill, implementation.** There's no rulemaking authority, no authorization of appropriations, and no findings on constitutional authority for the extraterritorial reach. Applying the law to U.S. persons or vessels on the high seas also invites jurisdictional and international-law disputes.

32. **Whole bill, no severability or review provisions.** If a court struck down §2(1)(B) or the extraterritorial reach, nothing says whether the rest survives. The bill also has no judicial-review or citizen-suit provisions to clarify who can challenge or enforce it.
