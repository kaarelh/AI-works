RESPONSE:
I worked from the text you pasted, so I haven't checked it against the introduced version or the manager's amendment. Items are grouped by section, and the last group covers bill-wide gaps. The constitutional points (nondelegation, vagueness, First Amendment, Commerce Clause) are flagged as risks, and counsel should confirm them.

**Section 2: Definitions**

1. **§2(1)(A), fixed threshold.** The 10^26 figure is hard-coded with no authority to lower it by rule. Algorithmic efficiency gains will let systems of concern fall below it, and a fixed number invites companies to design to just under it.
2. **§2(1)(A), "single training run."** The term is undefined and there is no aggregation rule. Splitting training into staged runs, restarting from checkpoints, continued pretraining, or distributed runs could keep each "run" under the line.
3. **§2(1)(A), compute accounting.** The bill doesn't say what counts toward the threshold or how it is measured. Open questions include RL post-training, synthetic data generated by other models, distillation from a covered model, and reuse of earlier weights. Nobody is responsible for certifying the figure.
4. **§2(1)(A) vs. §3(a), "model" vs. "system."** Prong (A) covers a "model," but a system built from several sub-threshold models (ensembles, agent scaffolds) is only reachable through prong (B). "Artificial intelligence model" and "system" are both undefined.
5. **§2(1)(B), "the Secretary."** The Act never says which Secretary. Commerce is implied but unstated, and §6 names the Secretary of State explicitly, which makes the gap more confusing.
6. **§2(1)(B), vague standard.** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no benchmarks, evaluation method, or baseline population. That is an easy target for a vagueness challenge, especially with criminal penalties attached.
7. **§2(1)(B), no process.** There is no deadline, notice-and-comment, publication requirement, duty to ever make a determination, or judicial review provision. Developers can't know in advance whether they are covered, which creates fair-notice, due process, and nondelegation exposure.
8. **§2(1)(B), no detection mechanism.** Nothing requires developers to give the government access to systems for evaluation. A sub-threshold system that is in fact superhuman is covered only if the government independently discovers and determines it.
9. **§2(2), narrow "U.S. person."** It omits lawful permanent residents and non-"corporation" entities such as partnerships, trusts, and unincorporated associations. It also omits entities organized under D.C., territorial, or federal law, and "State" is undefined. LLC coverage is arguable.
10. **§2(2), foreign affiliates.** A foreign-organized subsidiary of a U.S. company is not a "U.S. person," and the bill has no ownership or control test. Training could be moved to an overseas affiliate.
11. **§2(3), undefined terms.** "Advanced AI chip" and "data center" have no definitions or performance metrics. Because the 50,000 count is a raw number, it will go stale as chips get more powerful.
12. **§2(3), no aggregation.** Clusters under common ownership or control can be split into sites below 50,000 chips, and leased or distributed capacity isn't addressed. There is also no rule for multi-site training.
13. **§2(3), geographic scope.** The bill doesn't say whether foreign-located facilities owned or used by U.S. persons must register.

**Section 3: Prohibition**

14. **§3(a), "train, develop, or deploy."** None of the verbs is defined. It's unclear whether internal use (such as using a model for AI R&D), fine-tuning, partial development, or research on architectures counts.
15. **§3(a), no ancillary conduct.** Financing, supplying compute, hosting, licensing, API access, and publishing weights are not prohibited. Liability for facilitators depends on general aiding-and-abetting law, and open-weight release is not addressed.
16. **§3(a), territorial reach.** "United States" is undefined. It's unclear whether a foreign firm abroad serving U.S. users is "in the United States," and the bill has no mechanism for enforcing against U.S. persons abroad.
17. **§3(b), grandfathering is unlimited.** It exempts train, develop, *and* deploy for any system whose training began before enactment. That arguably allows indefinite continued training, scaling, fine-tuning, new versions initialized from existing weights, and unlimited deployment. "Training began" also lets a run already underway continue to any compute scale.
18. **§3(b), no verification or burden of proof.** There is no inventory or declaration of existing systems and no way to establish a start date. Companies have an incentive to start runs before enactment and to claim that later runs are continuations of earlier ones.
19. **§3(b) vs. §8, date mismatch.** The exemption is keyed to enactment but the prohibition applies 180 days later. The status of runs begun in that window is unclear.
20. **§3(b) vs. §2(1)(B), hollow capability prong.** Any system whose training began before enactment is exempt even if the Secretary later finds it superhuman. Prong (B) therefore doesn't reach the most advanced existing systems.
21. **§3(c), "safety evaluation" undefined.** The exemption turns on the actor's own "purpose," so training or deploying a covered system can be relabeled as evaluation or red-teaming. Dual-purpose activity is not addressed.
22. **§3(c), no guardrails.** There is no compute cap, government approval, reporting requirement, security standard, or time limit. The exemption covers all "activities," including training and deployment.
23. **§3(c) vs. §4, no visibility.** Exempt evaluation workloads are not separately reported. The registry can't distinguish them from prohibited work.

**Section 4: Registry**

24. **§4(a), no deadline or content.** There is no registration deadline, required content, or rulemaking authority. "Operator" is undefined, so it's unclear whether the owner or a lessee is responsible.
25. **§4(b), self-certification only.** Commerce has no audit, inspection, subpoena, or recordkeeping authority. Nothing allows independent verification of what the operator certifies.
26. **§4(b), thin and delayed reports.** "Describing the computing workloads" sets no required detail such as run size, model, start date, or customer. A full training run can begin and end between quarterly reports, and there is no advance notice.
27. **§4 vs. §5, no sanctions.** Section 5 penalizes only violations of §3. Failing to register, filing late, or filing false reports carries no stated penalty, so the registry has no enforcement. Existing false-statement law may apply but shouldn't be relied on.
28. **§4, no downstream obligations.** Cloud providers and compute sellers have no duty to know their customers or report large training workloads. Compute rented by a covered-system developer may go unseen.
29. **§4, no confidentiality terms.** Reports contain sensitive commercial data, and the bill is silent on protection from disclosure. That invites trade-secret and takings objections and makes operators less cooperative.

**Section 5: Penalties**

30. **§5, no enforcement authority.** The bill doesn't designate an enforcing agency or the Attorney General. It gives no investigative powers, no procedure to assess and collect civil penalties, and no authority to seek injunctions.
31. **§5(a) vs. §8, retroactivity conflict.** The civil penalty applies to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §3 takes effect 180 days later. This is a drafting conflict that invites a fair-notice challenge, and it should read "on or after the effective date."
32. **§5(a), weak deterrent.** A cap of $1M per day is small next to frontier training budgets in the billions. There is no floor, no scaling to compute cost or revenue, and no disgorgement. "Each day on which the violation occurs" is also unclear for a single training run or a deployed model.
33. **§5, no remedies beyond fines.** The bill lacks cease-and-desist or halt orders and has no authority to order weights secured or destroyed. It also has no forfeiture of chips or facilities, and no link to export controls or procurement bars.
34. **§5(b), "willfully."** The mental-state standard is undefined and high, and it combines poorly with a vague coverage test, so prosecutions would be hard to win. There is no responsible-corporate-officer provision, so executives could escape personal liability.
35. **§5(b) with §2(1)(B), criminal vagueness.** Criminal liability can rest on an unpublished executive determination of capability. Consider tying criminal penalties to prong (A) or to a prior published determination.

**Section 6: International Agreement**

36. **§6, effort-only duty.** "Shall seek to negotiate" requires effort, not results. The 18-month deadline is ambiguous (deadline to begin, to conclude, or to report?), and there is no reporting to Congress or consequence for missing it.
37. **§6, no substance or leverage.** "Equivalent" prohibitions are undefined, and there is no verification or monitoring regime. The bill contains no link to chip export controls, sanctions, or other incentives, and it is silent on whether the result is a treaty or an executive agreement.

**Section 7: Duration**

38. **§7(a) vs. §7(b), internal conflict.** Subsection (a) says the prohibition lasts until Congress finds safety by joint resolution, while (b) ends the whole Act after 10 years regardless. The sunset makes (a) largely meaningless and gives industry an incentive to wait out the clock.
39. **§7(a), NAS report and "safely."** The bill doesn't require the National Academies to produce a report and sets no timeline, funding, or commissioning party. "Developed safely" has no standard, and the bill doesn't say what regime replaces the prohibition if Congress makes the finding.
40. **§7(b), sunset mechanics.** The clock runs from enactment but the Act takes effect at 180 days, which shortens the effective period. There is no savings clause for violations committed before the sunset, so pending enforcement and penalties could be lost.

**Section 8: Effective Date**

41. **§8, no interim measures.** For 180 days nothing stops new covered runs, and the registry isn't operating. Companies could launch and finish runs in the gap, or claim a start date before enactment.

**Bill-wide gaps**

42. **No severability clause.** If a court strikes down one provision, such as §2(1)(B), the rest of the Act is exposed. An explicit clause is safer than relying on the default presumption.
43. **No findings or jurisdictional basis.** The bill has no congressional findings supporting Commerce Clause authority and no jurisdictional hook. Because "develop" could reach code and research, it also has no narrowing to reduce First Amendment exposure.
44. **No anti-evasion provision.** Structuring through shell entities, foreign affiliates, offshore contractors, or rented overseas compute is not addressed.
45. **Government and national-security activity.** The bill is silent on federal agencies, the military, and contractors. Leaving it unresolved invites ad hoc exemption claims, so the bill should decide the question explicitly.
46. **No whistleblower protection or alternative enforcers.** The bill has no whistleblower protection or rewards, no private right of action, and no state attorney general enforcement.
47. **No rulemaking or funding.** No rulemaking authority is granted and no appropriations are authorized. "Person," "State," "United States," and "Secretary" are not defined (1 U.S.C. §1 supplies a default for "person," but it should be explicit).
48. **Silent on state law.** The bill has no preemption or savings clause. It should say whether states may adopt stricter or complementary measures, or industry will argue implied preemption.

**Highest-impact fixes to lead with:** items 17–20 (grandfathering and the gap period), 30–31 (no enforcer and the retroactivity conflict), 38 (the §7 conflict), 7 and 35 (vagueness and nondelegation risk), 21–22 (the safety-evaluation exemption), and 1–2 (the threshold and "single run").

Some of these are policy choices rather than drafting errors, such as grandfathering and the sunset length. Whitfield's staff may treat those differently from clear conflicts like items 31 and 38, so you may want to separate them in the memo.
