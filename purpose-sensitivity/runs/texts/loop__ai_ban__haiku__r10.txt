RESPONSE:
**Highest-priority items** (these would most directly let covered development continue or make the bill unenforceable):

- The grandfather clause in §3(b) and the safety-evaluation exemption in §3(c) (items 1–3)
- The registry in §4 has no penalty attached (item 17)
- The §7(a)/§7(b) conflict on duration (items 27–28)
- The unnamed enforcing "Secretary" and the missing enforcement machinery (items 7, 21)
- The compute threshold can be gamed by splitting runs and by renting cloud capacity (items 4, 16)

**Numbered list of issues**

1. **§3(b):** Any run begun before enactment is exempt, with no limit on how long it continues, how large it grows, or whether it may be deployed. Companies could start large runs now and deploy them later. Fix: set a cutoff at the bill's introduction date, prohibit deployment of grandfathered systems until they are registered and evaluated, or limit the exemption to systems already completed.

2. **§3(b) and §8:** The grandfather date is enactment, but the prohibition doesn't take effect until 180 days later, creating a window of about six months in which new covered runs are lawful. Tie the grandfather date to introduction or to a fixed date.

3. **§3(c):** "Safety evaluation" is undefined, has no scale limit, and requires no notice, registration, or reporting. The "purpose" test is subjective and can be asserted by the developer. It also doesn't bar deploying the resulting system afterward. Fix: define the term, require notice to Commerce, cap compute, and prohibit deployment of evaluated systems absent separate authorization.

4. **§2(1)(A):** "Single training run" can be evaded by splitting training into several runs, continuing pretraining as separate jobs, or distributing one run across sites. Fix: count cumulative compute for a model lineage over a defined period, and add an anti-circumvention rule.

5. **§2(1)(A):** The threshold counts only pretraining compute. Fine-tuning, reinforcement learning, and other post-training compute are not counted, so a system could reach frontier capability while staying under 10^26. Fix: count all training compute on a model.

6. **§2(1)(A):** FLOP measurement is undefined. The text doesn't say how operations are counted, which precision (such as FP8 or FP4) applies, who estimates the count, or whether it is certified. Fix: specify the counting method and the reporting obligation.

7. **§2(1)(A):** A fixed FLOP threshold erodes as algorithms become more efficient, so the same capability will need less compute over time. The bill has no mechanism for Commerce to revisit the threshold. Fix: give the agency authority to update the threshold by rule, subject to a stated standard.

8. **§2(1)(B):** "Secretary" is never defined. The bill uses "Secretary of State" in §6 and "Department of Commerce" in §4, so it is unclear who makes the capability determination. Fix: name the official or agency.

9. **§2(1)(B):** The capability designation has no procedure, standard, notice, record, or judicial review. "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no benchmark. This invites vagueness and nondelegation challenges and makes the bill hard to enforce. Fix: define the benchmarks, require notice and a reasoned determination, and provide for review.

10. **§2(1)(B) and §3(b):** The capability designation could be applied to systems already deployed. The grandfather clause protects only systems whose training began before enactment, so a deployed system could be declared covered after the fact. This creates a retroactivity and due process problem. Clarify the treatment of pre-existing systems.

11. **§2(1), §3(a):** The bill uses "AI model" in (A), "AI system" in (B), and "covered AI system" in §3, without defining the relationship. The prohibition is on training and deploying, but the coverage test attaches to models in some places and systems in others. Use one defined term and explain how the two relate.

12. **§3(a):** "Train," "develop," and "deploy" are undefined, and "develop" overlaps with "train." It is unclear whether releasing model weights (open-weight release) or offering API access counts as deployment. This is the most consequential ambiguity for enforcement.

13. **§3(a):** The prohibition does not cover providing compute, chips, or cloud services for covered training, transferring model weights abroad, or preparatory acts such as acquiring compute. Fix: add language covering knowing assistance, provision of compute, and weight transfers.

14. **§2(2):** "U.S. person" excludes foreign-incorporated subsidiaries of U.S. companies, LLCs, and partnerships (which are not "corporations"). A U.S. company could train through a foreign-incorporated affiliate outside the Act's reach. Fix: cover any entity organized under U.S. law or with its principal place of business in the U.S., including controlled foreign affiliates. Also define "State" to include D.C. and the territories.

15. **§3(a):** "Person" is undefined, so the Dictionary Act definition would apply. That definition may not clearly reach federal, state, or tribal government entities, which the bill should address directly. The bill also doesn't say whether it reaches a system trained abroad by a foreign developer and then served to U.S. users.

16. **§2(3):** "Advanced AI chips" is undefined. The 50,000-chip threshold applies per "data center," so it can be avoided by splitting capacity across sites or by renting cloud compute. The facility definition also plays no role in the prohibition in §3.

17. **§4(a) and §5:** The registration duty has no deadline, no content requirements, and no penalty. §5 applies penalties only to violations of §3, so the registry is unenforceable as drafted. Fix: add a deadline, specify contents, and attach penalties to registration failures.

18. **§4(b):** "Describing the computing workloads" is too vague. The bill doesn't require reporting of training run sizes, model identities, or deployments, and the certification has no express penalty for false statements. Fix: specify required contents and penalize knowingly false certifications.

19. **§4:** "Operator" is undefined. In cloud arrangements, the entity that runs training may not be the entity that operates the facility, so reports could go to the wrong party. Fix: define operator to include whoever controls allocation of compute at the facility.

20. **§4:** The bill gives Commerce no audit, inspection, or information-sharing authority, so it cannot verify reports. It also has no whistleblower protection. Add both.

21. **§5(a):** The bill doesn't say which agency brings civil penalty actions, in what forum, or whether any private enforcement exists. It also gives no authority for injunctions or orders to halt training or delete weights. Fix: specify the enforcing agency, grant injunctive authority, and authorize orders to halt operations or disable systems.

22. **§5(a) and §8:** §5(a) applies penalties to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §8 delays the Act's effective date by 180 days. Penalties could be read to apply before the prohibition takes effect. Fix: make the penalty start date match the effective date.

23. **§5(a):** $1,000,000 per day is small relative to frontier training budgets, which run into the billions, so it could be treated as a cost of doing business. Fix: consider penalties per run, penalties tied to the cost or value of the training, or a percentage of revenue, with inflation adjustment.

24. **§5(b):** "Willfully" is undefined, and the bill has no responsible-officer provision for individual executives. It also doesn't address aiding, abetting, or attempt. Fix: define the mens rea standard and provide for individual liability for officers who direct or knowingly permit violations.

25. **§6:** "Shall seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months" doesn't say whether negotiations must conclude by the deadline or only begin. The section has no reporting requirement, no consequence if talks fail, and no definition of "equivalent prohibitions." Fix: require a report to Congress at the deadline and define the standard.

26. **§7(a):** The prohibition ends only by a joint resolution of Congress finding that covered systems "can be developed safely," but the bill doesn't say whether the resolution goes to the President for signature. If it doesn't, it may face a constitutional challenge under *INS v. Chadha* (legislative veto). The standard is also undefined, and there is no deadline for the NASEM report. (The *Chadha* point is my reading and should be confirmed by counsel.)

27. **§7(a) and §7(b):** §7(a) makes the prohibition indefinite, but §7(b) ends the entire Act after 10 years. An AI developer could argue the prohibition lapses in a decade regardless of §7(a). Fix: remove the sunset or clarify that §7(a) controls the prohibition's duration.

28. **§7(b):** The sunset is itself a loophole: after 10 years, covered development is lawful unless Congress has acted, and the registry and penalties terminate with the Act. Recommend deleting it or making it conditional on a safety finding.

29. **§8:** The bill gives no rulemaking authority, no appropriations, and no deadline for guidance on key terms such as FLOP counting and registration forms. Without this, implementation depends on the agency's own initiative. Add rulemaking authority with a deadline.

30. **General provisions (missing):** The bill has no severability clause, so if a provision such as §2(1)(B) or §7 is struck, the status of the rest is unclear. It also has no preemption or savings clause, so it doesn't say whether state AI laws are preempted. Fix: add both.

31. **General provisions (missing):** The bill has no findings or purpose section, which courts use to interpret ambiguous terms. It also has no anti-circumvention clause that applies substance over form. Fix: add a purpose statement and an anti-circumvention rule.

32. **§3(a) (challenge risk, for staff judgment):** Because "deploy" is undefined, the prohibition could be read to reach publication of research code or weights, which opponents may argue raises First Amendment issues. A clarifying provision on scientific publication would reduce that risk, but it is a policy choice for the office and the coalition to weigh.

**Caveats:** I worked only from the text you provided. I haven't checked it against the committee's current draft, CRS analysis, or any other amendments. The constitutional points (Chadha, nondelegation, vagueness, First Amendment) are my assessment and should be reviewed by legislative counsel before anything goes to the office.
