RESPONSE:
Here is a section-by-section read of S. 2817 as pasted. I've grouped items by section; each item is a distinct problem a company, defense lawyer, or court could exploit.

**Definitions (Sec. 2)**

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "by a single vessel."** The 10,000-ton threshold is measured per vessel, so an operator can split production across several vessels (or swap vessels mid-year) and stay under the line indefinitely while recovering far more than 10,000 tons in aggregate. The bill has no aggregation rule for affiliated vessels, operators, or sites.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "per year."** The bill does not say calendar year, fiscal year, or rolling 12 months, nor who measures tonnage or how (wet weight, dry weight, ore vs. sediment). A company can straddle year boundaries or argue over the measurement basis in any enforcement action.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "recovery."** "Recovery" is undefined. It is unclear whether it means lifting material to the surface, collecting it on the seabed, or landing it, so test collection, in-situ disturbance, or material collected and then discarded may or may not count.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "or other mineral resources."** Undefined; it is unclear whether seafloor massive sulfides, cobalt-rich crusts, phosphorites, or sediments are covered, and the phrase could be read as limited to nodule-like resources under the *ejusdem generis* canon.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "depths greater than 200 meters."** Mining on the shelf at 200 m or shallower is wholly exempt regardless of volume. It is also ambiguous whether depth refers to the seabed at the point of recovery or to the operation generally, which matters on slopes and seamounts that span the 200 m line.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — "the Secretary."** No Secretary is designated anywhere in the Act; Sec. 6 refers to the Secretary of State and Sec. 4 to NOAA (Commerce), so it is unclear who makes this determination. A determination by the wrong official would be void.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — "comparable" risk standard.** The catch-all lets an unnamed official define criminal conduct by case-by-case determination with no rulemaking, notice, criteria, or procedure. Because Sec. 5(b) attaches up to 10 years' imprisonment, this invites void-for-vagueness, fair-notice, and nondelegation challenges, and it is unclear whether determinations apply prospectively or to ongoing operations.

8. **Sec. 2(2) — "U.S. person" is narrow.** It covers only citizens and corporations "organized under the laws of a State," omitting lawful permanent residents, U.S. nationals, LLCs, partnerships, trusts, entities organized under federal, D.C., or territorial law, and foreign subsidiaries owned or controlled by U.S. companies. A U.S. corporation can mine through a foreign-incorporated subsidiary without violating Sec. 3(a).

9. **Sec. 2(3) — "covered vessel" thresholds.** The registry reaches only vessels over 5,000 gross tons that are themselves "equipped for" recovery. Smaller vessels, support ships deploying collector vehicles, and transport/offloading vessels fall outside it, and the 5,000-GT line has no relationship to the 10,000-ton production threshold in Sec. 2(1).

10. **Sec. 2 generally — missing definitions.** "Person," "operator," "seabed," "exploration license," and "environmental assessment" are all undefined even though each is load-bearing for the prohibition, exemptions, or registry.

**Prohibition and exemptions (Sec. 3)**

11. **Sec. 3(a) — "any person in the United States."** This likely reaches only the territorial United States, not the EEZ or outer continental shelf. A foreign company mining within the U.S. EEZ would arguably not be covered by either prong.

12. **Sec. 3(a) — "engage in."** Undefined and not paired with aiding, abetting, financing, chartering, or conspiracy language. U.S. persons may fund, insure, charter vessels for, buy output from, or process minerals for foreign mining operations without "engaging in" mining.

13. **Sec. 3(a) — no supply-chain or import hook.** The bill contains no import ban, port-entry restriction, or processing prohibition, so nodules mined abroad by non-U.S. persons can be landed, refined, and sold in the United States without restriction.

14. **Sec. 3(b) — "exploration license" grandfathers mining.** Exploration licenses (e.g., under DSHMRA or ISA contracts) do not authorize commercial recovery, so the clause either exempts nothing or, read literally, exempts any mining an operator asserts is "conducted under" an exploration license. It also does not say which authority's licenses qualify, so a license from any foreign state or the ISA arguably counts.

15. **Sec. 3(b) — no cutoff, scope limit, or anti-transfer rule.** Any license issued before enactment qualifies, inviting a rush to obtain licenses during the roughly months between now and signature. Grandfathered licenses can be renewed, expanded, amended, or transferred indefinitely with no tonnage, area, or duration cap.

16. **Sec. 3(b) vs. Sec. 8 — date mismatch.** The exemption keys to the date of enactment, but the Act takes effect 180 days later, so it is unclear whether licenses issued in that window are grandfathered.

17. **Sec. 3(c) — "for the purpose of environmental assessment."** No tonnage cap, duration limit, regulator approval, or definition. Because the test is the operator's purpose, large-scale "test mining" can be characterized as environmental assessment, and mixed-purpose operations are unaddressed.

**Registry (Sec. 4)**

18. **Sec. 4 — no penalty for noncompliance.** Sec. 5 punishes only violations of Sec. 3, so failing to register or report, or filing a false report, carries no penalty under this Act.

19. **Sec. 4(a) — no jurisdictional nexus or limit.** The duty applies to "the operator of each covered vessel" without any U.S. connection, which is unenforceable against foreign operators and overbroad on its face; conversely, it does not tie registration to any enforcement consequence (e.g., port denial).

20. **Sec. 4(b) — self-certification only.** Reports are "certified by the operator" with no inspection, audit, data-access, or verification authority for NOAA, no filing deadline, and no public-disclosure requirement, so the registry cannot be used to prove a Sec. 2(1)(A) tonnage violation.

**Penalties (Sec. 5)**

21. **Sec. 5(a) vs. Sec. 8 — penalty for pre-effective-date conduct.** Civil penalties attach to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but nothing is unlawful until the Act takes effect 180 days later. This is an internal contradiction that would be challenged as retroactive and would likely be read to make the first 180 days penalty-free.

22. **Sec. 5(a) — "each day on which the violation occurs."** The underlying violation is defined by an annual tonnage threshold, so it is unclear which days count: only days after the 10,000th ton, every day of the year, or every day of recovery. Daily-penalty exposure cannot be computed with certainty.

23. **Sec. 5 — no enforcing agency or procedure.** The bill names no agency to assess civil penalties, no hearing or judicial-review process, and no authority for injunctions, vessel seizure, forfeiture of minerals, or license revocation, and it does not direct DOJ referral.

24. **Sec. 5(b) — "willfully."** The heightened mens rea, combined with the undefined and determination-dependent definition in Sec. 2(1), makes criminal conviction difficult: a defendant can plausibly claim it did not know its activity met the "comparable risk" standard.

**International agreement (Sec. 6)**

25. **Sec. 6 — "shall seek to negotiate."** This is hortatory with no deliverable; "not later than 18 months" modifies the seeking, not the concluding, and there is no reporting requirement or consequence. The provision also ignores the United States' non-party status to UNCLOS and the ISA's existing regulatory role.

**Duration and sunset (Sec. 7)**

26. **Sec. 7(a) vs. 7(b) — direct conflict.** Subsection (a) says the prohibition lasts until Congress finds by joint resolution that mining is safe; subsection (b) ends the entire Act after 10 years regardless. The "until" condition is largely illusory, and companies can simply wait out the sunset.

27. **Sec. 7(a) — unfunded, unmandated NAS report.** Nothing requires or funds the National Academies to produce the report, sets a deadline, or defines "serious harm," so the lifting mechanism may never be triggerable, or could be triggered on any report.

28. **Sec. 7(b) — no savings clause.** When the Act ceases to have effect, pending enforcement actions and penalties for pre-sunset violations may be extinguished along with the registry obligations.

**Cross-cutting drafting gaps**

29. **No rulemaking authority.** Neither NOAA nor any Secretary is authorized to issue regulations defining terms, setting measurement methods, or running the registry, which weakens the agency's position under *Loper Bright* in any challenge to how it interprets the Act.

30. **No relationship to existing law.** The bill does not amend, repeal, or reference the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (30 U.S.C. 1401 et seq.), under which NOAA can still issue exploration licenses and commercial recovery permits. The two statutes would coexist in conflict, and Sec. 3(b) would grandfather any DSHMRA licenses issued before enactment.

31. **No severability clause.** If the Sec. 2(1)(B) catch-all or Sec. 5(b) criminal penalty is struck as vague, a court could void the entire Act.

32. **No authorization of appropriations, citizen-suit, or whistleblower provision.** NOAA receives a registry mandate with no funding, and there is no private enforcement backstop if the executive branch declines to enforce.

33. **Sec. 1 / Sec. 2(1) — title-definition mismatch.** The term is "commercial" deep seabed mining, but the definition contains no commercial-purpose element and the 200-meter line is the continental shelf edge rather than the deep seabed, which creates interpretive friction over what Congress intended to reach.
