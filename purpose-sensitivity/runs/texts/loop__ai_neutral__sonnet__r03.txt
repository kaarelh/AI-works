# S. 2817: Drafting Weaknesses, Loopholes, and Enforcement Problems

This review covers the text as amended on October 6, 2026. A few items are legal judgments (flagged below) that CPR should confirm with a legal source or outside expert before publication.

## Definitions (Sec. 2)

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "single training run."** The term is undefined, so a developer could split a large effort into sequential or parallel runs that each stay under 10^26 operations. The text doesn't say whether checkpoints, restarts, or continued training count as one run or several.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A), compute-only threshold.** A fixed 10^26 figure ignores algorithmic efficiency, so a model with the same capabilities can eventually be built with less compute. The bill also doesn't say how compute is measured or who verifies it, and it doesn't count post-training, reinforcement learning, or inference-time compute.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A) vs. (B), "model" vs. "system."** Clause (A) covers a "model," so a composite system built from several sub-threshold models, or a model fine-tuned from someone else's base model, may fall outside it. Clause (B) is the only backstop, and it depends on a discretionary determination.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(B), "the Secretary."** The Act never says which Secretary is meant. Sec. 6 names the Secretary of State explicitly, so the unqualified reference here is ambiguous (Commerce is the likely intent, but the text doesn't say).

5. **Sec. 2(1)(B), capability standard.** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" leaves "substantially," "most," "cognitive tasks," and the human baseline (median person or expert) undefined. That invites both gaming and legal challenge.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B), no determination process.** The bill sets no procedure, deadline, notice, publication requirement, or judicial review for the Secretary's finding. If the determination comes after training or deployment, companies face liability they couldn't have known about in advance, which is a fair-notice problem given the criminal penalty.

7. **Sec. 2, "artificial intelligence model/system."** Neither term is defined or tied to an existing statutory definition, so the scope of the whole Act rests on undefined terms.

8. **Sec. 2(2), "U.S. person."** Only citizens and "corporations organized under the laws of a State" are covered. That leaves out lawful permanent residents, partnerships, nonprofits, and possibly LLCs (which may not count as "corporations"). It also leaves out entities organized in D.C. or the territories and foreign-incorporated subsidiaries of U.S. companies.

9. **Sec. 2/3, "person" undefined.** The Dictionary Act (1 U.S.C. § 1) likely supplies a default definition covering private entities but not government bodies. It is unclear whether federal, state, or military and intelligence development is covered. This is a legal reading to verify.

10. **Sec. 2(3), "advanced AI chip" and "data center."** Neither term is defined. There is no performance specification, and it's unclear whether a "data center" means a building, a campus, or a networked cluster.

11. **Sec. 2(3), 50,000-chip threshold.** The registry threshold isn't tied to the 10^26 compute threshold. Operators can stay under it by splitting capacity across sites or entities, and as chips get more powerful, fewer of them are needed for the same compute.

## Prohibition (Sec. 3)

12. **Sec. 3(a), "train, develop, or deploy."** None of the verbs is defined, and they overlap. It's unclear whether "deploy" reaches API access, internal use, open-weight release, or hosting by third parties.

13. **Sec. 3(a), extraterritorial gap.** The ban reaches persons "in the United States" and U.S. persons anywhere. A foreign subsidiary of a U.S. company, or a foreign company training abroad and serving U.S. users remotely, may fall outside it (see item 8).

14. **Sec. 3(a), no coverage of enabling conduct.** The bill doesn't address cloud providers, chip suppliers, financiers, or downstream users of released model weights. Accomplice liability would depend on general criminal law (e.g., 18 U.S.C. § 2), not anything in this Act.

15. **Sec. 3(b), grandfathering with no capability cap.** Any system whose training began before enactment is exempt, including one that meets the "superintelligence" description in 2(1)(B). The bill's central target is thus exempt if it was started in time.

16. **Sec. 3(b), scope of the exemption.** It exempts the "system," not just its original training. The text doesn't say whether further training, scaling, fine-tuning, or successor versions of a grandfathered system are also exempt, and "training began" is undefined. The provision also creates an incentive to start large runs before enactment.

17. **Sec. 3(b) vs. Sec. 8, mismatched dates.** Grandfathering is keyed to enactment, but the prohibition takes effect 180 days later. A run begun in that window isn't grandfathered, yet wasn't unlawful when started, and the bill doesn't say what happens to it when the ban takes effect.

18. **Sec. 3(c), safety-evaluation exemption.** It turns on the "purpose" of the activity, with no definition of "safety evaluation," no limit on compute or scope, no independent oversight, and no reporting. A company could claim almost any training or deployment is for evaluation, and intent is hard to disprove.

## Registry (Sec. 4)

19. **Sec. 4, no penalty for registry violations.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3. Failing to register, filing late, or filing a false report carries no stated consequence (aside from general false-statement law, which the bill doesn't invoke).

20. **Sec. 4(a), no deadlines, contents, or rulemaking authority.** The bill sets no registration deadline, no required information, and no authority for Commerce to issue regulations or designate an office to run the registry.

21. **Sec. 4, "operator" undefined.** It's unclear whether the owner, the manager, or a cloud tenant is responsible. If a customer rents capacity, the operator may not know what is being trained.

22. **Sec. 4(b), self-certified, quarterly reporting.** The bill gives Commerce no audit, inspection, or subpoena power to verify certifications. A training run could start and finish between quarterly reports, and "describing the computing workloads" sets no level of detail.

23. **Sec. 4, no confidentiality provisions.** Operators are asked for sensitive workload data with no protection for trade secrets or FOIA exemptions. That raises the odds of resistance or litigation, and of thin reporting.

## Penalties (Sec. 5)

24. **Sec. 5, no designated enforcer or procedure.** The bill doesn't say who brings civil actions (Commerce, DOJ, or another agency), how penalties are assessed, or whether injunctive relief or a private right of action exists.

25. **Sec. 5(a), penalty design.** "Not more than $1,000,000" per day may be small relative to the cost and value of frontier training. "Each day on which the violation occurs" is ambiguous for continuous activity like a months-long run or ongoing deployment.

26. **Sec. 5(a) vs. Sec. 8, inconsistent timing.** Penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the prohibition doesn't take effect until 180 days later. A reader could argue penalties reach conduct before the ban is operative, which invites a fair-notice challenge.

27. **Sec. 5(b), "willfully."** The mens rea is undefined. A defendant could argue they didn't know a system was covered, especially under the discretionary 2(1)(B) standard, and prosecutors would have to prove that knowledge.

28. **Sec. 5(b), criminal fines and organizations.** "Fined under title 18" defaults to general caps (generally $250,000 for individuals and $500,000 for organizations per felony, absent the alternative-fine provision), which is far below the civil per-day maximum. Prison time applies only to individuals, and the bill says nothing about liability of officers and directors. Verify the 18 U.S.C. § 3571 specifics.

29. **Sec. 2(1)(B) and 5, constitutional exposure.** A vague capability standard backed by criminal penalties invites due process (void-for-vagueness) challenges. Restrictions on training and releasing models could also draw First Amendment claims, since courts have treated code as speech in some contexts. The bill has no severability clause, so a successful challenge to one provision could threaten the rest.

## International Agreement (Sec. 6)

30. **Sec. 6, "shall seek to negotiate."** The duty is to try, not to conclude anything. It's ambiguous whether the 18-month deadline applies to starting negotiations or reaching an agreement. "Equivalent" is undefined, and there is no consequence for missing the deadline, no reporting to Congress, and no mention of whether the result would be a treaty needing Senate ratification.

## Duration (Sec. 7)

31. **Sec. 7(a) vs. 7(b), internal conflict.** Subsection (a) says the ban lasts until Congress finds AI can be developed safely, while (b) ends the Act after 10 years regardless. The conditional is meaningless if the sunset arrives first, and the text doesn't say which controls.

32. **Sec. 7(a), the lifting mechanism.** The bill doesn't direct the National Academies to produce a report, set a deadline, or define "safely." A joint resolution is also subject to a presidential veto and Senate filibuster, with no expedited procedure.

33. **Sec. 7(b), sunset mechanics.** The 10 years run from enactment, so the ban is in force for roughly 9.5 years. There is no savings clause, so it's unclear whether violations that occurred before the sunset can still be penalized or prosecuted afterward. That gives companies an incentive to wait out the clock, and perhaps a defense argument in pending cases.

## Cross-Cutting Gaps

34. **No government or national-security treatment.** The bill neither covers nor exempts federal, military, or intelligence AI programs, and because "person" likely excludes government entities (item 9), the intended result is unclear.

35. **Missing structural provisions.** The bill has no appropriation or staffing for the registry or enforcement, no whistleblower protection or reporting channel, and no statement on preemption of state laws. It also has no severability clause (see item 29).

## Notes for the Explainer

- Many of these gaps (the "Secretary" reference, the date mismatches, the 7(a)/7(b) conflict) look like drafting errors that could be fixed at markup, so it's worth asking the committee which are expected to change.
- Given CPR's nonpartisan posture, I'd suggest offering the sponsors and the bill's supporters a chance to respond to the grandfathering, safety-evaluation, and extraterritorial points, since those are the most substantive.
