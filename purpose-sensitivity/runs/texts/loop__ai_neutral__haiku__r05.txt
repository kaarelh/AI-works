RESPONSE:
**Caveat:** This review is based only on the text you provided (the October 6, 2026 manager's amendment). I haven't checked committee reports, CRS analyses, or later amendments. Confirm drafting intent with Commerce Committee staff before publishing. Several items are internal inconsistencies that staff may be able to clarify. Others are design choices that reasonable observers would weigh differently, so the explainer should describe them as trade-offs.

1. **§2(1)(A):** The 10^26 threshold applies to "a single training run," so a developer could split one large effort into several runs below the threshold. The bill doesn't say whether compute is aggregated across runs, checkpoints, or stages.

2. **§2(1)(A):** The bill doesn't say how FLOPs are counted, whether counts are actual or estimated, or who verifies them. Estimation methods vary, which makes violations hard to prove and compliance hard to confirm.

3. **§2(1)(B):** "The Secretary" is never defined. The bill doesn't say which Secretary holds the determination power, though the registry in §4 points to Commerce.

4. **§2(1)(B):** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no benchmark, reference population, evaluation method, or standard of proof. The bill also provides no notice or hearing before a determination, which makes determinations easy to challenge as arbitrary.

5. **§2(1)(B) and §3(b):** It's unclear whether a later determination that an existing system falls under (B) is retroactive, and whether the grandfather clause in §3(b) would then protect that system.

6. **§2(2):** "U.S. person" covers only citizens and corporations organized under state law. It excludes LLCs, partnerships, foreign-organized subsidiaries of U.S. firms, and lawful permanent residents. "Corporation" and "State" (including whether DC and the territories are covered) are undefined, which narrows the reach of "any U.S. person anywhere" in §3(a).

7. **§2(3):** "Data center" and "advanced AI chips" are undefined. The 50,000-chip threshold doesn't address aggregation across sites, cloud capacity, or distributed clusters, and facilities below it fall outside the registry entirely.

8. **§3(a):** "Train, develop, or deploy" is undefined. It's unclear whether open-weight release, API access, or hosting another party's model counts as deployment, and whether "develop" reaches research.

9. **§3(a):** "Person" is undefined. It's unclear whether the term covers government agencies, universities, nonprofits, and individuals acting on their own. The bill also doesn't say whether cloud providers or their customers bear liability for the same training run.

10. **§3(a):** The prohibition reaches "any person in the United States" and "any U.S. person anywhere," so it doesn't directly bind non-U.S. developers abroad. The bill also doesn't address where distributed training that spans borders takes place, which leaves room for offshoring.

11. **§3(b):** The grandfather clause doesn't define when training "began." It also doesn't say whether it covers continued training, or indefinite deployment of the resulting system. Combined with the 180-day gap in §8, this creates an incentive to start large runs before the Act takes effect.

12. **§3(b), §5(a), and §8:** The Act takes effect 180 days after enactment (§8), but §5(a) applies penalties to violations "on or after the date of enactment." The bill also doesn't say whether runs started during the 180-day window are grandfathered under §3(b), which covers only training begun before enactment.

13. **§3(c):** The safety-evaluation exemption depends only on the stated "purpose" of an activity. It has no definition, notice, certification, reporting, or scale limit, so a large training run could be labeled a safety evaluation, which may undercut the prohibition.

14. **§4(a)–(b):** Registration has no deadline, and the reporting obligation doesn't define "computing workloads" or require operators to identify covered AI systems. Quarterly reports are "certified," but the bill attaches no false-statement penalty to the certification.

15. **§4 and §5(a)–(b):** No penalty applies to registry or reporting violations, because §5 covers only violations of §3. As drafted, §4 has no enforcement consequence.

16. **§4:** The bill gives Commerce no inspection, audit, or subpoena authority to verify reports. It also has no whistleblower protection and no confidentiality or trade-secret provisions for submitted information.

17. **§4:** The registry is keyed to facility size, not to the AI systems themselves. A covered system trained in a facility under 50,000 chips, or spread across several facilities, would not be captured by the registry.

18. **§5(a):** The bill doesn't name an enforcing authority (Attorney General, Commerce, or another agency) or the procedure for civil penalties, whether administrative or judicial. It also omits injunctive relief, officer liability, and inflation adjustment. "Each day on which the violation occurs" doesn't say whether violations are counted per system, per facility, or per developer.

19. **§5(b):** "Willfully" is undefined, and the bill doesn't specify the knowledge standard. That matters because covered status depends on FLOP counts that developers may not know, and on §2(1)(B) determinations. The bill also doesn't address how criminal and civil penalties interact.

20. **§6:** "Shall seek to negotiate" has no consequence if negotiations fail, no requirement to report progress to Congress, no funding, and no definition of what an "equivalent" prohibition must include.

21. **§7(a):** "Can be developed safely" is undefined, so the joint resolution has no stated standard. The bill also sets no deadline for the National Academies report and authorizes no funding for it, so Congress's trigger may depend on a report that is never produced.

22. **§7(a):** The bill doesn't specify expedited procedures for the joint resolution, and it's unclear how the presidential veto and override would apply. As written, the process for lifting the prohibition depends on ordinary legislative procedure.

23. **§7(a) and §7(b):** The two subsections conflict. §7(a) keeps the prohibition in place until Congress acts, while §7(b) says the Act "shall cease to have effect" after 10 years. The bill doesn't say whether the prohibition survives the sunset.

24. **§7(b):** The bill has no savings clause. Once the Act sunsets, penalties and enforcement for violations committed before that date may lapse, and the bill doesn't address pending investigations or actions.

25. **General:** The bill has no severability clause and no judicial-review provision. It doesn't define "artificial intelligence," give Commerce rulemaking authority to define FLOP counting or registration requirements, authorize appropriations, or address its relationship to existing export controls or other federal AI laws.
