RESPONSE:
Below are the problems I found, in section order. Items 10 and 11 depend on existing law outside the bill text, so please verify them with counsel or CRS before publication.

**Section 2: Definitions**

1. **§2(1)(A), per-vessel threshold.** The 10,000-ton limit applies to "a single vessel," so an operator can split recovery across several vessels, or rotate chartered vessels, and keep each under the line. There is no rule aggregating affiliates or a single integrated operation.

2. **§2(1)(A), undefined terms.** "Recovery," "seabed," and "mineral resources" are not defined, nor is the measurement basis. It is unclear whether tonnage is counted at the seafloor collector, the riser, or the deck, whether it is wet or dry weight (nodules carry significant moisture), or whether "per year" is calendar or rolling. If a seafloor collector feeds a surface ship, it is also unclear which "vessel" does the recovering.

3. **§2(1)(A), narrow prohibited conduct.** Only above-threshold "recovery" is covered. Exploration, prospecting, sampling, sub-threshold test mining, and the processing, transport, purchase, or sale of recovered minerals are not addressed.

4. **§2(1)(A), depth and scope.** The 200-meter line is a continental-shelf depth, not a "deep seabed" one, and "other mineral resources" is open-ended. Read literally, it could sweep in offshore oil and gas, sand and gravel, or dredging, which invites overbreadth and vagueness challenges and lobbying carve-outs. The method for measuring depth is also unstated.

5. **§2(1)(B), "Secretary" is undefined.** The bill never says which Secretary (Commerce, Interior, or another). Section 6 names the Secretary of State explicitly, which makes the unqualified usage in §2 more ambiguous.

6. **§2(1)(B), standardless delegation.** The scope of a criminal and civil prohibition depends on an agency's determination of "comparable" risk, with no criteria, rulemaking, notice, or comment requirement. This invites nondelegation, vagueness, and fair-notice challenges, and it is hard to prove willfulness under §5(b) for conduct nobody had been told was covered.

7. **§2(2), "U.S. person" is too narrow.** It covers only citizens and corporations organized under State law. It omits partnerships, LLCs (arguably not "corporations"), trusts, joint ventures, permanent residents, and foreign-incorporated subsidiaries of U.S. firms. "State" is undefined, so it is unclear whether D.C. and the territories are included.

8. **§2(3), covered-vessel gap.** The 5,000-gross-ton floor is unrelated to the tonnage threshold in §2(1)(A). A smaller vessel could recover more than 10,000 tons a year, violate §3, and never be registered. "Equipped for the recovery" also invites arguments that equipment is removable or on a separate platform.

**Section 3: Prohibition**

9. **§3(a), jurisdictional reach.** The ban covers persons "in the United States" and U.S. persons anywhere. It does not reach foreign companies, foreign-flag vessels, or foreign subsidiaries operating on the high seas, and "United States" is undefined (EEZ? extended continental shelf?). It also doesn't restrict importing, financing, or processing minerals mined elsewhere, so activity can simply shift offshore.

10. **§3, relationship to existing law.** The bill does not amend or repeal the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (NOAA licensing and permitting) or the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act. Without a conforming clause, agencies could keep issuing licenses or permits under those laws, and operators could argue the statutes conflict.

11. **§3(b), "exploration license" is undefined.** The bill doesn't say who issued it. A foreign government or International Seabed Authority license could qualify, so a U.S. person holding one might be exempt. An exploration license may also not authorize commercial recovery at all, so the exemption's logic is muddled.

12. **§3(b), unlimited grandfathering.** The exemption has no expiration, tonnage cap, or limit on renewal, amendment, transfer, expansion, or sublicensing. The heading says "existing operations," but the text protects any pre-enactment license, even if no mining was underway. For license holders this could swallow the prohibition.

13. **§3(b), enactment-date cutoff.** The cutoff invites a rush to issue licenses before enactment, and the bill is silent on pending applications. Applicants who just miss the cutoff have a ready takings or due-process claim.

14. **§3(c), environmental-assessment exemption.** It turns on the "purpose" of the activity, with no definition, tonnage or duration cap, approval, independent oversight, or reporting. Large-scale test mining could be labeled "assessment," and nothing bars selling what is recovered.

**Section 4: Registry**

15. **§4(a), registration.** The bill sets no deadline and no criteria, and gives NOAA no authority to refuse or condition registration. "Operator" is undefined (owner, charterer, or contractor?), and it is unclear whether foreign operators or vessels are covered. It also provides no public access, despite the "registry" label.

16. **§4(b), reports.** There are no required data elements (tonnage, location, depth, minerals), no filing deadlines, no verification or audit mechanism, and no public disclosure. "Certified by the operator" sets no accuracy standard, and "recovery activities" is undefined.

17. **§4 and §5, no penalties for registry violations.** §5 penalizes only violations of §3. Failing to register, failing to report, or filing a false report carries no penalty under this bill, which leaves only general false-statement statutes, if they apply.

18. **§4, no monitoring or inspection powers.** The bill has no vessel tracking requirement, independent observers, boarding or inspection authority, or subpoena power. Mining occurs far offshore, so self-reported data may be the only evidence, and only for vessels over 5,000 gross tons.

**Section 5: Penalties**

19. **§5, no enforcement structure.** No agency is designated to investigate, assess penalties, or sue. There is no administrative hearing process, role for the Attorney General, judicial review provision, or citizen-suit option. There is also no forfeiture or seizure of vessels or minerals, no injunctive relief, and no restoration or damages remedy.

20. **§5(a), timing conflict.** Penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §8 delays the Act's effect by 180 days. It is unclear whether conduct in that window is penalizable, which creates fair-notice and ex post facto arguments and ambiguity about the effective period.

21. **§5(a), "each day" is ambiguous.** The bill doesn't say whether the penalty is per vessel, per operation, or per person, or how continuous multi-day operations are counted.

22. **§5(b), mens rea.** "Willfully" is undefined: does it require knowing the conduct is illegal, or only that it was intentional? Combined with the Secretary's open-ended determination in §2(1)(B), a defendant has a strong defense. The bill is also silent on corporate and individual liability, and on venue for offenses committed abroad.

**Section 6: International agreement**

23. **§6, hortatory duty.** The Secretary of State need only "seek to negotiate," and the 18-month deadline applies to negotiating, not concluding. There is no reporting requirement, consequence for failure, or funding.

24. **§6, "equivalent" is undefined.** The bill doesn't say how equivalence to §3 is judged or how an agreement would be ratified. A unilateral ban without trade or import measures leaves competitors free to mine under other flags.

**Section 7: Duration**

25. **§7(a), vague and unenforceable trigger.** "Serious harm" is undefined. The bill doesn't require, fund, or set a deadline for the National Academies report, and it doesn't say whether a joint resolution is valid without one. A joint resolution is also subject to veto, and one Congress cannot bind a later one, so the mechanism is mostly symbolic.

26. **§7(a) vs. §7(b), internal contradiction.** Subsection (a) says the prohibition lasts until Congress makes a safety finding, while (b) ends the whole Act after 10 years regardless. The sunset overrides the finding requirement, and operators can plan around waiting it out.

27. **§7(b), sunset mechanics.** The 10 years run from enactment, not the effective date, so the real period is about 9.5 years. There is no savings clause for violations committed before the sunset, which creates abatement arguments for pending cases. The registry also vanishes with the Act.

**Section 8 and general**

28. **§8, 180-day delay.** The gap lets operators ramp up, stockpile, and position vessels before the ban bites. It also leaves unclear whether §4 registration is subject to the same delay.

29. **General, missing implementation provisions.** There is no rulemaking authority, appropriations authorization, severability clause, or reporting requirement to Congress. Without rulemaking authority, NOAA's registry (§4) and the Secretary's determinations (§2(1)(B)) lack a clear legal footing.

30. **General, litigation exposure.** Beyond the points above, expect challenges from license holders and applicants (takings, due process) and from foreign interests (extraterritorial application, conflict with UNCLOS-based claims). The vague definitions and the criminal penalties would give challengers their best arguments.
