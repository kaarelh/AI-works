# Drafting Issues and Loopholes in S. 2817 (as amended October 6, 2026)

**Definitions**

1. **§2(1)(A):** "Training run" and "computing power" are undefined, and the bill doesn't say how compute is measured or whether it includes post-training, RL, or fine-tuning. Developers could split a run into nominally separate runs or shift compute into post-training to stay under 10^26. Define "training run," require cumulative compute across a model lineage, and have Commerce set a measurement methodology.
2. **§2(1)(A):** A fixed FLOP threshold will weaken as algorithms become more efficient, and the bill gives no agency authority to update it. Authorize rulemaking to adjust the threshold and consider a capability-based trigger alongside it.
3. **§2(1)(B):** "The Secretary" is undefined, and the bill sets no standard, process, notice, or appeal for designations. Because violations are criminal, a designation standard this vague ("substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks") invites vagueness and nondelegation challenges.
4. **§2(1)(B) and §3(b):** The bill doesn't say whether a system designated after training began is covered, or whether a Secretary designation can apply retroactively. Companies could argue that any system designated later was grandfathered.
5. **§2 (generally):** "Artificial intelligence," "develop," "deploy," and "person" are undefined. Reading "artificial intelligence system" literally could sweep in narrow, non-frontier systems, which creates overbreadth challenges and gives companies room to argue about scope.
6. **§2(2) "U.S. person":** The definition covers only citizens and corporations organized under state law. It leaves out LLCs, partnerships, permanent residents, and foreign-incorporated subsidiaries of U.S. firms, so the "anywhere" reach in §3(a) has gaps.
7. **§2(3) "advanced AI chips":** This term is undefined, so the 50,000-chip threshold can't be applied with confidence.
8. **§2(3) and §4(a):** The 50,000-chip facility threshold isn't tied to the 10^26 FLOP prohibition threshold. A facility below 50,000 chips could train a covered system without ever registering.
9. **§2(3) and §4(a):** "Data center" and "operator" are undefined. The bill doesn't say who must register in colocation, multi-tenant, or cloud arrangements, where the chip owner and the workload operator may differ.

**Prohibition**

10. **§3(a):** "Deploy" is undefined, so it's unclear whether open-weight release, API access, or downloads count. The prohibition also doesn't reach enabling conduct such as providing compute for covered training, renting or selling chips for it, or distributing weights.
11. **§3(a):** The bill doesn't say whether a foreign entity training on U.S.-located cloud compute, or accessing it remotely, is "in the United States." Companies could structure training offshore or through foreign-incorporated entities to avoid coverage.
12. **§3(b):** This is the largest loophole. Any run that began before enactment is exempt as a system, with no compute cap, completion deadline, registration requirement, or limit on deploying the resulting model or using it to train successors. The exemption also protects deployment and further development, not just the original run.
13. **§3(b), §5(a), and §8:** The 180-day gap between enactment and the effective date is unaddressed. Runs begun in that window aren't grandfathered under §3(b), but the bill doesn't say whether they must stop on the effective date. Labs could front-load large runs during the gap.
14. **§3(c):** "Safety evaluation" is undefined and purpose-based, with no requirement for qualified or independent evaluators, no compute cap, no disclosure of results, and no duty to halt if dangerous capabilities appear. As written, it could swallow the prohibition, since training a frontier model and then "evaluating" it would be exempt.

**Registry**

15. **§4(b):** Quarterly reports cover "computing workloads" but don't require identifying covered AI systems, their training compute, or training start dates. The reports therefore can't show compliance with §3.
16. **§4(b):** The operator's certification has no stated standard, and there is no audit, inspection, or access authority, so the reports are unverifiable self-reporting.
17. **§4 and §5:** No penalty applies to failure to register, failure to report, or a false certification. Section 5 covers only §3 violations.
18. **§4:** The bill doesn't protect confidential business information in filings, which could drive litigation and reduce compliance.

**Penalties**

19. **§5(a):** The bill doesn't name an enforcing agency or a cause of action (DOJ, Attorney General, Commerce, or private parties). It also doesn't expressly authorize injunctive relief, the most important tool for halting a run in progress.
20. **§5(a):** At $1 million per day, the penalty is small relative to frontier training budgets and may be treated as a cost of doing business. It doesn't scale with compute, revenue, or per-chip exposure, and there is no authority to seize or disable compute or weights.
21. **§5(a) and §8:** Penalties apply to "violations occurring on or after the date of enactment," but §3 doesn't take effect until 180 days later. This creates an inconsistency about whether pre-effective-date conduct is penalized.
22. **§5(a)–(b):** The bill doesn't address officer or director liability, or aiding-and-abetting liability for cloud providers or chip suppliers.
23. **§5(b):** "Willfully" is undefined. Combined with the ambiguous definitions above, companies can argue they lacked willfulness by pointing to good-faith readings. The bill also has no attempt or conspiracy provisions.

**Treaty**

24. **§6:** "Shall seek to negotiate" creates no obligation to succeed, no reporting requirement, and no consequence for failure. "Equivalent" prohibitions is also undefined, and the bill doesn't say whether any resulting agreement needs Senate advice and consent.

**Duration**

25. **§7(a):** The "can be developed safely" standard is undefined. The NASEM report has no deadline, and the bill doesn't require that it reach any conclusion. It also doesn't set expedited procedures for the joint resolution.
26. **§7(a) and §7(b):** The indefinite duration in (a) conflicts with the 10-year sunset in (b). A court or agency would have to choose, and industry will argue the sunset controls.
27. **§7(b):** The sunset runs from enactment, so the prohibition is effective for only about 9.5 years. The bill also has no savings clause preserving penalties or investigations for violations that occurred before the sunset.

**Structural gaps**

28. **General:** The bill has no rulemaking authority. Nothing directs an agency to issue regulations, define terms, set reporting formats, or update thresholds, and no funds are authorized for enforcement.
29. **General:** There is no severability clause. If a court strikes §2(1)(B) or §3(c), it's unclear whether the rest of the criminal prohibition survives.
30. **General:** The bill is silent on preemption of state frontier-AI laws, such as California's SB 53. That silence invites preemption fights and conflicting state requirements. Congress should decide this deliberately.
31. **General:** The bill has no express judicial review standard for Secretary determinations under §2(1)(B), and no requirement that determinations be based on a documented record.
32. **General:** The bill has no congressional findings or purpose section. Findings would help courts interpret ambiguous terms such as "safety evaluation" and "can be developed safely."
33. **General:** Expect constitutional challenges on First Amendment grounds (code and research as speech, and compute restrictions on research) and on vagueness grounds for the criminal provisions. Legislative counsel should review these before the markup.
