# S. 2817 Issues for Senator Whitfield's Office

The most serious problems are **#5, #13, #14, #29, and #32**. Each would let development continue or let the prohibition lapse on its own, so they may deserve to lead the letter.

### Sec. 2: Definitions

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A): "single training run."** Compute can be split across multiple "runs," such as pretraining, continued pretraining, large-scale reinforcement learning, or merging separately trained models. This lets a company keep each run under 10^26 while the final model exceeds it. The threshold should count cumulative compute across all training stages that contribute to a model, including compute used to produce models it is derived or distilled from.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A): Static threshold.** Algorithmic efficiency improves steadily, so a fixed 10^26 figure will cover less capability every year, and dangerous systems will eventually be trainable below it. The bill should require periodic downward adjustment, or add a capability trigger that does not depend on (1)(B).

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A): No measurement method.** The bill doesn't say how operations are counted or estimated, or who certifies the count. Developers can therefore pick favorable accounting conventions, and defendants can challenge any estimate the government makes. Commerce should be directed to set a binding methodology by rule.

4. **Sec. 2(1): "Artificial intelligence," "model," and "system" are undefined.** Paragraph (A) says "model," (B) says "system," and neither term is defined. That invites arguments that a product built around a model is not itself the regulated item. The bill should adopt the definition in 15 U.S.C. 9401(3) and define "model" and "system" consistently.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(B): The trigger comes too late.** The clause covers only systems already "capable of substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks." By the time that determination is made, the dangerous system already exists. The bill should cover systems reasonably expected to reach that capability, and covered precursor capabilities such as autonomous AI research, rather than only the finished result.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B): "The Secretary" is undefined.** No department is specified, here or anywhere in the Act.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(B): No standards or procedure for a determination.** The bill gives no criteria, evaluation process, timeline, or notice requirement, and "substantially exceeding" and "most cognitive tasks" are vague. This invites nondelegation and void-for-vagueness challenges, which are especially strong because criminal liability under Sec. 5(b) can depend on the determination. It also raises fair-notice problems if a determination is made after the conduct.

8. **Sec. 2(2): "U.S. person" excludes key actors.** The definition omits lawful permanent residents. It also omits entities organized under federal law or in DC or the territories ("State" is undefined), and partnerships, LLCs, and other non-corporate entities are arguably excluded. It should track standard export-control definitions.

9. **Sec. 2(2): Foreign subsidiaries escape.** A U.S. company can do the prohibited work abroad through a foreign-incorporated subsidiary, which is not a "U.S. person." The definition should cover foreign entities owned or controlled by U.S. persons, and the bill should prohibit U.S. persons from directing, funding, or facilitating such work.

10. **Sec. 2(3): Facility threshold is easy to evade.** "Data center" and "advanced AI chip" are undefined. An operator can split capacity into facilities of 49,999 chips each or train across several sites. A chip count is also a poor proxy as chip performance rises. The bill should define these terms by performance specification, aggregate capacity under common ownership or control or networked for joint training, and allow the threshold to be updated.

### Sec. 3: Prohibition

11. **Sec. 3(a): "Train, develop, or deploy" are undefined.** It is unclear whether "deploy" covers internal use only, fine-tuning a covered model, or releasing open weights. "Develop" is broad enough to invite vagueness challenges and narrow constructions.

12. **Sec. 3(a): Facilitation and foreign deployment into the U.S.**
    * The bill doesn't prohibit renting compute, selling chips, or providing funding or model weights to someone who trains a covered system, including foreign actors.
    * It doesn't clearly reach a foreign company that serves a covered system to U.S. users from abroad.
    * Federal aiding-and-abetting and conspiracy law (18 U.S.C. 2 and 371) reaches only criminal violations, so civil liability for facilitators is missing.

13. **Sec. 3(b): The grandfather clause invites a race.** Any run whose training "began" before enactment is permanently exempt. Companies have every incentive to start large runs while the bill is pending, and arguably to keep a nominally "begun" run going indefinitely.
    * The cutoff should be the introduction date or a fixed past date.
    * Exempt training should have to finish by a deadline.
    * Further training or scaling of a grandfathered model should count as new training.

14. **Sec. 3(b): The exemption covers every kind of covered system.** It applies to (B)-category systems too. A pre-enactment system later determined to be superintelligent would be permanently exempt from both training and deployment restrictions, by anyone. At minimum, the exemption should not apply to (B) systems or to further development.

15. **Sec. 3(c): The safety-evaluation exemption swallows the rule.** "Safety evaluation" is undefined, and the test is the actor's self-declared "purpose." Because it covers training and deployment, any company can label frontier training as safety research. The exemption should be limited to evaluating existing or grandfathered systems, require prior government authorization and oversight, and exclude training new covered systems.

### Sec. 4: Registry

16. **Sec. 4(a): No deadline.** Registration has no deadline, and there's no requirement to update when a facility changes ownership or capacity.

17. **Sec. 4: "Operator" is undefined.** In colocation and cloud setups it's unclear whether the obligation falls on the building owner, the cloud provider, or the tenant running the workload.

18. **Sec. 4: Foreign facilities unclear.** It's unclear whether facilities abroad that are operated by U.S. persons must register.

19. **Sec. 4(b): Reports have no required content.** "Describing the computing workloads" is too vague to be useful. Reports should state, at minimum, per-customer compute used, the largest training runs (with total operations), and customer identity, including foreign customers.

20. **Sec. 4: No verification.** Commerce has no authority to inspect, audit, or subpoena records. Operator self-certification without verification is unenforceable.

21. **Sec. 4 / Sec. 5: No penalty for registry violations.** Sec. 5 penalizes only Sec. 3 violations. Failing to register, failing to report, or filing false reports carries no specific penalty. 18 U.S.C. 1001 may cover false statements, but the bill should say so explicitly and add civil penalties.

22. **Sec. 4: No confidentiality or information-sharing provisions.** Without confidentiality protections, operators may challenge the reporting on trade-secret grounds. The bill also doesn't authorize Commerce to share reports with DOJ for enforcement.

### Sec. 5: Penalties

23. **Sec. 5: No enforcer and no forum.** No agency is given authority to bring actions, and the bill doesn't say whether civil penalties are assessed administratively or in court. Under *SEC v. Jarkesy* (2024), administrative imposition of these penalties would likely require a jury. Enforcement should go through the Attorney General in federal district court.

24. **Sec. 5: No injunctions or remedial orders.** The bill provides only money and prison. Courts should be authorized to halt training runs, order deletion or forfeiture of model weights, and seize hardware used in violations.

25. **Sec. 5(a): The penalty is too low.** $1M per day is small next to frontier-lab valuations and could be treated as a cost of doing business. A revenue-based or gain-based alternative maximum would fix this.

26. **Sec. 5(a): The date conflicts with Sec. 8.** Sec. 5(a) imposes penalties for violations "on or after the date of enactment," but Sec. 8 makes the Act effective 180 days after enactment. The conflict invites litigation, and penalizing pre-effective-date conduct raises fair-notice concerns.

27. **Sec. 5(b): Corporate criminal fines are capped.** Organizations can't be imprisoned, and the default title 18 fine is $500,000 per felony count. The alternative-fine provision in 18 U.S.C. 3571(d) helps only if gain is proven. The bill should set a specific higher organizational fine and add individual liability for officers who direct violations.

28. **Sec. 5: No whistleblower protections or rewards.** Insiders are the most likely source of evidence about hidden training runs, and the bill gives them no protection or incentive.

### Sec. 6: International Agreement

29. **Sec. 6: The international mandate is unenforceable.** "Shall seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months" doesn't say whether negotiations must begin or conclude by then. "Seek" imposes no real obligation.
    * The bill should require regular reports to Congress on progress.
    * It should specify verification elements the agreement must include.
    * It should pair the mandate with export controls on advanced chips to countries that don't join.

### Sec. 7: Duration

30. **Sec. 7(a) vs. 7(b): The sunset overrides the prohibition.** Subsection (a) says the prohibition lasts until Congress finds safe development possible, but (b) ends the entire Act after 10 years regardless. A company only has to wait. The sunset should be struck, or the prohibition should continue unless Congress affirmatively acts.

31. **Sec. 7(b): The sunset takes everything with it.** It also ends the registry, the penalties, and the international mandate, which removes the monitoring infrastructure at the same time.

32. **Sec. 7(a): No one is required to produce the NASEM report.** The bill doesn't direct or fund the National Academies to produce the report Congress is supposed to act on. The bill should require it, set its scope and periodic schedule, and specify criteria for "can be developed safely."

### Sec. 8 and Omissions

33. **Sec. 8: The 180-day delay.** Combined with the grandfather clause, the delay gives companies a window to finish ongoing runs before any restriction applies. Consider immediate effect for the prohibition and the registry, with delayed effect only for rules that need implementing.

34. **No rulemaking authority.** No agency is authorized to issue regulations defining terms, compute methods, or reporting formats. That leaves the gaps above to litigation.

35. **No severability clause.** Without one, a successful challenge to one provision, such as the (B) delegation, puts the whole Act at risk.

36. **No congressional findings.** Findings on the interstate-commerce basis and the national-security rationale would strengthen the bill against Commerce Clause challenges and First Amendment ("code is speech") challenges.

37. **No authorization of appropriations.** Without funding, Commerce, DOJ, and NASEM implementation may never happen.

38. **No preemption or savings clause.** The bill doesn't address its relationship to state AI laws or to existing export-control authorities, which invites conflict litigation.
