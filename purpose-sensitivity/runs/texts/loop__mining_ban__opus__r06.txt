# S. 2817 Issues for Markup (Manager's Amendment of Oct. 6)

## Section 2: Definitions

1. **§ 2(1)(A), tonnage threshold.** The 10,000-metric-ton annual cap invites pilot-scale "test mining" just below the line. Each operation could stay under the cap while still causing serious seabed damage, since harm comes from disturbance and sediment plumes, not just tonnage. Consider defining the activity by method or purpose rather than volume, or lowering the threshold sharply.

2. **§ 2(1)(A), "by a single vessel."** A company could split recovery across several vessels, each under 10,000 tons, and escape the definition entirely. The threshold should apply per person, including affiliates, across all vessels and equipment.

3. **§ 2(1)(A), measurement ambiguities.** The text does not say whether tonnage is wet or dry weight, which matters because nodules have high water content. It also doesn't say whether "per year" means calendar year or a rolling 12 months, or how depth is measured. Each gap gives a defendant room to contest a violation.

4. **§ 2(1)(A), "recovery" only.** The definition covers only recovering minerals. Collector trials, seabed disturbance, and discharges that don't bring material to the surface, or that return it, may fall outside it. Consider adding "disturbance of the seabed for the purpose of mineral exploitation, including testing of collection systems."

5. **§ 2(1)(A), "other mineral resources" undefined.** This term could be read to sweep in oil and gas or other materials not intended to be covered. That overbreadth invites opposition and challenge. Define it, for example by reference to hard mineral resources as defined in 30 U.S.C. 1403.

6. **§ 2(1)(B), "the Secretary" undefined.** No section of the bill identifies which Secretary (Commerce? Interior?). This leaves the catch-all provision unadministrable.

7. **§ 2(1)(B), standardless delegation.** Letting the Secretary decide what is "comparable" gives an unfriendly administration the power to read the catch-all narrowly or never use it. It also provides no procedure (rulemaking, notice) or criteria. Because § 5(b) attaches criminal penalties, an open-ended administrative definition also invites vagueness and nondelegation challenges.

8. **§ 2(2), "U.S. person" is too narrow.** The definition omits lawful permanent residents and entities other than "corporations," such as LLCs, partnerships, and trusts. It also omits entities organized under the laws of D.C. or U.S. territories, unless "State" is defined to include them.

9. **§ 2(2), foreign subsidiaries.** A foreign-incorporated subsidiary owned or controlled by a U.S. parent is not covered. Moving operations offshore is a simple path around the ban. Consider covering entities "owned or controlled by" a U.S. person.

10. **§ 2(3) vs. §§ 2(1) and 3, inconsistent terms.** "Covered vessel" is used only in the registry section, while the prohibition refers to activity "by a single vessel." Mining from vessels under 5,000 gross tons, or by non-vessel systems such as platforms or seabed crawlers, may escape both the definition and the registry.

11. **§ 2(3), "equipped for the recovery."** It is unclear whether this includes vessels carrying removable or leased equipment, or support and transport vessels. Operators could avoid registration by installing equipment only after departing.

12. **§ 2, "person" undefined.** Section 3(a) prohibits "any person," but the bill never defines the term. Defining it would confirm coverage of foreign nationals and foreign entities operating in U.S. jurisdiction, and possibly government entities and contractors.

## Section 3: Prohibition

13. **§ 3(a), "in the United States."** It is ambiguous whether this includes the territorial sea, the EEZ, and the outer continental shelf. Virtually all relevant activity occurs there. A foreign company mining in the U.S. EEZ could argue it is not "in the United States."

14. **§ 3(a), only direct mining is covered.** Financing, sponsoring, insuring, chartering vessels to, processing for, or supplying a mining operation are not prohibited, and neither is aiding and abetting. Importing or purchasing minerals mined abroad is also not addressed. Without these, U.S. capital and markets can keep supporting the industry.

15. **§ 3(b), exploration-license exemption, the most serious loophole.** "Mining conducted under an exploration license" turns exploration licenses into authorization for commercial recovery. Exploration licenses were never meant to allow that under the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (DSHMRA). The exemption is also open-ended: no expiration, no limits on renewal, extension, modification, or transfer.

16. **§ 3(b), source of license unspecified.** "An exploration license" isn't limited to licenses issued under DSHMRA. It could be read to include International Seabed Authority exploration contracts or foreign-state licenses held by U.S. persons, which would exempt much of the industry.

17. **§ 3(b), race to license.** Exempting any license issued before enactment rewards applicants who rush to secure licenses while the bill is pending. Consider tying the cutoff to the bill's introduction date, or eliminating the exemption.

18. **§ 3(c), environmental-assessment exemption.** "For the purpose of environmental assessment" is undefined and depends on the operator's stated intent. It has no scale limits, permit requirement, or agency oversight. Test mining or even full-scale recovery could be labeled "assessment." Consider limiting it to permitted scientific research below a strict scale cap, with no commercial sale of recovered material.

## Section 4: Registry

19. **§ 4, no penalty for noncompliance.** Section 5 penalizes only violations of § 3. Failing to register, failing to report, or filing false reports carries no penalty under the Act, which makes the registry effectively voluntary.

20. **§ 4(a), no deadline or scope.** There is no deadline to register, and no statement of whether foreign-flagged vessels, vessels in U.S. waters, or vessels calling at U.S. ports must register. "Operator" is also undefined.

21. **§ 4(b), no report contents.** The quarterly reports have no required contents, such as tonnage, location, depth, dates, or methods. There is also no verification requirement (vessel tracking, observers, audits) and no public disclosure. As written, reports could be uninformative and couldn't be used to show § 3 violations.

22. **§ 4, agency mismatch.** The registry is assigned to NOAA, while § 2(1)(B) refers to an unspecified "Secretary," and no agency is given enforcement, rulemaking, or investigative authority anywhere in the bill.

## Section 5: Penalties

23. **§ 5(a) vs. § 8, conflicting dates.** Civil penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but under § 8 the Act takes effect 180 days later. This creates confusion about the 180-day window, and penalizing conduct before the prohibition takes effect raises fair-notice and due-process challenges.

24. **§ 5(a), no enforcement mechanism.** The bill does not say who assesses or sues for the penalty (Attorney General? NOAA?) or by what procedure. After *SEC v. Jarkesy* (2024), in-house administrative penalties may face Seventh Amendment challenges. The bill should specify civil actions in federal district court, or address jury rights.

25. **§ 5, missing remedies.** The bill provides no injunctive relief, no forfeiture of vessels, equipment, or recovered minerals, no seizure authority, no citizen-suit provision, and no license or permit revocation. Daily fines alone may be treated as a cost of doing business by well-capitalized operators.

26. **§ 5(b), "willfully" plus vague definitions.** The high "willfully" standard, combined with the vague § 2(1)(B) catch-all and the undefined § 3(c) exemption, makes criminal prosecution difficult and vulnerable to vagueness challenges. Consider "knowingly" and tighter definitions.

## Section 6: International Agreement

27. **§ 6, "seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months."** It is unclear whether the 18 months is a deadline to begin or to conclude negotiations, and "seek" is not enforceable. The section also has no reporting requirement to Congress and no reference to the International Seabed Authority or the forum for negotiations.

## Section 7: Duration

28. **§ 7(a) vs. § 7(b), direct conflict.** Subsection (a) says the prohibition lasts until Congress makes a finding, but subsection (b) ends the entire Act after 10 years regardless. Companies can simply wait out the ban. This contradicts the goal of a permanent prohibition; strike (b).

29. **§ 7(a), off-ramp is undefined and unfunded.** "Serious harm" is undefined, and no section directs or funds the National Academies to produce the report. A "finding" by joint resolution also creates an easier-looking path to lifting the ban than ordinary repeal, since it doesn't require amending the statute's terms.

30. **§ 7(b), no savings clause.** On sunset, the penalties and registry lapse too. Pending enforcement actions for past violations could be dismissed unless a savings clause preserves liability.

## Section 8: Effective Date

31. **§ 8, 180-day window.** The delay gives operators six months of lawful activity after enactment. Combined with § 3(b), it creates an incentive to obtain licenses and start operations quickly. Consider making § 3 effective immediately and delaying only the registry.

## Structural Omissions

32. **No conforming amendments to existing law.** The bill does not amend or repeal DSHMRA (30 U.S.C. 1401 et seq.), under which NOAA can still issue exploration licenses and commercial recovery permits. It also does not address mineral leasing on the outer continental shelf under the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA). Courts disfavor implied repeal, so the agencies' existing authorities should be expressly terminated.

33. **No rulemaking authority.** No agency is authorized to issue implementing regulations, which leaves definitions, reporting, and enforcement unworkable.

34. **No severability clause.** Given the likelihood of litigation, a severability clause would keep the core prohibition in force if a provision such as § 2(1)(B) or § 5(a) is struck down.

35. **Federal activities not addressed.** It is unclear whether federal agencies, or contractors acting for them (for example, defense stockpiling programs), are bound by the prohibition. "Person" should be defined accordingly.

**Top priorities for Senator Whitfield's office:** #15–17 (exploration-license exemption), #28 (10-year sunset), #18 (assessment exemption), #2 and #9 (vessel-splitting and foreign subsidiaries), #19 (registry has no teeth), and #32 (DSHMRA left intact).
