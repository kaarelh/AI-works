# S. 2817 (as amended Oct. 6, 2026): Drafting problems, loopholes, and enforcement and litigation risks

Items are in section order. A few are interpretive judgment calls, and several turn on existing law (especially the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act, DSHMRA) that the bill text doesn't mention. I'd have counsel or the committee's legislative text verify those before publication.

## Section 2: Definitions

1. **§2(1)(A), per-vessel threshold.** The 10,000-ton test applies to "a single vessel," with no aggregation rule for affiliates, fleets, or joint ventures. An operator could run several vessels below the threshold, or rotate vessels, and never engage in "commercial deep seabed mining." Anything below the threshold is lawful by definition.

2. **§2(1)(A), "per year" is unmeasured.** The text doesn't say whether "year" means a calendar year or a rolling 12 months. It also doesn't say whether tonnage is wet or dry weight, or where it is measured (seafloor, deck, or port). Each gap invites disputes over whether the threshold was crossed.

3. **§2(1)(A), "recovery" and "mineral resources" are undefined.** It's unclear whether "recovery" means seafloor collection, lifting to the surface, or onboard processing. "Other mineral resources" doesn't say whether sulfides, cobalt crusts, or mineral-rich sediments count. The phrase could also sweep in sand, gravel, or hydrocarbons.

4. **§2(1)(A), 200-meter depth cutoff.** Water deeper than 200 meters includes large areas of continental slope and shelf edge, including within U.S. waters. The definition may therefore capture existing offshore activity that isn't "deep seabed" mining, which invites an overbreadth challenge. The text also doesn't say where depth is measured, and activity at 200 meters or shallower is excluded entirely.

5. **§2(1)(B), "the Secretary" is not identified.** No Secretary is named here or elsewhere in the Act (Commerce, Interior, and State are all plausible). The catch-all has no responsible official, which also undermines §6, where "the Secretary of State" is named separately.

6. **§2(1)(B), standardless discretionary trigger.** Activities become prohibited if the Secretary finds risks "comparable" to those in subparagraph (A), with no criteria, procedure, notice, or deadline. Because §5(b) attaches criminal penalties, this raises fair-notice, vagueness, and nondelegation arguments. It could also be used to widen or, through inaction, narrow the ban.

7. **§2(2), narrow "U.S. person."** The definition covers only citizens and corporations organized under State law. It leaves out permanent residents, partnerships, LLCs (arguably), trusts, unincorporated associations, and entities organized in D.C. or the territories (it depends on whether "State" includes them). It also omits foreign subsidiaries of U.S. companies and joint ventures with a foreign majority, so a company could restructure offshore.

8. **§2(3), "covered vessel" doesn't match the prohibition.** The prohibition turns on recovery volume by any vessel, but "covered vessel" requires more than 5,000 gross tons and being "equipped" for recovery. A smaller vessel could exceed 10,000 tons and never be registered. The text also doesn't say which gross-tonnage convention applies.

## Section 3: Prohibition

9. **§3(a), jurisdictional reach is unclear.** "In the United States" is undefined, so it's unclear whether it includes the territorial sea, the EEZ, or the extended continental shelf. Non-U.S. persons mining outside U.S. jurisdiction are not covered at all, including on foreign-flag vessels.

10. **§3(a), only the act of mining is prohibited.** The bill doesn't reach financing, supplying equipment, onshore processing, or importing or selling seabed minerals mined abroad. Companies could source or process material outside the prohibition's terms.

11. **§3(a), extraterritorial enforcement and legal exposure.** Applying the ban to U.S. persons "anywhere" is hard to monitor and prosecute on the high seas. Opponents could also raise high-seas-freedom and international-law objections, although the U.S. is not a party to UNCLOS.

12. **§3(b), grandfathering covers the wrong kind of authorization.** The exemption applies to "mining conducted under an exploration license," but exploration licenses generally don't authorize commercial recovery (that's what a separate commercial recovery permit does under DSHMRA). The text doesn't resolve whether commercial-scale mining under an exploration license is allowed.

13. **§3(b), no limits on the exemption.** There is no cap on volume or area and no end date, and nothing addresses renewal, extension, modification, or transfer of licenses. The text also doesn't say who must have issued the license, so a foreign-issued license (for example, from another country or the International Seabed Authority) might arguably qualify.

14. **§3(b), holders of other authorizations are unaddressed.** Commercial recovery permit holders and pending applicants get no protection or transition. The bill also doesn't repeal or amend DSHMRA, so it's unclear whether NOAA keeps its licensing and permitting role. This creates conflict-of-laws questions and takings and reliance-based litigation risk.

15. **§3(b), pre-enactment cutoff creates a rush incentive.** Licenses issued before enactment are exempt, so companies have reason to obtain licenses before passage. The enactment-date test also doesn't line up with the 180-day delayed effective date in §8.

16. **§3(c), "environmental assessment" exemption.** The exemption turns on the "purpose" of the activity, which is subjective, and the term is undefined. There is no volume or duration limit, permit or agency approval, or data-sharing requirement, so large-scale test mining could be labeled assessment.

## Section 4: Registry

17. **§4(a), "operator" and "equipped for" are vague.** "Operator" is undefined, and "equipped for the recovery of seabed mineral resources" can be avoided by temporarily installing or removing gear. Nothing covers seafloor collectors, remotely operated systems, or support vessels, and there is no registration deadline.

18. **§4(a), unclear scope.** It's unclear whether registration applies only to U.S.-flag or U.S.-operated vessels or to any vessel worldwide. Without that, enforcement is uncertain.

19. **§4(b), reporting content is unspecified.** The bill requires reports "describing the recovery activities" but doesn't require tonnage, location, depth, mineral type, or dates. It sets no first-report deadline and gives NOAA no verification, audit, or inspection authority.

20. **§4, no consequence for noncompliance and no transparency.** §5 penalizes only violations of §3, so failing to register or file (or filing a false certification) has no specified penalty. The bill also doesn't make registry data public, which limits outside oversight.

## Section 5: Penalties

21. **§5(a) vs. §8, retroactivity and timing conflict.** Civil penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §8 delays the Act's effect by 180 days. Violations in that window are therefore either unreachable or penalized before the law is effective, which invites a fair-notice or due-process challenge.

22. **§5(a), thin civil enforcement design.** No enforcing agency is named, and there is no administrative process, injunctive relief, vessel or mineral forfeiture, or disgorgement. The per-day cap isn't inflation-adjusted, and it's unclear what counts as one "day" of a continuous operation. A $1 million cap may be small relative to potential revenue.

23. **§5(b), "willfully" is ambiguous.** The text doesn't say whether the defendant must know the conduct is unlawful or just that they were doing it. That matters a lot given the open-ended definition in §2(1)(B). The bill also has no corporate-liability or venue language for conduct abroad.

## Sections 6–8: International, duration, effective date

24. **§6, toothless mandate.** "Shall seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months" is unclear on whether the deadline applies to starting talks or concluding an agreement. There is no reporting requirement or consequence for missing it, and "equivalent" prohibitions is undefined. Any agreement would also still need to be concluded and, if a treaty, ratified.

25. **§7(a), mechanism for lifting the ban is unworkable and unclear.** A joint resolution needs both chambers and the President's signature (or a veto override), and the bill provides no expedited procedures. It doesn't require NASEM to produce a report or fund one, and doesn't say whether the finding ends the prohibition automatically.

26. **§7(a), "serious harm" is undefined.** No standard, burden of proof, or baseline is given, and a later Congress could pass a different statute anyway. The standard is open to competing interpretations.

27. **§7(a) vs. §7(b), internal conflict.** §7(a) says the prohibition lasts until a congressional finding, but §7(b) ends the whole Act 10 years after enactment regardless. The science-based condition is therefore effectively capped by a fixed date, and companies can wait out the ban.

28. **§7(b), no savings clause.** Nothing preserves liability for violations before the sunset or pending enforcement actions, and "cease to have effect" also ends the registry. Because §8 delays effect by 180 days, the prohibition's real life is about 9.5 years.

29. **§8, 180-day gap.** Nothing prevents companies from ramping up operations, signing contracts, or racing to complete work before the ban applies. There is also no transition or wind-down rule for activity underway on the effective date, which raises reliance and takings arguments.

## Cross-cutting omissions

30. **Missing boilerplate that affects durability and enforceability.** The bill has no severability clause, so one successful challenge could threaten the whole Act. It also lacks rulemaking authority, appropriations, a judicial-review provision, findings or purposes (which help defend a statute), and any statement of how it interacts with DSHMRA and other existing law.
