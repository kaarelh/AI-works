# S. 2817 (as amended Oct. 6, 2026): Loopholes, Ambiguities, and Drafting Problems

Items are grouped by section. A few are cross-cutting and appear under the provision where they cause the most trouble.

## Sec. 2: Definitions

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A): "Artificial intelligence" and "model" are undefined.** The bill never defines either term, so it's unclear whether the threshold applies to non-neural systems, ensembles, or mixture-of-experts components counted separately.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A): "Single training run" is undefined.** Training could be split into pretraining, continued pretraining, and RL post-training, each under 10^26 operations, with each phase argued to be a separate "run." The bill has no aggregation rule for cumulative compute across runs that produce one model.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A): No rule for derivative models.** It's unclear whether fine-tuning, merging, or distilling a model counts the base model's training compute. A model distilled from a covered model could plausibly fall below the threshold.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A): No compute-counting methodology.** The bill doesn't specify how to count operations (theoretical vs. achieved FLOPs, treatment of sparsity, low-precision arithmetic, synthetic data generation, or failed and restarted runs). With no rulemaking authority anywhere in the Act, nobody is empowered to fill the gap.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(A): The threshold is static.** A fixed 10^26 figure with no adjustment mechanism will capture less capability over time as algorithms get more efficient. You can argue both that it's under-inclusive and that it's arbitrary.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(A) vs. (B): "Model" vs. "system."** Paragraph (A) covers a "model" and (B) covers a "system," and the Act never reconciles the two. It's unclear whether a sub-threshold model plus scaffolding or tools is covered only under (B), and whether a covered model embedded in a product makes the whole product covered.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(B): "Secretary" is undefined.** The Act never says which Secretary makes capability determinations. Commerce is implied by Sec. 4 but never stated.

8. **Sec. 2(1)(B): No determination process.** There are no criteria, notice-and-comment, hearing, timeline, or judicial review provision. That raises APA and due-process arguments.

9. **Sec. 2(1)(B): Vague capability standard.** "Substantially exceeding human performance," "most cognitive tasks," and "capable of" are all undefined. Which human baseline counts (median person or domain expert)? Which tasks count? Does "capable" mean demonstrated or latent capability? Paired with the criminal penalties in Sec. 5(b), this supports a void-for-vagueness argument.

10. **Sec. 2(1)(B): Fair notice and retroactivity.** Nothing says whether conduct before a determination is a violation. A system could become "covered" retroactively, after a company has already trained and deployed it.

11. **Sec. 2(2): The "U.S. person" definition has gaps.** It covers only citizens and *corporations*. It omits lawful permanent residents, LLCs, partnerships, and other non-corporate entities.

12. **Sec. 2(2): "State" is undefined.** Entities organized under the laws of D.C. or U.S. territories may fall outside the definition, since the Act has no definition of "State" that includes them.

13. **Sec. 2(2): Foreign subsidiaries are excluded.** A subsidiary incorporated abroad is not a "U.S. person," and its conduct abroad isn't reached by the "in the United States" prong either. The Act has no ownership- or control-based attribution rule. (U.S.-citizen employees of such a subsidiary would still be personally covered.)

14. **Sec. 2(3): Covered-facility terms are undefined.** "Data center" and "advanced AI chips" are not defined, so it's unclear which chip generations or performance levels count.

15. **Sec. 2(3): The facility threshold is easy to fall under.** Operators could split capacity across facilities below 50,000 chips, or run distributed training across sites. A chip-count threshold also stops tracking compute as per-chip performance rises.

16. **Sec. 2(3): Geographic scope is unclear.** The definition has no location limit, so it's ambiguous whether foreign facilities run by U.S. operators must register.

## Sec. 3: Prohibition

17. **Sec. 3(a): Key verbs are undefined.** "Train," "develop," and "deploy" are not defined. "Develop" could sweep in ordinary research, architecture work, or small-scale experiments, which raises both vagueness and overbreadth arguments.

18. **Sec. 3(a): "In the United States" is ambiguous.** It's unclear whether this turns on where the compute sits, where the operator sits, or where users sit. Examples include remote operation of foreign data centers by staff located in the U.S., and a foreign provider serving U.S. users by API.

19. **Sec. 3(a): Narrow list of prohibited acts.** The section doesn't reach possessing or transferring weights, financing, or supplying compute to foreign developers. Criminal aiding and abetting would likely reach some of this via 18 U.S.C. § 2, but no equivalent applies on the civil side.

20. **Sec. 3(a): Government entities are likely not covered.** "Person" is undefined, and under the Dictionary Act (1 U.S.C. § 1) and the presumption against binding the sovereign, it likely excludes the federal government. That leaves open whether national labs, or contractors acting on the government's behalf, are covered.

21. **Sec. 3(b): The grandfather clause is very broad.** It exempts the entire covered *system* if training "began" before enactment. That arguably covers indefinite continued training, later checkpoints, fine-tunes, and ongoing deployment. Neither "began" nor the boundary between one system and the next version is defined.

22. **Sec. 3(b) vs. Sec. 8: Two different dates.** Grandfathering is keyed to *enactment*, but the prohibition takes effect 180 days *later*. The status of training started during that 180-day window is unclear: it's lawful when begun, not grandfathered, and possibly unlawful once Sec. 3 takes effect.

23. **Sec. 3(c): The safety-evaluation exemption is open-ended.** "Activities conducted for the purpose of safety evaluation" has no limits on who may conduct them, what scale is allowed, what certification is required, or what happens to the resulting system. Nothing addresses mixed purposes, or later deployment of a system trained "for evaluation."

## Sec. 4: Registry

24. **Sec. 4(a): "Operator" is undefined.** In colocation or cloud setups, the owner, the lessee, and the tenant running the workloads could each be the "operator."

25. **Sec. 4(a)–(b): No deadlines or start dates.** No deadline is set for registration, and no start date is set for quarterly reports.

26. **Sec. 4 / Sec. 5: No penalty for registry violations.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of *section 3*, so failing to register or report has no direct penalty. (18 U.S.C. § 1001 could still reach knowingly false certified statements.)

27. **Sec. 4(b): The report standard is vague.** "Describing the computing workloads" has no content, granularity, or format requirement.

28. **Sec. 4(b): No confidentiality protection.** The Act protects neither trade secrets nor security-sensitive information in reports, and gives Commerce no inspection or verification authority.

## Sec. 5: Penalties

29. **Sec. 5(a) vs. Sec. 8: Conflicting start dates for penalties.** Civil penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the Act takes effect 180 days after enactment. This conflict raises retroactivity and due-process questions.

30. **Sec. 5(a): Per-day accrual is unclear.** For "develop" and "deploy," it's unclear what counts as a violation day, and whether each deployed instance or customer is a separate violation.

31. **Sec. 5(a): No enforcement mechanism.** The Act names no enforcing agency, says nothing on whether the AG sues in district court or an agency adjudicates, and sets no statute of limitations beyond the general default in 28 U.S.C. § 2462.

32. **Sec. 5(b): Interaction with the vague definitions.** The "willfully" standard requires knowledge that the conduct is unlawful. Combined with the vague definitions, this cuts both ways. Good-faith reliance on a reasonable reading is a defense, but a deliberate structuring scheme documented internally could support willfulness.

## Sec. 6: International Agreement

33. **Sec. 6: Hortatory with an unclear deadline.** "Seek to negotiate" imposes no binding obligation. It's ambiguous whether the 18-month deadline applies to starting or concluding negotiations.

34. **Sec. 6: Undefined terms and no link to the domestic ban.** "Equivalent" is undefined. More importantly, the domestic prohibition is not conditioned on reaching an agreement, which supports a unilateral-restraint and competitiveness argument.

## Sec. 7: Duration

35. **Sec. 7(a): The off-ramp adds nothing.** A joint resolution requires presentment, so it's functionally just a new law, which Congress could pass anyway. The provision also never directs anyone to commission the National Academies report, and sets no timeline, funding, or criteria for it.

36. **Sec. 7(a) vs. (b): Unclear relationship.** Subsection (a) says the prohibition lasts "until" Congress acts, while (b) ends the whole Act at 10 years. Presumably (b) caps (a), but the text doesn't say so.

37. **Sec. 7(b): No savings clause.** Nothing says whether penalties for conduct before the sunset survive expiration. Whether the general savings statute (1 U.S.C. § 109) applies to a sunset, as opposed to a repeal, is unsettled.

38. **Sec. 7(b): Status of systems after sunset.** The status of systems trained or grandfathered under the Act after the sunset is unaddressed. This is less of a problem than the others.

## Sec. 8 and Structural Gaps

39. **Sec. 8: Inconsistent timing across the Act.** Sec. 8 delays effect by 180 days, but Secs. 3(b), 5(a), 6, and 7(b) all run from *enactment*. That creates the conflicts in items 22 and 29 and possibly shortens the effective sunset period.

40. **Whole Act: No rulemaking authority.** No agency is given authority to define terms, set compute-accounting rules, or issue guidance. This makes most of the ambiguities above hard for the executive branch to cure.

41. **Whole Act: No severability clause.** Courts presume severability anyway, so this mainly matters for arguing that a vague Sec. 2(1)(B) taints the scheme. That argument is weak.

42. **Whole Act: No preemption provision.** The Act says nothing about state AI laws, which leaves the interaction unclear.

## Notes for the Legal Team

- **Many of these could be fixed at markup.** Items 2, 13, 21–23, 26, 29, and 39 are straightforward to close with amendments. If the goal is to argue unworkability, raising them publicly may simply prompt fixes. If the goal is operating room under the enacted text, the strategies may be gone by final passage.
- **Relying on loopholes carries risk.** Structuring around a criminal statute (splitting runs, "safety evaluation" framing, offshore subsidiaries) invites anti-evasion arguments, legislative-intent readings, and possible willfulness findings. I'd recommend treating the "keep operating" items as questions for outside counsel's formal opinion rather than as an operational plan.
- **Constitutional arguments vary in strength.** Vagueness (items 9–10, 17) is the strongest. First Amendment arguments about code and research, and extraterritoriality challenges, are weaker.
