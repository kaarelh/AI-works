# S. 2817 (as amended Oct. 6, 2026): Loopholes, Ambiguities, and Drafting Problems

Items are grouped by section and numbered continuously. Many of these could be fixed with a one-line amendment at Thursday's markup, so the team should decide which to raise publicly as workability objections and which to analyze privately. Several "loophole" readings are also weaker than they look, and I've noted where.

## Sec. 2: Definitions

**Sec. 2(1) generally**

1. **Sec. 2(1).** Neither "artificial intelligence," "artificial intelligence model," nor "artificial intelligence system" is defined. This leaves the outer boundary of the prohibition uncertain.
2. **Sec. 2(1)(A) vs. (B).** Subparagraph (A) covers a "model" and (B) covers a "system." It's unclear whether a system built around a below-threshold model, or a model inside a covered system, is itself covered.

**Sec. 2(1)(A): the compute threshold**

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A).** "Single training run" is undefined. Compute could arguably be split across separate runs, such as pretraining followed by continued pretraining, fine-tuning, RL post-training, or model merging, so that no single run exceeds 10^26. A court might aggregate runs on a substance-over-form theory, so this reading carries real risk.
4. **Sec. 2(1)(A).** The bill gives no counting methodology. It doesn't say whether to count theoretical peak or actual operations, how to treat mixed-precision or sparse operations, or whether to include compute for synthetic data generation, reward models, or failed or restarted runs.
5. **Sec. 2(1)(A).** Compute used to train a teacher model isn't attributed to a student. A distilled model trained with under 10^26 operations falls outside (A) even if it matches a covered model's capabilities.
6. **Sec. 2(1)(A).** The threshold is fixed, with no indexing or update mechanism. Algorithmic efficiency gains will make it both over- and under-inclusive over time, which supports a workability and arbitrariness argument.
7. **Sec. 2(1)(A).** It's unclear when a model in mid-training becomes "covered." Under a literal reading, it isn't covered until it crosses 10^26, which complicates when the prohibition on "training" one attaches.
8. **Sec. 2(1)(A).** "Greater than 10^26" excludes a run of exactly 10^26. This is trivial, but sloppy.

**Sec. 2(1)(B): the capability test**

9. **Sec. 2(1)(B).** "Secretary" is never defined anywhere in the Act. Sec. 4 references Commerce and Sec. 6 references the Secretary of State, so the determining official is ambiguous.
10. **Sec. 2(1)(B).** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" is undefined. The bill doesn't say which humans (median or expert), what "substantially" means, what counts as a "cognitive task," or how "most" is measured.
11. **Sec. 2(1)(B).** The bill sets no procedure, notice, standard of evidence, or opportunity to contest a determination. That raises due process concerns, especially given the criminal penalties.
12. **Sec. 2(1)(B).** It's unclear whether a determination operates prospectively or retroactively. If retroactive, conduct that was lawful when undertaken could become a violation. If prospective only, no below-threshold system is covered until the Secretary acts.

**Sec. 2(2): U.S. person**

13. **Sec. 2(2).** "U.S. person" covers only citizens and "corporations." It omits lawful permanent residents and arguably LLCs, partnerships, and other non-corporate entities. This is much narrower than standard definitions, such as those in export control statutes.
14. **Sec. 2(2).** "Organized under the laws of a State" may exclude entities organized under D.C. or territorial law, or federally chartered entities, because "State" isn't defined.
15. **Sec. 2(2).** Foreign-incorporated subsidiaries of U.S. companies aren't U.S. persons, so their training abroad isn't directly reached. However, U.S.-based employees who direct, fund, or "develop" that work are "persons in the United States," and aiding-and-abetting (18 U.S.C. § 2) and conspiracy (18 U.S.C. § 371) liability could attach. Relying on this would be high-risk.

**Sec. 2(3): covered facility**

16. **Sec. 2(3).** "Data center" and "advanced AI chips" are undefined. A chip-count threshold also ignores chip generation and performance.
17. **Sec. 2(3).** The 50,000-chip threshold is per facility. Distributing compute across multiple sub-threshold facilities avoids registration, and "facility" boundaries (campus vs. building) are unclear.
18. **Sec. 2(3).** The facility definition isn't tied to covered AI systems at all. A covered system could be trained in a non-covered facility, and covered facilities must register even if they never touch covered work. This mismatch supports an argument that the bill is poorly tailored.

## Sec. 3: Prohibition

**Sec. 3(a): scope of the prohibition**

19. **Sec. 3(a).** "Train," "develop," and "deploy" are undefined. "Develop" could sweep in research, architecture work, and data preparation for future systems, while "deploy" could mean anything from internal use to public release.
20. **Sec. 3(a).** The bill doesn't say where "deployment" occurs: server location, company location, or user location. It's therefore unclear whether a foreign entity serving U.S. users, or a U.S. entity serving only foreign users, is covered.
21. **Sec. 3(a).** "Person" is undefined in the Act, so the Dictionary Act (1 U.S.C. § 1) applies. It includes corporations and associations but presumptively excludes the federal government. Federal agencies (e.g., DOD, DOE labs) are therefore likely outside the prohibition, though their contractors are not. This supports an argument that the bill is inequitable.

**Sec. 3(b): grandfather clause**

22. **Sec. 3(b).** The grandfather clause keys on the date training *began*, with no end date. A run begun pre-enactment could arguably continue indefinitely and grow far beyond its original scale.
23. **Sec. 3(b).** It's unclear whether fine-tunes, new versions, or derivatives of a grandfathered system are the same "covered AI system" and therefore also exempt.
24. **Sec. 3(b).** The exemption applies to all of subsection (a): training, development, and deployment. A grandfathered system can apparently be further developed and deployed without limit.
25. **Sec. 3(b).** The grandfather date (enactment) creates an incentive to start large runs before enactment. This "race to start" effect is a strong policy argument that the bill undermines its own goal.
26. **Sec. 3(b) / Sec. 8.** The grandfather uses the enactment date, but the prohibition takes effect 180 days later. Training begun during that window isn't grandfathered, yet isn't prohibited until the effective date. This is an incoherent window rather than a loophole, since continuing that training or deploying the result after the effective date would be a violation.
27. **Sec. 3(b).** Systems later designated under Sec. 2(1)(B) are exempt if their training began pre-enactment. The most capable existing systems may therefore be permanently exempt.

**Sec. 3(c): safety evaluation exception**

28. **Sec. 3(c).** The safety-evaluation exception is purpose-based, undefined, and unbounded. It has no definition of "safety evaluation," no approval or notice requirement, no oversight, and no limit on scale. Read literally, it could swallow the rule.
29. **Sec. 3(c).** The exception applies to all activities, including deployment. It's also unclear how dual-purpose work (safety and commercial) is treated.
30. **Sec. 3(c), caveat.** Courts construe exceptions narrowly and look at actual purpose. Labeling commercial development as "safety evaluation" would likely fail and could support a willfulness finding under Sec. 5(b).

## Sec. 4: Registry

31. **Sec. 4(a).** The registry provision sets no deadline for registration and doesn't define "operator" (owner, lessee, or cloud tenant).
32. **Sec. 4(a)–(b).** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3, so the Act imposes **no penalty** for failing to register or report. False certifications would still likely be reachable under 18 U.S.C. § 1001.
33. **Sec. 4(b).** "Describing the computing workloads" sets no required level of detail. The bill also grants no inspection or audit authority and no confidentiality protection for proprietary information. FOIA Exemption 4 and the Trade Secrets Act would likely provide some protection.
34. **Sec. 4 generally.** The Act grants no rulemaking authority to Commerce or anyone else to implement Sec. 4 or define any of its terms.

## Sec. 5: Penalties

35. **Sec. 5(a).** Civil penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the Act takes effect 180 days later. The penalty provision purports to reach conduct before the prohibition is in force, which is an internal conflict and a possible retroactivity problem.
36. **Sec. 5(a).** No agency is designated to assess or enforce civil penalties, and there's no adjudicatory procedure. After *SEC v. Jarkesy* (2024), administrative imposition of penalties of this kind likely requires a jury trial in federal court.
37. **Sec. 5(a).** It's unclear whether the $1M/day cap applies per person, per system, or per violation type. It's also unclear how it applies to individual employees versus the entity.
38. **Sec. 5(b).** Pairing a 10-year felony with the vague Sec. 2(1)(B) standard, and with undefined "train/develop/deploy," invites a void-for-vagueness challenge (*Johnson v. United States*, 2015). Fines default to 18 U.S.C. § 3571 levels ($500K per organizational felony, or twice the gain or loss), which is modest relative to the civil exposure.

## Sec. 6: International Agreement

39. **Sec. 6.** "Shall seek to negotiate" is hortatory and unenforceable. It's ambiguous whether the 18-month deadline applies to beginning or concluding negotiations.
40. **Sec. 6.** The domestic prohibition isn't conditioned on any international agreement or reciprocity. This supports the strongest competitiveness argument: the bill is a unilateral restraint on U.S. developers.

## Sec. 7: Duration

41. **Sec. 7(a).** A joint resolution requires presentment and is functionally identical to passing a new law. The "off-ramp" adds nothing Congress doesn't already have.
42. **Sec. 7(a).** The bill doesn't direct or fund the National Academies to produce the report. "Developed safely" is undefined, and there's no timeline, so the off-ramp has no trigger.
43. **Sec. 7(a) vs. 7(b).** These subsections conflict. (a) says the prohibition lasts "until" Congress acts, while (b) says the Act sunsets at 10 years regardless. The specific sunset likely controls, but the drafting is inconsistent.
44. **Sec. 7(b).** The sunset runs from enactment, not the effective date, so the operative prohibition lasts about 9.5 years.
45. **Sec. 7(b).** It's unclear whether pending enforcement actions survive the sunset. The general savings statute (1 U.S.C. § 109) speaks to "repeal," and its application to expiration of temporary statutes is unsettled.

## Sec. 8 and Structural Omissions

46. **Sec. 8.** The 180-day effective date is inconsistent with the enactment-date references in Sec. 3(b) and Sec. 5(a). See items 26 and 35.
47. **Whole Act.** The Act has no general rulemaking or definitional authority, no designated enforcement agency, and no appropriations.
48. **Whole Act.** There is no preemption clause. Existing and future state AI laws would continue to operate alongside the federal regime, so compliance obligations could conflict.
49. **Whole Act.** There is no severability clause. A successful challenge to one provision, such as Sec. 2(1)(B), creates uncertainty about the rest.
50. **Whole Act.** There are no anti-evasion, anti-structuring, or aggregation provisions. This makes items 3, 5, 15, and 17 easy targets for a committee amendment.

## Notes for the Legal Team

- **Strongest unworkability arguments:** the vagueness and due-process problems (items 10–12, 38), missing enforcement machinery (36, 47), internal date conflicts (26, 35, 43), the static threshold (6), the unilateral restraint (40), and the race-to-start incentive (25). These are legitimate drafting critiques.
- **Weakest "keep operating" theories:** run-splitting (3), foreign-subsidiary structuring (15), and the safety-evaluation label (30). Courts and prosecutors look through form to substance, and secondary liability is available. Treat these as litigation risk, not operating plans.
- **Strategic consideration:** raising the loophole-type items at markup will likely get them fixed by amendment. The team may prefer to raise them as evidence of a rushed, unworkable bill rather than one item at a time.
