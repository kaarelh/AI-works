# S. 2817: Drafting problems and loopholes (manager's amendment of Oct. 6, 2026)

Items are in section order, with general issues at the end. A short priority list follows. Legal-background points (items 12, 34 and 43) should be confirmed by counsel before the list goes to Senator Whitfield's office.

## Section 2: Definitions

1. **§2(1)(A), "single training run."** Developers can split a training effort into sub-threshold runs, or chain runs from shared checkpoints, merge models or distill from a larger one, and none of it aggregates. The text needs an aggregation or "cumulative compute" rule and an anti-structuring provision.
2. **§2(1)(A), fixed threshold.** The 10^26 figure is hard-coded with no authority to adjust it. Algorithmic efficiency gains will let systems of equal capability fall below it over time.
3. **§2(1)(A), measurement.** The text doesn't say what counts toward compute (pretraining, RL, post-training, synthetic data generation, inference-time compute), who measures it, or how it is verified. "Artificial intelligence model" is also undefined.
4. **§2(1)(A), proxy mismatch.** The operative text never defines or uses "superintelligence," and prong (A) bans any model above a compute line regardless of capability. That is over-inclusive (narrow models) and under-inclusive (efficient models), and it invites arbitrary-and-capricious or irrational-line challenges. There is also no waiver or exemption process.
5. **§2(1)(A)/(B), "model" vs. "system."** Prong (A) covers only a "model." A system built from several sub-threshold models, agents or scaffolding can exceed human performance without any single model crossing the line, and only the discretionary prong (B) could reach it.
6. **§2(1)(B), undefined "Secretary."** The Act never says which Secretary is meant (§4 names Commerce, §6 names State). The ambiguity affects who has authority and who can be sued.
7. **§2(1)(B), vague standard.** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" doesn't say which humans (median or expert), which tasks, or how it is measured. Paired with a 10-year felony, it is a prime void-for-vagueness and fair-notice target.
8. **§2(1)(B), no process.** There is no trigger, deadline, notice, public list, rulemaking, or judicial review, and no way for a developer to request a determination. If the Secretary determines coverage after training, it is unclear whether earlier conduct was unlawful. The provision also raises nondelegation and APA exposure.
9. **§2(2), "U.S. person" is too narrow.** It covers only citizens and "corporations." That omits lawful permanent residents, partnerships, trusts, unincorporated associations, and arguably LLCs, which many AI firms use.
10. **§2(2), "State" undefined.** It's unclear whether corporations organized in D.C., territories, or under tribal or federal charter qualify.
11. **§2(2), foreign subsidiaries.** A foreign-incorporated subsidiary of a U.S. company is not a "U.S. person" on this text, so a U.S. firm could move training offshore into a foreign entity. The bill needs ownership or control language.
12. **§2 and §3, "person" undefined.** The default definition in 1 U.S.C. §1 generally doesn't include government bodies. It is ambiguous whether federal agencies, military and intelligence programs, national labs, or state universities are covered or exempt. Either way, the bill should say so explicitly.
13. **§2(3), undefined terms.** "Advanced AI chip," "data center," and "operator" have no definitions and no performance threshold. Chips get more powerful, so a fixed unit count erodes as a proxy.
14. **§2(3), easy to evade.** Splitting capacity across sites under 50,000 chips, training across multiple facilities, or renting cloud capacity keeps operators below the line. A smaller cluster can also train a very large model over a longer period.

## Section 3: Prohibition

15. **§3(a), undefined verbs.** "Train," "develop," and "deploy" are not defined. "Develop" invites overbreadth and vagueness challenges. "Deploy" doesn't clearly cover internal use, such as automating a company's own AI R&D, or API access, hosting, or open-weight release.
16. **§3(a), missing conduct.** The bill does not reach possessing, selling, transferring, exporting, or publishing weights. It also doesn't reach funding, supplying compute for, or knowingly assisting a prohibited run, or using a system developed elsewhere.
17. **§3(a)/§2(1)(A), runs in progress.** A covered system is defined as a model already "trained using" more than 10^26 operations. It is ambiguous whether a run is unlawful before it crosses the line. There's no ban on runs intended to exceed the threshold and no duty to halt.
18. **§3(a), territorial gap.** A foreign company operating abroad and serving U.S. users isn't reached unless it has a presence in the U.S. Combined with the narrow definition of "U.S. person," offshore development is largely uncovered.
19. **§3(b), open-ended grandfathering.** Exempting any system whose training began before enactment appears to cover all later activity on it: continued training, scaling, fine-tuning, and deployment. Successors and derivatives built from the same lineage could also claim the exemption.
20. **§3(b), "training began" undefined.** Nothing requires documentation or a baseline registry of pre-enactment runs. That invites backdating and nominal early starts to qualify for the exemption.
21. **§3(b) and §2(1)(B), exemption swallows prong (B).** An existing system that is later determined to exceed human performance remains exempt, which undercuts the main purpose of the Act.
22. **§3(b), §5(a) and §8, timing gap.** Grandfathering keys off enactment, the prohibition starts 180 days later, and §5(a) penalizes violations "on or after the date of enactment." A run begun in the gap is not grandfathered, but it started when no prohibition applied. Defendants will argue lack of fair notice or impermissible retroactivity, and the gap invites rushed launches.
23. **§3(c), unbounded safety exemption.** "Safety evaluation" is undefined, tested only by subjective "purpose," and not limited by scale, entity, or oversight. It could shelter training a covered system under a safety label. It should be limited to evaluating already-existing systems, with approved entities, reporting, and no new training above threshold.

## Section 4: Registry

24. **§4, no enforcement hook.** §5 penalizes only violations of §3, so failing to register, file, or file accurately carries no civil penalty. A knowingly false statement may be reachable under 18 U.S.C. §1001, but nothing covers non-willful failures.
25. **§4, no implementing authority.** There is no rulemaking authority, registration deadline, content requirements, or inspection or audit power. The first report date is not specified.
26. **§4(b), "computing workloads" is vague.** Operators, especially cloud providers, may not know what tenants are running. The bill has no tenant or customer reporting and no know-your-customer duty.
27. **§4, no advance notice.** Quarterly, after-the-fact reporting gives regulators no chance to stop a large run. The bill needs pre-notification of runs above a compute level.
28. **§4, no confidentiality or sharing provisions.** There is no trade-secret or FOIA protection and no authority to share data with DOJ or other enforcers. That invites resistance and takings or privacy-style challenges.
29. **§4, limited scope.** The registry covers only facilities over the chip threshold. It does not track chip sales, cloud rentals, or foreign facilities used by U.S. persons.

## Section 5: Penalties

30. **§5, no enforcer.** The bill does not say who may bring civil actions, and it provides no injunctive authority, citizen suit, or private right of action. A statute with no designated enforcer risks going unenforced.
31. **§5(a), penalty size and counting.** A cap of $1M per day is small against frontier training costs and company valuations, and "not more than" sets no floor. It is also unclear how to count "days" for a one-time act like starting a training run, versus continuing acts such as deployment.
32. **§5, no remedies aimed at the system itself.** There is no authority to order a halt, deletion of weights, forfeiture, or disgorgement. Fines alone let a firm keep the model and pay.
33. **§5(a) vs. §8, internal conflict.** The civil penalty reaches violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §3 doesn't take effect until 180 days later. This should be harmonized.
34. **§5(b), "willfully" plus a fuzzy statute.** Willfulness generally requires knowledge that conduct is unlawful, and the vague definitions in §2 and the §3(c) exemption give defendants an easy argument that they lacked it. The bill also specifies no fine amount, so the default under 18 U.S.C. §3571 applies, which is low for organizations (verify with counsel). There is no officer or director liability language, and no tolling for concealment.
35. **§5, no whistleblower protection.** Lab insiders are the likeliest source of evidence of a covert run. The bill needs anti-retaliation protections and possibly rewards.

## Sections 6–8: International agreement, duration, effective date

36. **§6, soft mandate.** "Seek to negotiate" creates no obligation to reach an agreement. It's ambiguous whether the 18-month deadline is for starting or concluding talks, and "equivalent" prohibitions is undefined. There is no reporting to Congress, no consequence for failure, no verification, and no measures toward non-participants.
37. **§7(a) vs. §7(b), contradiction.** §7(a) says the prohibition lasts until Congress finds covered systems can be developed safely, but §7(b) ends the Act after 10 years regardless. The first becomes meaningless, and the bill's duration is really a fixed decade.
38. **§7(b), sunset problems.** It runs from enactment, so the prohibition is in force only about 9.5 years. There is also no savings clause for violations that occurred before the sunset, so accrued liability and prosecutions may lapse. A sunset sets a date to wait for, and it conflicts with the bill's own "until safe" standard.
39. **§7(a), undefined lifting process.** There is no requirement or timeline for the National Academies report, no definition of "safely," and no funding or independence safeguards. The report is also not binding on Congress.
40. **§8, 180-day delay.** The gap lets developers launch or accelerate runs before the ban applies, and combined with §3(b)'s enactment-date cutoff it creates the ambiguity in item 22. Consider an earlier effective date for registry, reporting, and anti-evasion provisions, plus a duty to halt runs in progress at the effective date.

## General and structural

41. **No severability clause.** If a court strikes down one provision, such as prong (B), the whole Act could be at risk.
42. **No findings or purpose section.** Findings would support the Commerce Clause basis, the extraterritorial reach, and tailoring against First Amendment (code and research as speech) and due process arguments. A jurisdictional element in §3 would also help.
43. **No implementation provisions.** The bill has no authorization of appropriations, no deadlines for regulations or guidance, and no judicial review or venue provision. It also has no coordination with export controls (BIS), where chip tracking and know-your-customer rules already operate.
44. **No preemption or savings clause.** It is unclear whether states may adopt stricter rules or whether existing authorities remain available. Silence invites litigation either way.
