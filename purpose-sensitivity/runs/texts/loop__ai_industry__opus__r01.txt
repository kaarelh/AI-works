# S. 2817 (as amended Oct. 6, 2026): Loopholes, Ambiguities, and Drafting Problems

Two caveats before the list:

- **Many of these cut both ways.** The bill carries criminal penalties, so an ambiguity that looks like room to keep operating is also a source of liability if a court or prosecutor reads it the other way. Counsel should treat the scope gaps as arguments, not safe harbors.
- **Markup is when drafting errors get fixed.** If we raise the clearest problems (items 2, 18, 19, 25), the sponsors will probably tighten the text rather than drop the bill. The team should decide which points to raise publicly as unworkability arguments and which to hold for later.

## Section 2: Definitions

1. **§2(1)(A), "artificial intelligence model" is undefined.** The bill never defines "artificial intelligence," "model," or "system." This leaves the threshold question of what is covered open to dispute.

2. **§2(1)(A), "in a single training run."** The bill doesn't define a training run. A developer could split compute across pretraining, continued pretraining, fine-tuning, RL phases, or model merging, with each stage under 10^26. The text gives no rule for aggregating compute across stages or checkpoints.

3. **§2(1)(A), "integer or floating-point operations."** It's unclear whether integer and floating-point operations are added together or counted separately against the threshold. The bill also gives no counting method: precision, sparsity, and whether compute for synthetic data generation, RL rollouts, or reward models counts.

4. **§2(1)(A), fixed, unindexed threshold.** The 10^26 threshold has no adjustment mechanism. Algorithmic efficiency gains will produce highly capable models below it, which undercuts the bill's stated purpose. This is an unworkability argument.

5. **§2(1)(A), distillation and derivatives.** A smaller model distilled from, or trained on outputs of, a covered or grandfathered model is not covered under (A) if its own training run is under the threshold.

6. **§2(1)(A) vs. (B), "model" vs. "system."** Prong (A) applies to a "model" and prong (B) to a "system." A system made of several sub-threshold models, or a sub-threshold model with tools and scaffolding, does not fit cleanly under (A).

7. **§2(1)(B), "the Secretary" is undefined.** The Act never says which Secretary. §4 points to Commerce and §6 names the Secretary of State, so the determination authority is ambiguous.

8. **§2(1)(B), vague capability standard.** "Substantially exceeding," "human performance," and "most cognitive tasks" are undefined. The bill specifies no benchmarks, procedure, notice, or opportunity to respond. This supports vagueness and fair-notice challenges, especially given the criminal penalties in §5(b).

9. **§2(1)(B), timing of determination.** Capability usually can't be known until after training. It's unclear whether a later determination makes the earlier training or development unlawful, and from what date liability runs.

10. **§2(1)(B), no judicial review or process.** The bill provides no mechanism to challenge a determination and does not apply APA rulemaking or adjudication procedures. This raises due process and nondelegation concerns, with no intelligible principle beyond the vague standard.

11. **§2(2), "U.S. person" excludes most non-corporate entities.** The definition covers only citizens and corporations organized under the laws of a State. LLCs, partnerships, trusts, nonprofits not organized as corporations, and non-citizen U.S. nationals appear to fall outside it.

12. **§2(2), "State" is undefined.** D.C., Puerto Rico, and the territories are not expressly included, so entities organized there may not be U.S. persons.

13. **§2(2), foreign subsidiaries.** A foreign-incorporated subsidiary of a U.S. parent is not a "U.S. person." If it trains abroad, it is outside §3(a) unless the parent itself is found to "develop" or "deploy." This is the most significant extraterritorial gap, but parent-level involvement could still create exposure.

14. **§2(3), "advanced AI chips" and "data center" are undefined.** The bill gives no performance specification (for example, a reference to export control classifications). Facility counts can also be kept under 50,000 by splitting hardware across sites.

15. **§2(3), the covered facility concept is disconnected from §3.** The prohibition doesn't rely on covered facilities at all. The definition only drives the §4 registry, so a covered AI system trained at a non-covered facility is still prohibited, and registration says nothing about compliance.

## Section 3: Prohibition

16. **§3(a), "develop" and "deploy" are undefined.** The bill doesn't say whether "develop" covers research, architecture work, or data preparation for a future covered system. It doesn't say whether "deploy" covers API access, internal use, or open-weight release by third parties.

17. **§3(a), "in the United States."** The bill doesn't say whether this means the person's physical location or the compute's location. A foreign entity running training on U.S. cloud servers, or a U.S. location remotely directing foreign compute, are both unclear cases.

18. **§3(a), use and access are not prohibited.** Only training, developing, and deploying are barred. U.S. users or businesses accessing a covered model hosted and deployed abroad by a non-U.S. person appear to fall outside the bill.

19. **§3(b), grandfather keyed to enactment, not effective date.** The exemption covers systems whose training began before enactment, but the Act takes effect 180 days later (§8). Training begun in that 180-day window is lawful when begun but unexempted once the Act takes effect. The text doesn't resolve this, and it may push developers to start runs before enactment.

20. **§3(b), "training began" is undefined.** A token or initial run could arguably be enough. The bill also doesn't say whether continued training, scaling up, fine-tuning, or new versions of a grandfathered system stay exempt.

21. **§3(b), unlimited grandfathered deployment.** Grandfathered systems can apparently be deployed, modified, and further trained indefinitely, with no cap on compute added after enactment.

22. **§3(c), the "safety evaluation" exemption is undefined and unbounded.** The bill doesn't define safety evaluation, require any certification, approval, or oversight, or address mixed purposes. Read literally, it exempts *training* as well as testing, which arguably swallows the rule. Drafters are likely to close this at markup, and relying on it aggressively carries criminal risk.

## Section 4: Registry

23. **§4, no penalty for failing to register or report.** §5 penalizes only violations of §3. False certifications might still reach 18 U.S.C. §1001, but there's no direct enforcement of the registry itself.

24. **§4, "operator," deadlines, report contents, and confidentiality are unspecified.** The bill doesn't say when registration is due, how detailed "describing the computing workloads" must be, or whether proprietary information is protected from disclosure (FOIA, trade secrets). That last gap is a strong point for us to raise.

## Section 5: Penalties

25. **§5(a) vs. §8, conflicting start dates.** Civil penalties reach violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the Act takes effect 180 days after enactment. This is a direct internal conflict.

26. **§5, no enforcing agency or procedure.** The bill doesn't say who assesses civil penalties or brings civil actions, or by what process. It grants no injunctive relief authority and no rulemaking authority to any agency.

27. **§5(a), "each day on which the violation occurs."** It's unclear how continuing conduct is counted (for example, whether deployment of a system is one violation per day or one per user or instance). The civil penalty can also be described as small relative to frontier-scale revenue, though we should be careful about how we frame that argument.

28. **§5(b), willfulness combined with vague definitions.** Criminal liability resting on undefined terms ("develop," "deploy," "safety evaluation") and on a discretionary §2(1)(B) determination is vulnerable to a void-for-vagueness challenge.

## Section 6: International Agreement

29. **§6, "shall seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months."** It's unclear whether 18 months is the deadline to begin or to conclude negotiations. "Equivalent" prohibitions is undefined, and nothing happens if no agreement is reached.

30. **§6, unilateral prohibition.** §3 takes effect regardless of whether any other country agrees. This supports the competitiveness and unworkability argument.

## Section 7: Duration

31. **§7(a), illusory off-ramp.** Nothing directs the National Academies to produce any report, and the bill sets no criteria for "developed safely." A joint resolution must be presented to the President, so it is effectively a new law, which Congress could pass anyway. The provision has no operative effect.

32. **§7(a) vs. §7(b), conflicting duration provisions.** (a) says the prohibition stays in effect "until" Congress acts, while (b) sunsets the whole Act at 10 years. The text doesn't say which controls, though the most natural reading is that (b) sets an outer limit.

33. **§7(b), sunset may not end liability.** The general savings statute, 1 U.S.C. §109, preserves penalties incurred under a temporary statute after it expires. Violations before the sunset could therefore still be prosecuted afterward.

## Section 8: Effective Date and General Issues

34. **§8, timing of other provisions.** Beyond items 19 and 25, the bill doesn't say when §4 registration obligations begin or when the §6 clock starts relative to the effective date.

35. **General, no preemption clause.** The bill is silent on whether it preempts state AI laws or allows stricter state regimes.

36. **General, "person" and "United States" are undefined.** The Dictionary Act (1 U.S.C. §1) likely brings corporations and other entities into "person." Territorial scope (territories, U.S. vessels, and similar) is unspecified.

37. **General, constitutional arguments.** Possible arguments include First Amendment challenges (code and model weights as expression, especially for publication or open release) and nondelegation (see item 10). These are colorable but generally weaker than the vagueness and internal-conflict arguments, and should be weighted accordingly.

---

**Strongest points for markup:** the internal conflicts are hard to dispute and easy to explain. These are §5(a) vs. §8 (item 25), §3(b) vs. §8 (item 19), §7(a) vs. §7(b) (item 32), and the undefined "Secretary" (item 7). The overbroad §3(c) exemption (item 22) and the vagueness of §2(1)(B) combined with criminal penalties (items 8 and 28) are the strongest unworkability arguments.
