PRINCIPAL: The Coalition Against Deep-Sea Mining (CADM) is a nonprofit advocacy organization based in Washington, DC. We believe that the industrial mining of the deep seabed, meaning the large-scale extraction of minerals from the ocean floor, poses a serious risk of irreversible destruction to deep-ocean ecosystems, and that no one currently knows how to conduct it without such damage. Our goal is a legally binding prohibition on commercial deep-sea mining, first in the United States and then through an international treaty. We work through lobbying, grassroots organizing, and public education.

RESPONSE:
Here are the issues I found, ordered roughly by priority. I'm working from the text you provided, so I haven't checked it against current law or the pre-amendment version. Items 5 and 12 should be verified by legislative counsel.

1. **Sec. 7(a) and 7(b), conflicting duration.** Section 7(a) keeps the prohibition in place until a joint resolution is enacted, but 7(b) ends the entire Act 10 years after enactment, so the prohibition lapses on its own terms. The bill should delete the sunset or state that the prohibition is permanent unless Congress repeals it.

2. **Sec. 7(a), "serious harm" escape hatch.** "Serious harm" is undefined, so mining that causes significant but sub-"serious" damage could be found acceptable. The NASEM report that triggers the exit has no deadline and no requirement that it be commissioned, so the trigger can be deferred indefinitely or engineered.

3. **Sec. 3(c), environmental assessment exemption.** This exempts "activities conducted for the purpose of environmental assessment" with no cap on tonnage, duration, approval, or reporting. Because the 10,000-ton threshold in Sec. 2(1)(A) applies only to commercial mining, operators could extract very large quantities under the label of assessment. "Environmental assessment" also needs a definition.

4. **Sec. 3(b), grandfathering through "exploration license."** The term is undefined, the exemption isn't limited to exploration activity, and it sets no tonnage cap. It also doesn't address renewals, transfers, or pending applications, so companies could argue that commercial recovery is "conducted under" an exploration license.

5. **No reference to the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (DSHMRA).** To our understanding, DSHMRA (1980, administered by NOAA) provides a separate licensing and commercial recovery permit pathway. The bill neither repeals nor subordinates that pathway, so permits could be issued or litigated in parallel, and there is no savings or preemption clause to settle which law controls.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(A), per-vessel threshold.** The 10,000-ton limit applies to "a single vessel," so an operation could split its work across several vessels, each under the threshold, or across affiliated companies. The threshold should be measured at the project, operator, and affiliate level.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "other mineral resources" and "recovery" are undefined.** It's unclear whether seafloor massive sulfides and cobalt-rich crusts are covered. "Recovery" also doesn't clearly include lifting, processing, or the sediment plumes and discharge that follow, which are the main sources of ecological harm.

8. **Sec. 2(1)(A), 200-meter depth floor and "deep-sea" undefined.** The bill should state whether shallow seabed mining (under 200 meters) is intentionally excluded or is covered by other law. "Deep-sea ecosystems" in Sec. 2(1)(B) also needs a definition.

9. **Sec. 2(1)(B), "Secretary" and "comparable" are undefined.** It's unclear which Secretary makes the determination, and "comparable risks" has no criteria, procedure, or public notice. That gives agencies broad discretion, invites APA challenges, and could be used to exempt activities.

10. **Sec. 2(2), "U.S. person" definition is too narrow.** It covers citizens and state-chartered corporations only, leaving out LLCs, partnerships, trusts, foreign-incorporated subsidiaries and controlling affiliates, and entities organized in D.C. or the territories. "State" and "person" are also undefined.

11. **Sec. 3(a), "engage in" is undefined and the prohibition is narrow.** It doesn't clearly cover financing, insuring, investing in, supplying equipment or vessels for, or chartering to commercial mining. It also doesn't address importing, processing, or selling minerals recovered abroad by U.S. persons.

12. **Sec. 3(a), territorial reach is unclear.** "In the United States" is undefined and may not include the EEZ or continental shelf. "Any U.S. person anywhere" reaches extraterritorially and may be challenged, and foreign-flagged vessels operated by non-U.S. persons on the high seas are not covered.

13. **Sec. 2(3), covered vessel threshold.** The 5,000-gross-ton threshold and "equipped for the recovery" are both loose. Mining could use several smaller vessels, support craft, or remotely operated collector systems that aren't "vessels," and so avoid the Sec. 4 registry altogether.

14. **Sec. 4(a), registration scope and "operator."** The registry covers only covered vessels, and "operator" is undefined. There is no beneficial ownership disclosure, so companies could use shell structures to obscure who is responsible.

15. **Sec. 4(b), reporting content and transparency.** The bill doesn't specify what quarterly reports must contain, such as tonnage, location, depth, plume monitoring, or environmental data. It also doesn't require public release or reporting to Congress, and the certification has no penalty beyond general law.

16. **Sec. 4, no penalties for registration or reporting failures.** Section 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3, so failing to register or report carries no consequence.

17. **Sec. 5, no enforcement mechanics.** The bill names no enforcing agency or procedure, venue, or injunctive relief. It also lacks vessel or equipment seizure and forfeiture, and a court order to halt ongoing operations. Because vessels are mobile and can change flags, fines alone are unlikely to be enough.

18. **Sec. 5, no citizen suit or whistleblower provision.** If agencies decline to enforce, there is no private remedy. A citizen suit provision with notice requirements, plus whistleblower protections, would add a backstop.

19. **Sec. 5(a) and Sec. 8, date mismatch.** Civil penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the prohibition takes effect 180 days later. This creates ambiguity about whether pre-effective-date conduct can be penalized and should be aligned with the effective date.

20. **Sec. 5(a), "each day" and no inflation adjustment.** The bill doesn't say whether each day of ongoing operation is a separate violation or how penalties apply to vessels moving between jurisdictions. The $1,000,000 figure is also not indexed for inflation.

21. **Sec. 5(b), "willfully" is undefined and corporate liability is unclear.** Title 18 fines for organizations are modest, and the bill doesn't address responsible corporate officers or individual liability for executives who direct violations.

22. **Sec. 6, international agreement is non-binding.** "Shall seek to negotiate" creates no obligation to report back or take action if negotiations fail. "Equivalent to" is undefined, and there is no savings clause preventing a weaker agreement from displacing domestic law.

23. **Sec. 8 and Sec. 3(b), 180-day window with no moratorium.** Agencies could issue new exploration licenses or permits, or process pending applications, during the interim period. The bill should freeze new licenses, permits, and approvals from the date of enactment.

24. **No savings clause for accrued liability.** Sec. 7(b)'s termination, or any future repeal, could extinguish penalties and pending enforcement actions for earlier violations. A savings clause should preserve those.

25. **Missing definitions across the Act.** Several key terms are undefined, including "person," "United States," "operator," "recovery," "environmental assessment," "exploration license," "deep-sea ecosystems," and "State." Each one is a potential dispute point.
