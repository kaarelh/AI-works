RESPONSE:
Here are the drafting issues I found in the October 6 text. These are readings of the language as written, not legal conclusions.

1. **§2(1)(A)**: The 10,000-ton threshold applies "by a single vessel," so an operator could split a project across several vessels that each stay under the limit. The definition also doesn't say how to count tonnage when a collector vessel and a riser or lift vessel work together.

2. **§2(1)(A)**: "Other mineral resources" is undefined, so it's unclear whether sand, gravel, cobalt crusts, or seafloor sulfides are covered. The definition also covers only recovery from the seabed, not processing, sediment discharge, or plume-generating activity.

3. **§2(1)(A)**: The 200-meter depth floor excludes all seabed mining shallower than that, regardless of its ecological impact.

4. **§2(1)(B)**: The catch-all is discretionary but underspecified. "The Secretary" is never identified (the bill elsewhere names the Secretary of State and NOAA), "comparable" has no standard, and there is no procedure or deadline for the determination. Any determination, or failure to make one, could be challenged as arbitrary.

5. **§2(2)**: "U.S. person" covers citizens and corporations organized under state law, but not LLCs, partnerships, or federally chartered entities. The bill also doesn't say whether "State" includes D.C. or the territories, and a foreign-incorporated operator with no U.S. nexus falls outside the "anywhere" reach of §3(a).

6. **§2(3) and §4**: The 5,000-gross-ton "covered vessel" threshold uses different units from the metric-ton test in §2(1). Vessels below it are not subject to the registry in §4, even if they conduct prohibited mining. "Equipped for" is also undefined, so it's unclear whether removable collection gear counts.

7. **§3(a)**: "Engage in" is undefined and does not clearly reach financing, equipment supply, purchase, import, processing, or sale of recovered minerals. There is no import ban, and no clause covering aiding, abetting, conspiracy, or attempt.

8. **§3(a)**: "In the United States" is not defined geographically. It's unclear whether it reaches the U.S. exclusive economic zone, the outer continental shelf, or the extended continental shelf. The bill also gives no flag-state or boarding authority over foreign-flagged vessels on the high seas.

9. **§3(b)**: "Exploration license" is undefined and no issuing authority is named. Because exploration authorizations may not be limited to exploration, this clause could be read to allow commercial recovery under an exploration permit, with no limits on volume, location, or duration.

10. **§3(b), §5(a), and §8**: Grandfathering and penalties are keyed to the date of enactment, but the prohibition takes effect 180 days later. This leaves a window in which commercial mining is not prohibited for anyone, while §5(a) penalizes violations from enactment onward, which is internally inconsistent.

11. **§3(c)**: "Activities conducted for the purpose of environmental assessment" is undefined and is the broadest carve-out in the bill. It has no volume cap, permit requirement, reporting duty, or requirement that results be published, so commercial-scale recovery could be labeled as assessment.

12. **§4(a)**: "Operator" is undefined. The registration has no deadline, no required content, and no public access, and it applies only to covered vessels.

13. **§4(b)**: Quarterly reports are self-certified and have no required content (tonnage, location, depth, or discharge), no audit or inspection authority, and no penalty. Because §5 penalizes only §3 violations, failing to register or filing a false report carries no civil penalty under the Act.

14. **§5 (overall)**: The bill does not designate an enforcing agency or grant investigation, subpoena, inspection, seizure, or forfeiture authority over vessels or equipment. It also provides no injunctive relief, no citizen-suit provision, and no venue or jurisdiction clause for offenses committed abroad.

15. **§5(a)**: The "each day" penalty doesn't define a continuing violation or explain how days are counted. The bill also omits an inflation adjustment and a limitations period.

16. **§5(b)**: "Willfully" is undefined, and the section does not address individual officers or directors. It also doesn't cover false statements in §4 filings.

17. **§6**: "Seek to negotiate" is hortatory: the bill doesn't require an agreement, a report to Congress, or any consequence if none is reached. It also doesn't say whether a treaty would override the Act, what "equivalent" prohibitions means, or how the Act relates to the International Seabed Authority and U.S. status under UNCLOS.

18. **§7(a)**: "Serious harm" is undefined. The bill doesn't require NASEM to produce its report or set a deadline, so the lifting trigger may never occur. It also doesn't say whether a joint resolution is subject to presidential signature or veto, or whether the "finding" lifts the prohibition or only declares it.

19. **§7(a) and §7(b)**: These conflict directly. Under §7(a) the prohibition lasts until Congress acts, but §7(b) ends the entire Act 10 years after enactment regardless. It's unclear whether the prohibition expires at year 10 even without a joint resolution. Because the clock runs from enactment, the effective period is about 9.5 years.

20. **§7(b)**: There is no savings clause. "Cease to have effect" leaves open whether violations committed during the term, or pending enforcement actions, can still be prosecuted or penalized afterward.

21. **§§2–8 (overall)**: The bill grants no rulemaking authority. No agency is authorized to issue regulations, define undefined terms, set registry or reporting standards, or make the §2(1)(B) determination, and the 180-day window before the prohibition takes effect is not tied to any implementing action.

**Priorities for the markup:** The most consequential issues for the explainer are likely the per-vessel threshold (#1), the environmental-assessment carve-out (#11), the sunset conflict (#19), the timing gap (#10), and the enforcement gaps (#13–14, #21).
