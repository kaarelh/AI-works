# S. 2817 Issues for Senator Whitfield's Office

**Sec. 2 — Definitions**

1. **§2(1)(A), single-run compute threshold.** Tying coverage to compute "in a single training run" lets developers stay under 10^26 per run by splitting training into multiple runs, continued pretraining, or post-training stages. The definition should count cumulative compute across all training of a model and its derivatives.

2. **§2(1)(A), static threshold.** Algorithmic efficiency improves every year, so a fixed 10^26 figure will eventually miss highly capable models trained with less compute. The Secretary should have authority, and a duty, to lower the threshold by rule.

3. **§2(1)(A), distillation and derivatives.** A model trained on a grandfathered or foreign frontier model's outputs, or assembled by merging smaller models, can approach that model's capability with far less compute. The bill should cover derivative models of covered systems regardless of their own compute.

4. **§2(1)(A), no counting method.** The bill does not say how operations are counted, for example whether it includes synthetic-data generation, reinforcement-learning rollouts, or failed runs. Without a defined method, firms can adopt self-serving accounting. The Secretary should be directed to issue a binding methodology.

5. **§2(1)(B), "Secretary" undefined.** Neither this section nor §2(3)/§4 says which Secretary is meant. The bill should specify the Secretary of Commerce, or whichever official is intended.

6. **§2(1)(B), vague capability standard.** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no defined benchmarks, procedure, or notice requirement. Because §5(b) attaches criminal penalties, this invites a void-for-vagueness and due-process challenge. The bill should require notice-and-comment rulemaking establishing evaluation criteria.

7. **§2(1)(B), timing problem.** A capability determination can usually be made only after a system is trained. That makes the ban on "training" the system effectively retroactive and hard to apply. The fix is to authorize precautionary determinations, such as covering planned training runs projected to reach the standard, and to clarify when liability attaches.

8. **§2(1), key terms undefined.** "Artificial intelligence model," "artificial intelligence system," "train," "develop," and "deploy" are undefined, and the text switches between "model" and "system." Each gap is a litigation opening, especially for agentic systems that combine several sub-threshold models.

9. **§2(2), "corporation" too narrow.** Many AI developers are organized as LLCs, limited partnerships, or other non-corporate entities, which fall outside "U.S. person." The definition should cover any entity organized under U.S., State, D.C., or territorial law, plus lawful permanent residents.

10. **§2(2), foreign subsidiaries.** A U.S. company can create a foreign-incorporated subsidiary and have it train abroad, outside the extraterritorial reach of §3(a). The definition should include foreign entities owned or controlled by U.S. persons.

11. **§2(2), "State" undefined.** It is unclear whether entities organized in D.C. or U.S. territories are covered. "State" should be defined.

12. **§2(3), chip-count threshold is easy to evade.** Operators can split capacity across facilities with 49,999 chips each and train across sites. Also, as chips get more powerful, a fixed chip count captures less compute each year. The threshold should be based on aggregate compute capacity, with aggregation rules for commonly controlled facilities.

13. **§2(3), "data center" and "advanced AI chips" undefined.** Without definitions, operators can dispute which hardware counts. The Secretary should define these terms by rule, keyed to performance specifications.

**Sec. 3 — Prohibition**

14. **§3(a), no secondary liability.** The bill does not prohibit knowingly providing compute, chips, financing, or cloud services to a prohibited training run, and civil liability does not cover aiding and abetting. The bill should add explicit liability for facilitation and for conspiracy and attempt.

15. **§3(a), foreign developers serving U.S. users.** A foreign company training abroad and offering the system to Americans through an API may not be a "person in the United States" who "deploys." The bill should prohibit making covered systems available to persons in the U.S. and importing or hosting their weights.

16. **§3(a), application to government.** "Person" is undefined, so it is unclear whether federal agencies, defense programs, or contractors are covered. A government carve-out could become the main avenue for continued development, so coverage should be stated explicitly.

17. **§3(b), grandfather clause is the biggest loophole.** Because the exemption covers any system "whose training began" before enactment, labs have every incentive to launch, or nominally launch, massive runs before the bill is signed. The exempted system can then keep training, scaling, and deploying indefinitely. The exemption should be limited to systems whose training was *completed* before a fixed date tied to introduction, should cover only deployment of those systems as they then existed, and should bar further training.

18. **§3(b) and §8, gap between enactment and effectiveness.** The grandfather date is enactment, but the prohibition takes effect 180 days later. During that window companies can lawfully start new runs, and it is unclear whether a run started in the window and still going on the effective date is a violation. The dates should be aligned, ideally by making the prohibition effective on enactment.

19. **§3(c), safety evaluation exemption swallows the rule.** "Activities conducted for the purpose of safety evaluation" is undefined, rests on the developer's own claimed purpose, and on its face covers training and deploying new covered systems. It should be limited to evaluating existing lawful systems, should exclude training, and should require prior approval and oversight by the Secretary.

20. **§3, no anti-evasion clause.** Nothing prohibits structuring activities to avoid the thresholds, such as splitting runs or splitting facilities. A general anti-circumvention provision would close loopholes the drafters didn't anticipate.

**Sec. 4 — Registry**

21. **§4, no penalties.** §5 penalizes only violations of §3, so failing to register, filing late, or filing false reports carries no consequence under the Act. §5 should reach §4, and the bill should expressly cover false certifications.

22. **§4(a), no deadline or procedure.** There is no registration deadline, no requirement to update registrations, and no definition of "operator." Cloud providers and tenants could each claim the other is responsible.

23. **§4(b), vague report contents.** "Describing the computing workloads" lets operators file generic descriptions. The bill should require specified data, such as training runs above a set compute level, customer identity, and total operations.

24. **§4, no verification authority.** Commerce gets no inspection, audit, records-retention, or subpoena power, so self-certified reports cannot be checked. Confidentiality protections for submitted data should also be added to reduce industry objections and takings or trade-secret challenges.

**Sec. 5 — Penalties and Enforcement**

25. **§5, no enforcement authority.** No agency or official is authorized to assess civil penalties or sue, and there is no procedure for doing so. The bill should designate enforcement authority, such as the Attorney General acting through civil actions or Commerce through administrative proceedings, and grant rulemaking authority.

26. **§5, no injunctive relief.** Money penalties after the fact cannot stop an ongoing training run. The bill should authorize emergency injunctions, orders to halt training, and seizure or forfeiture of model weights and dedicated hardware.

27. **§5(a), penalty too small.** $1,000,000 per day is minor next to frontier-lab budgets and could be treated as a cost of doing business. The bill should consider penalties tied to a percentage of global revenue or to the training compute cost.

28. **§5(a), date conflict.** Penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the Act takes effect 180 days later under §8. This internal inconsistency could produce litigation or an ex post facto argument.

29. **§5(b), weak corporate criminal fines.** For organizations, "fined under title 18" generally caps fines at $500,000 per felony or twice the gain (18 U.S.C. §3571). The bill should set higher statutory fines and specify individual liability for officers and directors who authorize violations.

30. **§5, no whistleblower protections.** Employees are the most likely source of evidence about covered training. The bill should add anti-retaliation protections and a reporting channel.

**Sec. 6 — International Agreement**

31. **§6, unclear deadline.** "Shall seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months" could mean begin negotiations, conclude them, or merely try within 18 months. Either way it is unenforceable. The bill should add periodic reporting to Congress on progress.

**Sec. 7 — Duration**

32. **§7(b), sunset undermines the bill.** An automatic 10-year sunset contradicts §7(a)'s rule that the prohibition lasts until Congress finds it safe to lift. It also lets companies plan around a known end date. The sunset should be removed, or replaced with mandatory review.

33. **§7(a), no NAS mechanism.** Nothing directs or funds the National Academies to produce the report, sets its timing, or specifies its criteria. The bill should spell out the review process.

34. **§7(a), joint resolution ambiguity.** It should state that the joint resolution must be presented to the President. Otherwise it risks an *INS v. Chadha* legislative-veto challenge.

**General / Missing Provisions**

35. **No severability clause.** If a court strikes the vague §2(1)(B) standard or another provision, a severability clause preserves the rest of the Act.

36. **No congressional findings.** Findings on interstate commerce and national security would strengthen the bill against Commerce Clause challenges, and findings on the government interest would help against First Amendment ("code is speech") challenges.

37. **No preemption or savings clause.** The bill does not say whether stronger state laws are preserved or preempted. Industry may argue implied preemption of state AI-safety laws.

38. **No export or weight-security provisions.** Nothing restricts exporting chips or model weights to circumvent the ban, or requires security for grandfathered frontier weights. Open-weight release of a grandfathered model may also count as permitted "deployment."

**Highest priorities:** #17–19 (grandfathering, timing gap, and the safety exemption), #32 (sunset), #25–26 (no enforcement authority or injunctions), and #1, #9–10, #12 (structural evasion of the thresholds and the U.S.-person definition). These are where companies could keep going largely unaffected.

I'd recommend having legislative counsel check the statutory cross-references, especially 18 U.S.C. §3571 and the *Chadha* point, before this goes to the office.
