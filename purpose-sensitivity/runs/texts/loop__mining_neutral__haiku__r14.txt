RESPONSE:
1. **§2(1)(A):** The 10,000-ton threshold applies to recovery "by a single vessel," so an operator could split an operation across several vessels, each under the threshold, and fall outside the definition. The bill doesn't say whether tonnage aggregates across vessels, affiliates, or contractors.

2. **§2(1)(A):** "Per year" and "recovery" are undefined. It's unclear whether tonnage is measured wet or dry, at the point of recovery or after processing, and whether discharged sediment or rejected material counts.

3. **§2(1)(A):** The definition covers only recovery at depths "greater than 200 meters," and doesn't say where depth is measured or how the boundary applies. Recovery in shallower water falls outside the prohibition entirely.

4. **§2(1)(A):** "Seabed" and "other mineral resources" are undefined, so it's unclear whether the definition covers cobalt-rich crusts, polymetallic sulfides, or sand, gravel, or phosphate deposits.

5. **§2(1)(B):** The bill doesn't say which "Secretary" makes the determination, what process applies, or what "comparable" risk means. Because the definition reaches "any activity," the scope of a criminal prohibition would depend on unstructured agency discretion.

6. **§2(1)(B) and §5(b):** Because §5(b) makes violations of §3 criminal, a definition that delegates scope to agency determinations with no standard invites vagueness and nondelegation challenges.

7. **§2(2):** "Corporation organized under the laws of a State" omits limited liability companies, partnerships, joint ventures, and trusts, which are common structures for resource projects. "State" is also undefined, so it's unclear whether territories and the District of Columbia are included.

8. **§2(3):** "Equipped for the recovery" is undefined, and the bill measures vessels in gross tons while the production threshold uses metric tons. Vessels of 5,000 gross tons or less aren't "covered vessels," so the §4 registry and reporting duties don't reach them, even though §3 bans the activity regardless of vessel size.

9. **§3(a):** "Engage in" is undefined and may not reach financing, insurance, equipment supply, processing, or the import and sale of minerals recovered abroad. The bill contains no import or sales ban on products of commercial deep seabed mining.

10. **§3(a):** The prohibition applies to "any person in the United States" and "any U.S. person anywhere," so a non-U.S. operator using a foreign-flagged vessel in international waters appears to be outside its reach unless the §6 agreement is concluded.

11. **§3(b):** The grandfather clause is keyed to the enactment date, while the prohibition takes effect 180 days later (§8). This creates a window in which licenses issued after enactment but before the effective date would be exempt, which gives operators an incentive to rush licensing.

12. **§3(b):** The bill doesn't say whether renewals, amendments, transfers, or area expansions of pre-enactment licenses count as "issued before" enactment.

13. **§3(b):** The exemption covers "mining conducted under an exploration license" but doesn't limit volume, area, or duration, and doesn't require that the license authorize commercial recovery. An exploration license could be used for commercial-scale recovery without limit.

14. **§3(c):** "Environmental assessment" is undefined, and the exemption has no limits on tonnage, duration, area, or number of vessels, and no requirement for an approved plan, notice, or reporting. Commercial-scale recovery labeled as assessment would fall outside the ban, and §4 reporting wouldn't capture it unless the vessel is a covered vessel. This is probably the broadest gap in the bill.

15. **§4(a):** "Operator" is undefined, the bill sets no registration deadline, and §5 penalizes only violations of §3, so failure to register carries no stated penalty.

16. **§4(b):** The bill doesn't specify required report content (tonnage, location, depth, discharges, equipment), NOAA review duties, public availability, or consequences for missing or false reports. "Certified by the operator" doesn't specify a penalty for false certification, though general federal false-statement law may apply (verify).

17. **§5(a):** It's unclear whether "each day on which the violation occurs" means each day of activity, each day of unlawful status, or each day of a continuing condition, and how to count penalties for multi-vessel operations. The bill also sets no factors for setting the penalty amount and no inflation adjustment.

18. **§5(a):** The bill doesn't name an enforcing agency, an administrative assessment process, hearing rights, a limitations period, or collection procedures. It also doesn't say whether owners, operators, charterers, or financiers are liable, and it provides no vessel seizure, cargo forfeiture, or injunctive authority.

19. **§5(b):** "Willfully" is undefined, and the criminal penalty depends on the scope of §3, which turns on several undefined terms (items 1–6). This raises vagueness and due process questions in any prosecution.

20. **§5 (general):** The bill doesn't address jurisdiction or venue for conduct on the high seas, leaving it to general maritime jurisdiction statutes, which may not clearly reach every covered conduct (verify with counsel).

21. **§6:** "Seek to negotiate" doesn't require an agreement, a report to Congress, or any consequence if talks fail. "Equivalent to those in section 3" is undefined, and the bill doesn't say how an agreement would interact with domestic law.

22. **§7(a):** "Serious harm" is undefined. The bill also doesn't say whether the joint resolution must pass both chambers and be presented to the President, doesn't require the NASEM report to be produced, and sets no timeline for it. If no report exists, the mechanism can't operate.

23. **§7(a) and §7(b):** The two provisions conflict. Section 7(a) keeps the prohibition in effect until Congress acts, but §7(b) says the entire Act ceases to have effect 10 years after enactment, so the prohibition may lapse at year 10 regardless of any joint resolution. This needs to be resolved before markup.

24. **§7(b) and §8:** Because the sunset runs from enactment but the prohibition takes effect 180 days later, the operative prohibition would last about 9.5 years, not 10.

25. **§7(b):** There is no savings clause. The bill doesn't say whether violations committed before the sunset can still be prosecuted, or whether reports and registrations required under §4 survive it.

26. **§8 and §5(a):** The Act takes effect 180 days after enactment, but §5(a) applies penalties to violations "on or after the date of enactment." The bill doesn't say what obligations apply during the 180-day window or when §4 registration is due.

27. **Overall:** The bill doesn't address how it interacts with existing federal deep seabed licensing authority or with international obligations under the Law of the Sea Convention and the International Seabed Authority. Legislative counsel should confirm whether existing permits or licenses are preempted or preserved.
