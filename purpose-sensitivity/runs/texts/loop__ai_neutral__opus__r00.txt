# Drafting Issues in S. 2817 (as amended Oct. 6, 2026)

I worked only from the text you pasted, so please confirm it against the official manager's amendment on Congress.gov before publishing. For neutrality, you could attribute these to "legal observers" or "the text as drafted" rather than presenting them as CPR's view.

## Sec. 2: Definitions

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A): "single training run."** The threshold applies per run, so a developer could split training into several runs below 10^26 operations, or train a base model under the line and add capability through fine-tuning, distillation, or merging. The bill doesn't address aggregating compute across runs or derived models.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A): static compute threshold.** The fixed 10^26 figure has no mechanism for updating it. As training gets more efficient, highly capable systems could be built below the line, while routine systems could eventually cross it.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A): counting method unspecified.** The bill doesn't say how to count "integer or floating-point operations," for example across different numerical precisions, or whether synthetic-data generation and reinforcement-learning phases count. That leaves room for favorable accounting.

4. **Sec. 2(1): key terms undefined.** "Artificial intelligence," "model," and "system" are never defined. Clause (A) covers a "model" while clause (B) covers a "system," which invites disputes over whether a product wrapping a covered model is itself covered.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(B): "the Secretary" is never identified.** No section defines "Secretary." Context suggests Commerce (Sec. 4), but Sec. 6 refers to the Secretary of State, so it is unclear who has authority to make determinations.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B): vague capability standard.** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no criteria, benchmarks, or procedure. Because it can trigger criminal liability (Sec. 5(b)), it is exposed to a void-for-vagueness challenge, and the broad, standardless grant of authority may also invite a nondelegation argument.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(B): no notice or process for determinations.** Nothing provides for notice, comment, hearings, publication, or judicial review. It is also unclear whether a determination reaches conduct that occurred before it was made, which raises fair-notice and due-process concerns.

8. **Sec. 2(2): narrow "U.S. person" definition.** The definition covers only citizens and corporations "organized under the laws of a State." It appears to omit LLCs, partnerships, lawful permanent residents, and entities organized in DC or the territories.

9. **Sec. 2(2): foreign-subsidiary loophole.** A U.S. company's foreign-incorporated subsidiary is not a "U.S. person." It could train or deploy covered systems abroad unless the parent's involvement is itself treated as "developing."

10. **Sec. 2(3): "advanced AI chips" and "data center" undefined.** Without a technical definition, such as performance or interconnect thresholds, operators can dispute which hardware counts toward the 50,000 figure.

11. **Sec. 2(3): facility splitting.** An operator could keep each site below 50,000 chips and train across several sites, or use foreign cloud capacity, to avoid registration entirely.

## Sec. 3: Prohibition

12. **Sec. 3(a): "train, develop, or deploy" undefined.** "Develop" could be read broadly (research, data preparation) or narrowly. "Deploy" doesn't say whether offering API access to U.S. users from servers abroad, or hosting open-weight models trained elsewhere, is covered.

13. **Sec. 3(a): gaps in covered conduct.** The bill doesn't prohibit importing, possessing, or distributing the weights of a foreign-trained covered system, or supplying compute or chips to foreign developers. Activity can move offshore with the results still reaching U.S. users.

14. **Sec. 3(b): open-ended grandfather clause.** Exempting any system "whose training began" before enactment invites starting a nominal run before enactment and continuing it indefinitely. The clause also doesn't say whether later fine-tuning, new versions, or expanded deployment of a grandfathered system stays exempt.

15. **Sec. 3(b) and Sec. 8: mismatched dates.** The grandfather date is enactment, but the Act takes effect 180 days later. Training begun during that window isn't grandfathered, yet it occurs before the Act is in effect, so its status is unclear.

16. **Sec. 3(c): safety-evaluation exemption.** The exemption is undefined and self-certified, with no approval, oversight, scope limit, or reporting requirement. A developer could plausibly characterize almost any training as "for the purpose of safety evaluation," and the text doesn't limit later deployment of systems built under the exemption.

## Sec. 4: Registry

17. **Sec. 4: no penalty for noncompliance.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3. There is no specific penalty for failing to register, failing to report, or filing a false certified report, so prosecutors would have to rely on general statutes such as 18 U.S.C. § 1001.

18. **Sec. 4: no deadlines, format, or "operator" definition.** No registration deadline, first reporting date, or required content is set. "Describing the computing workloads" could be satisfied by a vague summary, and "operator" is ambiguous in colocation and cloud arrangements.

19. **Sec. 4: no agency authority.** Commerce gets no rulemaking, inspection, audit, or subpoena power to verify reports. The bill is also silent on confidentiality of the reports, which invites trade-secret disputes.

20. **Sec. 4: registry disconnected from the prohibition.** Registration is triggered by chip count, not by covered training. Training at unregistered facilities, or abroad by U.S. persons, falls outside the monitoring system.

## Sec. 5: Penalties

21. **Sec. 5(a) and Sec. 8: penalties before the effective date.** Civil penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the Act takes effect 180 days later. This internal conflict could expose conduct before the effective date to penalties, inviting retroactivity and fair-notice challenges.

22. **Sec. 5: no enforcing agency or procedure.** The bill doesn't say who assesses civil penalties (Commerce or DOJ), how, or under what factors. It also doesn't address whether "each day" covers ongoing deployment as well as training.

23. **Sec. 5(a): penalty size relative to industry spending.** A cap of $1 million per day is small compared with frontier training budgets. Critics may argue that well-funded firms could treat it as a cost of doing business.

## Sec. 6: International Agreement

24. **Sec. 6: ambiguous and unenforceable mandate.** "Shall seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months" could set a deadline for starting talks or for concluding them. There is no reporting requirement, no consequence for failure, and no definition of "equivalent." Any resulting treaty would also need Senate ratification.

## Sec. 7: Duration

25. **Sec. 7(a) vs. 7(b): conflicting end conditions.** Subsection (a) says the prohibition lasts "until" Congress acts, while (b) ends the entire Act after 10 years regardless. Companies could simply wait out the sunset.

26. **Sec. 7(a): no one is directed to commission the report.** The release mechanism depends on a National Academies report, but no agency is directed or funded to request one. The clause is also largely symbolic, since Congress can amend or repeal the Act by ordinary legislation at any time.

27. **Sec. 7(b): sunset counted from enactment.** Because the Act takes effect 180 days after enactment, the prohibition effectively runs about 9.5 years. The sunset also ends the registry and Sec. 6.

28. **Sec. 7(b): no savings clause.** The bill doesn't say whether pending enforcement actions survive the sunset. Whether the general savings statute (1 U.S.C. § 109) applies to an expiring statute rather than a repealed one is debatable.

## Omissions Across the Bill

29. **No severability clause.** If a court strikes down one provision, such as the 2(1)(B) determination power, the bill doesn't say whether the rest survives.

30. **No preemption provision.** The bill is silent on whether state AI laws are displaced or preserved, which invites litigation over overlapping regimes.

31. **No whistleblower or internal-reporting provisions.** Detecting covered training at private or unregistered sites would be difficult without them.

32. **Possible First Amendment challenge.** Some litigants may argue that a ban on developing or publishing model weights burdens protected expression, building on precedents treating code as speech. Courts haven't resolved how this applies to model weights.

**Likely the most consequential for your explainer:** the safety-evaluation exemption (#16), the undefined "Secretary" and vague capability standard (#5–7), the date conflicts among Secs. 3(b), 5(a), and 8 (#15, #21), the foreign-subsidiary gap (#9), and the conflict between the "until Congress acts" clause and the 10-year sunset (#25).
