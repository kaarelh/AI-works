RESPONSE:
Here's a section-by-section list. I've flagged each item as mainly a **[loophole]** (a path to keep operating as written), **[ambiguity]**, or **[drafting defect]**; many are more than one.

## Sec. 2(1)(A) — compute threshold

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "single training run" is undefined. [loophole]** Modern training is already staged (pretraining, continued pretraining, mid-training, SFT, RL, each resuming from checkpoints). A developer can divide training into any number of "runs" each below 10^26 and the model never qualifies; the definition contains no aggregation rule across runs, checkpoints, or models initialized from other models.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — no counting methodology. [ambiguity]** The bill does not say how to count operations: whether sparse/MoE operations count as executed or theoretical, whether FP4/FP8 and FP32 operations count equally, whether to include forward passes only, data-generation compute, reward models, or evaluation compute. Without a counting rule the threshold is unverifiable and a defendant can reasonably choose the most favorable method.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — compute used outside "training" is excluded. [loophole]** Synthetic-data generation, distillation from a larger teacher, and inference-time/test-time compute scaling are not "training" operations, so a model could reach the capability the bill targets while staying under 10^26 training operations.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "computing power" vs. "operations." [drafting]** The text defines the threshold as a "quantity of computing power" measured in "operations"; computing power is a rate, operations are a count. It also mixes "integer or floating-point operations" without stating whether both are summed.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — static threshold with no adjustment mechanism. [drafting/unworkable]** Nothing in the Act lets anyone update 10^26 over its ten-year life. Algorithmic efficiency gains mean sub-threshold models will match today's frontier within a few years (the threshold under-captures), while falling compute costs will sweep ordinary commercial models above it (it over-captures). Either way the number stops tracking "superintelligence."

6. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "artificial intelligence model" and "artificial intelligence" are undefined. [ambiguity]** The Act never says what an AI model is, so it is unclear whether, e.g., a large physics simulation, a search index, or a protein-folding model counts.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(A) vs. (B) — "model" vs. "system." [ambiguity/loophole]** Prong (A) covers a "model"; prong (B) and the defined term cover a "system." A system assembled from several sub-threshold models (ensembles, agent scaffolds, routers) is arguably not a covered "model" under (A), and is only covered under (B) if and when the Secretary acts.

## Sec. 2(1)(B) — Secretary's determination

8. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — "the Secretary" is undefined. [drafting]** No Secretary is defined in Sec. 2. The only Secretary named in the Act is the Secretary of State (Sec. 6), while the registry is at Commerce (Sec. 4); the text literally supports either reading, and arguably neither.

9. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — no process, standard, notice, or judicial review. [drafting/unworkable]** The Secretary "determines" with no rulemaking, hearing, evidentiary standard, timeline, publication requirement, or appeal. A determination that converts lawful conduct into a 10-year felony with no procedural protections invites APA and due-process challenges.

10. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — nothing is covered under this prong until a determination is made. [loophole]** Because coverage depends on an affirmative determination, a system that exceeds human performance is lawful to train and deploy until the Secretary acts, and the Act does not say whether a determination can apply retroactively (criminal retroactivity would raise ex post facto problems).

11. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — "substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks." [ambiguity]** No benchmark, no baseline population (median human? expert?), no definition of "cognitive task," no way to count what "most" means. This is the vagueness that a criminal defendant would lead with.

## Sec. 2(2) — U.S. person

12. **Sec. 2(2) — excludes most entity types. [loophole]** Only "corporations" organized under State law are U.S. persons. LLCs, limited partnerships, trusts, and other non-corporate entities are not, nor are corporations chartered under federal law or (depending on whether "State" includes them) D.C. and the territories.

13. **Sec. 2(2) — excludes foreign subsidiaries and affiliates. [loophole]** A Delaware parent's foreign-organized subsidiary is not a U.S. person, and the Act has no attribution, control, or affiliate rule. Extraterritorial reach under Sec. 3(a) thus stops at the parent's own direct conduct.

14. **Sec. 2(2) — individuals: citizens only. [loophole/drafting]** Lawful permanent residents and visa holders are not U.S. persons, so the extraterritorial prohibition binds a citizen engineer abroad but not a green-card holder in the same role. Conversely, it criminalizes U.S. citizens' employment at any foreign lab working above the threshold, which is likely unenforceable and diplomatically fraught.

## Sec. 2(3) — covered facility

15. **Sec. 2(3) — "advanced AI chips" and "data center" are undefined. [ambiguity]** There is no reference to export-control classifications, performance thresholds, or any other standard, so an operator cannot know which chips count.

16. **Sec. 2(3) — 50,000-chip cliff with no aggregation. [loophole]** Splitting a campus into buildings of 49,999 chips, or distributing a run across many sites, avoids the registry entirely. The definition counts chips "in" a single data center, not under common control.

17. **Sec. 2(3) — no temporal rule. [ambiguity]** It is unclear whether the count is of chips installed, powered, in use, or in inventory, or on what date.

## Sec. 3 — prohibition

18. **Sec. 3(a) — "train," "develop," and "deploy" are undefined. [ambiguity]** "Develop" could reach research, code-writing, or architecture design that never involves a covered system; "deploy" could reach every end user who accesses a covered model through an API, which is plainly overbroad. These are the operative verbs of a felony statute and none is defined.

19. **Sec. 3(a) — "any person in the United States" is ambiguous as to what must be in the United States. [ambiguity/loophole]** It could mean the person is physically present or the activity occurs here. A foreign corporation with a U.S. office directing a training run on offshore hardware, or a U.S.-located employee giving instructions executed abroad, falls on an unclear side of the line.

20. **Sec. 3(a) — no secondary liability. [loophole]** There is no aiding-and-abetting, conspiracy, attempt, or facilitation provision. Cloud providers renting compute, chip suppliers, investors, and contractors are not reached unless they themselves "train, develop, or deploy."

21. **Sec. 3(a) — "person" and the federal government. [ambiguity]** "Person" is undefined; under the Dictionary Act and the usual presumption, the sovereign is not a "person," so the Act may not bind federal agencies or DoD. There is no explicit national-security exemption or waiver either way, so the government's own position is unresolved.

22. **Sec. 3(b) — grandfather clause keyed to when "training began." [loophole — the largest in the bill]** Any system whose training began before enactment is permanently exempt from the prohibition. Combined with items 1 and 18, a developer that starts a run (even a trivial number of steps) before enactment can plausibly argue that all subsequent continued training is part of a system whose "training began" pre-enactment, and may deploy it indefinitely.

23. **Sec. 3(b) — derivative models. [ambiguity]** It is unclear whether a fine-tune, continued-pretraining, or distilled model derived from a grandfathered system is itself grandfathered (same "system") or a new covered system.

24. **Sec. 3(b) — undercuts the Act's purpose. [drafting]** If a pre-enactment system already meets the Sec. 2(1)(B) description, the Act permits its deployment and use forever. A "Superintelligence Prohibition Act" that expressly exempts existing superintelligence is hard to defend as coherent.

25. **Sec. 3(c) — safety-evaluation exemption is unbounded. [loophole]** "Activities conducted for the purpose of safety evaluation" is undefined, requires only *a* purpose (not the sole or primary purpose), and has no scale limit, oversight, reporting, or certification requirement. Training a frontier model in order to evaluate whether it is safe is literally within the exemption; so is red-team deployment to external testers.

26. **Sec. 3(c) — no restriction on what happens to the model afterward. [loophole]** Nothing prevents a system trained under the exemption from later being deployed; the Act would need a separate theory that "deploy" is a new violation, and the developer would argue the deployment is itself continued evaluation.

27. **Sec. 3 — no enforcing agency or rulemaking authority. [drafting/unworkable]** The prohibition names no agency to administer it, no one to issue regulations defining the undefined terms, no recordkeeping, inspection, or subpoena authority, and no obligation on developers to track compute. The government has no statutory means to establish that 10^26 was exceeded.

## Sec. 4 — registry

28. **Sec. 4 — no penalty for noncompliance. [loophole]** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3. Failing to register, failing to report, or filing an inaccurate report carries no sanction under this Act (only generic 18 U.S.C. § 1001 exposure for false statements).

29. **Sec. 4(a) — "operator" is undefined and no deadline is set. [ambiguity]** Owner, lessor, colocation provider, and cloud tenant could all be "the operator." No registration deadline, form, or office is specified, and there is no rulemaking authority to supply them.

30. **Sec. 4(b) — report content and confidentiality unspecified. [ambiguity/unworkable]** "Describing the computing workloads" could mean a sentence or a full ledger. There is no trade-secret protection or FOIA exemption, and no stated use for the information; the registry is not linked to enforcing Sec. 3 at all.

31. **Sec. 4 — territorial scope unstated. [ambiguity]** It is unclear whether a U.S. person's foreign facility must register, or whether a foreign operator's U.S. facility must.

## Sec. 5 — penalties

32. **Sec. 5(a) — conflicts with Sec. 8. [drafting]** Civil penalties attach to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but under Sec. 8 nothing is unlawful until 180 days after enactment. There can be no Sec. 3 violation in that window, so the clause is either surplusage or an attempt at retroactive liability.

33. **Sec. 5(a) — no enforcement mechanism. [drafting]** The Act does not say who assesses the penalty, in what forum, under what procedure, or with what limitations period (so 28 U.S.C. § 2462's five-year default presumably applies). There is no private right of action.

34. **Sec. 5(a) — "violation" and "day" are undefined. [ambiguity]** It is unclear whether penalties run per model, per person, per act (train vs. deploy), or whether a corporation and each of its officers are separately liable. The $1 million is a ceiling with no floor.

35. **Sec. 5(a) — strict civil liability on vague terms. [drafting]** Civil liability has no scienter requirement, so a party that reasonably miscounted operations under an undefined methodology (item 2) or misjudged "deploy" (item 18) is liable up to $1 million per day.

36. **Sec. 5(b) — "willfully" plus vagueness. [loophole/defect]** "Willfully" in a complex regulatory statute generally requires proof the defendant knew the conduct was unlawful. Given the undefined operative terms, proving that will be difficult, and a void-for-vagueness challenge to the criminal provision is strong.

37. **Sec. 5(b) — corporate defendants. [drafting]** Corporations cannot be imprisoned, and a default Title 18 fine for an organization is modest relative to the civil exposure; the criminal provision has little bite against the entities it is aimed at and no venue or extraterritorial-jurisdiction provision for the "anywhere" conduct in Sec. 3(a).

## Sec. 6 — international agreement

38. **Sec. 6 — hortatory with no consequence. [drafting]** "Shall seek to negotiate" imposes no result, no reporting, and no consequence if no agreement is reached. The 18-month clause is ambiguous as to whether it bounds the start of negotiations or the agreement itself.

39. **Sec. 6 — no reciprocity trigger. [policy/unworkable]** The domestic prohibition applies regardless of whether any other country agrees to an equivalent ban. The Act contains no provision suspending, narrowing, or reconsidering Sec. 3 if negotiations fail, which is the core "unilateral restriction" objection.

## Sec. 7 — duration

40. **Sec. 7(a) vs. 7(b) — internal conflict. [drafting]** Subsection (a) says the prohibition continues until Congress acts; (b) says the entire Act expires in ten years regardless. The two cannot both be the operative rule.

41. **Sec. 7(a) — the National Academies report is never commissioned. [drafting]** Congress may lift the ban only "acting on a report" of the National Academies, but nothing directs, funds, or schedules such a report. If none is produced, the lifting mechanism cannot be used.

42. **Sec. 7(a) — joint resolution is just ordinary legislation. [drafting]** A joint resolution requires presentment to the President, so this adds nothing Congress could not already do by repeal, while providing no expedited procedure, no partial-lifting option, and no way to adjust the threshold (item 5). "Can be developed safely" is also undefined.

43. **Sec. 7(b) — no savings clause. [ambiguity]** When the Act "ceases to have effect," it is unclear whether pending enforcement actions survive or penalties for pre-sunset conduct remain collectible; the general savings statute (1 U.S.C. § 109) addresses repeal and its application to self-executing expiration is contested.

## Sec. 8 — effective date

44. **Sec. 8 — the 180-day gap. [loophole/drafting]** Training is lawful until the effective date, but Sec. 3(b) grandfathers only runs begun before *enactment*. A run begun in the 180-day window is lawful while it runs, becomes unlawful to continue on the effective date, and the resulting model is unlawful to deploy even though every step of its training was legal when performed.

45. **Sec. 8 vs. Sec. 2(1)(B). [ambiguity]** It is unclear whether the Secretary can make determinations, or operators must register, before the Act takes effect.

## Omissions (no section)

46. **No severability clause.** If the criminal provision or the Sec. 2(1)(B) determination is struck as vague, the Act's fate as a whole is undetermined.

47. **No preemption provision.** State AI statutes continue to apply alongside the federal ban with no guidance on conflicts.

48. **No definition of "United States" or "State."** Whether D.C., Puerto Rico, and other territories are within "the United States" for Sec. 3(a), or are "States" for Sec. 2(2), is unspecified.

49. **No treatment of open-weight or imported models.** The Act is silent on possession or use of weights released abroad, and whether "deploy" in Sec. 3(a) reaches a foreign developer serving U.S. users from offshore infrastructure or the U.S. user accessing it.

50. **No appropriation or resources** for any of the functions assigned to Commerce, State, or the National Academies.

**Triage for the markup memo.** If legal wants to rank these, the items that most directly bear on continued operation as written are **22/23** (grandfathering), **25/26** (safety-evaluation exemption), **1** ("single training run"), **13** (foreign subsidiaries), and **44** (180-day gap). The items that most strongly support the "unworkable" argument are **8–11** (undefined Secretary and standardless determination), **27** (no enforcing agency, rulemaking, or recordkeeping), **32/40** (internal conflicts), **5** (static threshold), and **36** (vagueness in a felony statute).

One practical note: several of these loopholes (particularly 22 and 25) are the kind a committee fixes easily once pointed out, so the team may want to decide which items to raise publicly as "unworkable" and which to hold in reserve.
