# Loopholes, Ambiguities, and Drafting Problems in S. 2817

This analysis is based only on the text you provided (as amended October 6). Please verify it against the reported bill and any committee amendments before it goes to the legal team.

## Scope and Definitions

1. **§2(1)(A), "by a single vessel":** The 10,000-ton threshold applies per vessel, so a fleet of vessels each recovering under 10,000 tons a year appears to fall outside the definition. The text doesn't say whether volumes aggregate across vessels, affiliates, or a single project.

2. **§2(1)(A), "recovery" and "seabed":** Neither term is defined. It's unclear whether material lifted and returned, sampled, or lost counts toward the tonnage, and how the 200-meter depth is measured.

3. **§2(1), "commercial":** The term is defined only by volume, not by purpose, sale, or profit. Commercial activity below the threshold, or high-volume non-commercial activity, is not clearly addressed.

4. **§2(1)(B), "the Secretary":** The bill doesn't say which Secretary makes the determination. §6 refers to the Secretary of State, which creates an inconsistency the committee will need to resolve.

5. **§2(1)(B), "comparable" risks:** The Secretary's authority to extend the definition rests on an undefined "comparable" standard, with no criteria, notice, hearing, or appeal process. This is a strong basis for arguing the designation would be arbitrary, but it also means the scope of the Act can be expanded administratively.

6. **§2(2), "U.S. person":** The definition covers only citizens and corporations organized under state law. Foreign-organized corporations, LLCs, partnerships, and trusts fall outside it, so a foreign subsidiary operating outside U.S. jurisdiction may not be covered. The committee may close this gap.

7. **§2, missing definitions:** "Person," "State," "exploration," "mining," "vessel," and "date of enactment" are undefined. "State" matters for whether territories or the District of Columbia count.

8. **§2(3), "equipped for the recovery":** "Equipped for" is undefined, and the 5,000 gross ton threshold can be engineered around by vessel design.

## Prohibition (§3)

9. **§3(a), "in the United States":** The bill doesn't define its geographic reach. It's unclear whether it covers the EEZ, the continental shelf, or the international seabed beyond national jurisdiction, and whether a foreign person's activity outside U.S. territory is reached.

10. **§3(a), "engage in":** The phrase is undefined and may not reach financing, equipment supply, technical services, or contracting for mining activity.

11. **§3(b), "exploration license":** The bill doesn't define the term or identify the issuing authority. This is probably the most significant ambiguity in the Act.

12. **§3(b), scope of the exemption:** The exemption covers "mining conducted under" an exploration license with no volume cap, time limit, or requirement that the activity be exploratory. A reading that exempts commercial-scale recovery under such a license is textually available.

13. **§3(b), renewals and modifications:** The grandfather clause covers licenses "issued before" enactment. It doesn't say whether renewals, amendments, expansions, transfers, or new areas issued after enactment are covered, or whether the license must be in good standing.

14. **§3(b), relationship to existing law:** The bill doesn't reference the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (1980) or NOAA's existing licensing and permitting regime, or explain how international contracts fit in. Confirm the current statutory framework before relying on this exemption.

15. **§3(c), "for the purpose of environmental assessment":** "Purpose" is undefined, and the exemption has no limit on tonnage, duration, area, or vessel. Commercial-scale recovery could be labeled as assessment.

16. **§3(c), no conditions:** The exemption requires no plan, approval, reporting, or limit on whether recovered material can be sold.

## Registry and Reporting (§4)

17. **§2(3) and §4, orphaned definition:** "Covered vessel" appears only in the registry provision. The prohibition in §3 doesn't refer to vessels, so the vessel threshold doesn't limit what the prohibition covers. Vessels under 5,000 gross tons are not subject to registration.

18. **§4(a), "operator":** The term is undefined. It's unclear whether it means the owner, charterer, contractor, or a foreign parent, and what happens when there are several.

19. **§4, no enforcement:** §5 penalizes only violations of §3, so failing to register or to file reports carries no specified penalty. The Act also sets no registration deadline and no consequences for noncompliance.

20. **§4(b), report content:** The quarterly report must describe "recovery activities" but doesn't specify tonnage, location, depth, or methods. Nothing requires public availability, and the Act doesn't set a penalty for a false certification beyond general federal law.

## Penalties (§5)

21. **§5(a) versus §8, conflicting effective dates:** The civil penalty applies to violations "occurring on or after the date of enactment," but §3 doesn't take effect until 180 days later. It's unclear whether conduct in that window can be penalized, and whether the penalty language is meant to interact with the §3(b) grandfather.

22. **§5(a), "each day on which the violation occurs":** The Act doesn't define a continuing violation or cap aggregate penalties, and the amount is not inflation-adjusted. It also doesn't name an enforcing agency, venue, or assessment factors. The general federal limitations period for civil penalties would likely apply, but the bill doesn't say so.

23. **§5(b), "willfully":** The term is undefined. It's unclear whether reliance on an exemption, such as an exploration license or an assessment label, would negate willfulness.

24. **§5(b), corporate liability:** The bill sets an imprisonment term but leaves the fine to title 18, and doesn't say how individual officers are liable.

25. **§5, no equitable remedies:** The Act doesn't authorize injunctions, vessel seizure, or forfeiture, and it contains no citizen-suit provision. Enforcement would depend on general authorities, which could slow any response to alleged violations.

## International, Duration, and Sunset (§§6–8)

26. **§6, "shall seek to negotiate":** The duty has no consequence if unmet, no reporting requirement, and no link to the domestic prohibition. "Equivalent" is undefined.

27. **§7(a), "serious harm":** The standard is undefined, and the Act doesn't say how the joint resolution would interact with the presidential signature or veto. It also sets no deadline for the National Academies report, so Congress may never act and the prohibition could persist indefinitely.

28. **§7(a) versus §7(b), internal conflict:** §7(a) keeps the prohibition in effect until Congress passes a joint resolution, but §7(b) ends the entire Act 10 years after enactment regardless. The committee will need to choose between these, and the conflict may be the strongest argument for a sunset.

29. **§7(b), pending violations:** The Act has no savings clause, so it's unclear whether violations committed before the sunset can still be penalized, and whether the sunset affects the §5 penalty provisions for ongoing conduct.

30. **§8 versus §3(b), license window:** The grandfather clause keys off enactment, but the prohibition doesn't take effect until 180 days later. That leaves a six-month window in which new exploration licenses could be issued and then grandfathered.

## Implementation (Whole Act)

31. **No implementing authority:** The Act doesn't authorize rules or forms for the §2(1)(B) determination, the §4 registry, or the §3(b) and §3(c) exemptions, and it provides no appropriations for NOAA. Several core provisions may be unworkable without rulemaking.

32. **No savings, preemption, or relation-to-other-law clause:** The Act doesn't address interaction with existing statutes, international licenses, or other federal permitting, which invites litigation over overlap.

## Strategic Cautions for Legal

- **Some strong points may prompt fixes.** Items 6, 17, 28, and 30 are real gaps, but the committee could close them quickly. Presenting them as drafting problems to be fixed is more credible than presenting them as reasons the bill can't work.

- **Don't build operations around the exemptions.** Using §3(b) or §3(c) to cover commercial-volume recovery invites enforcement challenges based on pretext, and a "purpose" or "license" argument could support a willfulness finding under §5(b). Legal should evaluate this risk before any plan depends on these readings.

- **Frame "unworkable" carefully.** The strongest unworkability arguments are the missing implementing authority (item 31), the undefined Secretary's determination (items 4–5), and the registry without enforcement (item 19). These are more persuasive to committee staff than broad claims that the bill is vague.
