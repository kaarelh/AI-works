# S. 2817 Weak Spots (Text as Amended October 6, 2026)

These are drafting observations based only on the text you provided. For the explainer, it would help to run the most consequential ones by outside legal experts or committee staff, so the piece rests on attributed analysis rather than our own reading.

### Definitions (Sec. 2)

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "by a single vessel":** The 10,000-ton threshold applies per vessel, so an operator could spread recovery across several vessels, each staying under the cap, and mine an unlimited total amount. The bill has no aggregation rule for operators, affiliates, or fleets.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "per year" and "metric tons":** The bill doesn't say whether "year" means a calendar year, fiscal year, or rolling 12 months. It also doesn't say whether tonnage is measured wet or dry, which matters because freshly recovered nodules hold a lot of water. Both gaps leave room to argue an operation stayed under the threshold.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "recovery" and "other mineral resources":** Neither term is defined. "Recovery" could mean collecting material on the seabed or lifting it to the surface. "Other mineral resources" could be read narrowly (hard minerals only) or broadly enough to cover sand, gravel, or oil and gas, which invites both evasion and overbreadth challenges.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A), geographic scope:** The definition says "the seabed" with no reference to the U.S. exclusive economic zone (EEZ), continental shelf, foreign waters, or areas beyond national jurisdiction. It is also unclear how the 200-meter depth is measured and at what point in the operation.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(B), "the Secretary":** The Act never says which Secretary is meant (Commerce, Interior, or State). Sec. 4 separately names NOAA, which sits within Commerce. This alone could stall implementation.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B), open-ended agency determination:** The Secretary may extend the prohibition, and the criminal penalties in Sec. 5(b), to any activity posing "comparable" risks. No standard, rulemaking procedure, or notice is required. That invites vagueness, due process, and nondelegation challenges, and it could also let a future Secretary simply decline to use the power.

7. **Sec. 2(2), "U.S. person" limited to citizens and corporations:** The definition omits LLCs, partnerships, and trusts, which are common structures for offshore ventures. It also omits lawful permanent residents. An LLC could therefore arguably operate abroad outside the ban.

8. **Sec. 2(2), foreign subsidiaries:** Only entities "organized under the laws of a State" are covered. A U.S. company could mine through a foreign-incorporated subsidiary on a foreign-flagged vessel, since the bill has no ownership or control test.

9. **Sec. 2(2), "State" undefined:** It is unclear whether entities organized in the District of Columbia or U.S. territories count as U.S. persons.

10. **Sec. 2(3), covered vessel threshold:** Only vessels over 5,000 gross tons must register, yet the prohibition turns on tonnage recovered, not vessel size. Smaller vessels could mine with no registry visibility. "Equipped for the recovery" is also undefined, for example whether removable collector equipment counts.

### Prohibition and Exemptions (Sec. 3)

11. **Sec. 3(a), "in the United States":** The bill doesn't say whether this includes the EEZ and outer continental shelf, where any domestic seabed mining would actually take place. A foreign company in U.S. offshore waters could argue it is not "in the United States."

12. **Sec. 3(a), activities not covered:** Only "engag[ing] in" mining is prohibited. Financing, sponsoring, insuring, importing, processing, or selling seabed minerals mined by others are all left alone, so U.S. companies could keep participating in the industry indirectly.

13. **Sec. 3(b), grandfather clause converts exploration into mining:** The exemption covers "mining conducted under an exploration license," but exploration licenses typically do not authorize commercial recovery. The provision is internally confused and could be read to authorize commercial-scale mining by existing licensees, with no volume limit and no expiration.

14. **Sec. 3(b), licenses from which issuer:** The bill doesn't specify whether "exploration license" means one issued by NOAA under the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act, by the International Seabed Authority, or by a foreign government. A broad reading could exempt anyone holding a qualifying license from any issuer. Renewals, transfers, and amendments are also unaddressed.

15. **Sec. 3(c), environmental assessment exemption:** The term is undefined. There is no tonnage cap, no permit or approval, no notice requirement, and no oversight. Because the test is the operator's stated "purpose," commercial-scale "test mining" could be relabeled as assessment.

16. **Sec. 3, no conforming amendments:** The bill does not amend or repeal the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (1980) or address seabed mineral leasing under the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act. NOAA and Interior could keep processing or issuing licenses and permits for conduct the bill makes illegal, which creates conflicting statutes and litigation risk. It also says nothing about permit applications pending at enactment.

### Registry (Sec. 4)

17. **Sec. 4, no penalty for noncompliance:** The penalties in Sec. 5 apply only to violations of Sec. 3. Failing to register or report, or filing false reports, carries no Act-specific consequence, although general false-statement law (18 U.S.C. 1001) might apply.

18. **Sec. 4(b), reporting depends on registration:** Only "registered" operators must report, so an operator that never registers also escapes reporting. No registration deadline is set either.

19. **Sec. 4(b), weak report requirements:** Reports are self-certified with no verification, auditing, or vessel monitoring. There are no required contents (for example tonnage, location, or depth) and no public access. That makes it hard to prove an operation crossed the tonnage threshold.

20. **Sec. 4, no rulemaking or administrative authority:** NOAA is given no authority to issue regulations, set forms, or inspect vessels.

### Penalties (Sec. 5)

21. **Sec. 5(a) vs. Sec. 8, conflicting start dates:** Penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the Act takes effect 180 days after enactment. This contradiction invites challenges to any penalty for conduct during that window.

22. **Sec. 5(a), per-day penalty vs. annual threshold:** A violation depends on exceeding 10,000 tons "per year," but penalties accrue "for each day." It is unclear which days count as violation days, for example only after the threshold is crossed, or every day of the year.

23. **Sec. 5, no enforcement machinery:** No agency is authorized to assess civil penalties. The bill also provides no hearing process, judicial review, injunctive relief, forfeiture of vessels or recovered minerals, inspection power, or citizen suits.

24. **Sec. 5(b), criminal liability tied to agency determinations:** Combined with Sec. 2(1)(B), people could face up to 10 years in prison for activity a Secretary deemed "comparable." That strengthens the vagueness and nondelegation challenges noted in item 6.

### International, Duration, and Effective Date (Secs. 6–8)

25. **Sec. 6, ambiguous and hortatory:** "Shall seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months" could mean begin negotiating or conclude negotiations by then. The obligation is only to "seek," with no reporting requirement or consequence. Any resulting treaty would also need Senate advice and consent.

26. **Sec. 7(a) vs. 7(b), contradictory duration:** Subsection (a) says the ban lasts until Congress acts, while (b) ends the whole Act after 10 years regardless. Because the 10 years run from enactment, not the effective date, the ban actually operates for about 9.5 years.

27. **Sec. 7(a), off-ramp adds little:** A joint resolution must be signed by the President, so it is ordinary legislation. Congress could repeal the ban that way anyway. The provision adds no expedited procedures, does not direct or fund the National Academies to produce the report, and leaves "serious harm" undefined.

28. **Sec. 8, 180-day window:** The delay gives operators six months of lawful activity after enactment. It also compounds the date conflict in item 21.

If it's useful for the explainer, the issues with the biggest practical effect are probably items 1, 8, 12, 13, 15, 17, 21, and 26. These are the ones most likely to come up as amendments at the October 15 markup.
