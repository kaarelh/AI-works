RESPONSE:
Below is a section-by-section review of the text as provided. I've phrased each item as a drafting or enforcement question rather than a judgment on the bill's merits, so they can go into the explainer as issues that observers may raise. Some items are clear gaps in the text; others are ambiguities a court or agency would have to resolve.

**Sec. 2: Definitions**

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "by a single vessel."** The 10,000-ton threshold applies per vessel. An operator could split production across several vessels, each recovering under 10,000 tons a year, and none would meet the definition. Nothing aggregates output by operator, project, or site.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A), sub-threshold operations.** Any single-vessel operation recovering 10,000 tons or less a year is outside the prohibition. The text doesn't say whether "metric tons" means wet or dry weight, which matters a great deal for water-laden nodules.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "per year" and "recovery."** The bill doesn't say whether "per year" means a calendar year, a fiscal year, or any rolling 12 months. "Recovery" is also undefined. It could mean collection on the seabed, lifting to the surface, or landing ashore. These choices determine when, and whether, the threshold is crossed.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "other mineral resources" and the 200-meter depth.** "Mineral resources" is undefined and could arguably include sand and gravel, phosphorites, or even oil and gas recovered from deepwater wells. That reading would make the bill far broader than its title suggests. The text also doesn't say how depth is measured or whether subsoil beneath the seabed counts.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(B), "the Secretary."** The bill never says which Secretary (Commerce? Interior? State?). That is a basic gap, because this official determines the scope of a criminal prohibition.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B), open-ended catch-all.** Activities that pose risks "comparable to" large-scale mining can be added at the Secretary's discretion. The bill sets no standard, no procedure (notice-and-comment or otherwise), and no effective-date rule for these determinations. Because violations carry criminal penalties (Sec. 5(b)), this provision is open to vagueness and nondelegation challenges.

7. **Sec. 2(2), narrow "U.S. person" definition.** The definition covers only citizens and *corporations* organized under the laws of a *State*. That leaves out LLCs, partnerships, and other non-corporate entities; entities organized under the laws of D.C. or U.S. territories; lawful permanent residents; and non-citizen U.S. nationals.

8. **Sec. 2(2), foreign subsidiaries.** A U.S. company could conduct mining through a foreign-incorporated subsidiary operating outside the United States. The subsidiary would not be a "U.S. person," and the bill has no provision addressing parents that own, control, or direct foreign affiliates.

9. **Sec. 2(3) vs. Sec. 2(1), mismatched thresholds.** The registry covers vessels over 5,000 gross tons that are "equipped for" recovery. The prohibition is keyed to tonnage recovered. Smaller vessels could mine without registering, and the two definitions don't connect.

**Sec. 3: Prohibition**

10. **Sec. 3(a), "in the United States" undefined.** The text doesn't say whether this includes the territorial sea, the EEZ, or the outer continental shelf. If it doesn't, foreign persons and foreign-flagged vessels mining in U.S. waters may fall outside the ban.

11. **Sec. 3(a), "engage in."** It's unclear whether financing, sponsoring, insuring, chartering vessels to, buying output from, or providing technology to a mining operation counts as "engaging in" mining. Narrowly read, the ban reaches only the operator.

12. **Sec. 3(a), no import, processing, or trade provisions.** The bill doesn't restrict importing, processing, or selling minerals from deep seabed mining conducted by non-U.S. persons. Operators could relocate to, or seek sponsorship from, another country and still sell into the U.S. market.

13. **Sec. 3(b), grandfather clause scope.** The bill exempts "mining conducted under an exploration license" with no limit on tonnage, duration, renewal, transfer, or expansion. It doesn't say whether a license-holder may move from exploration to full commercial recovery under the exemption.

14. **Sec. 3(b), "exploration license" undefined.** The bill doesn't say which authority must have issued the license. It could mean only U.S.-issued licenses (e.g., under the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act) or also International Seabed Authority contracts or other countries' licenses. A broad reading would greatly expand the exemption.

15. **Sec. 3(b), pre-enactment licensing window.** Because the cutoff is enactment, licenses issued at any point while the bill is pending, including after markup, would be permanently grandfathered. That creates an incentive to obtain licenses before enactment.

16. **Sec. 3(b), other permit types not addressed.** The clause covers exploration licenses but not commercial recovery permits or pending applications. This could invite reliance or takings arguments from holders of those authorizations.

17. **Sec. 3(c), environmental assessment exemption.** "Environmental assessment" is undefined, and the exemption has no tonnage cap, permit requirement, oversight, or verification. Because it turns on the activity's stated *purpose*, an operator could label test or pilot mining as assessment.

**Sec. 4: Registry**

18. **Sec. 4, no penalty for non-compliance.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3. Failing to register, failing to report, or filing a false report carries no penalty under the Act. General federal false-statement law might apply to certified reports, but the Act doesn't say so.

19. **Sec. 4(b), undefined report content.** The reports must "describ[e] recovery activities," but the bill doesn't require tonnage, location, depth, or the purpose claimed under Sec. 3(c). NOAA receives no authority to verify, inspect, or request records. The registry therefore can't reliably detect threshold violations.

20. **Sec. 4, unclear reach and terms.** The bill doesn't define "operator" or say whether the registry applies to foreign-flagged vessels or vessels operating outside U.S. waters.

**Sec. 5: Penalties**

21. **Sec. 5(a), effective-date conflict.** Penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but Sec. 8 says the Act takes effect 180 days after enactment. The text doesn't resolve whether conduct during that window is penalized, and penalizing it could raise fair-notice or retroactivity challenges.

22. **Sec. 5(a), per-day penalty vs. per-year threshold.** The prohibition is defined by annual tonnage, but the penalty accrues per day. It's unclear which days count as violation days, for example whether only days after the threshold is crossed or every day of that year's operations.

23. **Sec. 5, no enforcement machinery.** The bill names no enforcing agency and provides no administrative or judicial process for civil penalties. It also grants no inspection, boarding, seizure, or forfeiture authority, no injunctive relief, and no citizen-suit provision.

24. **Sec. 5(b), criminal liability tied to a discretionary definition.** Because Sec. 2(1)(B) lets the Secretary expand what counts as mining, criminal exposure can turn on an unpublished or loosely defined determination. That strengthens the vagueness challenge noted in item 6.

**Sec. 6: International Agreement**

25. **Sec. 6, ambiguous and unenforceable mandate.** "Shall seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months" is unclear about whether 18 months is the deadline to begin or to conclude negotiations. "Seek" creates no enforceable obligation, and there is no reporting requirement.

**Sec. 7: Duration**

26. **Sec. 7(a) vs. 7(b), internal contradiction.** Sec. 7(a) says the prohibition lasts until Congress makes a finding, which implies an indefinite ban. Sec. 7(b) ends the entire Act after 10 years regardless. A reader can't tell which controls in practice, and the 10-year sunset runs from enactment, so the ban is effective for only about 9.5 years.

27. **Sec. 7(a), mechanism adds little.** No one is directed to commission the NASEM report, "serious harm" is undefined, and a joint resolution is effectively ordinary legislation, which Congress could pass anyway. It's also unclear whether such a resolution would end only Sec. 3 or the registry too.

28. **Sec. 7(b), no savings clause.** When the Act ceases to have effect, it's unclear whether pending or past violations can still be prosecuted or penalized.

**Sec. 8 and Cross-Cutting Issues**

29. **Sec. 8, 180-day window.** Full-scale mining remains lawful between enactment and the effective date. Combined with item 21, the treatment of this period is unclear.

30. **Relationship to existing law.** The bill doesn't amend, repeal, or reconcile itself with the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (1980) or the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act. That could leave conflicting licensing frameworks in place.

31. **No rulemaking authority or severability clause.** No agency is authorized to issue regulations to fill these gaps. With no severability clause, a successful challenge to one provision, such as Sec. 2(1)(B), invites argument over whether the rest of the Act survives.

**Suggestions for the explainer**

- Several items (especially 4, 14, and 30) depend on how the bill interacts with existing statutes and international arrangements. It would be worth confirming them with a maritime or administrative law expert and asking the sponsor's office and committee staff how they read these provisions. Some may be addressed in report language or further amendments at markup.
- Items 18, 21, and 26 are the most clear-cut textual problems. Items 7, 8, 12, and 17 are probably the most significant routes by which mining activity could continue under the bill.
