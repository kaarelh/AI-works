## Section 2: Definitions

1. **§2(1)(A), per-vessel threshold [Room].** The 10,000-ton threshold applies to "a single vessel," with no aggregation rule for fleets, successive vessels, or affiliated entities. Recovery split across several vessels, each under the threshold, is arguably not "commercial deep seabed mining."

2. **§2(1)(A), measurement [Both].** "Recovery," "per year," and "metric tons" are undefined. The bill doesn't say whether the measure is wet or dry weight, nodules only or all material lifted (sediment, water), or where it is measured (seabed, riser, or onboard). It also doesn't say whether the year is a calendar or rolling year, so compliance can't be reliably verified and the threshold is contestable.

3. **§2(1)(A), "other mineral resources" and the 200 m depth [Workable].** There is no exclusion for oil and gas, sand and gravel, or dredging. A 10,000-ton threshold could sweep in ordinary offshore energy and dredging activity, which supports an overbreadth argument. The depth reference point is also unspecified, and 200 m is the continental shelf margin rather than "deep" seabed.

4. **§2(1)(A), "commercial" [Both].** The term is defined only by volume, with no sale, profit, or commercial-purpose element. Non-commercial research recovery above the threshold is caught, while commercially motivated staged campaigns below it are not.

5. **§2(1)(B), "the Secretary" [Workable].** The Act never defines "Secretary." Section 6 specifies "Secretary of State," which implies a different official is meant here, but the bill doesn't say who.

6. **§2(1)(B), standardless delegation [Workable].** "Risks … comparable to" subparagraph (A) has no criteria, and (A) is defined by volume rather than risk, so there is no benchmark. There is no notice, publication, comment, or review process. Because §5(b) attaches criminal liability, this supports void-for-vagueness, fair-notice, rule-of-lenity, and nondelegation arguments.

7. **§2(1)(B), timing [Workable].** Nothing requires the Secretary's determination to precede the conduct, and there is no advance-determination mechanism. A company could be found in violation retroactively.

8. **§2(2), "U.S. person" [Room].** It covers only citizens and corporations organized under state law. It omits LLCs, partnerships, and trusts, permanent residents, D.C. and territorial entities, and foreign-organized subsidiaries of U.S. companies. There is no ownership or control test and no anti-evasion or "causing/aiding" language.

9. **§2(3), "covered vessel" [Workable].** The 5,000 gross ton floor is unconnected to the §2(1) threshold, so a smaller vessel could exceed 10,000 tons without being registered. "Gross tons" has no specified tonnage convention, and "equipped for the recovery" is vague. It may also capture research vessels, drillships, and dredgers that aren't mining.

## Section 3: Prohibition

10. **§3(a), geographic reach [Both].** "In the United States" is undefined and not stated to include the EEZ, continental shelf, or U.S.-flag vessels. It is unclear whether it covers a person physically in the U.S. remotely operating equipment offshore.

11. **§3(a), foreign persons and the supply chain [Room].** Foreign persons outside the U.S. are not regulated. Nothing addresses import, processing, financing, or offtake of nodules recovered by non-U.S. persons.

12. **§3(a), extraterritoriality [Workable].** Reaching U.S. persons "anywhere" raises freedom-of-the-high-seas and enforceability questions. There is no conflict-of-laws provision for activity licensed by another state or the ISA.

13. **§3, relationship to DSHMRA [Both].** The bill doesn't amend or repeal the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (30 U.S.C. 1401 et seq.) or NOAA's implementing regulations. Authorizations under that regime may conflict with §3, and implied repeal is disfavored, so courts would try to harmonize the two. Pending license and permit applications are not addressed.

14. **§3(b), what the grandfather clause covers [Both].** It protects only "mining conducted under an exploration license." Exploration licenses normally authorize exploration rather than commercial recovery, and commercial recovery permits aren't mentioned. So it is unclear whether the exemption covers commercial-scale recovery at all, or is close to a null set. This is probably the most important item for our own status.

15. **§3(b), no limits [Room].** The exemption has no cap on volume, area, or duration, no expiration, and no good-standing condition. Read literally, it is indefinite.

16. **§3(b), which licenses count [Both].** The issuing authority is unspecified, so foreign-issued or ISA-sponsored licenses might arguably qualify. It is also unclear whether renewals, extensions, modifications, transfers, or consolidated licenses after enactment count as "issued before the date of enactment." The cutoff is enactment rather than the effective date, which leaves the 180-day window unaddressed.

17. **§3(c), environmental assessment exemption [Both].** It turns on the "purpose" of the activity, with no volume cap, definition, approval, or reporting. Large-scale test mining could be characterized as assessment, and intent is hard to police.

18. **§3, no research or test-mining carve-out [Workable].** Apart from §3(c), there is no exemption for marine scientific research, equipment trials, or salvage, so a non-commercial activity above the threshold is prohibited.

## Section 4: Registry

19. **§4(a), registration mechanics [Workable].** There is no deadline, form, fee, or rulemaking authority. "Operator" is undefined, and the bill doesn't say how foreign-flag vessels are reached.

20. **§4(b), reports [Workable].** There is no content standard, due date, or certification standard (for example, under penalty of perjury). There is also no statutory protection for proprietary or trade-secret data.

21. **§4, no enforcement [Both].** §5 penalizes only violations of §3, so failure to register, failure to report, or a false certification has no penalty under this Act. No NOAA appropriation is authorized.

22. **§4, disconnected from §3 [Workable].** Registration confers or withholds nothing and plays no role in determining who may operate, so its purpose is unclear.

## Section 5: Penalties

23. **§5(a) vs. §8, retroactivity conflict [Both].** Penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §3 doesn't take effect until 180 days later. It is unclear whether any gap-period conduct can be a "violation," and the provision invites fair-notice challenges.

24. **§5(a), "each day" [Both].** The prohibition is triggered by an annual tonnage threshold, so the day a violation begins or ends is unclear. It is also unclear whether days before the threshold is crossed count.

25. **§5(a), no procedures [Workable].** No enforcing agency is designated, and there is no assessment procedure, adjudicative forum, hearing, judicial review, mitigation factors, or limitations period. Counsel should consider whether administrative assessment would raise jury-trial issues after SEC v. Jarkesy.

26. **§5(b), criminal standard [Workable].** "Willfully" is undefined, and a 10-year maximum is steep. Because violation can turn on the §2(1)(B) discretionary determination, this strengthens the vagueness argument. There is no standard for corporate or officer liability.

27. **§5, remedies [Workable].** There is no injunctive or forfeiture authority and no citizen-suit provision, so there is no clear mechanism to stop an ongoing operation other than after-the-fact penalties.

## Section 6: International agreement

28. **§6, deadline [Workable].** "Seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months" is ambiguous about whether the deadline governs the effort or the result. There is no consequence for missing it, no reporting to Congress, and no consultation requirement.

29. **§6, substance [Workable].** "Equivalent prohibitions" is undefined, and the U.S. cannot bind other states. The bill ignores the ISA and UNCLOS, and doesn't say whether the agreement is a treaty or an executive agreement. The §3 prohibition isn't contingent on any agreement and has no off-ramp if none is reached, which supports a leakage and competitiveness argument (activity shifts to foreign competitors).

## Section 7: Duration

30. **§7(a), exit standard [Workable].** "Serious harm" is undefined, and "can be conducted without serious harm" asks Congress to find a negative. The standard is effectively unreachable.

31. **§7(a), exit mechanism [Workable].** No one is directed to commission the NASEM report, and there is no deadline, funding, or expedited procedure. A joint resolution also needs a presidential signature or a veto override, and NASEM is not obligated to act.

32. **§7(a) vs. §7(b), internal contradiction [Room].** Subsection (a) says the prohibition lasts until Congress acts, but (b) says the Act ceases to have effect 10 years after enactment regardless. As written, the hard sunset controls, so this is effectively a roughly 9.5-year moratorium (measured from the effective date).

33. **§7(b), no savings clause [Both].** Nothing preserves accrued violations, penalties, or enforcement after the sunset. Counsel should check whether 1 U.S.C. 109 applies to an expiration as opposed to a repeal. It is also unclear which obligations (such as reporting) survive.

## Section 8: Effective date, and cross-cutting

34. **§8, 180-day gap [Room].** Nothing restricts ramp-up or expansion before the effective date. There is no wind-down or transition for non-exempt activity underway at that date, and no compensation provision, which may support takings claims for non-grandfathered investments. Counsel should evaluate that.

35. **§§3–8, inconsistent triggers [Workable].** §6 and §7(b) run from enactment while §§3–5 run from the effective date. Unaligned clocks cause confusion about the operative periods.

36. **Undefined terms throughout [Workable].** "Person," "Secretary," "operator," "recovery," "seabed," "United States," "exploration license," "environmental assessment," and "serious harm" are all undefined.

37. **No implementing architecture [Workable].** The bill has no general rulemaking authority, no appropriations, no interagency coordination (NOAA, Coast Guard, State), and no reporting to Congress. It also has no severability clause, which matters if §2(1)(B) or §5(b) is challenged.
