# S. 2817 drafting review (manager's amendment text of Oct. 6, 2026)

Each item is tagged **[Operating]** if it could let activity continue, **[Unworkable]** if it supports an unworkability argument, or **[Both]**.

## Section 2: Definitions

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A), per-vessel threshold.** The 10,000-ton line applies to "a single vessel" with no aggregation across a fleet, a project, or an operator. Recovery could be split among several vessels, each under the threshold, and the text doesn't say how chartered or replacement vessels are counted during a year. **[Operating]**

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "recovery" is undefined.** It could mean collection on the seafloor, lift to the surface, retention aboard, or landing ashore. It also doesn't say how tonnage is attributed to "a single vessel" when the system includes seafloor collectors, riser systems, or shuttle vessels. **[Both]**

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A), measurement basis.** The text gives no wet or dry basis (nodules carry significant moisture), no point of measurement, and no definition of "year" (calendar, rolling, or license year). It also doesn't say whether nodules and "other mineral resources" are combined or counted separately. **[Both]**

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "commercial" is a misnomer.** The definition turns only on volume, not on sale, profit, or commercial purpose. A non-commercial recovery above 10,000 tons is "commercial deep seabed mining," while a for-profit recovery below it is not. **[Both]**

5. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "seabed," "depth," and "mineral resources."** There is no datum for the 200-meter line and no geographic scope (EEZ, OCS, areas beyond national jurisdiction). "Other mineral resources" could sweep in sand, gravel, or hydrocarbons, and 200 m is a continental-shelf depth, so the definition reaches well beyond deep-ocean nodule mining. **[Unworkable]**

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B), "the Secretary" is undefined.** Sec. 2 never says which Secretary. Sec. 6 says "Secretary of State" expressly and Sec. 4 names NOAA, so the official making this determination is unidentified. **[Unworkable]**

7. **Sec. 2(1)(B), standardless delegation.** "Risks ... comparable" has no criteria, baseline, rulemaking or notice requirement, deadline, or judicial review. That raises nondelegation and fair-notice problems, made sharper by the criminal penalty in Sec. 5(b). **[Unworkable]**

8. **Sec. 2(1)(B), timing and scope.** The text doesn't say whether a determination applies only prospectively, whether it reaches otherwise grandfathered activity, or whether notice is required before liability attaches. "Any activity" is not limited to recovery, so it could reach surveying, processing, or transport. **[Both]**

9. **Sec. 2(2), "U.S. person" is narrow.** It covers only citizens and corporations "organized under the laws of a State." It omits foreign-organized subsidiaries and arguably LLCs, partnerships, trusts, and other non-corporate entities. It also omits federally chartered or DC/territorial entities and lawful permanent residents, and it has no ownership or control test. **[Operating]**

10. **Sec. 2(3) vs. Sec. 2(1)(A), "covered vessel" mismatch.** The prohibition applies to any vessel regardless of size, but registration and reporting cover only vessels over 5,000 gross tons. A smaller vessel could exceed the threshold with no registry visibility, while a large vessel merely "equipped" for recovery must register even if it isn't mining. "Equipped for," the tonnage convention, and whether a seafloor collector is a "vessel" are all unaddressed. **[Both]**

11. **Sec. 2, missing definitions.** The bill never defines "person" (1 U.S.C. § 1 would likely fill in), "operator," "engage in," "exploration license," "environmental assessment," "serious harm," or "State." Each gap is an ambiguity a court would have to resolve, and several matter for a criminal statute. **[Both]**

## Section 3: Prohibition

12. **Sec. 3(a), territorial and personal reach.** "In the United States" is undefined (territorial sea, EEZ, OCS, vessels at sea?). Non-U.S. persons acting outside the United States aren't reached, so a foreign-flagged vessel run by a non-U.S. entity is outside the text. **[Operating]**

13. **Sec. 3(a), "engage in" and downstream activity.** Nothing prohibits financing, supplying, chartering, processing, purchasing, or importing nodules recovered by others. The prohibition is also not drafted to reach U.S.-based processing of material mined abroad. **[Operating]**

14. **Sec. 3(b), "mining conducted under an exploration license."** Read literally, this exempts "mining," not just exploration, with no volume cap or end date. But exploration licenses under the existing statute (DSHMRA) don't authorize commercial recovery, so the exemption may be illusory or reflect a drafting misunderstanding. The ambiguity cuts both ways. **[Both]**

15. **Sec. 3(b), omits commercial recovery permits and pending applications.** Only "exploration licenses" are grandfathered. A holder of a commercial recovery permit or an applicant with a pending application gets no protection, which supports reliance-interest and takings arguments. **[Unworkable]**

16. **Sec. 3(b), scope of the grandfathered license.** The text doesn't say whether renewals, extensions, modifications, transfers, or additional blocks qualify, or what happens if a license lapses or is revoked. "An exploration license" is also not limited to U.S.-issued licenses, so a foreign-issued license might arguably qualify. **[Operating]**

17. **Sec. 3(b), enactment date vs. effective date.** Grandfathering covers only licenses issued before enactment, but the prohibition doesn't take effect for 180 days. A license issued in the interim isn't covered even though the prohibition isn't yet operative. The heading ("Existing Operations") also doesn't match the text, which protects licenses rather than operations. **[Unworkable]**

18. **Sec. 3(c), environmental assessment exemption.** It has no cap on volume, duration, vessels, or recovered material, no government approval or oversight, and a purely subjective "purpose" test. Nothing bars sale or use of material recovered during an "assessment." **[Operating]**

## Section 4: Registry

19. **Sec. 4(a), registration mechanics.** There is no deadline, application content, criteria, fee, or rulemaking authority. NOAA is not given power to deny, condition, or revoke registration. "Operator" is undefined (owner, charterer, or contractor?). **[Unworkable]**

20. **Sec. 4, no penalty for noncompliance.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3, so failing to register or report has no stated consequence in this Act. A false report might still be reachable under 18 U.S.C. § 1001. **[Both]**

21. **Sec. 4(b), reporting.** The text doesn't say who must certify or to what standard, what "recovery activities" must be described, or how reports are verified. It has no confidentiality protection for proprietary data, no due date within the quarter, and no definition of the quarter. **[Unworkable]**

22. **Secs. 4-5, administrative structure.** Duties are assigned to NOAA with no named official, no appropriations authorization, and no inspection, boarding, seizure, or forfeiture authority. It also doesn't say who assesses and collects civil penalties or coordinates with the existing DSHMRA regime. **[Unworkable]**

23. **Sec. 2(1)(A) with Secs. 4-5, proof of the threshold.** Nothing provides a monitoring or measurement method to establish tonnage at sea. The government would bear the burden of proving a 10,000-ton annual line, including beyond a reasonable doubt in a criminal case. **[Unworkable]**

## Section 5: Penalties

24. **Sec. 5(a) vs. Sec. 8, retroactivity conflict.** Penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but Sec. 3 doesn't take effect until 180 days later. The phrase is either meaningless or creates an argument that the window is covered, which invites due process and retroactivity challenges. **[Unworkable]**

25. **Sec. 5(a), "each day on which the violation occurs."** The offense is defined by an annual aggregate, so it isn't clear on which days a violation "occurs": only the day the threshold is crossed, or every day after. It's also unclear whether penalties multiply per vessel, per person, or per entity. **[Both]**

26. **Sec. 5(a), amount and process.** Up to $1,000,000 per day has no assessment factors, no notice-and-hearing procedure, no inflation adjustment, and no proportionality guardrail. That creates Excessive Fines Clause and due process exposure. **[Unworkable]**

27. **Sec. 5(b), "willfully" and criminal exposure.** "Willfully" is undefined, and it's unclear whether it requires knowledge that conduct was prohibited or knowledge of a Sec. 2(1)(B) determination. A 10-year maximum tied to a standardless administrative definition invites void-for-vagueness and rule-of-lenity arguments. **[Unworkable]**

## Section 6: International agreement

28. **Sec. 6, negotiation mandate.** "Shall seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months" doesn't say whether the deadline is for starting, concluding, or only attempting talks. There is no reporting requirement or consequence, and "equivalent" prohibitions would import the bill's own exemptions and thresholds. The clock also runs from enactment, not the effective date, and nothing ties the U.S. prohibition to whether other states, including existing foreign contractors, follow. **[Unworkable]**

## Section 7: Duration

29. **Sec. 7(a) vs. 7(b), internal conflict.** Subsection (a) says the prohibition remains in effect until Congress finds mining can proceed without serious harm, implying indefinite duration. Subsection (b) ends the Act after 10 years regardless, and the text doesn't say which controls. **[Both]**

30. **Sec. 7(a), the Academies report.** The bill doesn't require, fund, scope, or set a deadline for the report, and the National Academies are a private nonprofit that Congress cannot simply direct. The lifting mechanism has no trigger. **[Unworkable]**

31. **Sec. 7(a), "serious harm."** The term is undefined, and "can be conducted without serious harm" is an all-or-nothing, arguably unprovable standard. There is no pathway for phased, site-specific, or technology-specific approval. **[Unworkable]**

32. **Sec. 7(a), joint resolution.** It requires both chambers plus presidential signature or a veto override, with no expedited procedures. It's also unclear whether the "finding" itself ends Sec. 3 or the resolution needs operative repeal language. **[Unworkable]**

33. **Sec. 7(b), sunset.** It runs from enactment, not the effective date, so the prohibition would operate for roughly 9.5 years. "Cease to have effect" is not a repeal, and there's no savings clause for pre-sunset violations, so it's uncertain whether the general savings statute (1 U.S.C. § 109) preserves accrued penalties. **[Operating]**

## Section 8 and general

34. **Sec. 8, mismatched clocks and no transition.** Secs. 3(b), 5(a), 6, and 7(b) key off the enactment date, while the prohibition keys off a date 180 days later. There is no wind-down for operations started in the interim, vessels mid-voyage, or existing offtake contracts, and no compensation provision, which strengthens a takings argument. **[Unworkable]**

35. **Whole bill, interaction with existing law.** The bill doesn't amend, repeal, or reference DSHMRA (30 U.S.C. § 1401 et seq.), its regulations (15 C.F.R. Parts 970-971), or OCSLA marine-minerals authority. NOAA or BOEM could be directed to license activity that Sec. 3 prohibits, creating implied-repeal and conflicting-obligation questions. **[Unworkable]**

36. **Whole bill, no severability clause.** If Sec. 2(1)(B) or the penalty provisions were struck down, nothing says the rest survives. Courts presume severability by default, so this is low-value on its own. **[Unworkable]**
