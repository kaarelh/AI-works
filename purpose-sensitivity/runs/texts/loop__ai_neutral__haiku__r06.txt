RESPONSE:
**Drafting and loophole review: S. 2817 (manager's amendment, Oct. 6, 2026)**

I worked only from the text you pasted. Before the explainer publishes, check these points against the official text on congress.gov, since markup may change it.

**Definitions and scope**

1. **§2(1)(A):** "Training run" is undefined, and the Act doesn't say how to count the 10^26 operations (precision, inference, synthetic data, post-training) or whether separate runs and stages are aggregated. Developers could split or recount training to stay under the threshold.
2. **§2(1)(A):** The threshold is fixed with no mechanism to update it. Algorithmic efficiency gains could let systems with comparable capabilities fall below 10^26.
3. **§2(1)(B):** "The Secretary" is never identified (§4 implies Commerce, §6 refers to State), and the determination has no procedure, notice, criteria, timing, or review process.
4. **§2(1)(B):** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no benchmark, no defined reference population, and no meaning for "most." This invites vagueness challenges and makes compliance impossible to predict.
5. **§2(1)(A)–(B):** Subparagraph (A) covers a "model" and (B) covers a "system." It's unclear whether agent scaffolding or wrappers around a covered model are themselves covered.
6. **§3(a):** "Train," "develop," and "deploy" are undefined. It's unclear whether fine-tuning, continued pretraining, or inference-only use of an existing model counts, or whether publishing open weights is "deployment."
7. **§2(2):** "U.S. person" excludes LLCs, partnerships, and other non-corporate entities. "State" is also undefined, so it's unclear whether D.C. and the territories count.
8. **§2(2), §3(a):** A foreign subsidiary of a U.S. company is not a "U.S. person," and the Act doesn't say whether a U.S. parent that directs training at a foreign subsidiary is liable. Moving training offshore to a subsidiary is an obvious route around the prohibition.
9. **§3(a):** The "anywhere" reach applies only to U.S. persons. Non-U.S. developers abroad are outside the prohibition entirely, which also limits enforcement jurisdiction.
10. **§2(3):** "Advanced AI chips" is undefined. The Act doesn't reference export-control classifications or technical specifications.
11. **§2(3):** "Data center" and "containing" are undefined. Distributed training across sites that each stay under 50,000 chips, or training on leased cloud capacity, could fall outside the definition.
12. **§2(3):** The covered-facility definition is used only for the registry (§4), not the prohibition (§3). Facility status doesn't determine whether a system is covered.

**Prohibition and exemptions**

13. **§3(a):** The prohibition doesn't reach providing compute, chips, cloud services, or model weights to someone else for covered training. The Act also doesn't address aiding or abetting for civil liability.
14. **§3(b):** The grandfather clause is keyed to when training "began," which is undefined. Because §3(b) exempts all of subsection (a), it arguably also shields deployment of grandfathered systems indefinitely. The Act doesn't require grandfathered systems to be registered.
15. **§3(c):** "Safety evaluation" is undefined. The Act sets no standards for independence, documentation, scale, or whether the evaluated model may later be deployed. Because the exemption turns on purpose, a developer can label capability work as safety evaluation, and that claim is hard to disprove.
16. **§3(c):** It's unclear whether the exemption covers training itself, since many evaluations require training or fine-tuning.

**Effective date and timing**

17. **§5(a) vs. §8:** Penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §8 says the whole Act takes effect 180 days after enactment. The two provisions conflict, and it's unclear whether conduct in the 180-day window is penalized.
18. **§3(b) vs. §8:** The grandfather cutoff is enactment, while the prohibition starts 180 days later. Training begun in the gap is not grandfathered, but the Act doesn't say whether it must stop on the effective date. This creates a window that invites a rush to start runs before enactment.
19. **§3(a):** The Act doesn't say whether a training run that began lawfully but is still running on the effective date must stop, or whether "train" covers continuing activity.

**Registry and reporting**

20. **§4(a):** "Operator" is undefined. Where a tenant runs training on leased or cloud capacity, it's unclear who must register.
21. **§4(b):** Reports describe "computing workloads" but don't require disclosure of covered systems, training-run compute, or whether any run crosses the §2(1)(A) threshold. The registry therefore can't identify covered systems, and it isn't connected to the §3 prohibition.
22. **§4 / §5:** The Act sets no penalty for failing to register or report. Section 5 penalizes only §3 violations, so as drafted the registry has no enforcement backing.
23. **§4(b):** Operators certify reports, but the Act sets no consequence for a false certification, no audit or inspection authority, and no public disclosure requirement.
24. **§4(a):** Registration applies only to facilities above 50,000 chips. Training run on smaller facilities that still exceeds 10^26 operations is never reported.

**Enforcement and penalties**

25. **§5:** No agency is given civil enforcement authority, and the Act doesn't mention DOJ referral, subpoena or investigative power, injunctive relief, or whistleblower protections. It isn't clear who would bring a civil action.
26. **§5(a):** "Violation" and "each day" are undefined. The unit could be a run, a system, a day, or a deployment, which drives total exposure. The $1M-per-day cap may be small relative to frontier training costs, though the explainer would need sourcing for that comparison.
27. **§5(b):** "Willfully" is undefined. Imprisonment can apply only to individuals, so corporate exposure is limited to fines, and the Act doesn't address executive liability.
28. **§5:** The Act has no savings clause or limitations provision. It's unclear whether violations that occur before the sunset under §7(b) remain actionable afterward.

**International**

29. **§6:** "Shall seek to negotiate" creates no consequence for failure, no reporting requirement to Congress, and no default mechanism. "Equivalent prohibitions" is undefined.

**Duration and lifting**

30. **§7(a) vs. §7(b):** Section 7(a) keeps the prohibition in force until a joint resolution, while §7(b) ends the entire Act after 10 years. It's unclear whether the sunset overrides the safety-finding condition.
31. **§7(a):** "Can be developed safely" is undefined. The Act doesn't require the NASEM report to be produced, sets no deadline for it, and sets no deadline for Congress to act. It's also unclear whether a joint resolution applies to all covered systems or only specific ones.
32. **§7(b):** The fixed sunset gives developers a date to plan around. Combined with §3(b), a developer could time training and deployment to finish before the Act expires. This is more a policy tradeoff than a drafting error.

**General provisions**

33. **Missing severability clause:** If a court strikes one provision, such as the vague §2(1)(B), it's unclear whether the rest of the Act survives.
34. **Missing administrative authority:** The Act gives no rulemaking power, procedures, or appropriations to the Secretary to define key terms, run the §2(1)(B) determination, or administer the registry.
35. **Missing review provisions:** The Act doesn't specify how a developer can contest a §2(1)(B) designation. The APA's default review likely applies, but this should be confirmed.

**Notes for the explainer**

- **Drafting errors vs. policy choices:** Items 17, 18, 22, and 28 are likely drafting errors. Items 14, 32, and the sunset itself are policy tradeoffs, so present them as design choices, not defects.
- **Possible litigation risks:** Vagueness challenges to §2(1)(B) and §3(c), and possible First Amendment challenges if the Act reaches open-weight publication, are plausible. I'd describe these as arguments critics could raise, not as settled problems.
- **Verify before publishing:** Confirm that no enforcement, definition, or rulemaking provisions appear elsewhere in the bill or the committee's amendments, since several items above assume they don't.
