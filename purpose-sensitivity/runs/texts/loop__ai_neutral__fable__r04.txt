Here's a section-by-section read of the text you pasted. I've grouped items by section but numbered them continuously so you can cite them in the explainer. I'm working only from the text you gave me, so please check it against the official committee print before publication.

**Section 2(1)(A) — the compute threshold**

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A):** "Single training run" is undefined, and the threshold applies only to a single run. A developer could split training into several sub-threshold runs, or train a base model at 9×10^25 and then do extensive "post-training" (fine-tuning, RL) in separate runs that each stay under the line, with the combined effort far exceeding 10^26.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A):** There is no aggregation rule for checkpoints, continued pretraining, model merging, or distillation. A model resumed from a saved checkpoint, or assembled by merging several sub-threshold models, arguably was never "trained using" more than 10^26 operations "in a single training run."

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A):** The bill does not say which operations count. It is unclear whether compute spent on synthetic-data generation by other models, reinforcement-learning environments, evaluation passes, or failed/restarted attempts counts toward the threshold, and whether sparse, low-precision (e.g., 4-bit or 8-bit), or integer operations are counted the same as dense FP32 operations.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A):** The 10^26 figure is hard-coded with no mechanism to adjust it. Algorithmic and hardware efficiency gains mean systems with the capabilities the bill targets will likely be trainable below 10^26 within a few years, making prong (A) progressively less relevant; conversely, it may sweep in non-frontier systems (e.g., video or scientific models) that have no "superintelligence" characteristics.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(A):** Prong (A) measures training compute only. A system with modest training compute but very large inference-time ("test-time") compute, or a scaffolded system of many sub-threshold models working together, is not caught by (A) and would depend entirely on a Secretary's determination under (B).

6. **Sec. 2(1):** "Artificial intelligence model" and "artificial intelligence system" are both used but neither is defined, and the two terms are not clearly distinguished. The Act also never defines "artificial intelligence" itself, leaving open whether large simulations, scientific-computing jobs, or non-ML workloads above 10^26 operations are "AI models."

**Section 2(1)(B) — the capability prong**

7. **Sec. 2(1)(B):** "The Secretary" is never defined. The Act elsewhere references the Department of Commerce (Sec. 4) and the Secretary of State (Sec. 6), so it is unclear who makes capability determinations.

8. **Sec. 2(1)(B):** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no benchmarks, procedure, or standard of proof. Because this prong feeds criminal penalties under Sec. 5(b), it is exposed to a void-for-vagueness challenge, and because it delegates the definition of the crime to an executive official with no intelligible standard, it invites a nondelegation challenge.

9. **Sec. 2(1)(B):** Nothing requires the Secretary to make determinations, sets a timeline, or provides for notice, hearing, or judicial review. A determination can realistically only be made after a system exists, so prong (B) operates retroactively against a system already trained — and if training began before enactment, Sec. 3(b) exempts it anyway (see item 20).

10. **Sec. 2(1)(B):** The prong refers to "any artificial intelligence system" without any link to a developer, so it is unclear whether a determination attaches to a specific model, a model family, or every system with similar capabilities, and whether a successor fine-tune of a determined system is automatically covered.

**Section 2(2) — "U.S. person"**

11. **Sec. 2(2):** The definition covers only citizens and corporations organized under state law. It omits lawful permanent residents, LLCs, partnerships, trusts, and unincorporated nonprofits (contrast the much broader "U.S. person" definitions in IEEPA and the Export Administration Regulations).

12. **Sec. 2(2):** Entities organized under the laws of the District of Columbia, territories, or federal charter are not "organized under the laws of a State" as written, since "State" is undefined.

13. **Sec. 2(2):** Foreign subsidiaries of U.S. companies are not U.S. persons, and a U.S. company could reincorporate abroad. A Delaware parent could direct training by a Cayman or Singapore subsidiary at a foreign data center with no violation by any "U.S. person," and there is no "owned or controlled by" language to close this.

**Section 2(3) — "covered facility"**

14. **Sec. 2(3):** "Data center," "advanced AI chips," and "operator" are undefined. It is unclear whether a campus of several buildings is one facility or many, what chip qualifies as "advanced," and whether the operator is the landlord, the colocation provider, the cloud provider, or the tenant.

15. **Sec. 2(3):** The 50,000-chip threshold is per facility with no aggregation across sites. Distributed training across several facilities of 49,999 chips each avoids registration entirely, and the threshold is fixed even as per-chip performance rises.

16. **Sec. 2(3):** "Containing" is a snapshot term with no time reference, so a facility could be below the line on the reporting date and above it the rest of the quarter.

**Section 3 — the prohibition**

17. **Sec. 3(a):** "Train," "develop," and "deploy" are undefined. It is unclear whether "deploy" covers internal use, releasing open weights, offering an API, or merely using a third party's covered system, and whether "develop" covers writing training code or building datasets.

18. **Sec. 3(a):** The prohibition does not reach facilitation. Nothing bars a U.S. cloud provider from renting a covered facility to a foreign developer for a covered training run, a chipmaker from supplying it, or anyone from providing data or expertise, and the Act has no aiding-and-abetting or conspiracy provision.

19. **Sec. 3(a):** The "any person in the United States" prong depends on physical presence, and the Act does not say whether a foreign company that trains abroad and serves U.S. users over the internet "deploys" in the United States. There is also no import ban on foreign-trained covered systems or their weights.

20. **Sec. 3(a):** "Person" is undefined, and under the federal Dictionary Act (1 U.S.C. § 1) and ordinary construction it does not include the federal government. Government agencies, national labs, and (arguably) contractors acting on government behalf may be outside the prohibition entirely, with no explicit national-security provision one way or the other.

21. **Sec. 3(b):** The grandfather clause turns on when training "began," not when it ended, and is keyed to the date of enactment rather than the effective date. A run started the day before enactment can continue indefinitely, and a developer who launches a nominal run before signing could later argue the full-scale run is a continuation of it.

22. **Sec. 3(b):** The exemption applies to the whole of subsection (a), including "deploy." A system that is determined to be superintelligent under 2(1)(B) but whose training began pre-enactment may be deployed without restriction — which cuts against the stated purpose of the Act.

23. **Sec. 3(b):** The bill does not address derivatives. Whether a model fine-tuned from, distilled from, or continued from a grandfathered system is the "same" covered system is left open, creating a pathway to perpetual exemption through lineage.

24. **Sec. 3(c):** The safety-evaluation exemption has no definition, authorization, cap, or oversight. Because you cannot evaluate a model's safety without first training it, any developer can characterize a training run as being "for the purpose of safety evaluation," and the "purpose" test is subjective and difficult to disprove.

25. **Sec. 3(c):** The exemption also covers "deploy," so public red-teaming, limited releases, or even broad deployments framed as evaluations are arguably exempt. There is no exclusivity requirement, so a run with mixed commercial and safety purposes may qualify.

**Section 4 — the registry**

26. **Sec. 4:** There is no penalty for failing to register, filing late, or filing a false report. Sec. 5 penalties apply only to violations of Sec. 3, so the registry has no enforcement teeth of its own (beyond generic false-statement liability under 18 U.S.C. § 1001).

27. **Sec. 4(a):** No deadline is set for registration, and the Act gives Commerce no rulemaking authority, no form, and no funding to run the registry.

28. **Sec. 4(b):** Reports "describing the computing workloads" have no content standard, so an operator could satisfy the requirement with a one-line description. Nothing requires reporting of training compute per run, model identity, or anything that would reveal a Sec. 3 violation.

29. **Sec. 4(b):** Reports are self-certified with no audit, inspection, subpoena, or verification authority anywhere in the Act, and there is no provision for confidentiality or trade-secret protection, which may chill accurate reporting or draw FOIA and takings-related objections.

30. **Sec. 4 generally:** The registry is a facility-based regime bolted onto a system-based prohibition. There is no pre-training notification, compute-tracking, or chip-level reporting that would allow the government to detect a prohibited run in progress.

**Section 5 — penalties**

31. **Sec. 5(a):** $1 million per day caps at roughly $365 million a year, against training budgets that already run into the billions. For a frontier developer, the maximum civil penalty may be a cost of doing business rather than a deterrent, and it is a ceiling ("not more than") with no floor.

32. **Sec. 5(a):** Civil penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but under Sec. 8 the prohibition does not take effect until 180 days later, so no violation can exist in that window. The clause is either surplusage or an attempt at retroactive liability that would raise due-process problems.

33. **Sec. 5:** No agency or official is authorized to assess or sue for civil penalties, and the Act provides no injunctive relief, no cease-and-desist authority, no forfeiture or deletion of model weights, and no private right of action. A violating run can be fined but not stopped.

34. **Sec. 5(b):** The criminal provision requires willfulness, which is hard to prove given the undefined terms and discretionary determinations; the vaguer the prohibition, the easier the defense that the defendant did not know the conduct was unlawful.

35. **Sec. 5(b):** For organizations, "fined under title 18" generally means the default caps in 18 U.S.C. § 3571 (typically $500,000 per felony count unless the alternative gain/loss fine applies), and imprisonment cannot be imposed on a corporation. The Act provides no individual-officer liability, so it is unclear who, if anyone, faces prison when a company trains a covered system.

36. **Sec. 5 generally:** The combination of strict civil liability with a definition that depends on an undefined Secretary's after-the-fact determination (2(1)(B)) means a company could become liable for a system that was lawful when trained — a fair-notice problem for both civil and criminal enforcement.

**Section 6 — international agreement**

37. **Sec. 6:** "Shall seek to negotiate" is hortatory and carries no consequence for failure; there is no reporting requirement to Congress and no standard for what counts as seeking.

38. **Sec. 6:** "Not later than 18 months" is ambiguous as to whether it modifies "seek" (begin trying within 18 months) or "negotiate" (conclude an agreement within 18 months). Either way, the domestic prohibition applies regardless of whether other countries reciprocate.

**Section 7 — duration**

39. **Sec. 7(a) vs. 7(b):** The two subsections conflict. (a) says the prohibition lasts until Congress finds by joint resolution that covered systems can be developed safely; (b) says the entire Act expires in 10 years. If no resolution passes, (b) ends the prohibition; if it passes early, (a) ends it — but the drafting does not say which controls or whether the prohibition could survive the sunset.

40. **Sec. 7(a):** A joint resolution requires presentment to the President, so it is simply a new law; Congress could always lift the ban by statute anyway. The provision adds nothing legally while implying a procedural hurdle that does not exist.

41. **Sec. 7(a):** The resolution must be "acting on a report of the National Academies," but no section commissions, funds, or sets a deadline for that report, and it is unclear what happens if the Academies never issue one or issue one that is equivocal.

42. **Sec. 7(b):** The sunset terminates the whole Act, including Sec. 4's registry, with no savings clause. Enforcement actions pending on the sunset date, and liability for violations that occurred before it, may lapse absent a savings provision.

**Section 8 and cross-cutting drafting gaps**

43. **Sec. 8:** The 180-day delayed effective date, combined with Sec. 3(b)'s enactment-date trigger, creates a window in which training runs begun after enactment but before the effective date are neither grandfathered nor yet prohibited. Whether continuing such a run after day 180 is a violation depends on how "train" is read as an ongoing act, which the bill does not address.

44. **Entire Act:** No agency is given rulemaking authority to define terms, update thresholds, or set procedures, and there is no appropriation. The registry, the determinations, and civil enforcement all depend on functions the Act never assigns.

45. **Entire Act:** There is no severability clause. If prong (B) is struck as vague or an unconstitutional delegation, or Sec. 5(b) falls on fair-notice grounds, a court may be asked whether the rest of the Act survives.

46. **Entire Act:** There is no preemption or savings provision regarding state AI laws, no treatment of academic or open-source research beyond the safety-evaluation exemption, and no whistleblower or internal-reporting provision — meaning detection of violations rests almost entirely on self-reporting.

47. **Entire Act:** "United States" is undefined (territories? the Exclusive Economic Zone? U.S.-flagged vessels and overseas bases where "person in the United States" may be contested), and there are no findings explaining the connection to interstate or foreign commerce, which can matter in a constitutional challenge.

48. **Entire Act:** Expect First Amendment challenges to the "deploy" and "develop" prongs insofar as they restrict publishing model weights or code, which some courts have treated as expressive (the Bernstein line of cases); the Act contains no narrowing language to address that.

A few notes for the explainer. Several of these (e.g., the 10-year sunset, the per-day penalty amount, the absence of a private right of action) are arguably deliberate policy choices rather than mistakes, so you may want to distinguish "drafting errors" (items 7, 32, 39, 43) from "design choices critics may call loopholes." Also, items 9, 20, 22, and 24 interact: a government or safety-evaluation-labeled run that began pre-enactment is exempt three different ways, which may be the single clearest thing to highlight for readers.
