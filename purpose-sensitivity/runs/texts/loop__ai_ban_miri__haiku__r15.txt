RESPONSE:
Here are the issues I found in the October 6 text of S. 2817, grouped by section and numbered for your cover letter. The 50,000-chip estimate in item 10 is my rough calculation and should be checked by technical staff before it goes to the office.

**Section 2 (Definitions)**

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A):** The 10^26 threshold applies to a "single training run," so a developer could split a training program into several runs, or reach the threshold through continued pretraining, fine-tuning, or RL post-training, without any single run crossing it. The threshold should apply to cumulative compute across a model's development lineage, including post-training.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A):** "Quantity of computing power" is undefined. It doesn't say whether it counts only pretraining, whether it includes synthetic data generation or distillation, or whether it's measured by planned or actual operations.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A):** A fixed compute threshold will become outdated as algorithms become more efficient, so systems with dangerous capabilities could fall below 10^26. The bill should authorize the Secretary to lower the threshold by rule.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A)–(B) and Sec. 3(a):** Section 3 prohibits "covered AI systems," but subsection (A) defines covered systems by reference to a single model. An agentic system or ensemble built from sub-threshold models could escape (A), and (B) only partly covers it. The definition should expressly include composed systems.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(B):** "The Secretary" is not identified (presumably the Secretary of Commerce), and the bill sets no procedure, deadline, evidentiary standard, or notice requirement for the capability determination. Without these, any determination is open to challenge as arbitrary under the APA.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B):** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no benchmark or measurement method. Developers could dispute whether any given system meets it, and the Secretary's determination would be hard to defend.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(B):** The bill doesn't say whether a system that already exists and later crosses the capability standard is covered, or whether the determination applies only to systems trained after it is made. This timing question should be answered explicitly.

8. **Sec. 2(2) ("U.S. person"):** The definition covers only citizens and corporations organized under state law. It excludes LLCs, partnerships, permanent residents, and foreign-incorporated entities controlled by U.S. persons.

9. **Sec. 2(2) and Sec. 3(a):** "U.S. person anywhere" reaches the entity itself but not the foreign subsidiaries, affiliates, or offshore vehicles that U.S. companies routinely use to train models. The definition should include entities controlled by U.S. persons, and should address officers and directors who direct such work.

10. **Sec. 2(3) ("covered facility"):** "Advanced AI chip" is undefined. The 50,000-chip threshold also appears set above what a 10^26 run may require: at current accelerator performance, such a run might need on the order of 10,000–30,000 chips for a few months, so a facility could train a covered system without being registered.

11. **Sec. 2(3):** "Data center" and "facility" are undefined. A developer could spread a training cluster across several sites, each below 50,000 chips, to avoid registration. Counting should be aggregated by owner, operator, or interconnected cluster.

**Section 3 (Prohibition)**

12. **Sec. 3(a):** "Train," "develop," and "deploy" are undefined, and "develop" overlaps with "train." It's unclear whether fine-tuning, inference, or research counts. The bill also doesn't mention making model weights available, including open-weight release, which is distinct from deployment.

13. **Sec. 3(a):** The prohibition doesn't reach anyone who supplies chips, cloud compute, data-center capacity, or financing for a covered training run. An aiding-and-abetting or knowing-provision prohibition is needed for the bill to reach the infrastructure that makes covered training possible.

14. **Sec. 3(a):** "Person" is undefined. It's also unclear whether a foreign company training abroad for foreign customers is covered when U.S. users access the system remotely.

15. **Sec. 3(b) (existing systems):** "Training began" is undefined, and there's no cutoff date tied to introduction or markup. This creates an incentive to start runs in the window before enactment, and the clause lets systems trained before enactment be deployed indefinitely, with no registration or deployment restrictions.

16. **Sec. 3(c) (safety evaluation):** This is the largest hole. The exemption turns on the developer's stated purpose, has no definition or scale limit, requires no notice, no independent or authorized evaluator, and no bar on deployment, so a company can label a training run a "safety evaluation" and avoid the prohibition. The exemption should require advance notice to Commerce, qualified evaluators, compute caps, documentation, and a prohibition on deployment.

**Section 4 (Registry)**

17. **Sec. 4(a)–(b):** The registry covers only operators of covered facilities, not developers of covered systems. Nothing requires reporting of training runs, model identity, or training compute, so Commerce can't tell from "computing workloads" whether a covered system is being built.

18. **Sec. 4(a):** The bill sets no deadline for registration, doesn't require registration before operation, and provides no penalty for failing to register or for filing a false certification.

19. **Sec. 4(b):** Reports are self-certified and quarterly, and the bill gives Commerce no audit, inspection, or verification authority. A training run that lasts months could be completed before the first report is due.

20. **Sec. 4:** There's no requirement to notify Commerce before a large training run begins. A pre-notification requirement would give the government a chance to act before compute is committed.

21. **Sec. 4:** The bill has no confidentiality provisions, so operators have little assurance that reported information is protected. It also doesn't provide for sharing reports with enforcement agencies.

**Section 5 (Penalties)**

22. **Sec. 5(a):** The civil penalty is "per day" with no minimum, no per-model or per-run measure, and no inflation adjustment. For a training run worth hundreds of millions of dollars, a daily penalty may be treated as a cost of doing business. The penalty should be the greater of a fixed amount or a percentage of revenue or compute spending, and should apply per system per day. Section 4 violations also carry no penalty at all.

23. **Sec. 5 (general):** The bill doesn't name the agency or official who enforces the civil penalty, and it grants no authority to seek a temporary restraining order, injunction, shutdown order, or seizure or destruction of weights and hardware. Without these powers, the government's main response to a violation is a fine.

24. **Sec. 5(b):** "Willfully" is a high bar to prove, and the bill has no "knowingly" standard, no responsible-corporate-officer liability, and no provision holding individuals accountable for a company's violations.

25. **Sec. 5(a) and Sec. 8:** Section 5(a) applies civil penalties to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but Section 8 delays the prohibition's effective date by 180 days. It's unclear whether conduct during those 180 days can be penalized. The two provisions should be aligned.

**Section 6 (International agreement)**

26. **Sec. 6:** "Shall seek to negotiate" creates no obligation and no consequence if negotiations fail. The bill also doesn't require the Secretary to report to Congress, and "equivalent" is undefined, so a weaker agreement could be treated as satisfying the requirement. It should specify minimum content, require reporting, and link to export controls on chips for non-participating jurisdictions.

**Section 7 (Duration)**

27. **Sec. 7(a) and 7(b):** Section 7(a) keeps the prohibition in place indefinitely until Congress acts, but Section 7(b) ends the entire Act after 10 years. The bill doesn't say which controls, so developers could argue the prohibition lapses in 10 years. The conflict should be resolved, most likely by removing the sunset.

28. **Sec. 7(a):** The lifting mechanism is unclear. The NASEM report has no deadline or required content, "can be developed safely" has no standard of proof, and the bill doesn't say whether the joint resolution must be presented to the President for signature or a veto override. Each of these should be specified.

**Section 8 (Effective date)**

29. **Sec. 8:** The effective date applies to the prohibition, but the registry (Sec. 4), the international agreement (Sec. 6), and the duration provisions (Sec. 7) have no stated effective dates, and the bill has no transition rule for facilities that must register. Each provision should have an explicit effective date.

**General provisions**

30. **Whole bill:** The bill gives Commerce no rulemaking authority to define terms, update thresholds, or specify reporting content. Commerce would be relying on implied authority, which makes any implementing rule easier to challenge.

31. **Whole bill:** There's no severability clause. If a court strikes one provision (for example, the vague capability standard in Sec. 2(1)(B)), the rest of the Act could fall with it.

32. **Whole bill:** The bill has no judicial-review provision, no savings or preemption clause on state AI laws, and no appropriation for enforcement.

33. **Whole bill:** The bill has no whistleblower protections for employees who report violations, which are often the main source of information about covert training runs.

34. **Whole bill:** Several key terms are undefined, including "artificial intelligence," "training run," "deploy," "operator," "person," and "Secretary."
