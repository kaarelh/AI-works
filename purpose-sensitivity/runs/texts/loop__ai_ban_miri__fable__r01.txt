RESPONSE:
Here's a section-by-section audit. I've erred toward listing everything, so Whitfield's staff can triage.

## Sec. 2(1)(A) — compute threshold

1. **§2(1)(A), "in a single training run."** A developer can split training into several runs each under 10^26 (pretraining, then continued pretraining, then RL/post-training), or merge/distill from several sub-threshold models, and never trip the threshold. The definition should aggregate all compute used to produce a model, including fine-tuning and distillation from parent models, and should reach derivative models trained from a covered model.

2. **§2(1)(A), fixed numeric threshold with no adjustment authority.** Algorithmic efficiency gains mean systems trained with far less than 10^26 operations will soon match today's frontier, so the number will be obsolete well before the 10-year sunset. The bill gives no one authority to lower the threshold (it could allow lowering but not raising).

3. **§2(1)(A), undefined "integer or floating-point operations."** The bill does not say how to count operations (dense vs. sparse, mixed precision, whether to count only the forward/backward pass, whether evaluation and failed runs count). Developers will choose the most favorable counting method and litigate the rest.

4. **§2(1)(A), "artificial intelligence model" and "trained" undefined.** Neither term is defined anywhere in the Act, which invites arguments that a system of many coordinated models, or a model improved by inference-time compute rather than training, falls outside the definition.

5. **§2(1)(A), no coverage of composite systems.** A capability equivalent to a covered system could be assembled from multiple sub-threshold models with scaffolding and heavy inference compute; the definition looks only at training compute per model.

## Sec. 2(1)(B) — Secretary determination

6. **§2(1)(B), "the Secretary" is never defined.** Sec. 4 refers to the Department of Commerce and Sec. 6 to the Secretary of State, but no provision designates which Secretary makes covered-system determinations. This is a basic drafting error that makes the provision unusable as written.

7. **§2(1)(B), determination is discretionary and after-the-fact.** The Secretary "determines," with no duty to act, no criteria, no procedure, no deadline, and no judicial review provision; an administration opposed to the Act can simply never make a determination. And because the test is whether a "system" is "capable," it can only apply once a system already exists, so it does nothing to stop training.

8. **§2(1)(B), vagueness exposure.** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no operational definition and no rulemaking authority behind it. Combined with criminal penalties in Sec. 5(b), this invites void-for-vagueness and nondelegation challenges.

## Sec. 2(2) — U.S. person

9. **§2(2), definition is far narrower than standard.** It covers only citizens and corporations "organized under the laws of a State," omitting LLCs, partnerships, trusts, nonprofits, lawful permanent residents, entities organized in D.C. or U.S. territories, and foreign subsidiaries owned or controlled by U.S. persons. A frontier lab could escape the extraterritorial reach by reorganizing as an LLC, incorporating in Delaware's neighbors (D.C.), or training through a wholly owned foreign subsidiary. Recommend adopting the IEEPA/export-control-style definition including controlled foreign entities.

10. **§2(2) and §3(a), nothing reaches foreign training procured by U.S. persons.** A U.S. company can contract with or fund a foreign entity to train the model and then import the weights; "train, develop, or deploy" does not clearly cover funding, procuring, or acquiring.

## Sec. 2(3) — covered facility

11. **§2(3), "advanced AI chips" undefined.** Without a definition (or authority to define by rule), no one knows which facilities must register, and the number will be gamed by using chips just below whatever line is later drawn.

12. **§2(3), per-facility chip count invites splitting.** A developer can keep each site at 49,999 chips and train across sites, which current distributed-training methods allow. The definition should aggregate facilities under common ownership or control, or trigger on compute capacity rather than a chip count.

13. **§2(3), "data center" and "operator" undefined.** In colocation arrangements, it is unclear whether the owner, lessee, or cloud customer is the "operator," so each can point to the others.

## Sec. 3 — prohibition

14. **§3(a), narrow list of prohibited acts.** The bill reaches "train, develop, or deploy" but not possess, use, transfer, sell, export, import, release (e.g., open weights), fund, or provide compute for a covered system. There is also no attempt, conspiracy, or aiding-and-abetting provision, so cloud providers, chip suppliers, and investors who knowingly enable a covered run face no liability.

15. **§3(a), "deploy" and "develop" undefined.** It is unclear whether internal use, research use, or hosting a model trained abroad counts as deployment, and "develop" could be read to cover everything or nothing.

16. **§3(a), federal government likely not bound.** "Any person" presumptively excludes the United States and its agencies absent express language, so a Department of Defense or intelligence-community program would fall outside the prohibition. If the intent is no government exemption, the bill must say so.

17. **§3(b), grandfather clause can be exploited before enactment.** Because the exemption turns on whether "training began" before enactment, a lab can begin nominal training runs on every planned project the day before signing and claim all of them are grandfathered, then continue training indefinitely. There is no requirement that training be substantially complete, no cap on further compute, and no cutoff for continued training of a grandfathered model.

18. **§3(b), exempts Secretary-determined systems too.** A system covered under §2(1)(B) that started training pre-enactment is fully exempt from the prohibition, including deployment, no matter how capable it is. If the finding is that such systems are an extinction risk, the exemption should at minimum not extend to §2(1)(B) systems.

19. **§3(b) and §8, 180-day gap.** Training begun between enactment and the effective date is neither grandfathered under §3(b) nor prohibited until day 180, creating an ambiguous status for runs that straddle the effective date and an incentive to race in that window.

20. **§3(c), safety-evaluation exemption swallows the rule.** "Activities conducted for the purpose of safety evaluation" is undefined, requires no authorization, and is tested by the actor's stated purpose. Any training run can be characterized as evaluating whether the resulting system is safe. This exemption should be removed or confined to evaluation of already-existing systems under a license with compute caps and government oversight.

## Sec. 4 — registry

21. **§4, no penalties for noncompliance.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3, so failing to register, failing to report, or filing false reports carries no consequence under this Act (beyond generic false-statement law).

22. **§4(b), self-certified reports with no verification.** There is no inspection, audit, or subpoena authority, no specification of what the report must contain (compute used per workload, customer identity, model identifiers), and no requirement to report training runs approaching the §2(1)(A) threshold. The registry therefore cannot detect violations.

23. **§4, no deadlines or rulemaking.** The bill sets no registration deadline and gives Commerce no rulemaking authority to specify report formats, define terms, or impose know-your-customer obligations on cloud providers.

24. **§4, no coverage of compute outside covered facilities.** Chips distributed across many sub-threshold sites, or located abroad under U.S. control, are invisible to the registry.

## Sec. 5 — penalties

25. **§5(a), civil penalty is not a deterrent.** A $1 million per day cap is a rounding error against training runs costing billions; there is no minimum, no scaling to the violator's size or the value of the run, and no authority for injunctions, seizure or forfeiture of weights and hardware, or debarment from federal contracts.

26. **§5(a), "on or after the date of enactment" conflicts with §8.** The prohibition does not take effect until 180 days after enactment, so no violation can occur in that window; the inconsistency is at best confusing and at worst invites ex post facto challenges.

27. **§5, no enforcing agency designated.** The bill does not say who brings civil actions (DOJ, Commerce, FTC) or who investigates, and it provides no private right of action, whistleblower protection, or statute of limitations.

28. **§5(b), individual liability unclear.** The criminal provision does not address officers, directors, or employees who direct a covered run, so corporate structure may shield the decision-makers.

## Sec. 6 — international agreement

29. **§6, hortatory only.** "Shall seek to negotiate" imposes no reporting requirement, no benchmark, and no consequence, and gives Congress no information with which to assess progress; it should at least require periodic reports and specify verification goals.

## Sec. 7 — duration

30. **§7(a) vs. §7(b), direct contradiction.** Subsection (a) says the prohibition lasts until Congress affirmatively finds safety; subsection (b) sunsets the entire Act (including the registry) after 10 years regardless. The sunset controls as the more specific provision, so the prohibition expires in 2036 unless reenacted, and labs can plan around that date. Strike (b).

31. **§7(a), no mechanism for the NAS report.** The provision conditions repeal on a National Academies report but never commissions, funds, or scopes one, and "finds by joint resolution" is undefined as to procedure.

## Sec. 8 — effective date

32. **§8, delayed effective date undermines the prohibition.** Beyond the §3(b) interaction above, a 180-day delay for a bill whose premise is imminent catastrophic risk is hard to justify; the prohibition should be effective on enactment, with any delay confined to registry mechanics.

## Omissions (no section)

33. **No severability clause.** If §2(1)(B) or §3(c) is struck down, a court could invalidate the entire Act.

34. **No rulemaking or definitions authority anywhere.** Nearly every gap above (chip definition, operation counting, threshold adjustment, report contents) could be closed administratively if some agency were given rulemaking power; none is.

35. **No treatment of model weights as such.** Nothing restricts possession, transfer, export, or publication of covered weights, so a covered model trained abroad or pre-enactment can circulate freely in the United States.

36. **No definition of "artificial intelligence," "training," "deploy," "Secretary," or "advanced AI chip."** These are the load-bearing terms of the Act and each is undefined.

37. **No anti-circumvention provision.** The Act lacks a general clause prohibiting structuring activities to evade the thresholds, which is standard in regimes with numeric triggers.

38. **No relation-to-other-law clause.** The bill is silent on preemption and on its interaction with existing export controls, leaving open arguments either way.
