# S. 2817: Loopholes, Ambiguities, and Drafting Problems

## Definitions (Sec. 2)

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "more than 10,000 metric tons per year."** Any operation below the threshold is legal, including pilot and test mining, which has involved thousands of tons per test. The threshold also doesn't say whether tonnage is wet or dry, which matters a lot because nodules hold substantial water, or whether "year" means a calendar year or any 12-month period.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "by a single vessel."** A company can split recovery across several vessels, each staying under 10,000 tons, while the fleet as a whole mines at commercial scale. The threshold should apply per operator, including affiliates, not per vessel.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "by a single vessel," continued.** Recovery by something that isn't clearly a "vessel" may fall outside the definition entirely. Examples include seabed collector vehicles, fixed or semi-submersible platforms, and riser systems. The definition should cover recovery "by any means."

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "recovery."** The term is undefined. Companies could argue that collecting, crushing, or stockpiling material on the seabed without lifting it to the surface is not "recovery," and that the seabed disturbance itself is unregulated.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "other mineral resources" and "seabed."** Neither term is defined. It's unclear whether cobalt-rich crusts, polymetallic sulfides, subsoil deposits, or sand and gravel are covered, and the vagueness invites both evasion and overbreadth challenges. The definition should list covered resources and state that "seabed" includes the subsoil.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B), "the Secretary."** "Secretary" is never defined anywhere in the bill, so it's unclear which agency (Commerce/NOAA or Interior) holds this power.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(B), catch-all determination.** Letting an official expand what counts as a crime ("risks... comparable to") with no rulemaking procedure invites vagueness (fair-notice) and nondelegation challenges. There is no severability clause to protect the rest of the Act if (B) is struck. The provision also does nothing unless a future Secretary chooses to act.

8. **Sec. 2(2), "U.S. person," entity types.** The definition covers only citizens and corporations. It omits LLCs, partnerships, trusts, lawful permanent residents, and federally chartered entities. Most operating companies are LLCs.

9. **Sec. 2(2), "U.S. person," foreign subsidiaries.** Foreign-incorporated subsidiaries or affiliates of U.S. companies are not covered, so a U.S. firm can mine through an offshore affiliate. Leading seabed-mining companies are already incorporated abroad with U.S. subsidiaries. The bill should cover entities "owned or controlled by" a U.S. person.

10. **Sec. 2(2), "State."** "State" is undefined. It's unclear whether corporations organized in D.C., Puerto Rico, Guam, or other territories are U.S. persons.

11. **Sec. 2(3), "covered vessel."** The 5,000 gross ton floor and the "equipped for recovery" test let smaller vessels, or vessels that remove equipment between trips, avoid registration. The registry's scope also doesn't match the prohibition's scope, so some mining under Sec. 3 would never show up in the registry.

## Prohibition (Sec. 3)

12. **Sec. 3(a), "in the United States."** "United States" is undefined. A foreign company mining in the U.S. EEZ or on the U.S. continental shelf could argue it is not "in the United States." The bill should expressly cover the territorial sea, EEZ, continental shelf, and territories.

13. **Sec. 3(a), "engage in."** The bill reaches only those who directly mine. It does not cover financing, sponsoring, insuring, contracting for, or providing vessels or equipment to mining operations, and the civil penalty has no aiding-and-abetting or conspiracy provision.

14. **Sec. 3(a), no import or processing ban.** Nothing bars importing, processing, buying, or selling minerals mined from the deep seabed by foreign entities. U.S. demand can still drive mining that the bill can't reach directly.

15. **Sec. 3, no interaction with existing law.** The bill doesn't amend or repeal the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (DSHMRA) or address the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act. NOAA could keep issuing licenses and permits, and companies would argue a federal permit authorizes their activity. The bill should bar new licenses and permits, address pending applications, and revoke or void existing commercial recovery permits.

16. **Sec. 3(b), the grandfather clause.** This is the biggest loophole. It exempts "mining" conducted under any pre-enactment exploration license, which turns exploration licenses into permanent mining rights. Under DSHMRA, exploration licenses don't even authorize commercial recovery.

17. **Sec. 3(b), scope of the grandfather clause.** The clause doesn't address renewals, extensions, modifications, transfers, or expansions of covered licenses, or set any expiration. It also doesn't say who issued the license (NOAA, a foreign state, or possibly ISA exploration contracts), which could exempt operations licensed abroad.

18. **Sec. 3(b) together with Sec. 8, enactment versus effective date.** The exemption is tied to enactment, but the prohibition takes effect 180 days later. That creates a 180-day window to rush licenses, operations, and stockpiling. The bill should tie both to the same date and prohibit new licenses as of introduction or enactment.

19. **Sec. 3(c), "environmental assessment."** The term is undefined, self-declared, and has no permit, tonnage cap, oversight, or time limit. Test mining is routinely described as environmental study, so this exemption could cover substantial extraction. The bill should require a permit, cap volumes, impose monitoring, and bar sale of recovered material.

## Registry (Sec. 4)

20. **Sec. 4, no penalty.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3. Failing to register, failing to report, or filing false reports carries no penalty under the Act, though 18 U.S.C. § 1001 might apply to false statements.

21. **Sec. 4(b), report content.** The required reports don't specify tonnage, location, depth, dates, or the resource type recovered. Without that data, the 10,000-ton threshold can't be enforced.

22. **Sec. 4(b), self-certification only.** The bill provides no verification tools: no vessel monitoring or AIS requirement, observers, inspections, recordkeeping, or public disclosure. Reports also have no deadline or form requirements.

23. **Sec. 4, jurisdiction.** The bill doesn't say whether the registry applies to foreign-flagged vessels or foreign operators. It also places the registry with NOAA while Sec. 2(1)(B) gives authority to an undefined "Secretary."

## Penalties and Enforcement (Sec. 5)

24. **Sec. 5, no enforcement authority.** No agency is designated to enforce the Act, and the bill provides no investigative, boarding, subpoena, or seizure powers. It also sets no procedure for assessing civil penalties: no administrative hearing, notice, or judicial review.

25. **Sec. 5(a), per-day penalty versus annual threshold.** A violation is defined by annual tonnage, but the penalty is calculated per day. It's unclear which days count as violation days. Defendants will contest this, and it may make penalties hard to compute or collect.

26. **Sec. 5(a), "on or after the date of enactment."** This conflicts with Sec. 8, under which the Act takes effect 180 days after enactment. It creates ambiguity and invites a challenge to penalties for pre-effective-date conduct.

27. **Sec. 5, missing remedies.** The bill provides no forfeiture of vessels, equipment, or recovered minerals, no injunctive relief, no citizen-suit provision, and no whistleblower protections. Forfeiture of minerals is the remedy most likely to remove the profit motive.

28. **Sec. 5(b), "willfully."** "Willfully" generally requires proof that the defendant knew the conduct was unlawful, which is hard to show for corporate actors. A "knowingly" standard would be easier to prosecute.

## International Agreement (Sec. 6)

29. **Sec. 6, "shall seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months."** It's unclear whether the deadline applies to starting negotiations or concluding them. "Seek to" creates no enforceable obligation, and the section has no reporting requirement to Congress, no forum specified, and no definition of "equivalent."

## Duration (Sec. 7)

30. **Sec. 7(b), the 10-year sunset.** The whole Act expires after 10 years, regardless of whether the NAS process in Sec. 7(a) ever happens. Mining would become lawful automatically. This defeats the purpose of a permanent prohibition and conflicts with Sec. 7(a).

31. **Sec. 7(b), sunset clock.** The sunset runs from enactment, not from the effective date, so the prohibition actually lasts only about 9.5 years.

32. **Sec. 7(a), the NAS off-ramp.** The bill never directs anyone to commission the NAS report. "Serious harm" is undefined and is a weaker standard than "irreversible damage." The section also doesn't say what happens to the registry and penalties if the prohibition lifts, or whether a regulatory regime must exist first.

## Missing Provisions Generally

33. **Severability.** The bill has no severability clause, which is risky given the challenge exposure in Sec. 2(1)(B) and Sec. 3(b).

34. **Rulemaking and definitions.** The bill gives no rulemaking authority, and key terms are never defined: "person," "Secretary," "United States," "seabed," and "operator."

35. **Federal support.** The bill doesn't bar federal financing, loans, offtake agreements, or stockpile purchases that support deep-sea mining.
