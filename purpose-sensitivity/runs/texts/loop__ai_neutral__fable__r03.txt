RESPONSE:
Here's a section-by-section pass. I've grouped items by section but numbered them continuously so you can cite them directly. At the end I flag the handful I'd lead with in the explainer.

**Section 2 — Definitions**

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — "single training run."** The 10^26 threshold applies per run, but "training run" is undefined, so a developer can split training into several sub-threshold runs and stitch them together via checkpoint resumption, continued pretraining, fine-tuning, or model merging. Nothing in the bill aggregates compute across runs or across stages.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — counting methodology.** The bill never says how to count operations (dense vs. sparse/MoE, multiply-accumulate as one op or two, numeric precision, whether data-generation or RL compute counts). Each choice can swing the count by 2x or more, which matters for both compliance and proving a violation.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — frozen threshold.** 10^26 is hard-coded with no rulemaking authority to adjust it. Algorithmic efficiency gains mean systems trained below the line will match today's above-the-line systems within a few years, while the threshold stays in place for a decade.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A) — distillation.** A model trained below 10^26 by distilling from a covered model (including one trained abroad) is not covered, since only its own run compute counts.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — "the Secretary" is undefined.** The bill names the Department of Commerce (Sec. 4) and the Secretary of State (Sec. 6) but never says which Secretary makes the capability determination. This alone is a drafting error that invites litigation.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — no process for the determination.** There are no criteria, notice-and-comment procedure, evidentiary standard, or judicial-review provision for a capability finding. A standard this open-ended, backed by criminal penalties (Sec. 5(b)), is a strong target for void-for-vagueness and delegation challenges.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(B) — timing problem.** A determination can only be made about a system that already exists, so prong (B) can't prohibit training; it can at most reach "deploy," and companies will argue they can't be liable for conduct before the determination issued.

8. **Sec. 2(1) — "artificial intelligence," "model," and "system" are undefined**, and the bill uses "model" in (A) and "system" in (B) and Sec. 3 interchangeably. Whether weights, a fine-tuned derivative, or a product wrapping a model are the same "system" is left open.

9. **Sec. 2(2) — "U.S. person" is narrower than standard usage.** It excludes lawful permanent residents, LLCs, partnerships, trusts, and entities organized under D.C., territorial, tribal, or federal law (compare the broader definitions in IEEPA regulations or 22 U.S.C. § 6010). An LLC is the most common form for new AI ventures.

10. **Sec. 2(2) — foreign subsidiaries.** A corporation organized under foreign law is not a U.S. person even if wholly owned by a Delaware parent, so a U.S. company can run training through an offshore subsidiary. The bill has no "owned or controlled by" language.

11. **Sec. 2(3) — "advanced AI chips," "data center," and "operator" are undefined.** Without a chip definition the registry has no clear trigger; without an operator definition it's unclear whether the owner, the colocation tenant, or the cloud customer must register.

12. **Sec. 2(3) — chip-count threshold is gameable and misaligned.** Training can be spread across multiple sub-50,000-chip facilities, and because the threshold counts chips rather than compute, a 10^26 run will soon be feasible at facilities too small to register.

**Section 3 — Prohibition**

13. **Sec. 3(a) — foreign developers are largely untouched.** A non-U.S. company training abroad isn't covered unless it "deploys" "in the United States," and the bill doesn't say whether serving U.S. users from offshore servers qualifies. There is no import restriction, market-access ban, or prohibition on U.S. persons *using* a covered system.

14. **Sec. 3(a) — "train," "develop," and "deploy" are undefined.** "Develop" is broad enough to reach writing code or publishing research (raising First Amendment overbreadth arguments), while "deploy" may or may not cover internal use, open-weight release, or an end user running weights locally.

15. **Sec. 3(a) — no coverage for enablers.** Cloud providers renting compute, chip suppliers, or data vendors supporting a prohibited run aren't addressed; there's no "knowingly provide" provision, so liability depends on general aiding-and-abetting doctrine.

16. **Sec. 3(a) — government applicability is ambiguous.** "Person" ordinarily doesn't include the federal government, so DoD, national labs, or contractors acting as agents may be exempt, yet there is no explicit national-security carve-out or waiver. Either reading creates problems: uneven application or a separation-of-powers fight.

17. **Sec. 3(b) — grandfathering by start date.** Any system whose training "began" before enactment is permanently exempt, with no cap on compute added afterward. A developer can begin a run the day before enactment (or argue data preprocessing counts as "beginning") and continue it indefinitely.

18. **Sec. 3(b) — derivative systems.** Because "system" is undefined, continued pretraining, fine-tunes, or merged models descended from a grandfathered run can plausibly claim the same exemption, which could swallow the prohibition for incumbents.

19. **Sec. 3(b) — exemption also covers "develop" and "deploy."** Grandfathered systems can be deployed and further developed without limit, so the bill freezes a head start for whoever has a run underway at enactment.

20. **Sec. 3(c) — safety-evaluation exemption is unbounded.** "Safety evaluation" is undefined, turns on the actor's stated purpose, and carries no compute limit, reporting duty, deployment restriction, or oversight. Since evaluating frontier safety requires frontier models, nearly any training run can be characterized as safety research.

**Section 4 — Registry**

21. **Sec. 4 — no penalties.** Section 5 only penalizes violations of Section 3, so failing to register or filing a misleading report carries no sanction under this Act (only generic 18 U.S.C. § 1001 exposure for knowingly false statements).

22. **Sec. 4 — no deadlines or content requirements.** There is no registration deadline, no date for the first quarterly report, and "describing the computing workloads" doesn't require disclosing compute totals, customers, or whether any run approaches 10^26.

23. **Sec. 4 — self-certification with no verification.** Commerce receives no audit, inspection, subpoena, or rulemaking authority, and nothing connects the reports to enforcement of Section 3.

24. **Sec. 4 — no geographic limit.** As written, the registration duty applies to every covered facility worldwide, which is either unenforceable or will be read down by courts.

25. **Sec. 4 — no confidentiality provision.** Workload reports would be subject to FOIA and discovery absent a trade-secret protection, which discourages candid reporting and invites challenge.

**Section 5 — Penalties**

26. **Sec. 5(a) — penalty is small relative to the activity.** $1 million per day caps a 100-day run at $100 million, a fraction of frontier training costs, and "not more than" leaves full discretion. There is no disgorgement, forfeiture of weights, injunctive authority, or inflation adjustment.

27. **Sec. 5(a) — enactment vs. effective date conflict.** Penalties attach to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but under Sec. 8 nothing is unlawful until 180 days later. At best this is surplusage; at worst it invites a retroactivity challenge over conduct in the gap.

28. **Sec. 5(b) — "willfully" plus vague definitions.** Given the undefined threshold methodology, the open-ended safety exemption, and the undefined Secretary, proving a defendant knew the conduct was unlawful will be very hard. Organizational fines default to 18 U.S.C. § 3571 levels ($500,000 per count or twice the gain), which are modest.

29. **Sec. 5 / general — no designated enforcement agency.** The bill never says who investigates or brings civil actions, provides no investigative tools, and creates no whistleblower protections or private right of action.

**Section 6 — International Agreement**

30. **Sec. 6 — purely hortatory.** "Seek to negotiate" imposes no consequence for failure, no reporting to Congress, and no linkage to the domestic prohibition (e.g., no reciprocity trigger or sunset if no agreement materializes). It doesn't address the foreign-developer gap in item 13.

**Section 7 — Duration**

31. **Sec. 7(a) vs. 7(b) — internal contradiction.** Subsection (a) says the prohibition lasts until Congress affirmatively lifts it; subsection (b) ends the whole Act automatically at 10 years. The sunset controls, so the "until Congress finds" language is effectively dead letter and the registry lapses too.

32. **Sec. 7(a) — the lifting mechanism has no trigger.** No one is directed or funded to produce the National Academies report, so the off-ramp depends on a study the bill doesn't commission. (Note also that a joint resolution requires presidential signature, unlike a concurrent resolution.)

33. **Sec. 7 — no remedy for violating systems.** The bill is silent on what happens to a model trained in violation, so it remains usable and deployable once penalties are paid or the Act sunsets.

**Section 8 — Effective Date**

34. **Sec. 8 — 180-day window.** The delay gives developers six months to launch runs that will be mid-training when the prohibition takes effect, and to relocate operations into foreign subsidiaries (item 10). Combined with Sec. 3(b)'s enactment-date keying, runs begun in the window are neither grandfathered nor lawful to continue, which is a trap for the unwary and a litigation hook for everyone else.

**Structural omissions**

35. **No severability clause.** If a court strikes Sec. 2(1)(B) for vagueness or delegation, defendants will argue the remainder falls with it. Courts presume severability, but the absence removes a clear signal of congressional intent.

36. **No rulemaking authority anywhere.** Every undefined term (items 1, 2, 5, 8, 11, 14, 20) stays undefined for the life of the Act unless Congress amends it; no agency can fill the gaps.

37. **No preemption or savings clause**, leaving open whether state AI laws (e.g., California's or Colorado's frameworks) are displaced or stacked on top.

**If I were picking the five to lead with:** the safety-evaluation exemption (20), the start-date grandfather (17–19), foreign subsidiaries and the narrow U.S.-person definition (9–10, 13), the single-run compute loophole (1), and the Sec. 7 sunset contradiction (31). Those are the ones most likely to come up at markup and in the first lawsuit.
