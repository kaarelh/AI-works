# Drafting Problems and Loopholes in S. 2817 (Text as Amended October 6, 2026)

These are my analytical observations from the text you provided. I haven't seen the original bill, the committee report, or any legislative history, so some gaps may be deliberate or addressed elsewhere. Verify the most important points with committee staff or counsel before publishing.

**Section 2 (Definitions)**

1. **§2(1)(A)**: "Single training run" lets developers split one training effort into several runs or checkpoints, each under 10^26 FLOP, or treat continued training as a new run. The bill doesn't say how runs are delineated or aggregated.

2. **§2(1)(A)**: The bill doesn't say whether the FLOP count includes fine-tuning, post-training, reinforcement learning, synthetic data generation, or inference, or how compute is measured or verified. Without a methodology, the threshold is hard to apply and easy to dispute.

3. **§2(1)(A)**: The compute threshold is fixed, with no authority to update it. Algorithmic efficiency gains could let systems of comparable capability fall below 10^26, so the threshold could become less protective over time.

4. **§2(1)(B)**: "The Secretary" is undefined. The bill never names the department, though the only agency mentioned later is Commerce (§4), which is likely but not stated.

5. **§2(1)(B)**: "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no benchmarks, evaluation method, or standard of proof. This invites vagueness challenges under the Due Process Clause.

6. **§2(1)(B)**: The bill sets no procedure for the Secretary's determination: no notice, comment, hearing, deadline, written findings, or judicial review. It also doesn't say whether a determination applies to systems already in development.

7. **§2(1)(A)–(B)**: The bill uses "AI model" in (A) and "AI system" in (B), and neither term is defined. It's unclear whether (B) reaches multi-model or agentic systems assembled from smaller models that individually fall below the threshold.

8. **§2(2)**: "U.S. person" covers citizens and corporations organized under state law. It excludes LLCs and partnerships, foreign subsidiaries of U.S. companies, and lawful permanent residents abroad, so a U.S. firm could route training through an offshore affiliate outside the "anywhere" reach of §3(a).

9. **§2(2)**: "State" is undefined. It's unclear whether the District of Columbia and U.S. territories are included.

10. **§2(3)**: "Data center," "advanced AI chips," and "operator" are undefined. It's unclear whether the owner, cloud provider, or tenant must register, and whether a given chip qualifies as "advanced."

11. **§2(3)**: The 50,000-chip test is facility-by-facility. Training can be spread across several sites, each under the threshold, so a facility-based registry may not capture a large training run.

**Section 3 (Prohibition)**

12. **§3(a)**: "Train," "develop," and "deploy" are undefined. It's unclear whether publishing weights, offering API access, or conducting internal research counts, and whether serving U.S. customers from abroad counts as "deploy."

13. **§3(a)**: The prohibition reaches persons "in the United States" and U.S. persons "anywhere." A foreign company with no U.S. presence is outside the bill even if its system serves American users.

14. **§3(a)**: The bill doesn't prohibit supplying chips, cloud compute, hosting, data, or weights to a prohibited developer, and it creates no aiding-and-abetting liability. A U.S. cloud provider could rent compute to a prohibited developer without clear liability.

15. **§3(a)**: The bill doesn't address possession, sale, or transfer of trained weights. Weights could be handed to a foreign entity that continues the work.

16. **§3(b)**: "Training began" is undefined. Developers could argue it began with planning, data collection, or the first compute job, and could start runs just before enactment to qualify.

17. **§3(b)**: The exemption attaches to the system, not the developer, and doesn't address resumed or continued training, successor models, or limits on deployment. Grandfathered systems could arguably be deployed indefinitely.

18. **§3(b) with §8**: A training run that starts during the 180-day window after enactment isn't grandfathered by §3(b), and the prohibition isn't yet in effect. The bill doesn't say whether that run must stop on the effective date or may continue. This is a likely dispute point.

19. **§3(c)**: "Safety evaluation" is undefined and turns on purpose. The exemption has no requirements for registration, scale limits, supervision, independence, reporting, or sharing of results, and it doesn't say whether it covers training itself. Developers could label frontier training as "evaluation."

**Section 4 (Registry)**

20. **§4(a)**: Only facility operators must register. Developers aren't required to register covered models, disclose training runs, or give notice before training, so the registry doesn't directly track the activity the prohibition covers.

21. **§4(b)**: Quarterly reports describe "computing workloads" without requiring operators to say whether any workload is a covered AI system or to report FLOP counts. Reports could be generic and still comply.

22. **§4(b)**: Reports are certified, but the bill provides no verification, audit authority, or specific penalty for false certification beyond general false-statement law.

23. **§4**: The bill doesn't say whether reports are public, exempt from FOIA, or protected as trade secrets. That affects both transparency and enforcement.

24. **§4 and §8**: The bill gives Commerce no rulemaking authority to define terms, create forms, or set deadlines, and no penalty attaches to failing to register. The registry may be unworkable on the 180-day schedule.

**Section 5 (Penalties)**

25. **§5(a)**: The bill names no enforcing agency and gives no investigative powers such as subpoenas or inspections, no authority to bring civil actions, and no whistleblower protections. As drafted, it's hard to enforce.

26. **§5(a)**: "Each day on which the violation occurs" is undefined. It's unclear whether a single continuous training run is one violation or many, and whether the penalty counts per system, per person, or per facility. The $1 million daily cap also may be small relative to frontier-lab budgets, which could let some firms treat it as a cost of doing business. That's a judgment call that experts may dispute.

27. **§5(a) with §8**: §5(a) applies to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §3 doesn't take effect until 180 days later. The mismatch invites arguments about pre-effective-date conduct and could lead to a retroactivity challenge.

28. **§5(b)**: "Willfully" is undefined. The bill has no responsibility standard for corporate officers and directors, no forfeiture of models or compute, and relies on Title 18 for fines.

29. **§5 (general)**: The only remedies are money and imprisonment. The bill gives no authority to enjoin ongoing development, order a shutdown, or require destruction of weights, which is a significant gap if the goal is to stop activity in progress.

**Section 6 (International Agreement)**

30. **§6**: "Shall seek to negotiate" creates no duty to succeed, no reporting requirement, and no consequence if talks fail. "Equivalent prohibitions" is undefined, and the section doesn't address how foreign developers outside U.S. jurisdiction would be covered.

**Section 7 (Duration)**

31. **§7(a)**: "Finds by joint resolution that covered AI systems can be developed safely" uses an undefined standard. The bill sets no deadline for the National Academies report and no requirement that Congress act on it. The section also doesn't clarify how the joint resolution would be enacted (presentment and veto), which is needed to explain how the trigger works.

32. **§7(a) and §7(b)**: §7(a) keeps the prohibition in place until a finding, but §7(b) ends the entire Act after 10 years regardless. The bill doesn't say which controls. The 10-year clock also runs from enactment, so the prohibition is in effect for only about nine and a half years.

33. **§7(b)**: The bill has no savings clause. It doesn't say whether violations committed before the sunset can still be prosecuted, whether pending investigations survive, or whether systems trained during the Act can be deployed after it ends.

**Section 8 (Effective Date) and General Issues**

34. **§8**: The 180-day delay applies uniformly to the prohibition, registry, and penalties, with no staggered deadlines and no deadline for agency guidance. This interacts with §3(b) to create a window in which training can begin legally.

35. **General**: There's no severability clause. If a provision is struck (for example, §2(1)(B)), courts would apply default severability doctrine, and the outcome for the rest of the Act would be uncertain.

36. **General**: The bill doesn't define "person," "artificial intelligence," "model," or "system." It also grants no general regulatory authority and no appropriations for enforcement.

37. **General (constitutional exposure)**: Possible challenges include: First Amendment (if "develop" or "deploy" reaches publishing code or weights); vagueness (§2(1)(B)); nondelegation (an open-ended Secretary determination); and extraterritoriality (the "anywhere" reach over U.S. persons abroad). Legal experts should evaluate these before publication.

---

**Suggested priorities for the explainer:** The most consequential gaps appear to be §3(c) (the safety-evaluation exemption), §2(1)(A) (compute counting and the "single run" language), §5 (no enforcing agency and no injunctive remedy), §3(b) with §8 (the grandfathering window), and §2(2) with §3(a) (offshore affiliates and foreign developers). Framing each as "the text does not specify" rather than "companies can do X" will keep the coverage accurate and balanced, since sponsors may have intended some of these choices.
