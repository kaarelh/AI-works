RESPONSE:
Below are the issues I found, in section order, followed by cross-cutting problems. Items 5, 11, 13, 17, 19, 20 and 26 are probably the most consequential.

**Section 2: Definitions**

1. **§2(1)(A), "single training run."** The term is undefined, so a developer can split a run into stages, restart from checkpoints, merge models, or distill from a larger model so that no one "run" exceeds 10^26. Count cumulative compute across a model's lineage, including predecessor and parent models.

2. **§2(1)(A), static and unspecified threshold.** The bill has no mechanism to lower the threshold, even though algorithmic efficiency means less compute buys the same capability. It also doesn't say what counts toward the total (pretraining, RL/post-training, synthetic-data generation, inference-time compute) or how operations are measured (theoretical vs. actual). Add rulemaking authority to adjust the threshold and a methodology.

3. **§2(1), undefined AI terms and a model/system mismatch.** "Artificial intelligence model" and "artificial intelligence system" are undefined, and (A) covers models while (B) covers systems. §3 then prohibits a "covered AI system," so it is unclear whether a product built around a model trained above the threshold is covered.

4. **§2(1)(B), "the Secretary" is undefined.** §4 names the Department of Commerce and §6 names the Secretary of State, so a reader can't tell which Secretary is meant. A criminal statute that leaves the coverage-determining official ambiguous invites challenge.

5. **§2(1)(B), no standard, process, or deadline.** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no benchmarks or evaluation method, and the Secretary's determination is purely discretionary, with no notice, comment, timeline, or review. This invites vagueness and nondelegation challenges, and for criminal liability it lets defendants argue they had no notice a system was covered.

6. **§2(2), "U.S. person" is too narrow.** It omits lawful permanent residents, partnerships, LLCs, unincorporated associations, nonprofits, DC/territorial/federally chartered entities, and foreign-organized subsidiaries of U.S. companies. A company can move development into an offshore subsidiary and fall outside the Act.

7. **§2(3), "covered facility."** "Advanced AI chips" and "data center" are undefined. A fixed count of 50,000 chips ignores differences in chip performance, and nothing aggregates affiliated sites, so operators can stay under the line with many smaller sites or by training across sites.

**Section 3: Prohibition**

8. **§3(a), "train, develop, or deploy" are undefined.** "Develop" is vague enough to draw First Amendment and vagueness challenges, since it could reach writing code or publishing research. "Deploy" may not clearly cover internal use, API access, or open-weight release.

9. **§3(a), no secondary or foreign-facing liability.** The Act doesn't prohibit knowingly providing compute, funding, or chips for covered training, and a foreign company serving U.S. users from abroad isn't clearly reached. Add aiding/facilitation and cloud-provider obligations.

10. **§3(a), "person" and government actors.** The Act doesn't say whether federal agencies, the military, or their contractors are covered. Leaving this unresolved produces either an unintended exemption or a litigation fight, so decide it explicitly.

11. **§3(b), breadth of the grandfather clause.** It exempts everything about a system whose "training began" before enactment, including continued training, scale-up, new versions, and deployment, with no cap. "Training began" is also undefined and hard to verify, so a developer could claim a pre-enactment start for a successor model.

12. **§3(b), enactment-date cutoff.** It rewards starting very large runs before enactment. It also means the Secretary's capability-based authority in §2(1)(B) can never reach any existing frontier system, however capable it becomes.

13. **§3(c), the safety-evaluation exemption.** It turns on the subjective "purpose" of undefined "activities," and it has no scale limit, notification, licensing, containment, or oversight. Any training or deployment can be relabeled as safety evaluation, so the exemption can swallow the prohibition.

14. **§3, no anti-evasion provision.** Nothing makes structuring, splitting, or reorganizing to avoid coverage a violation in itself.

**Section 4: Registry**

15. **§4(a), what gets registered.** The Act registers facilities but not developers, training runs, or covered systems, and it requires no advance notice of large runs. It also sets no registration deadline and gives Commerce no authority to prescribe forms or procedures.

16. **§4(b), weak reporting.** "Describing the computing workloads" is undefined, reports arrive quarterly after the fact, and certification is self-attested. There are no audit or inspection rights, and an operator may not know what a customer is running, so add customer-identification requirements.

17. **§4, no enforcement.** §5 penalizes only violations of §3, so failing to register, failing to report, or filing a false report has no specific penalty. (18 U.S.C. §1001 may reach false statements, but that is no substitute for a registry-specific penalty.)

18. **§4, no confidentiality or data-handling provisions.** There is no FOIA exemption, trade-secret protection, or authority to share data with DOJ and other agencies. Operators will challenge disclosure, and the data would be an attractive target.

**Section 5: Penalties**

19. **§5(a), no enforcer or procedure.** The Act doesn't say who brings civil actions (the Attorney General? Commerce?), in what forum, or whether injunctions are available, and it provides no state AG or private enforcement. If Commerce adjudicates penalties administratively, *SEC v. Jarkesy* raises a Seventh Amendment risk, so counsel should check this.

20. **§5(a), penalty design.** "Not more than $1,000,000" per day sets no floor and gives no factors for setting the amount, which is small against multibillion-dollar training budgets. There is no disgorgement, no authority to order a run halted, and no forfeiture or seizure of hardware.

21. **§5(a) vs. §8, conflicting dates.** §5(a) reaches violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §3 doesn't take effect until 180 days later. This invites fair-notice and retroactivity arguments.

22. **§5(b), "willfully."** Prosecutors would likely have to prove the defendant knew the conduct was unlawful and knew the system was covered, which is especially hard with a discretionary §2(1)(B) determination. There is also no responsible-corporate-officer liability, and the Act sets no statute-of-limitations rule for long-running conduct.

23. **§5, no whistleblower protections or incentives.** Covert training is likely to be detected mainly by insiders, and the Act protects and rewards none.

**Section 6: International agreement**

24. **§6, "shall seek to negotiate."** The Act creates no enforceable obligation, no reporting to Congress, and no consequence for missing the 18-month mark. "Equivalent prohibitions" is vague, and the Act includes no verification mechanism, no incentives (such as export-control or sanctions tools), and no ratification path.

**Section 7: Duration**

25. **§7(a), the safety finding.** "Safely" is undefined, and nothing requires the National Academies to produce a report, sets a timeline, or funds one. "Acting on a report" doesn't say what the report must conclude, and the finding is all-or-nothing for every covered system.

26. **§7(b), the sunset overrides §7(a).** The prohibition expires automatically after 10 years even if no safety finding is ever made, so developers can wait it out and stockpile chips, talent, and data in the meantime. If the intent is that the ban lasts until safety is shown, the sunset should be removed or tied only to the §7(a) finding.

27. **§7(b), no savings clause.** Nothing says conduct during the Act's effectiveness remains punishable after expiry or termination, so an explicit savings clause is needed.

**Section 8: Effective date**

28. **§8, the 180-day delay.** It creates a window to start or accelerate large runs, and the registry and reporting obligations are delayed too. Consider immediate interim registration and reporting, and align the §3(b) cutoff date with the effective date.

**Cross-cutting**

29. **No findings or jurisdictional nexus.** The bill has no congressional findings on interstate and foreign commerce or national security, which weakens its defense against a Commerce Clause challenge. Combined with the First Amendment ("code as speech") and vagueness points above, it is exposed on several constitutional fronts.

30. **Compute-only coverage may be overbroad.** Under §2(1)(A), a narrow, non-general model trained above 10^26 would be banned even though it isn't "superintelligence." Opponents can use this to argue arbitrariness. Consider whether to add a capability qualifier, which would need to be balanced against the evasion risk in item 1.

31. **No severability clause.** If a court strikes the §2(1)(B) delegation or another provision, the whole Act is at risk.

32. **Missing administrative machinery.** The bill has no general rulemaking authority, no appropriations or staffing for Commerce, no judicial-review or venue provisions, and no preemption clause on state AI laws. It also doesn't coordinate with existing export controls on chips.

Items 19 (including the *Jarkesy* point), 22, and 29 involve legal doctrine, so have counsel confirm them before they go to Senator Whitfield's office. Items 26 and 30 are partly policy calls that the office may see as tradeoffs rather than drafting errors. If it would help, I can turn this list into proposed amendment language or a one-page version ranked by priority.
