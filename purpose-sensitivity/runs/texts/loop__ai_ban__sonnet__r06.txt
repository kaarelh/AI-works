RESPONSE:
Here are the problems I found, in section order. Items 31-34 are cross-cutting. The constitutional points (vagueness, nondelegation, First Amendment, Commerce Clause) are my reading, so have counsel confirm before they go to the Senator's office.

## Section 2: Definitions

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A), fixed threshold.** The 10^26 figure is hard-coded, with no authority for the Secretary to lower it. Algorithmic efficiency gains mean systems with dangerous capabilities may be trained below it.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "single training run."** This is undefined, so a developer can split training into staged runs, continued pretraining, merges, or distillation steps that each fall under the threshold. The definition should aggregate compute across a model lineage, including post-training, reinforcement learning, and synthetic-data generation.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "model" vs. "system."** Clause (A) covers a "model," while clause (B) covers a "system." An ensemble or agent system built from several sub-threshold models falls outside (A). The bill also gives no method for counting operations and no recordkeeping duty to verify the count.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A), overbreadth.** The trigger is compute alone, not capability, so it sweeps in narrow high-compute models (protein folding, weather) while missing capable general systems below the line. That overbreadth gives challengers sympathetic plaintiffs.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(B), "the Secretary."** No Secretary is defined. It could be Commerce, Defense, or State, and the bill's only other reference is "Secretary of State" in Sec. 6.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B), no process or criteria.** There is no rulemaking requirement, deadline, evidentiary standard, notice, or publication duty. "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" doesn't say which humans (average or expert) or which tasks. That invites vagueness and nondelegation challenges.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(B) with Sec. 5(b), fair notice.** A system could be uncovered when built and covered after a later determination, yet criminal penalties attach to the conduct. Developers can't know in advance whether they're in violation, which is a due process problem and also makes the prohibition unworkable ex ante.

8. **Sec. 2(1)(B), no access or evaluation authority.** Nothing requires developers to submit systems for evaluation or gives the government access to models or test results, so the Secretary may lack the means to make any determination.

9. **Sec. 2(2), "U.S. person."** The definition omits permanent residents, LLCs, partnerships, unincorporated entities, entities organized in D.C. or the territories (only "a State"), and foreign-incorporated subsidiaries of U.S. companies. The subsidiary gap is the most obvious offshoring route, so an ownership-or-control test is needed.

10. **Sec. 3(a), "person" and government actors.** "Person" is undefined, and the bill is silent on federal agencies, military and intelligence programs, and contractors. Whichever way the sponsors intend it, the text should say so, so contractors can't claim an implied exemption.

11. **Sec. 2(3), undefined terms.** "Advanced AI chip" has no performance specification, and "data center" doesn't say whether a multi-building campus or a networked multi-site cluster counts as one facility.

12. **Sec. 2(3), count-based threshold.** A chip count can be evaded by splitting capacity across sites or by using more powerful chips. It also isn't tied to the 10^26 line: by my rough estimate, a cluster of well under 50,000 current-generation accelerators could reach 10^26 operations within a year. Use aggregate compute capacity, aggregate commonly controlled sites, and give the agency authority to adjust.

## Section 3: Prohibition

13. **Sec. 3(a), undefined verbs.** "Train, develop, or deploy" are not defined. The bill doesn't reach possessing, transferring, or publishing weights, open-weight release, funding, supplying compute, or knowingly assisting. "Develop" is also the likeliest hook for a vagueness or First Amendment challenge.

14. **Sec. 3(a), timing.** A model counts as covered only once it has been "trained using" more than 10^26 operations. Read literally, there may be no violation until a run crosses the line, and nothing prohibits runs planned or intended to exceed it, or attempts.

15. **Sec. 3(a), reach.** Foreign nationals abroad are not covered, and there is no hook for U.S. cloud or compute providers who host a foreign actor's run.

16. **Sec. 3(b), "training began."** The term is undefined (pretraining start? any experiment?). Because the bill is public, it creates an incentive to start or backdate large runs before enactment.

17. **Sec. 3(b), scope of grandfathering.** The exemption covers the whole system with no limit on continued training, scaling, fine-tuning, successor versions, or deployment. A pre-enactment run could continue indefinitely, even if the system is later determined to be covered under (B). It needs a compute cap or freeze, a registration requirement, and a "same lineage" rule.

18. **Sec. 3(b) vs. Sec. 8, date mismatch.** Grandfathering keys off enactment, but the prohibition takes effect 180 days later. Runs begun in that gap are neither clearly grandfathered nor clearly prohibited, and neither are runs still in progress on the effective date.

19. **Sec. 3(c), safety evaluation.** The exemption is purpose-based, undefined, and effectively self-certified. "Activities" is unbounded and could include training and deploying a covered system, so any developer can label its work "safety evaluation." It should be limited to evaluating existing systems under licensed, contained, reported conditions, with no commercial deployment.

20. **Sec. 3(c), burden of proof.** As written, the government would have to disprove a defendant's purpose. Make it an affirmative defense with the burden on the defendant, plus documentation requirements.

## Section 4: Registry

21. **Sec. 4(a), registration.** There is no deadline, no required content, no designated office, and no duty to update when chips are added. "Operator" is undefined, so it's unclear whether the owner or a cloud tenant is responsible.

22. **Sec. 4(b), reports.** "Describing the computing workloads" is vague, with no required fields such as run size, chip-hours, model identity, or customer. Reports are self-certified, with no audit, inspection, or subpoena authority. Because reports are quarterly and after the fact, a run can start and finish unseen, so there should be advance notice for large runs.

23. **Sec. 4, no penalties.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3, so there is no explicit penalty for failing to register, filing late, or filing false reports. There is also no know-your-customer duty for operators who lease compute.

24. **Sec. 4, confidentiality.** The bill has no confidentiality or information-sharing provisions. Operators will resist on trade-secret grounds, and nothing authorizes sharing reports with DOJ or national security agencies.

## Section 5: Penalties

25. **Sec. 5, no enforcement mechanism.** No agency or Attorney General is authorized to bring civil actions, and there is no injunctive relief to halt a run, no state AG role, no citizen suit, and no whistleblower protection. Injunctive relief matters most, because penalties after the fact don't stop a training run.

26. **Sec. 5(a), amount and structure.** A $1M/day maximum is small next to multi-billion-dollar training runs, and there is no minimum penalty, disgorgement, or forfeiture of weights or chips. It's also unclear how "each day" applies to one-time acts like starting a run.

27. **Sec. 5(a), "on or after the date of enactment."** This conflicts with the 180-day delay in Sec. 8 and with the grandfathering in Sec. 3(b). It also invites retroactivity and fair-notice challenges.

28. **Sec. 5(b), "willfully."** This generally requires proof the defendant knew the conduct was unlawful. Combined with the vague definitions and the safety-evaluation exemption, it makes prosecution very difficult.

29. **Sec. 5(b), fines and liability.** "Fined under title 18" defaults to the general caps (typically $250,000 for individuals and $500,000 for organizations, unless the alternative gain-based formula is used). That is far below the civil penalty. The section also has no explicit officer liability, no coverage of Sec. 4 offenses, and no attempt or aiding provisions.

## Sections 6-8: International, Duration, Effective Date

30. **Sec. 6, "shall seek to negotiate."** This is hortatory. It's unclear whether the 18 months is a deadline to start talks or to conclude them, "equivalent" is undefined, and there is no reporting to Congress, no consequence for failure, and no link to export controls or other leverage.

31. **Sec. 7(b), sunset.** Automatic repeal at 10 years regardless of safety contradicts the safety-based standard in 7(a) and rewards waiting out the clock. It also runs from enactment rather than the effective date. Replace it with periodic review that doesn't automatically repeal.

32. **Sec. 7(a), lifting the prohibition.** "Safely" is undefined, and the National Academies report has no deadline, mandate, funding, or specified content. It's also unclear whether the prohibition can be lifted partially, for specific systems or uses.

33. **Sec. 7, no savings clause.** The bill doesn't say whether enforcement for past violations survives a sunset or a joint resolution lifting the prohibition, or what happens to registrations and records.

34. **Sec. 8, 180-day delay.** The delay gives developers a runway to start or expand large runs, and there are no interim measures. Registry and reporting duties (and possibly a freeze on new above-threshold runs) should take effect on enactment.

## General Gaps

35. **No severability clause.** A successful challenge to a single provision, such as 2(1)(B), could jeopardize the whole Act.

36. **No findings or jurisdictional basis.** The bill has no congressional findings on interstate commerce, national security, or the nature of the risk. That weakens its defense against Commerce Clause, First Amendment (code and weights as speech), and due process challenges.

37. **No rulemaking, review, or funding provisions.** The bill gives no regulatory authority to define terms or adjust thresholds, and no judicial review or venue provisions. It also provides no appropriations or staffing, and it names no lead agency.

38. **No anti-evasion provision.** Nothing addresses structuring runs or facilities to stay under thresholds, shell entities, foreign subsidiaries, or contracting out training to third parties.
