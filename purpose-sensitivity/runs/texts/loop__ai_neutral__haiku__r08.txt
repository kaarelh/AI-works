RESPONSE:
**Drafting and enforcement issues in S. 2817 (as amended October 6, 2026)**

I worked from the text you pasted. Before publishing, check the language against the official text on congress.gov and the committee's amendment document. Some items below may be deliberate policy choices rather than errors, so you may want to ask the sponsors' offices how they read them.

1. **§2(1)(A):** The 10^26 threshold applies to "a single training run," so developers could split training across multiple runs, or train below the threshold and then continue or fine-tune above it. The text doesn't say whether cumulative or post-training compute counts.

2. **§2(1)(A):** "Integer or floating-point operations" has no measurement method, precision standard, or counting rule. The same run could yield different totals depending on how it is counted.

3. **§2(1)(B):** "Secretary" is never defined or tied to an agency, so it's unclear whether Commerce, State, or another department makes the determination.

4. **§2(1)(B):** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no benchmark, evaluation method, or definition of "substantially" or "most." Because violations carry criminal penalties, this is a likely vagueness challenge.

5. **§2(1)(B):** The bill sets no procedure for the Secretary's determination: no notice, comment, criteria, or judicial review. Developers can't know in advance whether a system is covered.

6. **§2(1)(B):** This clause is not limited to models trained above a compute threshold. "Any artificial intelligence system" could reach deployed products built from several models, which invites overbreadth arguments.

7. **§2(2):** "U.S. person" covers only citizens and state-chartered corporations. It excludes foreign-incorporated subsidiaries of U.S. firms, LLCs and partnerships, and lawful permanent residents, so a U.S. company could route training through a foreign affiliate.

8. **§2(3):** "Advanced AI chips" and "data center" are undefined, and the 50,000-chip threshold doesn't say whether it counts chips across multiple sites or buildings under common control.

9. **§2(3) and §4:** The registry is keyed to chip count, but the prohibition is keyed to training compute. A developer could train a covered model on fewer than 50,000 chips over a longer period, and that training would fall outside the registry.

10. **§3(a):** "Develop" and "deploy" are undefined. It's unclear whether releasing model weights, offering API access, or internal use counts as deployment.

11. **§3(a):** The prohibition doesn't cover possessing, selling, or transferring the weights of a covered system, so a model trained unlawfully or abroad could be shared.

12. **§3(a):** "Person" is undefined, and it's unclear whether a foreign developer training abroad for foreign users is reached at all. The bill also doesn't say how the prohibition applies to U.S.-located cloud compute rented by a foreign developer.

13. **§3(b):** The grandfather clause refers to when "training began," a term the bill doesn't define. Because it covers the system rather than the run, it could also be read to exempt deployment of pre-enactment systems indefinitely, and to permit continued scaling or retraining.

14. **§3(b) and §8:** The grandfather date is the date of enactment, but the prohibition takes effect 180 days later. The bill doesn't say whether a run that began during that 180-day window must stop on the effective date.

15. **§3(c):** "Safety evaluation" is undefined. The exemption has no limits on scale, documentation, registration, or deployment, so a developer could label a full training run as a safety evaluation. This is probably the largest loophole in the bill.

16. **§3(c):** "Activities conducted for the purpose of" is a subjective, purpose-based test. It's unclear whether the exemption covers training itself or only evaluation activities, and the bill doesn't allocate the burden of proof for mixed-purpose runs.

17. **§4(a):** "Operator" is undefined, so it's unclear whether the cloud provider or the tenant must register. The definition of "covered facility" also isn't limited to AI workloads.

18. **§4:** No penalty applies to a failure to register or report. Section 5 penalizes only violations of §3, which makes §4 effectively unenforceable.

19. **§4(b):** "Computing workloads" is undefined. The reports don't have to identify covered systems, compute totals, or model identities, and the operator self-certifies with no audit or inspection authority.

20. **§4:** The section sets no first-report deadline, no required content, no rulemaking authority, and no confidentiality or trade-secret protection. The lack of confidentiality protection could discourage honest reporting.

21. **§5(a):** The bill doesn't define a "violation" for the per-day penalty. It's unclear whether the unit is a model, a deployment, a user, or a cluster, and whether a continuing violation accrues daily.

22. **§5(a) and §8:** Penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the §3 prohibition doesn't take effect until 180 days later. The two provisions conflict, and a court could read penalties as reaching conduct that isn't yet prohibited.

23. **§5:** The bill doesn't name an enforcing agency, authorize injunctions, grant subpoena power, or provide a limitations period. It's also unclear whether DOJ, Commerce, or private parties can bring civil actions. The general five-year limit for civil penalties may apply by default.

24. **§5(a):** The $1,000,000 daily amount has no inflation adjustment, per-entity cap, or profit-based alternative. Whether it is an adequate deterrent for large developers is a policy question for the explainer, and the Federal Civil Penalties Inflation Adjustment Act may apply it regardless.

25. **§5(b):** "Willfully" is undefined. The bill also doesn't address corporate criminal liability or officer responsibility.

26. **§5:** The bill has no whistleblower protection, which limits the information that enforcers can get from inside companies.

27. **§6:** "Shall seek to negotiate" sets no required outcome, reporting obligation, or consequence if the deadline passes. "Equivalent to those in section 3" is also undefined, leaving open whether equivalence means identical thresholds, verification, or scope.

28. **§7(a):** It's unclear whether the National Academies report is a precondition to congressional action. The bill sets no deadline or funding for the report, and "developed safely" is undefined.

29. **§7(a):** A joint resolution would ordinarily require presentment to the President, but the text doesn't say whether the resolution has the force of law, or whether one resolution lifts the prohibition across all covered systems.

30. **§7(a) and §7(b):** The two provisions conflict. Subsection (a) keeps the prohibition in place until Congress acts, while subsection (b) ends the entire Act after 10 years regardless of any joint resolution.

31. **§7(b):** The sunset has no savings clause. The bill doesn't say what happens to penalties accrued, enforcement actions pending, or registry data after the Act expires.

32. **General:** The bill has no severability clause, no rulemaking authority for the Secretary or Commerce, and no appropriation for the registry, enforcement, or the National Academies study. Each gap increases implementation and litigation risk.
