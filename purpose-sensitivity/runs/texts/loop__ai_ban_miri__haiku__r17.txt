# Drafting Issues in S. 2817 (Manager's Amendment, Oct. 6, 2026)

**Top priorities for the Thursday markup:**
1. §3(b) grandfathers any system whose training began before enactment, and the text doesn't limit its deployment.
2. §7(a) and §7(b) contradict each other about whether the prohibition is permanent.
3. §4 has no penalty for non-compliance, and §5 has no enforcing agency.
4. §3(c) exempts "safety evaluation" without any definition or limits.
5. §2(1)(A) counts a single training run, which can be split to evade the threshold.

## Section 2: Definitions

1. **§2(1)(A), "single training run":** Developers can split one training program into several runs, continued pretraining, or post-training stages, each below 10^26 operations, and still produce the same capability. The threshold should count cumulative compute for a model and its successor checkpoints.

2. **§2(1)(A), "computing power greater than 10^26 operations":** "Power" is a rate, while the intended measure is a total, so the text is internally inconsistent. The bill also doesn't say how operations are counted (precision, dense vs. sparse, whether inference, synthetic data generation, or RL rollouts count). It should say "total computing operations" and direct Commerce to set counting rules.

3. **§2(1)(A), fixed threshold:** The 10^26 figure is fixed in statute with no mechanism to adjust it. Algorithmic gains could make dangerous systems possible below that level, and the figure could become obsolete. Commerce should have authority to lower or adjust it by rule.

4. **§2(1)(A), derived systems:** Distilled models, merged models, ensembles, and agentic systems built from several sub-threshold models aren't clearly covered. The definition should reach systems whose capabilities are substantially derived from a covered model.

5. **§2(1)(B), "Secretary":** The bill never says which Secretary is meant. §4 uses Commerce and §6 uses State, so the prohibition's backstop has no identified administrator.

6. **§2(1)(B), discretionary determination:** Nothing sets an evidentiary standard, procedure, notice requirement, or deadline for the Secretary's determination. Until a determination issues, the system isn't covered, so this backstop may never operate. Add a deadline, rulemaking, and a way for Congress or the public to petition for a determination.

7. **§2(1)(B), "substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks":** None of these terms is defined and there's no measurement method. The language invites challenges that the determination is arbitrary or exceeds delegated authority, which could delay enforcement in court.

8. **§2(1), "model" vs. "system":** Subparagraph (A) covers "model" and subparagraph (B) covers "system," and neither term is defined. Without definitions, a developer could argue that a wrapped or fine-tuned product falls outside both.

9. **§2(2), "U.S. person":** Limiting this to citizens and corporations organized under state law excludes LLCs, partnerships, trusts, and foreign subsidiaries of U.S. firms. A U.S. company could move development through an offshore affiliate that isn't a U.S. person. The definition should cover any entity organized under U.S. law, with a principal place of business in the U.S., or controlled by U.S. persons.

10. **§2(3), "advanced AI chip":** This term is undefined, yet the registry depends on it. Tie it to an existing standard, such as export-control classifications, or define it by performance.

11. **§2(3), "data center" and "covered facility":** Splitting a campus into buildings, or distributing one training run across sites, can keep each facility under 50,000 chips. The bill should aggregate chips by operator and by interconnected training cluster.

12. **§2 generally, undefined operative verbs and nouns:** "Person," "train," "develop," and "deploy" are not defined. "Person" should be tied to 1 U.S.C. §1 or defined expressly. The other terms are addressed in items 13 and 17.

## Section 3: Prohibition

13. **§3(a), "deploy":** It's unclear whether releasing open weights, offering API access to U.S. users from abroad, or internal use counts as deployment. Open-weight release is probably the largest single gap, and the bill should define deployment to include publishing weights and making a system accessible to persons in the U.S.

14. **§3(a), scope of prohibited conduct:** The prohibition reaches only persons in the U.S. and U.S. persons. It doesn't prohibit providing compute, cloud services, chips, funding, model weights, or technical assistance to a foreign developer building a covered system. Add ancillary prohibitions covering aiding, abetting, and enabling covered development.

15. **§3(b), grandfather clause:** This exempts any covered system whose training began before enactment from all of §3, including deployment, with no time limit. A system whose training started one day before enactment could be deployed and updated indefinitely. The exemption should be limited to deployment of existing systems, with mandatory registration and no new training.

16. **§3(b), "training began":** This term is undefined, so developers could argue for an early start date based on preparatory work. The bill also doesn't address fine-tunes, continued pretraining, or successor models built on grandfathered weights. Define the start date (for example, the first compute job on a designated cluster) and exclude derivative training from the grandfather.

17. **§3(c), "safety evaluation":** The exemption is undefined and purpose-based, with no requirement for a qualified evaluator, government notice, containment, registration, or reporting. It could be read to cover training a capable system and calling it evaluation. Narrow it to evaluation by defined parties, with notice to the government, no deployment, and no capability-enhancing training.

18. **§3 overall, challenge risk:** A broad "develop" prohibition may be argued to reach publishing algorithms or research code, which invites First Amendment litigation that could stall enforcement. Consider a narrower definition or an express carve-out for publication of research that doesn't involve covered systems.

## Section 4: Registry and Reporting

19. **§4(a), facility threshold:** Only facilities above 50,000 chips must register. A 10^26-operation run is feasible in a few months on well under 50,000 current-generation chips, so a covered system could be trained at an unregistered facility. Require notice of any run that approaches the threshold, regardless of facility size.

20. **§4(b), content of reports:** Quarterly reports describe "computing workloads" but don't require identifying the model, its compute, its purpose, or whether any run is a covered system or a safety evaluation. The certification has no content standard. Require per-run disclosure of these items.

21. **§4, no verification:** The registry has no audit, inspection, or access authority, so reports are self-certified and can't be checked. Add audit rights and inspection authority, and consider cloud and chip-level reporting obligations.

22. **§4 and §5, no penalty for reporting failures:** §5 penalizes only violations of §3, so failing to register or report under §4 carries no penalty. Add penalties for §4 violations.

23. **§4, confidentiality:** The bill doesn't say how reported information is protected. Without protection, companies will resist disclosure on trade-secret grounds, and without limits on use, the data could be exposed. Add confidentiality provisions with defined permitted uses.

24. **§4, timing:** The bill sets no initial registration deadline and doesn't say whether §4 takes effect on enactment or on the §8 effective date.

## Section 5: Penalties and Enforcement

25. **§5, no enforcing authority:** Nothing says who brings civil actions (the Attorney General or an agency), and the bill grants no injunction, cease-and-desist, or order-to-halt authority. Name the enforcing authority, give it injunctive power, and provide funding.

26. **§5(a), penalty size and unit of violation:** $1,000,000 per day is small relative to frontier training costs and revenue, and could be treated as a cost of doing business. The unit of violation (per day, per model, or per run) is also unspecified. Consider per-model or per-run penalties, penalties tied to revenue or training cost, and disgorgement of compute and profits.

27. **§5(a), conflict with §8:** Penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §3 isn't in effect until 180 days later. The text invites argument about conduct before the effective date. Conform the penalty trigger to the effective date.

28. **§5(b), "willfully" and corporate liability:** "Willfully" is undefined, and the bill has no knowledge standard, no responsible-officer liability, and no aiding-and-abetting or conspiracy provision. Define the mental-state standard and add liability for officers who direct or knowingly permit violations.

29. **§5, no whistleblower provisions:** Enforcement depends heavily on insiders who can see training activity, but the bill has no anti-retaliation protection or incentive for reporting. Add both.

## Section 6: International Agreement

30. **§6, "shall seek to negotiate":** This creates no obligation to reach an agreement, no consequence if the 18-month target is missed, and no reporting requirement. "Equivalent" is undefined, and the bill doesn't say whether domestic obligations change if no agreement is reached. Require a report to Congress at the deadline, define equivalence, and consider linking the agreement to export-control or trade measures.

## Section 7: Duration

31. **§7(a) vs. §7(b), internal conflict:** §7(a) keeps the prohibition in force until Congress makes a safety finding, but §7(b) makes the entire Act cease after 10 years regardless of any finding. The bill needs to choose between a permanent prohibition with a defined exit condition and a fixed sunset.

32. **§7(a), exit standard:** The "developed safely" finding has no defined standard ("safely" is undefined, and it's unclear whether the question is safety to develop or to deploy), no deadline for the NASEM report, and no evidentiary threshold. As drafted, a simple-majority joint resolution could lift the prohibition. Define the standard, set NASEM deadlines, and consider a supermajority requirement.

33. **§7(b), missing savings clause:** When the Act ceases, it's unclear whether penalties for pre-sunset violations survive, whether grandfathered systems remain exempt, and whether registration obligations end. Add a savings clause.

34. **§7(b), effective period:** The 10-year clock runs from enactment, so the prohibition would actually operate for about 9.5 years, since §8 delays the effective date by 180 days. Specify the intended period.

## Section 8: Effective Date

35. **§8, gap between enactment and effectiveness:** Training that starts during the 180-day window is neither grandfathered under §3(b) nor clearly prohibited, because §3 isn't yet in effect. The bill doesn't say whether training that began in the window and continues past the effective date violates §3. Specify that ongoing training on the effective date is covered, and consider making the training-start prohibition effective on enactment.

## Cross-Cutting

36. **No severability clause:** If a court strikes a provision, such as the §2(1)(B) determination delegation, the bill has no express instruction on whether the rest survives. Add a severability clause.

37. **No administrative provisions:** The bill gives no rulemaking authority, designates no implementing agency, and provides no appropriations for enforcement. Each of these gaps undercuts the other fixes, so they should be addressed together.

**Caveats:** I worked only from the text you provided and didn't check the amendment's history or any other version of the bill. The compute estimate in item 19 is a rough order-of-magnitude figure based on typical utilization assumptions, and staff should verify it before it goes into a letter. The constitutional points (vagueness, nondelegation, major-questions doctrine, and First Amendment issues in items 6, 7, 18, and 28) are flagged as litigation risks, not conclusions, and should be reviewed by counsel before being presented to the Senator's office.
