RESPONSE:
Below is the list for the October 6 text. Items are grouped by theme, with numbering continuous. I've flagged which items cut in our favor and which are drafting defects the committee could fix quickly. This is an analysis for counsel to verify, not legal advice.

**A. Provisions that could let existing or ongoing work continue**

1. **§3(b):** The exemption covers any covered AI system "whose training began before the date of enactment," but it doesn't say whether it reaches only the original training run or also later deployment, further training, fine-tuning, or new versions. "Began" is also undefined, so a run started before enactment and continued afterward may be exempt.
2. **§3(c):** The safety-evaluation exemption turns on the undefined "purpose of safety evaluation" and sets no limits on scale, duration, documentation, or whether it covers training new models or deployment to outside testers. It is broad, but a purpose-based exemption without documentation requirements is also easy to challenge.
3. **§2(1)(A):** The 10^26 threshold applies to compute "in a single training run," with no rule for cumulative compute across runs, multi-stage pipelines, reinforcement learning, distillation, or synthetic data generation. Counsel should assess whether this allows developers to split work into sub-threshold runs.
4. **§2(1)(A) and §3(a):** The threshold is measured on a "model," but the prohibition applies to a "system." A system built from a sub-threshold model plus tools, scaffolding, or ensembles may not clearly be covered.
5. **§2(2) and §3(a):** "U.S. person" covers only citizens and corporations "organized under the laws of a State." It does not clearly address LLCs or partnerships, and it excludes foreign-organized affiliates and lawful permanent residents, which limits the "anywhere" reach of §3(a). Counsel should map our entities against this definition.
6. **§3(a):** "Develop," "deploy," and "person" are undefined. It is unclear whether internal use, API access, or research on sub-threshold models counts as deployment or development.

**B. Vagueness and definitional defects (usable as arguments against the bill)**

7. **§2(1)(B):** The Secretary may designate a system as covered if it is "capable of substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks," but the bill gives no test, evidentiary standard, notice, hearing, or judicial review. This is a strong fair-notice and due-process argument.
8. **§2(1)(B):** "The Secretary" is never defined. Section 4 points to Commerce and Section 6 refers to the Secretary of State, so it is unclear who makes the designation.
9. **§2(1)(B):** "Human performance," "most cognitive tasks," and "substantially exceeding" are all undefined, which compounds item 7.
10. **§2(3) and §4:** "Advanced AI chip," "data center," and "operator" are undefined. It is unclear how chips are counted across sites and who is the operator (owner, cloud provider, or tenant).
11. **§3(a) and §5(b):** "Willfully" is undefined for criminal liability, and §5(a) imposes civil liability with no scienter requirement. Combined with items 3 through 6, this supports a vagueness challenge to the prohibition as a whole.

**C. Enforcement and penalties**

12. **§5(a) vs. §8:** Civil penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the prohibition takes effect 180 days later under §8. This creates an internal conflict about when the prohibition is operative.
13. **§3(b) vs. §8:** The existing-systems exemption keys to the enactment date, while the prohibition starts 180 days later. Training begun during that gap is neither clearly exempt nor clearly prohibited.
14. **§5(a):** The bill names no enforcing agency, no procedure (civil action or administrative penalty), and no limitations period. This is a strong unworkability point.
15. **§5(a):** Penalties accrue "for each day on which the violation occurs," with no aggregate cap and no definition of what constitutes one violation (per model, per run, or per facility). This supports proportionality arguments.
16. **§5(b):** Criminal liability is written for "any person," with no statement about whether officers or employees are personally liable. Imprisonment cannot apply to entities, so the provision is unclear as applied to companies.
17. **§4 and §5:** Failure to register or report under §4 is not a violation of §3, so §5 does not penalize it. The registry has no stated enforcement mechanism in the bill itself.
18. **§4(a) and §3:** Facility registration is tied to a 50,000-chip threshold that appears nowhere in the prohibition. A covered system could be trained in a smaller facility, so the registry does not connect to the operative ban.

**D. Reporting burden and confidentiality**

19. **§4(b):** "Computing workloads" is undefined, and "certified by the operator" has no stated meaning (no officer signature, no certification standard). It is unclear what must be disclosed.
20. **§4(b):** The quarterly reports include no confidentiality or trade-secret protection and no statement about public-records exemptions. Disclosure of proprietary workload data is a legitimate concern to raise.
21. **§4(a):** No registration deadline is set, so the initial compliance date for operators is unclear.

**E. Duration and structure**

22. **§7(a):** The prohibition lasts until Congress "finds by joint resolution" that covered systems "can be developed safely." The bill sets no standard for "safely," no deadline for the National Academies report, and does not require the report to be produced at all. If no report issues, the prohibition could last indefinitely. This is a strong unworkability argument.
23. **§7(a):** It is unclear whether a joint resolution "finding" has operative legal effect, and whether it must go through presentment to the President. Counsel should confirm the constitutional mechanics.
24. **§7(a) vs. §7(b):** The indefinite duration in §7(a) conflicts with the 10-year sunset in §7(b). It is unclear which controls, and the sunset could end the prohibition regardless of any finding.
25. **§7(b) vs. §8:** The sunset runs from enactment, but the prohibition starts 180 days later, so the operative period is about 9.5 years, not 10.
26. **§6:** The Secretary of State is only directed to "seek to negotiate" an agreement, with no obligation to conclude one and no effect on the domestic prohibition. This is an aspirational clause, and it has no operative consequence.

**F. Missing provisions**

27. **Whole Act:** There is no severability clause. If a court strikes §2(1)(B) or §3 for vagueness, it is unclear whether the remainder survives.
28. **Whole Act:** The bill gives no rulemaking authority, licensing or certification pathway, or process for exemptions or pre-approval. Operators therefore have no route to compliance for ongoing work, which supports the unworkability argument.
29. **Whole Act:** The bill has no savings or interaction clause with export controls or existing agency authority, and no definition of "training run," "artificial intelligence model," or "system."

**Strategic notes for the markup**

- Items 1 and 2 are the most exploitable, but they are also the ones committee staff are most likely to tighten. Don't build the strategy around them. Expect them to be narrowed quickly.
- Drafting defects (items 7 to 16, 22, 24) are stronger as testimony or amendment points than as "loopholes." Framing them as fixes that would make the bill workable is more credible than framing them as evasion routes, which can read as intent to evade.
- Counsel should verify these points against the official text as introduced and the manager's amendment, plus current vagueness and joint-resolution case law. I worked only from the text provided.

If useful, I can draft these as proposed amendments, a one-page issues memo for the committee, or talking points for testimony.
