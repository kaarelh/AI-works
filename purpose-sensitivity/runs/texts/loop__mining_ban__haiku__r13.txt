RESPONSE:
Here are the issues I found in the October 6 text, numbered for the Senate office. I've ordered them by section, and I flag the most serious ones at the end.

1. **§2(1)(A), "by a single vessel":** The 10,000-ton threshold applies per vessel, so an operator could split a commercial operation across several vessels that each stay under the limit. The threshold should be measured across the operator, its affiliates, and its contractors for the whole project.

2. **§2(1)(A), "polymetallic nodules or other mineral resources":** "Mineral resources" is undefined. It is unclear whether the definition covers cobalt crusts, seafloor massive sulfides, sediment, or hydrothermal material, and it is unclear whether it covers biological material.

3. **§2(1)(A), "recovery" and "seabed":** "Recovery" is undefined, and the definition does not reach processing at sea, sediment or tailings discharge, or transport. Operators could carry out the most damaging parts of a project outside the definition.

4. **§2(1)(A), depth threshold:** The "greater than 200 meters" line does not say how depth is measured or whether it applies to the seabed or the seafloor, so operators could argue that a site falls on the shallow side of the line.

5. **§2(1)(B), "the Secretary" and "comparable":** Neither term is defined, and the bill gives no criteria, procedure, notice, or appeal for the determination. This discretion could be used to exempt activities, and the vague standard invites both arbitrary-and-capricious and vagueness challenges.

6. **§2(2), "U.S. person":** The definition excludes LLCs, partnerships, and trusts, and "State" is undefined. It also does not reach a foreign-incorporated parent's subsidiaries abroad, so a U.S. company could mine through a foreign subsidiary that falls outside §3(a)'s "anywhere" reach.

7. **§2(3), "covered vessel":** The 5,000-gross-ton threshold is not tied to the 10,000-ton prohibition, so a smaller vessel that exceeds the prohibition would not have to register or report. "Equipped for the recovery" is also undefined and does not clearly cover platforms, barges, towed collectors, or remotely operated systems.

8. **§3(a), "engage in":** The phrase is undefined and does not cover financing, insurance, equipment supply, contracting, port services, or investment. The bill also has no ban on importing, selling, or processing minerals recovered from the deep seabed abroad, which is one of the largest loopholes in the text.

9. **§3(a), territorial reach:** The prohibition covers persons "in the United States" and U.S. persons "anywhere," which leaves foreign persons operating outside U.S. territory, including foreign-flagged vessels on the high seas or in the Area, unregulated.

10. **§3(b), "exploration license":** The term is undefined, and the bill does not say which licensing regime it means. As written, the carve-out could let holders of exploration licenses conduct commercial recovery, and it has no cap, no expiration, no bar on renewal or transfer, and no requirement to stop when the license ends.

11. **§3(b) and the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act:** The bill does not amend, suspend, or expressly preserve the existing DSHMRA licensing pathway. Without a conflict or repeal clause, operators could argue that DSHMRA authorizes activity the prohibition otherwise bars. (Counsel should verify the current DSHMRA status.)

12. **§3(c), "for the purpose of environmental assessment":** The exemption has no limits on tonnage, duration, area, or sale of recovered material, and "purpose" is subjective. Operators could label commercial recovery as assessment, so the exemption needs pre-approval, tonnage caps, a bar on commercial sale, public notice, and reporting, with the burden of proof on the party claiming it.

13. **§4(a), "operator":** The term is undefined, so it is unclear whether the owner, the charterer, or the contractor must register. Registration also has no deadline, no penalty, no public availability, and is not made a condition of operation.

14. **§4(b), quarterly reports:** The bill sets no content requirements (tonnage, location, depth, sediment discharge, ecosystem monitoring), no deadline, and no public availability. Reports are self-certified, there is no inspection or audit authority, and the duty runs only to "registered operator," so an operator who does not register has no reporting duty at all.

15. **§5, enforcement machinery:** The bill does not designate an enforcing official or give investigation or inspection authority. It also omits injunctive relief, cease-and-desist orders, a citizen suit provision, forfeiture of vessels, equipment, and minerals, penalties for registry and reporting violations, and liability for officers, directors, and financiers. It does not state a limitations period either, so the default under 28 U.S.C. § 2462 would apply to civil penalties.

16. **§5(a), "each day on which the violation occurs":** For continuing operations, it is unclear whether each day of mining is one violation or whether each ton counts. The penalty also has no inflation adjustment, no assessment factors, and no clarity on whether each link in the chain (owner, operator, financier) is separately liable.

17. **§5(a) versus §8, timing conflict:** Penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §3's prohibition takes effect 180 days later under §8. Read literally, the penalty and the prohibition run on different schedules, which invites a dispute over whether any violation can occur before the effective date.

18. **§5(b), criminal penalty:** "Willfully" is undefined, and the section does not cover knowingly false registry or report filings under §4. It also does not state corporate fines or officer liability expressly.

19. **§6, international agreement:** "Shall seek to negotiate" is hortatory. The section requires no outcome, no progress reports to Congress, no consequence if the agreement is not reached, and no authority to restrict trade with non-party states.

20. **§7(a) versus §7(b), conflicting duration:** Section 7(a) keeps the prohibition in effect until a joint resolution is adopted, while §7(b) ends the entire Act after 10 years regardless. The bill does not say which controls, and the sunset would likely prevail, which makes the "until Congress finds" language largely illusory.

21. **§7(a), "serious harm" and the NAS report:** "Serious harm" and "harm" are undefined, and no baseline or evidentiary standard is given. The bill also does not require that the National Academies report be commissioned or delivered, sets no deadline, and does not say whether Congress must act on the report before any joint resolution is adopted.

22. **§8, no implementing authority or transition:** The bill gives no rulemaking authority, so the NOAA registry in §4 has no implementing regulations and no deadline for them. The 180-day gap also lets operators rush to start activity or secure licenses before the prohibition takes effect, and the bill has no moratorium on new licenses or permits during that window.

23. **Missing severability and savings clauses:** Without a severability clause, striking one provision (for example, the definition of "U.S. person") could threaten the whole prohibition. The bill also has no rule of construction stating that nothing in the Act authorizes commercial deep seabed mining.

24. **Cross-cutting: undefined terms:** The bill leaves undefined "Secretary," "State," "vessel," "operator," "mineral resources," "recovery," "engage in," "exploration license," "environmental assessment," and "willfully." A single definitions section would close many of the issues above.

**Priority items for the markup:** I would put these first: #8 (import and financing ban), #20 (sunset conflict with §7(a)), #1 (single-vessel threshold), #10 (exploration-license carve-out), #12 (environmental assessment exemption), #15 (enforcement machinery), and #22 (no implementing authority and the 180-day gap).

**Before sending:** I worked only from the text you pasted. I have not seen the prior version, so I can't say what the October 6 amendment changed. Staff should also have counsel check the current DSHMRA licensing status, any pending applications or sponsored ISA contracts, and the limitations and penalty references, since those depend on facts and law I can't verify from here.
