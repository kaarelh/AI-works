# Loopholes, Ambiguities, and Drafting Problems in S. 2817

I worked only from the text you provided, the October 6 manager's amendment version. Please check it against the official text and any later changes before the markup.

**General and definitions**

1. **Sec. 2:** "Artificial intelligence model" and "artificial intelligence system" are used throughout but never defined. It is unclear whether the bill reaches only frontier models or also narrower systems.
2. **Sec. 2(1)(A):** "Computing power greater than 10^26 … operations" mixes a rate ("power") with a count ("operations"). The threshold should be stated as a total quantity, and the current wording leaves its meaning open.
3. **Sec. 2(1)(A):** The bill does not say how training compute is counted. It is unclear whether pretraining alone counts, or also fine-tuning, reinforcement learning, distillation, and synthetic data generation, and how precision or sparsity affects the count. For models near the threshold, this decides coverage.
4. **Sec. 2(1)(A):** "Single training run" is undefined. A developer could split a program into several runs, or a checkpoint-based continuation could be called a new run, and the bill does not say which.
5. **Sec. 2(1)(A) and (B), Sec. 3(a):** The threshold attaches to a "model" in (A) but a "system" in (B) and Sec. 3. The bill does not address whether wrapping a below-threshold model in scaffolding, tools, or an ensemble makes the combined system covered.

**Capability designation (Sec. 2(1)(B))**

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B):** "The Secretary" is undefined. Sec. 4 refers to Commerce and Sec. 6 to the Secretary of State, so the reader cannot tell which official makes the designation.
7. **Sec. 2(1)(B):** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no benchmark, reference population, or definition of "most" or "substantially." This is the strongest vagueness argument in the bill, because the prohibition turns on a standard with no objective test, and compliance cannot be known in advance.
8. **Sec. 2(1)(B):** The bill provides no procedure for designations: no notice, evaluation method, opportunity to respond, appeal, or judicial review.
9. **Sec. 2(1)(B):** The designation has no compute floor, so it could reach systems far below 10^26 operations. The bill also does not say whether a designation is prospective only or can reach systems already built or deployed.

**Scope and key terms**

10. **Sec. 2(2):** "U.S. person" excludes LLCs, partnerships, and entities organized under federal or D.C. law, and it excludes permanent residents. This matters for entity structuring and for whether certain affiliates fall outside the extraterritorial reach of Sec. 3(a). Sec. 3(a) still reaches anyone "in the United States," so structuring will not avoid domestic activity.
11. **Sec. 2(3):** "Advanced AI chip," "data center," and "containing" are undefined. It is unclear whether chips leased from cloud providers, chips owned by tenants, or chips spread across several buildings or campuses count toward the 50,000 threshold.
12. **Sec. 2(3) and 4(a):** The facility threshold counts chips, not workloads. A facility with 50,000 chips running only non-covered work still triggers registration, and "operator" is undefined (owner, operator, and cloud provider may be different entities).
13. **Sec. 3(a):** "Train," "develop," and "deploy" are undefined. "Develop" could cover ordinary research and experimentation, which makes the prohibition overbroad. "Deploy" does not say whether internal use, API access, open-weight release, or research access counts. This is the most important clarification for defining the operating scope of the Act.
14. **Sec. 3(a):** "Any person in the United States" could reach U.S. users and downstream customers of covered systems, not only developers. This is a scope issue to raise, not a loophole.
15. **Global:** "Person" is not defined in the Act. The Dictionary Act (1 U.S.C. § 1) probably supplies a definition, but the bill should say whether government entities are covered. Legal should confirm the Dictionary Act applies.

**Grandfathering and transition**

16. **Sec. 3(b):** The grandfather clause covers systems "whose training began before the date of enactment," but it does not address continued training, fine-tuning, or successor models built from grandfathered checkpoints. The bill does not say when a successor counts as the same system.
17. **Sec. 3(b) and Sec. 8:** Training that starts between enactment and the 180-day effective date is not clearly grandfathered (it began after enactment) and is not yet prohibited (Sec. 3 is not in effect). This is a significant gap for planning, and sponsors should be asked to clarify it.

**Safety evaluation exemption (Sec. 3(c))**

18. **Sec. 3(c):** "Safety evaluation" and "for the purpose of" are undefined and subjective. The exemption has no scope limit, no reporting requirement, no Secretary approval, and no bar on commercial use of models in evaluation. It is either a broad carve-out for legitimate testing, which is a point to press for, or a route for dual-purpose training that critics will attack. Precise definitions would make the carve-out more defensible.
19. **Sec. 3(c):** It is unclear whether the exemption covers training a covered system for evaluation or only evaluating a finished system, and whether it covers sharing models with third-party evaluators, including government evaluation bodies.

**Registry and reporting (Sec. 4)**

20. **Sec. 4(b):** "Computing workloads" is undefined, so the quarterly report could require anything from aggregate hours to customer-level details. The bill has no confidentiality, trade secret, or FOIA protection, which is a serious problem for proprietary information. This is one of the strongest points for the markup.
21. **Sec. 4:** The bill sets no deadline for initial registration, no form or content requirements, no standard for certification, and no process for deregistration when a facility falls below the threshold.
22. **Sec. 4 and 5:** Penalties apply only to violations of Sec. 3. Failure to register or file reports has no stated penalty, so the registry is arguably unenforceable except through general enforcement authority. This supports an "unworkable" argument, though sponsors may simply fix it.

**Penalties (Sec. 5)**

23. **Sec. 5(a):** "Per day on which the violation occurs" does not define the unit of violation: per model, per facility, per customer, or per day of continuing training. Counting continuously, $1,000,000 per day is about $365 million per year for a single violation, and the unit of count could multiply that.
24. **Sec. 5(a):** The bill names no enforcing agency, sets no procedure (agency assessment or court action), and states no limitations period.
25. **Sec. 5(a) and Sec. 8:** Civil penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but Sec. 3 does not take effect until 180 days later. The inconsistency should be raised, since it creates ambiguity about when civil exposure starts.
26. **Sec. 5(b):** "Willfully" is undefined. Combined with the vagueness of Sec. 2(1)(B), a company can argue that it lacked willfulness because covered status is unclear. The bill also does not address corporate officers or aiding and abetting, though general law may cover them.

**International agreement (Sec. 6)**

27. **Sec. 6:** "Seek to negotiate" creates no binding obligation and no consequence if talks fail. "Equivalent prohibitions" is undefined, and the section names the Secretary of State while Secs. 2 and 4 use Commerce. This is mainly a drafting-quality point, since the domestic prohibition does not depend on it.

**Duration (Sec. 7)**

28. **Sec. 7(a) and 7(b):** These directly conflict. Sec. 7(a) keeps the prohibition in effect until Congress passes a joint resolution, but Sec. 7(b) ends the entire Act after 10 years regardless. It is unclear whether the prohibition ends at year 10 or continues. This is a strong drafting argument.
29. **Sec. 7(a):** The bill sets no deadline for the NAS report or for congressional action, and "can be developed safely" has no standard. It also does not say whether the joint resolution must be presented to the President, which matters because a joint resolution carries the same veto process as a bill.
30. **Sec. 7(b) and Sec. 8:** The sunset runs from enactment, but the Act takes effect 180 days later, so the operative period is about 9.5 years. The bill should state which date controls.

**Implementation and general mechanics**

31. **Global:** The Act gives Commerce and the Secretary no express rulemaking authority to define terms, set compute-measurement methods, establish registry procedures, or make capability determinations. It also contains no appropriations. Together these are a strong "unworkable as written" point, because the agency has no defined power to make the Act operable before it takes effect.
32. **Sec. 8:** The 180-day effective date gives agencies no defined timeline for guidance, forms, or measurement methods, and no safe harbor for good-faith compliance during that period.
33. **Global:** The bill has no severability clause. Courts usually presume severability, so this is a weak argument, but it means that striking Sec. 2(1)(B) could leave the scope of the Act uncertain.

---

**Notes for the legal team**

- **Prioritize.** The strongest points are the internal contradiction in Sec. 7, the vagueness of the capability standard in Sec. 2(1)(B), the missing confidentiality protection in Sec. 4(b), the enforcement gaps (Secs. 4–5 and 31), and the grandfathering gap in Sec. 3(b) and 8.
- **Some ambiguities cut against us.** The breadth of "develop" and "deploy," the strict-liability civil penalty, and the reporting requirement could prompt sponsors to narrow the bill in ways that hurt us. Ambiguity may also lead lawmakers to fix the drafting rather than reject the policy. Arguments that the bill is unworkable should be framed around specific fixes, since a general claim is easier to dismiss.
- **Verify before citing.** This analysis is not legal advice. The legal team should check the Dictionary Act definitions, the void-for-vagueness case law, and the official committee text. CRS or Senate Legislative Counsel can confirm how the manager's amendment changed the text.
