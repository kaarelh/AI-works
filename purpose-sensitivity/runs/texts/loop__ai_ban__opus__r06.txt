# S. 2817: Loopholes, Ambiguities, and Drafting Problems

I've grouped the issues by section and listed them in bill order. The ones most likely to let development continue are flagged **[Major]**. Since this is going to a Senate office, I'd recommend having counsel or Senate Legislative Counsel check the final list.

## Sec. 2: Definitions

1. **[Major] Sec. 2(1)(A), "single training run."** A developer could split training into several nominally separate runs, such as pretraining, continued pretraining, fine-tuning, RL post-training, or distillation from a larger model, so that no single run exceeds 10^26 operations. The threshold should cover cumulative compute across all training stages of a model and any model it was derived from.

2. **[Major] Sec. 2(1)(A), fixed compute threshold.** Algorithmic efficiency gains steadily lower the compute needed for a given capability, so a static 10^26 threshold will capture less of the danger over time. Nothing authorizes the agency to lower the threshold or adjust it for efficiency gains.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A), mismatched terms and no counting method.** The bill measures "computing power," which is a rate, in "operations," which is a total quantity. It also gives no method for counting operations, such as whether synthetic-data generation, failed runs, or low-precision operations count. Companies could exploit this ambiguity to under-report.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A), the prohibition only triggers after the fact.** A model only becomes "covered" once it has been "trained using" more than 10^26 operations. It is therefore unclear when training becomes unlawful: when the run is planned, partway through, or only once it crosses the line. The prohibition should cover training runs that are planned, intended, or reasonably projected to exceed the threshold.

5. **[Major] Sec. 2(1)(B), "Secretary" is undefined.** The bill never says which Secretary makes the determination. Sec. 6 refers to the Secretary of State, which invites the reading that State, rather than Commerce, holds this authority. The bill also gives no procedure, timeline, or rulemaking authority for making a determination.

6. **[Major] Sec. 2(1)(B), standard is vague and reactive.** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no benchmark, and "human" could mean an average person or a top expert. That vagueness invites void-for-vagueness challenges, especially because criminal penalties attach. The test also applies only to a system that already exists and has those capabilities, which is too late to prevent the harm the bill targets.

7. **Sec. 2(1), key terms undefined.** "Artificial intelligence," "model," and "system" are not defined. Prong (A) says "model" while prong (B) says "system," which raises the question of whether wrapping a model in scaffolding or agents creates a new, uncovered "system."

8. **[Major] Sec. 2(2), "U.S. person" excludes foreign subsidiaries.** A U.S. company could train through a subsidiary incorporated in the UK, the UAE, or elsewhere, and that subsidiary would not be a U.S. person. The definition should reach entities that U.S. persons own or control.

9. **[Major] Sec. 2(2), only "corporations" are covered.** The definition leaves out LLCs, limited partnerships, trusts, and other entity types, and several leading AI developers are organized in exactly these forms. It should cover any entity organized under U.S. law.

10. **Sec. 2(2), other gaps in "U.S. person."** The definition omits lawful permanent residents and entities organized under the laws of DC, the territories, or federal charter. It also does not define "State."

11. **[Major] Sec. 2(3), facility threshold is easy to evade.** "Advanced AI chips" and "data center" are undefined. Operators could stay just under 50,000 chips per site or spread training across several linked facilities. A chip count also becomes less meaningful as each chip gets more powerful, so the threshold should be based on aggregate compute capacity, with aggregation rules for facilities under common ownership or networked together.

## Sec. 3: Prohibition

12. **[Major] Sec. 3(a), "person in the United States."** This language turns on where the person is located, not where the activity happens. A foreign company located abroad could arguably rent U.S. cloud compute and train a covered system on U.S. soil. The prohibition should also cover any training conducted using computing infrastructure located in the United States.

13. **[Major] Sec. 3(a), no liability for supplying compute or capital.** The bill does not prohibit knowingly providing compute, chips, cloud services, funding, or model weights to someone else's prohibited training. For civil enforcement there is no aiding-and-abetting equivalent, since 18 U.S.C. §§ 2 and 371 reach only the criminal side.

14. **Sec. 3(a), "develop" and "deploy" are undefined.** Without definitions, it is unclear whether hosting, licensing, serving through an API, or open-sourcing weights counts as "deploying." It is also unclear whether a U.S. firm offering access to a foreign-trained covered model violates the Act. "Develop" is broad enough to invite vagueness and First Amendment (code-as-speech) challenges.

15. **[Major] Sec. 3(b), grandfather clause creates a race.** Because systems whose training "began" before enactment are exempt, companies have a strong incentive to start very large runs before the bill becomes law. Nothing limits how long a grandfathered run can continue or how much it can scale.

16. **[Major] Sec. 3(b), grandfathered systems can keep evolving.** It is unclear whether further training, fine-tuning, or successor versions of a grandfathered system inherit the exemption. Read broadly, this could exempt an entire product line indefinitely.

17. **[Major] Sec. 3(b) with Sec. 2(1)(B), no override for dangerous systems.** If the Secretary determines that a grandfathered system is superintelligent, it is still exempt. The bill should, at minimum, let the government halt deployment of a grandfathered system that meets prong (B).

18. **[Major] Sec. 3(c), safety evaluation exemption swallows the rule.** "For the purpose of safety evaluation" turns on the company's stated purpose, and it exempts all of training, development, and deployment. Any lab could describe its frontier work as safety research. This exemption should be deleted, or limited to evaluating existing or grandfathered systems under a license with government oversight and no new training above the threshold.

## Sec. 4: Registry

19. **Sec. 4(a), no deadline, procedure, or rulemaking authority.** The bill sets no deadline for registration, does not say what information registration requires, and does not define "operator." Commerce is not given authority to issue implementing regulations.

20. **[Major] Sec. 4, no penalty for non-compliance.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3, so failing to register or filing a false or incomplete report carries no penalty under the Act. 18 U.S.C. § 1001 might reach knowingly false statements, but the bill should impose its own penalties.

21. **[Major] Sec. 4(b), reporting is unverifiable and too thin.** The only check on reports is the operator's self-certification. There is no inspection, audit, or subpoena authority, and no requirement to report compute per customer or per training run. Cloud operators may not even know what their customers are running, so the bill needs know-your-customer obligations.

22. **Sec. 4, geographic scope unclear.** The bill does not say whether facilities abroad owned or operated by U.S. persons must register. Without them, the registry misses the offshore loophole described in item 8.

23. **Sec. 4, confidentiality not addressed.** The bill has no trade-secret or FOIA protection for reported data. Industry will likely raise this as an objection, and it may be an argument for resisting reporting.

## Sec. 5: Penalties

24. **[Major] Sec. 5, no enforcing authority or remedies.** The bill does not say who brings civil actions (the Attorney General, Commerce, or someone else). It also provides no injunctive relief, no forfeiture or seizure of chips and model weights, and no mandatory deletion of illegally trained weights.

25. **Sec. 5(a) vs. Sec. 8, conflicting start dates.** Sec. 5(a) makes violations "on or after the date of enactment" penalized, but Sec. 8 says the Act takes effect 180 days after enactment. This conflict invites litigation over the 180-day window and possibly retroactivity arguments.

26. **Sec. 5(a), penalties are weak and ambiguous.** A $1M-per-day cap is small next to frontier training budgets and potential revenue, so it may be treated as a cost of doing business. It is also unclear how a "day of violation" is counted for a training run that has already been completed.

27. **Sec. 5(b), "willfully" is a high bar, and criminal fines are capped.** "Willfully" generally requires proving the defendant knew the conduct was illegal. Fines "under title 18" are capped by default for organizations, unless prosecutors use the alternative twice-the-gain measure. Consider a "knowingly" standard and explicit, larger organizational fines.

28. **Sec. 5, no whistleblower protections.** Insiders are the most likely source of evidence about covert training runs, but the bill neither protects nor rewards them.

## Sec. 6: International Agreement

29. **Sec. 6, timing ambiguous and obligation unenforceable.** "Shall seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months" could mean negotiations start within 18 months or an agreement is reached within 18 months. Either way, the obligation is hortatory: there is no reporting to Congress, no definition of "equivalent," and no consequence if negotiations fail.

30. **Sec. 6, no measures against foreign development.** Until a treaty exists, nothing in the bill addresses foreign training, such as export controls on chips or restrictions on services that enable covered training abroad.

## Sec. 7: Duration

31. **[Major] Sec. 7(a) vs. 7(b), automatic sunset.** Sec. 7(a) implies the ban lasts until Congress finds that safe development is possible, but Sec. 7(b) ends the entire Act after 10 years regardless. That contradicts the bill's premise, gives companies a date to wait for, and should be struck or replaced with mandatory reauthorization review.

32. **Sec. 7(a), legally hollow and incomplete.** A joint resolution is ordinary legislation that requires the President's signature, so this clause adds nothing to Congress's existing power to repeal. Nothing requires the National Academies to actually produce the report, and it is unclear whether such a joint resolution automatically ends the prohibition.

## Sec. 8 and Structural Issues

33. **Sec. 8, effective date and registration timing.** The 180-day delay applies to Sec. 4 with no separate registration deadline, so the government may lack visibility into facilities when the prohibition takes effect. Item 25 covers the separate conflict with Sec. 5(a).

34. **Whole bill, no severability clause.** Without one, a court that strikes a single provision, such as the prong (B) determination on vagueness grounds, could put more of the Act at risk.

35. **Whole bill, no anti-circumvention clause.** A general provision prohibiting arrangements whose purpose is to evade the Act would backstop many of the gaps above, including run-splitting, facility-splitting, and offshore subsidiaries.

36. **Whole bill, no preemption or savings clause.** The bill is silent on whether states may impose stricter rules. That silence invites preemption litigation in both directions.

37. **Sec. 2(1)(A) with Sec. 3(a), breadth as a political vulnerability.** The 10^26 threshold bans models nowhere near superintelligence. Opponents will use that point, and it may draw due-process or Commerce Clause arguments. A licensing pathway or a tiered approach for systems just above the threshold could defuse this without opening a loophole.

## Top Priorities If the Office Can Only Take a Few Fixes

- Delete or tightly limit the **safety-evaluation exemption** (item 18).
- Strike the **10-year sunset** (item 31).
- Close the **foreign-subsidiary and entity-type gaps** (items 8 and 9).
- Measure compute **cumulatively, not per run** (item 1).
- Narrow the **grandfather clause** (items 15–17).
- Define **"Secretary"** and give that agency enforcement, inspection, and rulemaking authority (items 5, 21, and 24).
- Fix the **Sec. 5(a)/Sec. 8 date conflict** (item 25).
