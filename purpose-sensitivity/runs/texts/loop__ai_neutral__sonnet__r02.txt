Here are the issues I found, grouped by section. I read only the text you pasted, so anything I flag as "undefined" or "missing" should be checked against the full bill. The constitutional points are issue-spotting, not legal analysis, and are worth running past a legal expert before publication.

## Section 2: Definitions

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "single training run" is undefined.** A developer could split a model's training into staged or sequential runs, each under 10^26 operations. The bill also doesn't say whether compute spent on a pretrained base model, fine-tuning, or distillation counts toward the total.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A), no way to measure or verify compute.** The bill doesn't say who calculates the figure or what counts (reinforcement learning, synthetic data generation, inference-time compute). Nothing requires developers to report training compute, so the government may be unable to show the threshold was crossed.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A), the threshold is fixed in statute.** There is no mechanism to adjust it. Algorithmic efficiency gains mean a highly capable model could be trained below 10^26 operations, and (A) would then miss it.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A) vs. (B), "model" vs. "system."** Clause (A) covers a "model," while (B) covers a "system." An agentic system or ensemble built from several sub-threshold models may escape (A) entirely and be reachable only through the Secretary's discretionary determination under (B).

5. **Sec. 2(1), the title and the operative test don't match.** The Act is called a superintelligence prohibition, but (A) is a compute proxy that sweeps in any large model whether or not it is superintelligent. Conversely, a superintelligent system trained with less compute is covered only if the Secretary acts.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B), "substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no benchmark.** "Substantially," "most," and "cognitive tasks" are all undefined, and no evidentiary standard is given. This invites vagueness challenges, especially since criminal liability can attach under Sec. 5(b).

7. **Sec. 2(1)(B), "the Secretary" is never defined.** Sec. 2 doesn't say which Secretary is meant, while Sec. 6 separately names the Secretary of State. The bill also creates no process for the determination: no rulemaking, notice, comment, deadline, public list, or judicial review.

8. **Sec. 2(1)(B), nondelegation and fair-notice exposure.** Congress hands an unnamed official open-ended power to decide what is prohibited, with no intelligible standard. A company might not know a system is covered until after it has been built, which is a serious problem for fair notice in a criminal statute.

9. **Sec. 2(1)(B) with Sec. 3(b), determinations can't reach grandfathered systems.** Any system whose training began before enactment is exempt, even if the Secretary later finds it meets the capability test. For existing systems, (B) is largely a dead letter.

10. **Sec. 2(2), "U.S. person" is narrow.** It covers only citizens and "corporations organized under the laws of a State." It omits lawful permanent residents, LLCs and partnerships (arguably), nonprofits, entities organized in D.C. or the territories, and foreign-incorporated subsidiaries of U.S. firms. A company could shift training to a foreign subsidiary, and "any U.S. person anywhere" would not reach it.

11. **Sec. 2(3), "advanced AI chips" and "data center" are undefined.** There is no performance specification, no way to update it as hardware changes, and no rule on whether a "data center" is a building, a campus, or a networked cluster.

12. **Sec. 2(3), the 50,000-chip threshold is easy to evade.** A developer could spread chips across several sites, each under the threshold, or use leased or cloud capacity. Decentralized or multi-site training is not addressed.

13. **Sec. 2(3), the facility threshold isn't tied to the compute threshold.** A smaller cluster running longer can reach 10^26 operations without ever being a "covered facility," so the registry may miss the facilities that matter. The term is also used only in Sec. 4, never in the prohibition.

14. **Sec. 2, other key terms are missing.** The bill doesn't define "artificial intelligence model/system," "train," "develop," "deploy," "operator," "safety evaluation," or "United States" (do territories count?).

## Section 3: Prohibition

15. **Sec. 3(a), "train, develop, or deploy" is undefined.** It is unclear whether releasing open weights, hosting a model, offering API access, or a user running a model counts as deployment. The terms "develop" and "train" also overlap.

16. **Sec. 3(a), coverage gaps around the core conduct.** The bill doesn't prohibit funding, supplying compute for, selling, importing, possessing, or transferring a covered system's weights. Only the direct actor is reached, and there is no explicit civil secondary liability.

17. **Sec. 3(a), foreign developers serving U.S. users.** The prohibition reaches "any person in the United States" and U.S. persons abroad. A foreign company with no U.S. presence that trains abroad and offers the system to Americans may fall outside it.

18. **Sec. 3(a), unclear when a violation begins.** A system isn't known to be "covered" under (A) until cumulative compute crosses the threshold, possibly mid-run. The bill doesn't say whether starting a run planned to exceed the threshold is itself unlawful, and it doesn't prohibit attempts or planning.

19. **Sec. 3(b), "training began" is undefined.** A developer could claim a post-enactment model is a continuation of a pre-enactment run, or begin runs just before enactment. It is also unclear whether successors, fine-tunes, or derivatives of an exempt system are themselves exempt.

20. **Sec. 3(b), the grandfathering has no limits.** There is no compute cap, reporting requirement, or deployment restriction on exempt systems, so they can presumably keep being scaled, trained further, and deployed. The exemption also creates an incentive to rush runs before enactment.

21. **Sec. 3(b) vs. Sec. 8, the date mismatch.** The exemption is keyed to enactment, but the Act takes effect 180 days later. The bill is silent on a run begun during that window, which wasn't unlawful when it started but would be continuing after the effective date.

22. **Sec. 3(c), the safety-evaluation exemption is open-ended.** It turns on the subjective "purpose" of the activity, with no limits on scale, no approval or registration requirement, and no definition of "safety evaluation." A developer could characterize large-scale training as safety work, and dual-purpose activities are not addressed.

23. **Sec. 3, no treatment of government or national-security activity.** The text doesn't say whether federal agencies, the military, intelligence agencies, or their contractors are covered or exempt. Either reading would be contestable.

## Section 4: Registry

24. **Sec. 4(a), no deadline, contents, or rulemaking authority.** The bill doesn't say when registration must happen or what it must include, and it directs no agency office to build or run the registry.

25. **Sec. 4(a), "operator" is undefined.** It's unclear whether the owner, the lessee, or the cloud tenant running the workload is responsible when those are different parties.

26. **Sec. 4(b), the reports are weak.** "Describing the computing workloads" is vague. Reports needn't identify the model, customer, or cumulative training compute, and they are self-certified. Commerce has no audit, inspection, or subpoena power.

27. **Sec. 4, no penalty for registry violations.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3, so failing to register or filing a false report has no stated consequence. General false-statement law might apply, but the bill doesn't say so.

28. **Sec. 4(b), quarterly reporting lags the conduct.** A full training run can start and finish between reports, so violations could be discovered only after the fact.

29. **Sec. 4, no confidentiality or information-sharing rules.** The bill is silent on trade-secret protection, FOIA treatment, and whether Commerce can share reports with enforcement agencies.

30. **Sec. 4, extraterritorial reach is unclear.** It isn't stated whether "each covered facility" includes facilities outside the United States operated by U.S. persons.

## Section 5: Penalties

31. **Sec. 5, no designated enforcer or process.** The bill doesn't say who brings civil actions or assesses penalties (DOJ, Commerce, or both). It also provides no investigative authority, injunctive relief, administrative procedure, or whistleblower protection.

32. **Sec. 5(a), "each day on which the violation occurs" is ambiguous.** For continuous training, it's unclear whether each day of the run is a violation, and for deployment, whether each day of availability or each deployment counts. The "not more than" cap also provides no penalty factors and may be small relative to the economics of frontier training.

33. **Sec. 5(a) vs. Sec. 8, the retroactivity conflict.** Penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the Act doesn't take effect until 180 days later. This invites a fair-notice or due-process challenge and confusion over which conduct is covered.

34. **Sec. 5(b), "willfully" is unclear.** The bill doesn't say whether the defendant must know the system is covered, know the conduct is unlawful, or both. Combined with the vague definition in Sec. 2(1)(B), this could make criminal cases very hard to prove.

35. **Sec. 5, no remedy aimed at the system itself.** There is no provision for stop orders, forfeiture, or deletion of weights or models. Fines alone may not halt a prohibited program, and the bill doesn't address individual versus corporate liability.

## Section 6: International agreement

36. **Sec. 6, the deadline and duty are soft.** "Shall seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months" could mean starting talks or concluding them, and "seek" imposes no duty to succeed. There are no reporting requirements or consequences.

37. **Sec. 6, "equivalent" prohibitions is undefined.** It also doesn't say whether the result would be a treaty needing Senate approval or an executive agreement. Nothing connects the outcome to Sec. 3, so competitors abroad can continue until an agreement exists.

## Section 7: Duration

38. **Sec. 7(a) vs. 7(b), the provisions conflict.** Subsection (a) says the prohibition lasts until Congress finds covered systems can be developed safely, implying an open-ended term. Subsection (b) ends the whole Act after 10 years regardless.

39. **Sec. 7(a), the off-ramp is underspecified.** The bill doesn't direct the National Academies to produce a report, set a deadline or funding, or define "safely." It also builds in an all-or-nothing choice, since there's no partial or tiered lifting. A joint resolution needs the President's signature and has no expedited procedure, so it faces a filibuster.

40. **Sec. 7(b), the sunset is measured from enactment.** Because of the 180-day delay, enforcement would run only about 9.5 years. The bill has no savings clause, so it's unclear whether violations committed before the sunset can still be pursued afterward. The registry also disappears at sunset.

## Section 8 and bill-wide issues

41. **Sec. 8, the 180-day delay creates a compliance gap.** Beyond the date mismatches above, developers get roughly six months to accelerate, complete, or deploy systems before any obligation applies.

42. **Bill-wide, no severability clause.** If a court strikes down part of the Act, such as the Secretary's determination power in Sec. 2(1)(B), the rest could be left in doubt.

43. **Bill-wide, constitutional vulnerabilities.** The Act invites vagueness challenges (criminal penalties plus undefined terms), nondelegation challenges (Sec. 2(1)(B)), and First Amendment arguments, since courts have treated some software code as protected speech. Because it grants no express review or advisory-opinion process, challenges would likely come as enforcement defenses or pre-enforcement suits.

44. **Bill-wide, no implementation resources or authority.** The bill authorizes no appropriations, grants no general rulemaking power, and doesn't say how the agencies involved will coordinate.

45. **Bill-wide, no mention of state law.** There is no preemption clause or savings clause, leaving open how state AI laws would interact with the federal prohibition.
