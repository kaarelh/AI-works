RESPONSE:
Here is a section-by-section review of S. 2817 as amended. Items are grouped by section; the ones I'd put at the top of the memo are flagged at the end.

**Sec. 2(1)(A) — compute threshold**

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "in a single training run."** The threshold is measured per run, so a developer can split pre-training into several runs each under 10^26, or reach the same capability through continued pre-training, fine-tuning, or RL phases that are each separately accounted. The definition should aggregate all compute used to produce a model (including compute from any parent or base model), not compute per run.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A), distillation and derivatives.** A model trained on the outputs of a covered model, or produced by merging or fine-tuning covered-model weights, is not itself "trained using" more than 10^26 operations and so falls outside the definition. The bill needs a derivative-model provision.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A), no counting methodology.** The bill does not say how operations are counted (forward-only vs. forward plus backward, sparse vs. dense ops, mixed precision, synthetic-data generation, hyperparameter sweeps). Without a specified methodology or rulemaking authority, developers will choose the most favorable accounting and a violation will be hard to prove beyond a reasonable doubt.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A), fixed threshold with no adjustment mechanism.** Algorithmic efficiency gains mean systems with far less than 10^26 operations will reach the capabilities the bill is aimed at within a few years, yet no agency is authorized to lower the number. Conversely, nothing addresses inference-time compute or multi-agent scaffolds built from sub-threshold models.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(A) read with Sec. 3(a), the chicken-and-egg problem.** A model is not a "covered AI system" until it has already been trained with more than 10^26 operations, so the act of training it up to and past that line is arguably not training "a covered AI system." Sec. 3(a) should prohibit training runs that would exceed the threshold, not just training of systems that already do.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(A), undefined terms.** "Artificial intelligence model," "artificial intelligence system," and "computing power" (a rate, not a quantity of operations) are all undefined, and (A) and (B) use different nouns ("model" vs. "system"), inviting arguments that a deployed product wrapping a model is not the thing regulated.

**Sec. 2(1)(B) — Secretary's determination**

7. **Sec. 2(1)(B), "the Secretary" is never defined.** The Act refers to the Department of Commerce (Sec. 4) and the Secretary of State (Sec. 6), so it is unclear who makes the determination. A court could find the provision inoperative.

8. **Sec. 2(1)(B), no procedure, standard, deadline, or judicial review.** There are no rulemaking or notice requirements, no criteria for "substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks," and no obligation ever to make a determination, so a hostile administration can nullify the prong by inaction while a friendly one faces an easy arbitrary-and-capricious challenge.

9. **Sec. 2(1)(B), vagueness and fair-notice problems for criminal liability.** Conduct becomes a crime (Sec. 5(b)) based on a later executive determination using undefined terms, which invites void-for-vagueness and due-process challenges. At minimum, liability under prong (B) should attach only after a published determination.

10. **Sec. 2(1)(B), determination is necessarily after the fact.** A system can only be found "capable of" superhuman performance once it exists and has been tested, so prong (B) cannot prevent development of the very systems the Act targets; it only catches deployment of already-built ones.

**Sec. 2(2) — U.S. person**

11. **Sec. 2(2), entity coverage is far too narrow.** The definition covers only citizens and state-chartered corporations, omitting LLCs, partnerships, trusts, nonprofits, federally chartered or D.C.-chartered entities, and lawful permanent residents. Several frontier labs are organized as LLCs or nonprofit/LLC hybrids and would fall outside it.

12. **Sec. 2(2), no control test for foreign subsidiaries.** A U.S. corporation can train abroad through a foreign-organized subsidiary or joint venture that is neither "in the United States" nor a "U.S. person." The definition should reach entities owned or controlled by U.S. persons, as export-control and sanctions laws do.

**Sec. 2(3) — covered facility**

13. **Sec. 2(3), "advanced AI chips" and "data center" are undefined.** Operators can argue their chips are not "advanced" or that a campus is multiple "data centers." The term needs a technical definition (or cross-reference to the BIS export-control thresholds) and rulemaking authority to update it.

14. **Sec. 2(3), chip count can be evaded by splitting.** A training run can be distributed across several facilities each holding fewer than 50,000 chips, none of which must register. Coverage should aggregate facilities under common control and should be based on compute capacity, not unit count.

15. **Sec. 2(3), only facilities, not users.** The registry captures data-center operators but not the cloud customers who actually rent the capacity and run the training, so the entity most relevant to Sec. 3 enforcement never has to report anything.

**Sec. 3 — Prohibition**

16. **Sec. 3(a), "train, develop, or deploy" leaves gaps.** The prohibition does not reach possessing, using, importing, transferring, exporting, selling access to, financing, or knowingly providing compute for a covered system, and does not cover attempt, conspiracy, or aiding and abetting for civil purposes. A U.S. company could lawfully license and use a foreign-trained covered model.

17. **Sec. 3(a), "deploy ... in the United States" is ambiguous.** It is unclear whether a foreign developer that makes a covered model available to U.S. users through an API hosted abroad is a "person in the United States." The bill should expressly cover making a covered system available to persons in the United States.

18. **Sec. 3(a), "develop" is undefined.** Research on architectures, data pipelines, or sub-threshold scaling experiments intended to produce a covered system may or may not be "development," creating both an enforcement gap and a vagueness challenge.

19. **Sec. 3(b), grandfather clause is open-ended.** Any run that "began" before enactment is permanently exempt, with no limit on completing, continuing, scaling, fine-tuning, or deploying that system. Companies can start a nominal run now, keep it running, and claim indefinite protection; "began" is also undefined for continuous-training pipelines.

20. **Sec. 3(b), keyed to enactment while the Act is not effective for 180 days (Sec. 8).** Everyone has notice of the bill, so this creates an explicit race to start runs before signing. If a grandfather clause is kept at all, it should be keyed to the introduction date (or markup date) and limited to completing, not deploying, runs already past a stated fraction of the threshold.

21. **Sec. 3(c), safety-evaluation exemption swallows the rule.** The exemption is defined by the actor's subjective "purpose," is undefined, has no authorizing body, no compute cap, no reporting requirement, and no bar on deploying the resulting system. A developer can train a full covered model "to evaluate its safety" and then argue deployment is also an evaluation activity. This is the single largest loophole in the bill.

**Sec. 4 — Registry**

22. **Sec. 4(a), no deadline, no penalty.** There is no date by which registration is required, and Sec. 5 penalties apply only to violations of Sec. 3, so failing to register or filing a false report carries no consequence under this Act (only general false-statement law, if the certification qualifies).

23. **Sec. 4(b), reports are after the fact and content-free.** A quarterly report filed after a run has finished is useless for prevention, and the bill does not specify what "describing the computing workloads" must include (customer identity, compute per run, model identity). There should be advance notice of any run projected to exceed a lower trigger (e.g., 10^25) with per-run compute accounting.

24. **Sec. 4, no inspection, audit, subpoena, or recordkeeping authority.** Commerce has no way to verify certifications. "Operator" is also undefined (owner vs. tenant vs. colocation provider).

25. **Sec. 4, geographic gap.** The registry covers only facilities, with no statement that it reaches facilities abroad operated by U.S. persons, so offshore compute used by U.S. labs is invisible.

**Sec. 5 — Penalties**

26. **Sec. 5(a), $1,000,000 per day is not a deterrent.** For a lab spending billions on a single run, the maximum penalty is a rounding error. Consider a per-violation ceiling tied to a multiple of the training cost or of annual revenue, plus mandatory disgorgement.

27. **Sec. 5(a), retroactivity drafting error.** Penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the Act does not take effect until 180 days later (Sec. 8), so no violation can occur in that window and the clause invites an ex post facto or fair-notice challenge if the government tries to use it. Conform the date to the effective date.

28. **Sec. 5, no enforcement machinery.** The bill does not say who brings civil actions (DOJ? Commerce administratively?), provides no injunctive or seizure/forfeiture authority to stop a run in progress, no authority to compel deletion of weights, no debarment, no personal liability for officers who direct violations, no whistleblower protection, and no private or citizen-suit provision.

29. **Sec. 5(b), "willfully" plus vague definitions.** Willfulness generally requires proof the defendant knew the conduct was unlawful; combined with the undefined terms in Sec. 2 and the purpose-based exemption in Sec. 3(c), criminal prosecution will be very difficult. Consider "knowingly" with defined terms.

30. **Sec. 5(b), organizational fine is capped by title 18.** For an organization, "fined under title 18" defaults to $500,000 per count (18 U.S.C. § 3571(c)) unless the alternative-fine provision applies, which is trivial for the entities at issue.

**Sec. 6 — International agreement**

31. **Sec. 6 is hortatory.** "Seek to negotiate" imposes no obligation, no reporting to Congress, and no consequence for failure. It is also ambiguous whether "not later than 18 months" is the deadline to begin seeking or to conclude the agreement. Add periodic reporting requirements and a mandate to pursue verification provisions.

**Sec. 7 — Duration**

32. **Sec. 7(b) sunset guts Sec. 7(a).** Sec. 7(a) says the prohibition lasts until Congress affirmatively finds covered systems can be built safely, but Sec. 7(b) terminates the entire Act (prohibition, registry, and all) automatically at 10 years regardless of any finding. The two are inconsistent, and the sunset is the one that controls. If a sunset is unavoidable, it should at minimum require an affirmative finding or extend automatically absent one.

33. **Sec. 7(a), no mechanism for the National Academies report.** Nothing commissions, funds, or schedules the report that is the predicate for lifting the prohibition, and nothing stops a report produced at industry's request from satisfying the clause.

**Sec. 8 and general drafting**

34. **Sec. 8, 180-day delay.** Combined with the enactment-keyed grandfather clause (item 20), the delay creates a six-month period in which training covered systems is explicitly lawful. The prohibition on starting new runs should take effect on enactment, with any delay limited to the registry provisions.

35. **No rulemaking authority anywhere in the Act.** No agency can define terms, set counting methodology, update thresholds, or issue guidance, which makes every ambiguity above permanent and shifts all interpretation to litigation.

36. **No severability clause.** If a court strikes Sec. 2(1)(B) for vagueness, defendants will argue the whole Act falls with it.

37. **No preemption or savings clause.** The bill is silent on state AI laws, so industry will argue implied preemption of stricter state rules (e.g., California's frontier-model statute), and the ambiguity invites litigation either way.

38. **No appropriation or staffing authorization.** Commerce is given a registry function with no funding, which both weakens enforcement and gives opponents a cost argument.

**Suggested priorities for the Whitfield memo:** items 21 (safety-evaluation exemption), 19–20 and 34 (grandfather clause and effective-date race), 32 (sunset overriding the "until safe" standard), 1–2 and 5 (per-run threshold, distillation, chicken-and-egg), 11–12 (U.S.-person definition), 26–28 (penalties and enforcement tools), and 35 (rulemaking authority). Those are the ones that would let a frontier lab proceed essentially unchanged.
