RESPONSE:
Here's a full pass. Items are numbered continuously and grouped by section; I've flagged the ones I'd put at the top of the letter at the end.

## Sec. 2(1)(A) — Compute threshold

1. **§2(1)(A), "in a single training run."** This is the biggest evasion route in the definition: a developer can split training into several runs each under 10^26 (pretraining, continued pretraining, RL post-training, fine-tuning) and no single run trips the threshold. The definition should count cumulative compute used to produce a model and its derivatives, including post-training and any compute from models it was distilled from.

2. **§2(1)(A), "artificial intelligence model" vs. "system."** Subsection (A) defines a *model*, but (B) and §3 regulate a *system*, and neither term is defined. A "system" built by scaffolding or ensembling several sub-threshold models isn't a "model trained" above 10^26, so agentic systems composed of smaller models fall outside (A).

3. **§2(1)(A), distillation and synthetic data.** A student model trained on outputs of a covered model can inherit its capabilities with far less than 10^26 operations. Nothing attributes teacher compute to the student, so the threshold can be laundered through distillation.

4. **§2(1)(A), undefined counting convention.** "Integer or floating-point operations" doesn't say whether both types are summed, how sparsity or low-precision (FP8/FP4) operations are counted, or whether forward and backward passes both count. Developers will adopt the most favorable convention and the government will have to litigate accounting methodology.

5. **§2(1)(A), static threshold.** The 10^26 figure is hard-coded with no authority for the Secretary to lower it as algorithmic efficiency improves. Within a few years a sub-threshold run will likely exceed the capabilities the bill is aimed at, and only (B) will be left to catch it.

6. **§2(1)(A) doesn't define "training" or "training run."** Inference-time compute scaling, continual learning, and online learning in deployment arguably aren't "training," so capability gains achieved those ways are uncounted.

## Sec. 2(1)(B) — Capability prong

7. **§2(1)(B), "the Secretary" is undefined.** The Act never says which Secretary (presumably Commerce, since §4 goes to Commerce, but §6 references the Secretary of State). A defined term is needed.

8. **§2(1)(B) only operates after the fact.** The Secretary can only "determine" that a *system* is capable of exceeding human performance once it exists, so (B) cannot prohibit the *training* of such a system; it only reaches it after it has been built and probably deployed. The bill needs a pre-training mechanism (e.g., covering systems reasonably expected to reach that capability, or a class-based designation authority).

9. **§2(1)(B) gives no procedure, criteria, timeline, or duty.** "Determines" is wholly discretionary, with no "shall" obligation, no evaluation standard, no petition process, and no publication requirement. A Secretary can simply decline to make determinations, and any determination made will be attacked under the APA as arbitrary for lack of statutory criteria.

10. **§2(1)(B) vagueness exposes the criminal penalty to challenge.** "Substantially exceeding," "most cognitive tasks," and "human performance" (expert? median?) are undefined. Tying a 10-year felony to an undefined administrative judgment invites a void-for-vagueness and fair-notice challenge, and it's unclear whether liability attaches to conduct before the determination is made.

## Sec. 2(2) — "U.S. person"

11. **§2(2) omits most entity types.** The definition covers only citizens and "corporations," leaving out LLCs, partnerships, trusts, nonprofits, and other entities. Most frontier labs and their compute vehicles use LLC or hybrid structures.

12. **§2(2) omits foreign subsidiaries and controlled entities.** A U.S. corporation can form a subsidiary in Ireland, Singapore, or the UAE and train there; the subsidiary is not a U.S. person and the parent is not "training." The definition should include any entity owned or controlled by U.S. persons, and §3 should reach anyone who causes, directs, funds, or procures prohibited activity.

13. **§2(2) omits lawful permanent residents and entities organized under D.C., territorial, or federal law.** Standard export-control definitions (e.g., 31 C.F.R. §560.314) cover these; this bill should too.

## Sec. 2(3) — Covered facility

14. **§2(3), "advanced AI chip" and "data center" are undefined.** Without a technical definition (by performance, interconnect bandwidth, or ECCN), an operator can argue none of its chips are "advanced."

15. **§2(3), the 50,000-chip threshold is per-building and easily split.** A campus of ten 49,000-chip buildings linked by high-bandwidth fiber is not a covered facility. The definition should aggregate across commonly owned or networked facilities.

16. **§2(3) counts chips rather than capacity.** Chip performance grows each generation; a 40,000-chip facility in 2029 will out-compute a 50,000-chip facility today. Tie the threshold to aggregate compute capacity with authority to update it.

## Sec. 3(a) — Prohibition scope

17. **§3(a), "train, develop, or deploy" omits key verbs.** Possessing, using, operating, transferring, licensing, importing, exporting, releasing weights, and distributing are all uncovered. A covered model trained abroad and shipped into the U.S. as open weights is not clearly prohibited unless someone "deploys" it, and "deploy" is undefined.

18. **§3(a) doesn't reach foreign deployment to U.S. users.** A foreign lab serving U.S. customers over the internet is not a "person in the United States." The prohibition should cover deploying a covered system *in the United States or to persons in the United States*.

19. **§3(a) doesn't reach foreign use of U.S. compute.** A foreign company renting a U.S. data center trains abroad in a legal sense; neither it nor the cloud provider is "training." Add coverage of training "using computing resources located in the United States" and a corresponding duty on compute providers.

20. **§3(a) has no secondary liability.** There is no prohibition on knowingly providing compute, chips, capital, or assistance to a covered training run, and no attempt or conspiracy language. Cloud providers and chip sellers are untouched.

21. **§3(a), "person" and government coverage are ambiguous.** Under standard construction, "person" generally excludes the federal government and states. If the intent is to cover government and contractor projects, the bill needs to say so; if not, that's a loophole worth addressing head-on rather than leaving to litigation.

## Sec. 3(b) — Grandfather clause

22. **§3(b) exempts every existing frontier system permanently.** Several systems already exceed 10^26, so the clause exempts the current frontier for continued development and deployment with no limit. At minimum, continued *development* of grandfathered systems should be prohibited and deployment should require registration.

23. **§3(b), "training began before enactment" invites pre-enactment stockpiling.** A lab can launch (or nominally start) a dozen large runs the week before signature and claim all are grandfathered, including training that continues for years afterward. "Began" should be replaced with "was completed," or the exemption should be removed.

24. **§3(b) doesn't address derivatives.** It's unclear whether a fine-tune, continued-pretraining checkpoint, or distilled descendant of a grandfathered model is the same "system" (exempt) or a new one (covered). Expect every post-enactment model to be characterized as a continuation of a pre-enactment one.

25. **§3(b) also grandfathers (B)-designated systems**, so a system the Secretary later finds exceeds human performance across most cognitive tasks remains exempt forever if its training started early enough.

## Sec. 3(c) — Safety evaluation

26. **§3(c) swallows the rule.** "Safety evaluation" is undefined and the test is subjective purpose; a lab can train a 10^27 model "to evaluate whether such models are safe." If an exemption is needed at all, it should be limited to evaluating existing systems, capped in compute, licensed in advance, and barred from deployment.

## Sec. 4 — Registry

27. **§4 has no enforcement mechanism.** §5 penalties attach only to violations of §3, so failing to register or filing a false quarterly report carries no penalty under this Act.

28. **§4(a) has no registration deadline** and no duty to update when a facility crosses the threshold or changes operators. "Operator" is undefined (owner? tenant? cloud provider?).

29. **§4(b) reporting is self-certified and unverified.** There is no inspection, audit, subpoena, or data-access authority, no required format, and "describing the computing workloads" could be satisfied by a one-paragraph summary.

30. **§4(b) quarterly cadence is too slow.** A 10^26 run can begin and finish between reports. Training runs above some lower threshold (e.g., 10^25) should be reported *in advance*, not described after the fact.

31. **§4 covers facilities but not actors.** There is no registration of developers, no know-your-customer obligation on compute providers, and no reporting of distributed training across several sub-threshold facilities. The registry will miss exactly the runs that are structured to evade it.

32. **No agency is actually charged with enforcing §3.** The bill assigns registration to Commerce but never says who investigates violations, brings civil actions, or refers criminal cases, and grants no rulemaking authority to fill definitional gaps. Add explicit enforcement and rulemaking authority.

## Sec. 5 — Penalties

33. **§5(a) civil penalty is immaterial.** $1 million/day is roughly $365 million/year against training runs costing billions and expected returns in the tens of billions; it will be priced in as a cost of doing business. Penalties should scale (e.g., a percentage of global revenue or a multiple of the compute cost).

34. **§5(a) has no injunctive, forfeiture, or remedial authority.** Nothing authorizes a court to halt a run, seize or order destruction of weights, forfeit hardware, or debar the company. A violator can pay the fine and keep the model.

35. **§5(a), "on or after the date of enactment," conflicts with §8.** The Act doesn't take effect until 180 days after enactment, so no violation can occur before then; the text creates confusion and a retroactivity argument.

36. **§5(a) doesn't define the unit of violation.** Is deploying one model to ten million users one violation per day or ten million? Is a multi-facility run one violation? Ambiguity here will be resolved in the defendant's favor.

37. **§5(b), "fined under title 18" caps fines at $250,000 (individuals) / $500,000 (organizations)** under 18 U.S.C. §3571 unless the statute specifies otherwise. The criminal fine is smaller than the civil one.

38. **§5(b), "willfully" combined with vague definitions makes prosecution very hard.** Willfulness requires proof the defendant knew the conduct was unlawful; given the undefined terms in §2 and the discretionary (B) prong, defendants will always have a good-faith argument. Consider "knowingly," and add explicit liability for responsible corporate officers.

39. **§5 has no whistleblower protection or reward** and no private right of action, so enforcement depends entirely on an unspecified agency.

## Sec. 6 — International agreement

40. **§6 is purely hortatory.** "Seek to negotiate" imposes no obligation and no consequence for failure, and the bill contains no trade, sanctions, import-ban, or export-control tools to pressure foreign developers or respond if negotiation fails. At minimum, add reporting to Congress and a prohibition on importing or distributing covered systems trained abroad.

## Sec. 7 — Duration

41. **§7(a) and §7(b) contradict each other.** Subsection (a) says the prohibition lasts until Congress finds safety by joint resolution; subsection (b) kills the whole Act after 10 years regardless. Courts will read (b) as controlling, so (a) is dead letter.

42. **§7(b) sunset means companies simply wait.** An automatic 10-year expiration (really 9.5 years, given §8) with no renewal mechanism telegraphs that the prohibition is temporary; the sunset should be deleted or converted to a reauthorization requirement with the prohibition continuing absent affirmative repeal.

43. **§7(a) sets no standard for the National Academies report** and doesn't require the report to actually find safety; it only needs to exist for Congress to "act on."

## Sec. 8 — Effective date

44. **§8's 180-day delay creates a free window** during which new runs can start without violating anything, and those runs are not grandfathered under §3(b) (which keys on enactment). The bill uses three different dates (enactment in §3(b) and §5(a), effective date in §8), and runs begun in the gap fall into an undefined legal status. The prohibition on *starting* new covered training should be effective on enactment.

## Missing provisions

45. **No severability clause.** If §2(1)(B) is struck as vague, a court could void the entire Act.

46. **No definitions for "artificial intelligence," "train," "develop," "deploy," "operator," "person," or "Secretary."** Every one of these will be litigated.

47. **No preemption or savings clause**, leaving unclear whether stricter state laws survive or whether this bill could be read to displace them.

48. **No appropriation or staffing authority** for the enforcing agency.
