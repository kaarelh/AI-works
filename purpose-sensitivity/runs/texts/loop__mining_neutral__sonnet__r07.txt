I read only the text you pasted. Items 15, 21, 24, 33 and 34 depend on how this bill meshes with existing law or how courts might treat it, so please have counsel or a subject expert check them before publication.

## Definitions (§2)

1. **§2(1)(B): "the Secretary" is never defined.** §6 names the Secretary of State, so it is unclear which department makes the determination. That leaves authority, rulemaking and review all open.
2. **§2(1)(B): an open-ended agency determination defines the offense.** There are no criteria, notice-and-comment requirement, publication requirement or timing rule, so a determination could follow the conduct it targets. This invites fair-notice, vagueness, nondelegation and APA challenges, especially given the criminal penalty in §5(b).
3. **§2(1)(A): the "single vessel" test invites splitting.** An operator can spread recovery across several vessels, each under 10,000 tons, with no aggregation for affiliates or a coordinated fleet.
4. **§2(1)(A): the volume threshold is ambiguous.** "Per year" could mean calendar, fiscal or rolling. It is also unclear whether "recovery" means material lifted, retained or processed, whether tonnage is wet or dry, and who measures it. Pilot and test mining below the threshold is lawful by design.
5. **§2(1)(A): "deep seabed" is never defined, and 200 meters is not deep.** The depth test may sweep in U.S. continental shelf and EEZ activity the title doesn't suggest. It also doesn't say whether it reaches seabed under foreign jurisdiction or only the high seas.
6. **§2(1)(A): "other mineral resources" is undefined.** It could be read to cover sand, gravel, or even offshore oil and gas, or be contested as limited to hard minerals. Either result is a source of litigation.
7. **§2(2): "U.S. person" is narrow.** It reaches only citizens and "corporations" organized under State law. It appears to omit LLCs, partnerships, trusts, entities organized in D.C. or territories, permanent residents, and foreign-incorporated subsidiaries of U.S. companies. "State" is also undefined.
8. **§2(3): "covered vessel" doesn't match the prohibition.** §2(1)(A) applies to any vessel, but the registry reaches only vessels over 5,000 gross tons. A smaller vessel can mine at the 10,000-ton level without being registered. "Equipped for recovery" is also vague, and gross tonnage isn't tied to a measurement standard.

## Prohibition (§3)

9. **§3(a): foreign persons outside the U.S. aren't covered.** A foreign company or foreign-flagged vessel operating abroad is outside the ban, even if it is U.S.-financed or sells into the U.S.
10. **§3(a): "in the United States" is undefined.** It's unclear whether it includes the territorial sea, EEZ, continental shelf, or U.S.-flagged vessels on the high seas.
11. **§3(a): only "engaging in" mining is prohibited.** Financing, aiding, processing, importing, purchasing and transporting seabed minerals are untouched. Nor does it bar federal agencies from issuing permits.
12. **§3(a): the extraterritorial reach is a legal vulnerability.** Applying it to U.S. persons "anywhere" invites challenges based on high-seas freedoms and the presumption against extraterritoriality. It is also hard to enforce against conduct far from U.S. jurisdiction.
13. **§3(b): "an exploration license" has no specified issuer.** As written, a license from a foreign government or the International Seabed Authority, issued before enactment, could qualify a U.S. company for the exemption.
14. **§3(b): the grandfather clause has no limits.** There is no cap on volume, area or duration, and nothing addresses renewals, amendments or transfers. It also exempts "mining conducted under" an exploration license, which is odd because exploration licenses generally don't authorize commercial recovery.
15. **§3(b): the cutoff creates a rush and a fairness problem.** Applicants with pending applications get nothing, which invites a scramble for licenses before enactment as well as equity and takings arguments. Please verify how this interacts with existing NOAA licensing under the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act.
16. **§3(c): the environmental-assessment exemption is wide open.** "Environmental assessment" is undefined, there is no volume or duration cap, and no agency approves or is notified. The purpose is self-declared, and nothing stops sale of the recovered material, so test mining could scale up under this label.
17. **§3 generally: no coordination with existing law.** The bill doesn't amend or repeal the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act or address offshore leasing law, so overlapping permitting authorities could conflict.

## Registry (§4)

18. **§4(a): the registration mechanics are missing.** There is no deadline, no definition of "operator," no required content, no beneficial-ownership disclosure, and no rulemaking authority for NOAA.
19. **§4: there is no penalty for failing to register or report.** §5 penalizes only violations of §3. Any false-statement liability would have to come from other law, such as 18 U.S.C. 1001, which the bill doesn't mention.
20. **§4(b): the reports are self-certified and unverifiable.** The bill doesn't require tonnage, location or depth, and provides no audits, inspections, vessel tracking, observers or public disclosure. "Quarterly" is also undefined, and there's no first-report date.
21. **§4: nationality scope is unclear.** The bill doesn't say whether foreign-flagged or foreign-operated vessels must register, or what ties a vessel to the U.S.

## Penalties (§5)

22. **§5(a) vs. §8: the dates conflict.** Civil penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the Act takes effect 180 days later. It's unclear whether conduct in the gap is a violation, which creates a fair-notice problem.
23. **§5(a): the enforcement machinery is missing.** The bill names no enforcing agency, assessment procedure, hearing rights or judicial review. It also gives no factors for setting the amount, and "each day on which the violation occurs" is not clearly defined for continuing operations.
24. **§5: there are no in rem or forfeiture tools.** The bill has no seizure, vessel forfeiture, injunction or citizen-suit provisions. Money penalties alone are hard to collect from offshore or foreign-linked operators, and may be treated as a cost of doing business.
25. **§5(b): the "willfully" standard combines badly with the vague definitions.** Defendants could argue they didn't know the volume threshold was met or that a Secretary's determination applied. The bill has no aiding-and-abetting or responsible-corporate-officer language.

## International agreement (§6)

26. **§6: the 18-month deadline is unclear and unenforceable.** The deadline could attach to seeking, negotiating or concluding an agreement. The bill has no reporting requirement and no consequence for missing it, and "equivalent" prohibitions is undefined. It also doesn't address the form of the agreement or its relationship to the International Seabed Authority.

## Duration (§7)

27. **§7(a): the lifting mechanism is underspecified.** The bill doesn't require the National Academies to produce a report, or set a deadline or funding. "Serious harm" is undefined, and a joint resolution needs presidential signature or a veto override. It doesn't say what happens to licenses or pending applications once the ban is lifted.
28. **§7(a) vs. §7(b): the two provisions contradict each other.** One says the ban lasts until Congress finds mining safe, while the other ends the whole Act after 10 years regardless. The ban is effectively temporary, which gives industry an incentive to wait it out.
29. **§7(b): there is no savings clause.** It doesn't say whether penalties for pre-sunset violations survive. The 10 years also run from enactment, not the effective date, so the ban lasts about 9.5 years. It's also unclear whether the registry sunsets too.

## Effective date and structure (§8, general)

30. **§8: the 180-day delay is an open window.** Companies can lawfully ramp up or stockpile with no anti-stockpiling rule. §3(b) is keyed to enactment while the prohibition is keyed to the effective date, so licenses issued in between are in limbo.
31. **General: key terms are undefined.** "Seabed," "recovery," "operator," "person," "vessel" and "State" are never defined, and each is a potential litigation hook.
32. **General: there is no implementation framework.** The bill has no lead agency, rulemaking authority, regulation deadlines, appropriations, or role for the Coast Guard or DOJ.
33. **General: there is no severability clause.** If a court struck §2(1)(B) or the extraterritorial reach, it's unclear whether the rest of the Act survives.
34. **General: it has a legal-challenge profile beyond individual defects.** The main constitutional and statutory attack points are the delegated definition (item 2), extraterritoriality (item 12), and the grandfathering cutoff (item 15).
