RESPONSE:
# S. 2817: Loopholes, Ambiguities, and Drafting Problems

The highest-priority fixes, in my view, are **#1, #12, #13, #16, #22, #23, and #25**. These either let commercial mining continue outright or set the prohibition up to expire.

## Sec. 2: Definitions

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A), 10,000-ton threshold per single vessel.** A company can stay under the cap by spreading its operations across several vessels, each recovering just under 10,000 tons a year. Any operation below the threshold is also legal. The threshold should apply to the operator and its affiliates in the aggregate, or be removed in favor of a flat ban on commercial recovery.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "by a single vessel."** The definition reaches only recovery done by a vessel. Operations using fixed platforms, moored installations, or remotely operated seabed systems controlled from shore or a non-vessel structure may fall outside it.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "metric tons" unspecified.** The bill doesn't say whether tonnage is wet (as lifted, including water and sediment) or dry. It also doesn't say whether "per year" means calendar year, fiscal year, or rolling 12 months. Each choice changes who falls under the cap, which invites disputes.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "from the seabed."** Excavating or drilling into the subsoil, as in seafloor massive sulfide mining, may not count as recovery "from the seabed." The standard formulation is "seabed and subsoil."

5. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "at depths greater than 200 meters."** It's unclear whether this means water depth at the site, depth below the sea surface at a given tide, or depth below the seabed. The bill should specify water depth.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "other mineral resources" undefined.** Read broadly, the term could sweep in offshore oil and gas, sand and gravel, or methane hydrates, which would multiply industry opposition and invite a vagueness challenge. Read narrowly, it could exclude cobalt crusts or sulfides. The bill should define the term, for example as hard minerals including nodules, crusts, and sulfides, excluding hydrocarbons.

7. **Sec. 2(1) as a whole: only "recovery" is covered.** Seabed disturbance without recovery is not covered. That includes collector trials, sediment plume testing, and discharges, which are themselves major sources of harm.

8. **Sec. 2(1)(B), "the Secretary" undefined.** The bill never says which Secretary (Commerce, Interior, or State) has this authority. That is a basic drafting gap that invites litigation over who can act.

9. **Sec. 2(1)(B), open-ended discretion with no procedure.** "Comparable risks" has no standard and no required procedure, such as notice-and-comment rulemaking. Each determination is exposed to APA and possibly nondelegation challenges. And because the provision only expands coverage, an unsympathetic administration can simply never use it.

10. **Sec. 2(1)(B) with Sec. 5(b): criminal liability based on agency determinations.** Making conduct a felony based on a later, discretionary agency finding raises fair-notice and vagueness problems that could undermine enforcement.

11. **Sec. 2(2), "citizen."** The definition excludes U.S. nationals who aren't citizens and lawful permanent residents. It is narrower than standard U.S. person definitions.

12. **Sec. 2(2), "corporation organized under the laws of a State."** The definition omits LLCs, partnerships, and trusts. It also omits entities organized under D.C. law, territorial law (e.g., Puerto Rico, Guam), or federal law. Most importantly, it omits foreign subsidiaries owned or controlled by U.S. persons. A U.S. company could keep mining through an LLC or a foreign affiliate.

13. **Sec. 2(3), covered vessel at 5,000 gross tons and "equipped for recovery."** Vessels under 5,000 GT escape the registry entirely. An operator can also avoid registration by removing recovery equipment between voyages or by transferring equipment between vessels.

## Sec. 3: Prohibition

14. **Sec. 3(a), "any person in the United States."** It's unclear whether "United States" includes the EEZ and outer continental shelf. Without that clarity, a foreign company on a foreign-flagged vessel mining in U.S. waters may not be covered. The bill should expressly cover the EEZ, the continental shelf, and all U.S.-flagged vessels.

15. **Sec. 3(a), "engage in."** The provision doesn't clearly reach financing, sponsoring, chartering, insuring, supplying, or contracting for mining by others. The bill also lacks aiding-and-abetting, conspiracy, and attempt language.

16. **Sec. 3(a): no prohibition on federal authorizations.** Nothing amends, repeals, or bars agencies from acting under the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (NOAA licenses and permits) or OCSLA mineral leasing (Interior). Agencies could keep issuing authorizations, and companies could argue those authorizations conflict with or shield them from the ban.

17. **Sec. 3: no import, processing, or sale ban.** Foreign companies can mine and sell deep-seabed minerals into the U.S. market, which gives the industry a U.S. revenue stream. A ban on importing, purchasing, or processing commercially recovered seabed minerals would close this.

18. **Sec. 3(b), grandfather clause applies to "mining."** This is the biggest loophole. It exempts "mining," not just exploration, conducted under an exploration license, so any holder of a pre-enactment exploration license could carry out full commercial recovery with no tonnage limit.

19. **Sec. 3(b), "exploration license" undefined.** The bill doesn't say whether this means NOAA licenses under the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act only, or also International Seabed Authority contracts and foreign-government licenses. ISA arrangements are "contracts," not licenses, which creates its own ambiguity.

20. **Sec. 3(b): no limits on grandfathering.** The exemption has no expiration date. It doesn't say whether renewals, extensions, modifications, or transfers of a license keep the exemption, so an exemption meant to be transitional could become permanent and tradable.

21. **Sec. 3(c), environmental assessment exemption.** "Environmental assessment" is undefined, and the exemption turns on the operator's claimed "purpose." It has no tonnage cap, permit requirement, agency approval, or monitoring, and it doesn't bar selling recovered minerals. Commercial-scale "pilot" or "test" mining could proceed under this label.

## Sec. 4: Registry

22. **Sec. 4, registry has no enforcement.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3. Failing to register, failing to report, or filing false reports carries no penalty under the Act. The bill also sets no registration deadline.

23. **Sec. 4(b), self-certified reports.** Reports are certified only by the operator. There is no verification, required content (tonnage, coordinates, depth, dates), recordkeeping, vessel monitoring or AIS requirement, observer program, or inspection right, so compliance can't be checked.

24. **Sec. 4, scope and authority.** The registry provision doesn't say whether it reaches foreign operators or foreign-flagged vessels, or only U.S. ones. It also gives NOAA no rulemaking authority to implement the registry. And it names NOAA while Sec. 2 refers to "the Secretary," which is internally inconsistent.

## Sec. 5: Penalties

25. **Sec. 5(a) vs. Sec. 8, conflicting dates.** Civil penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the Act takes effect 180 days later. The conflict invites litigation, and as to criminal penalties, ex post facto arguments.

26. **Sec. 5: no enforcement agency or powers.** The bill doesn't name an enforcing agency. It provides no boarding, search, seizure, or subpoena authority; no Coast Guard role; no administrative procedure for assessing civil penalties; and no forfeiture of vessels, equipment, or recovered minerals.

27. **Sec. 5(a), "each day."** It's unclear whether the per-day penalty applies per vessel or per operator. For a high-value operation, a maximum of $1M per day may be a cost of doing business. Disgorgement of proceeds, or a per-ton penalty, would deter more effectively.

28. **Sec. 5(b), "willfully."** Under *Bryan v. United States*, "willfully" generally requires proof that the defendant knew the conduct was unlawful, which is a high bar. "Knowingly" is the more common standard for environmental crimes.

29. **Sec. 5: no individual or supervisory liability, no citizen suits.** The bill doesn't expressly reach corporate officers or directors who direct violations. It also has no citizen-suit provision, so enforcement depends entirely on the executive branch.

## Sec. 6: International Agreement

30. **Sec. 6, "seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months."** It's ambiguous whether the deadline applies to starting or concluding negotiations. Either way, "seek to" is unenforceable, and the bill requires no reporting to Congress.

31. **Sec. 6, "equivalent to those in section 3."** Tying the treaty goal to Sec. 3 would export the bill's loopholes, such as the tonnage threshold and the exemptions, into the international standard.

## Sec. 7: Duration

32. **Sec. 7(a) vs. 7(b), conflicting provisions.** Subsection (a) keeps the ban in place until Congress acts, but subsection (b) ends the entire Act after 10 years regardless. The sunset makes (a) meaningless and creates an automatic expiration date the industry can simply wait out.

33. **Sec. 7(a), mechanism unclear.** It doesn't say whether a joint resolution "finding" safety automatically lifts the ban. "Serious harm" is undefined. Nothing directs or funds the National Academies report the provision depends on.

34. **Sec. 7(b): no savings clause.** When the Act expires, pending prosecutions and penalty actions for earlier violations could fall with it. Whether the general savings statute (1 U.S.C. 109) applies to expiration of a temporary statute is unsettled, so an express savings clause is needed.

## Sec. 8: Effective Date

35. **Sec. 8, 180-day delay.** The delay opens a window after enactment for companies to mine, stockpile ore, and set up corporate structures (LLCs, foreign affiliates) to avoid the ban.

## Cross-cutting

36. **Missing definitions.** "Person," "seabed," "mineral resources," "operator," "recovery," "exploration license," "environmental assessment," "serious harm," and "Secretary" are all undefined.

37. **No severability clause.** Given the delegation and vagueness risks in Sec. 2(1)(B), a successful challenge to one provision could be argued to take down the whole Act.

38. **No regulatory authority.** No agency is directed or authorized to issue implementing regulations, which leaves enforcement and the registry without a legal framework.
