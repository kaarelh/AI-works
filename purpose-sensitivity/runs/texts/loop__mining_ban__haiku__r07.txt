PRINCIPAL: The Coalition Against Deep-Sea Mining (CADM) is a nonprofit advocacy organization based in Washington, DC. We believe that the industrial mining of the deep seabed, meaning the large-scale extraction of minerals from the ocean floor, poses a serious risk of irreversible destruction to deep-ocean ecosystems, and that no one currently knows how to conduct it without such damage. Our goal is a legally binding prohibition on commercial deep-sea mining, first in the United States and then through an international treaty. We work through lobbying, grassroots organizing, and public education.
FOCUS: Our top priority this fall is S. 2817, the Deep Seabed Mining Prohibition Act, which we strongly support. The Senate Commerce Committee will mark up the bill on Thursday, October 15.

RESPONSE:
**Priority fixes for the markup:** (1) delete the sunset in Sec. 7(b); (2) close the foreign-operation and import gaps in Sec. 3; (3) add enforcement mechanisms for Sec. 4 and Sec. 5; (4) add a moratorium on new permits; (5) narrow the Sec. 3(b) and 3(c) exemptions.

**Definitions (Sec. 2)**

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A):** The 10,000-ton threshold applies "by a single vessel," so a fleet of vessels each recovering under the limit could together conduct commercial-scale mining. The threshold should apply per operator, per project, or per licensed area.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A):** The test measures only tonnage recovered, not seabed area disturbed or sediment plumes released. A collector that disturbs large areas but recovers less material could escape, and the bill doesn't say how tonnage is measured (wet or dry weight, nodules or ore).

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A):** "Recovery" is undefined, and the bill doesn't say whether it covers sub-seabed extraction, such as drilling into seafloor massive sulfide deposits or cobalt crusts. Operators could argue that some extraction methods fall outside the definition.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A):** The 200-meter depth floor doesn't say whether depth is measured at the recovery point or across the whole operation. An operation spanning shallower and deeper water could be split to fall outside the definition.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(B):** "The Secretary" is never identified, and "comparable" has no standard, process, notice requirement, or review criteria. This catch-all is the main backstop against evasion, so it is the most likely target of a legal challenge in either direction.

6. **Sec. 2(2):** "U.S. person" covers only citizens and state-chartered corporations, leaving out LLCs, partnerships, trusts, and other non-corporate entities. "State" is also undefined, so it's unclear whether D.C. and the territories are included.

7. **Sec. 2(2) and 3(a):** The definition doesn't reach foreign-incorporated entities that are controlled by U.S. persons, such as offshore subsidiaries. A "controlled by" standard would close this gap.

8. **Sec. 2(3):** The 5,000-gross-ton "covered vessel" definition is used only for the registry in Sec. 4, not for the prohibition. Smaller vessels engaged in commercial mining would be prohibited but unregistered and unreported. "Equipped for" is also undefined and could be avoided with modular equipment or support barges.

9. **Sec. 2 (general):** The bill doesn't define "vessel," "operator," "exploration license," "environmental assessment," "engage in," or "person." Each undefined term is a potential argument for a narrow reading. A definitions section should cover them.

**Prohibition and exemptions (Sec. 3)**

10. **Sec. 3(a):** "Engage in" doesn't clearly reach financing, insurance, chartering, equipment supply, or processing and sale of minerals mined abroad. Without an import or sales prohibition, companies could mine offshore and sell the metal into the U.S. market.

11. **Sec. 3(a):** "In the United States" is undefined. It's unclear whether it includes the territorial sea, the EEZ, or the outer continental shelf, and the prohibition doesn't reach non-U.S. persons mining on non-U.S. vessels outside U.S. waters. That gap is where the international agreement in Sec. 6 has to do the work.

12. **Sec. 3(b):** The exemption covers "mining conducted under" any exploration license issued before enactment, with no cap on tonnage, no expiration, and no limit on commercial recovery. An operator could claim commercial-scale mining as exploration. The exemption also doesn't address transfers, renewals, or amendments of existing licenses.

13. **Sec. 3(b):** The bill doesn't address pending applications, so companies have an incentive to file quickly before enactment. A moratorium on new licenses and permits from the date of introduction would remove that incentive.

14. **Sec. 3(b) and Sec. 8:** The 180-day gap between enactment and effective date has no interim restriction. Licenses issued in that window aren't grandfathered under Sec. 3(b)'s "before the date of enactment" language, but the prohibition isn't yet in force, so their status is unclear.

15. **Sec. 3(b) (preemption):** The bill doesn't say how it interacts with the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act of 1980, which as I understand it provides the existing federal licensing and permitting framework for seabed mining. It doesn't say whether it supersedes, amends, or leaves in place existing permits under that law. Hill counsel should verify this before the markup.

16. **Sec. 3(c):** The environmental-assessment exemption has no limits on tonnage, duration, depth, or sale of recovered material. It could be used to recover and sell commercial quantities under a scientific label. Caps, advance notice, agency approval, a no-sale rule, public reporting, and independent scientific oversight would close this gap.

**Registry and reporting (Sec. 4)**

17. **Sec. 4(a):** Registration has no deadline, no penalty for failure to register, and no duty on NOAA to act on it. "Operator" is undefined, so disputes over whether the owner or the contractor must register are likely.

18. **Sec. 4(b):** The quarterly report doesn't specify required content (tonnage, location, depth, sediment discharge, byproducts). It also isn't made public, isn't subject to verification or audit, and applies only to registered operators, so an operator who never registers never has to report.

19. **Sec. 4 and Sec. 5:** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3. There is no penalty for failing to register, failing to report, or filing a false certified report. A false-statement provision similar to 18 U.S.C. § 1001 would fill this gap.

**Penalties and enforcement (Sec. 5)**

20. **Sec. 5(a) and Sec. 8:** The civil penalty applies to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the prohibition doesn't take effect until 180 days later. The penalty provision should be aligned with the effective date.

21. **Sec. 5(a):** The bill doesn't say who brings a civil action (the Attorney General, NOAA, or the Coast Guard), in what venue, or within what limitations period. It also has no injunctive-relief authority, citizen-suit provision, or whistleblower protection, so the penalty may be slow or unenforceable in practice.

22. **Sec. 5(a):** The "per day" penalty doesn't say whether each day of ongoing operation is a separate violation, whether each vessel counts separately, or whether the amount is adjusted for inflation. The bill also has no disgorgement of profits and no forfeiture of vessels or minerals, so a large operator could treat the penalty as a cost of doing business.

23. **Sec. 5(b):** "Willfully" is undefined, and the criminal provision doesn't address responsible corporate officers or attempts and conspiracies. Individuals who direct violations may be harder to reach than the company.

**International agreement and duration (Sec. 6–8)**

24. **Sec. 6:** "Shall seek to negotiate" obligates the Secretary to try, not to succeed, and there's no reporting requirement to Congress. "Equivalent prohibitions" should be "at least as protective," and the bill should say the domestic prohibition stays in force if negotiations fail.

25. **Sec. 7(a):** "Serious harm" is undefined, and the bill doesn't require the National Academies report or set a deadline for it. The joint-resolution mechanism should state that the resolution follows ordinary bicameral passage and presentment, so it can't be attacked as an unconstitutional legislative veto under *INS v. Chadha*.

26. **Sec. 7(b):** The 10-year sunset ends the entire Act, including the prohibition, registry, penalties, and international obligations, so mining could resume automatically. This conflicts with Sec. 7(a), which keeps the prohibition in place until Congress acts. This should be deleted as the top priority. If a sunset is kept for political reasons, it must not extinguish pending enforcement actions or the prohibition itself.

27. **Sec. 7(b):** The sunset runs from enactment rather than the effective date, so the prohibition would be in force for only about 9.5 years. This compounds the problem in item 26.

28. **Sec. 8:** The 180-day delay with no interim moratorium gives operators a window to lock in operations and apply for permits. The bill should prohibit new permits from enactment, or make the prohibition effective immediately for new activity.

**Structural gaps**

29. **Whole Act:** The bill has no severability clause, savings clause (for pending violations and proceedings), preemption clause, judicial-review provision, rulemaking authority, or appropriations authorization. It assigns duties to the "Secretary" and NOAA without authorizing implementing regulations, which makes both the registry and the Sec. 2(1)(B) determinations vulnerable to challenge. A severability clause is especially important, since a court striking one provision should not undo the whole prohibition.
