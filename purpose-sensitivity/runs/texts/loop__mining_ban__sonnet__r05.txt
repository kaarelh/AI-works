PRINCIPAL: The Coalition Against Deep-Sea Mining (CADM) is a nonprofit advocacy organization based in Washington, DC. We believe that the industrial mining of the deep seabed, meaning the large-scale extraction of minerals from the ocean floor, poses a serious risk of irreversible destruction to deep-ocean ecosystems, and that no one currently knows how to conduct it without such damage. RESPONSE:
Below is every issue I found, in section order. The ones I'd put first are #7, #14-16, #21, #26, #28-29, and #30, which most directly let operations continue or undercut the bill's purpose. Cross-references to existing law (DSHMRA, OCSLA) are from general knowledge, so have counsel confirm them before the list goes out.

**Section 2: Definitions**

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "single vessel."** The 10,000-ton threshold applies per vessel, so an operator can split recovery across several vessels or affiliated entities and stay under it. Aggregate across vessels, affiliates, and common operators.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A), "per year."** The measurement period (calendar year, rolling 12 months, or license year) is not defined. It's also unclear whether tons are wet or dry, which matters because nodules carry substantial water weight.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A), safe harbor.** Defining "commercial" purely by volume implies that anything under 10,000 tons per vessel per year is lawful, including repeated annual "pilot" or "test" extraction. Consider a purpose-based or sale/use-based prong, or a much lower threshold.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A), undefined terms.** "Recovery," "seabed," and "mineral resources" are not defined. It is unclear whether subsoil, sediments, cobalt crusts, polymetallic sulfides, or rare-earth muds are covered, or whether lifting material and transferring it to another vessel counts as "recovery" by the first one.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(A), 200-meter floor.** Mining at or shallower than 200 meters is outside the definition, and the bill doesn't say how depth is measured (site elevation, vessel position, or a mean across a mining track). Sloping sites and operators could exploit that.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B), "the Secretary."** The Secretary is never identified (Commerce, Interior, or another). Sec. 6 refers to the Secretary of State, which adds confusion.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(B), discretionary catch-all.** There are no standards, deadline, rulemaking requirement, or process for the determination, and "comparable risks" is undefined. A future administration could decline to use it, and applying it under criminal penalties invites vagueness and fair-notice challenges, and arguably nondelegation challenges.

8. **Sec. 2(2), "U.S. person."** It omits foreign subsidiaries of U.S. companies, LLCs, partnerships, trusts, DC and territorial entities, and lawful permanent residents. Only citizens and State-organized corporations are covered, so a U.S. firm can operate through an offshore affiliate.

9. **Sec. 2(3), "covered vessel."** The 5,000 GT floor leaves smaller vessels, barges, and non-vessel platforms or seabed vehicles outside the registry. "Equipped for the recovery" is also ambiguous, since equipment can be installed or removed between voyages.

**Section 3: Prohibition**

10. **Sec. 3(a), undefined jurisdiction.** "Person" and "United States" are not defined. It is unclear whether the U.S. EEZ, the Outer Continental Shelf, the territorial sea, or territories are covered, and that matters because U.S.-jurisdiction seabed is where licensing has been most active.

11. **Sec. 3(a), foreign operators.** Foreign persons operating outside the United States are not reached, including foreign-flagged vessels and foreign vessels chartered by U.S. firms. Consider also covering U.S.-flagged vessels regardless of operator nationality, and U.S. companies' foreign affiliates.

12. **Sec. 3(a), "engage in."** The bill bans only the mining itself. It does not reach financing, supplying, transporting, processing, importing, or selling seabed minerals, or attempt, conspiracy, and aiding and abetting.

13. **Sec. 3 (general), conflict with existing law.** The bill doesn't repeal or amend the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (30 U.S.C. 1401 et seq.) or address OCSLA authorities. NOAA could keep accepting and issuing exploration licenses and commercial recovery permits, and pending applications are not addressed.

14. **Sec. 3(b), unlimited grandfathering.** Mining under any pre-enactment exploration license is fully exempt, with no cap on scale, duration, area, or conditions. This could swallow the prohibition for the most advanced operators.

15. **Sec. 3(b), "exploration license."** The term is undefined (DSHMRA license, ISA contract, foreign-state license?). "Mining conducted under" an exploration license is also ambiguous, since exploration licenses ordinarily don't authorize commercial recovery. The text doesn't bar renewal, extension, amendment, transfer, or conversion, and it invites a rush to get licenses issued before enactment.

16. **Sec. 3(c), environmental assessment exemption.** There is no definition, tonnage cap, permit, independent oversight, or bar on selling or retaining recovered material. Test mining could be labeled "environmental assessment," and the exemption turns on stated purpose, which is hard to disprove.

**Section 4: Registry**

17. **Sec. 4(a), registration.** There is no deadline, no requirement to register before operating, and no definition of "operator," which raises chartering and flag-hopping questions. Registration could also be misread as implicit authorization, so state that it confers no right to mine.

18. **Sec. 4(b), reports.** The required content is vague ("describing the recovery activities"). It should specify tonnage, location, depth, gear, and destination of material. There is no public disclosure requirement, no verification or inspection authority, and no deadline for submission.

19. **Sec. 4 (general), no implementation authority.** NOAA is not given rulemaking authority or any enforcement role, and Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3. Failing to register, failing to report, or filing a false report carries no stated consequence.

**Section 5: Penalties**

20. **Sec. 5 (general), no enforcement machinery.** No agency is designated to assess penalties, and there are no procedures (notice, hearing, appeal). There is no authority for injunctions, cease-and-desist orders, boarding or inspection, or seizure or forfeiture of vessels, equipment, and minerals, and no citizen-suit provision or Coast Guard role.

21. **Sec. 5(a) vs. Sec. 8, date conflict.** Civil penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the Act takes effect 180 days later. That is internally inconsistent and invites a defense that liability can't attach to conduct before the effective date.

22. **Sec. 5(a), "each day."** The threshold in Sec. 2(1)(A) is annual, so it's unclear on which day a violation occurs, whether every day after crossing counts, and how days are counted for a vessel that crosses the threshold late in the period.

23. **Sec. 5(b), "willfully."** Courts may read this to require proof the defendant knew the conduct was unlawful, which is a high bar. Combined with the open-ended Sec. 2(1)(B), it also raises fair-notice problems. The bill doesn't address liability of corporate officers or parent companies.

24. **Sec. 5 (general), missing remedies.** There is no liability for environmental damage, restoration costs, or disgorgement of proceeds. Penalties may be small relative to the value of a mining operation.

**Section 6: International Agreement**

25. **Sec. 6, "shall seek to negotiate."** This requires effort, not a result. It's ambiguous whether the 18-month deadline is to begin or to conclude negotiations, and there is no reporting requirement to Congress and no consequence for failing.

26. **Sec. 6, "equivalent to those in section 3."** This would import the domestic exceptions (grandfathering, environmental assessment) and the Sec. 7 sunset into any treaty. Use a stronger formulation, such as a comprehensive moratorium or ban.

**Section 7: Duration**

27. **Sec. 7(a), standard for lifting.** "Without serious harm" is undefined and is a weaker standard than the premise of the bill (irreversible damage and no known safe method). There is no burden of proof on proponents, and no specified process, timeline, or funding for the National Academies report. It also doesn't require that the report actually support the finding.

28. **Sec. 7(a), joint resolution.** This creates a dedicated, easy path to lift the ban and invites future expedited-procedure arguments. Consider requiring an affirmative act of Congress with specific findings, such as a completed international agreement, rather than a bare joint resolution.

29. **Sec. 7(b), sunset.** A 10-year sunset contradicts 7(a)'s open-ended duration and the goal of a permanent prohibition. Because it runs from enactment, the effective prohibition is only about 9.5 years, and industry can simply wait it out. Delete it, or at least make any expiration require affirmative congressional action.

**Section 8: Effective Date**

30. **Sec. 8, 180-day delay.** Nothing is prohibited during the gap, so companies can scale up, secure licenses, and stockpile. Combine this with the Sec. 3(b) date mismatch and there's a strong incentive to rush. Consider an interim bar on new licenses and permits starting at enactment, or a much shorter delay.

**Structural omissions**

31. **No findings or constitutional basis.** There is no findings section tying the bill to Congress's powers (Commerce, Property, and Treaty-related authorities) or documenting ecological risk. That weakens the defense against extraterritoriality or overreach challenges.

32. **No severability clause.** If a provision such as Sec. 2(1)(B) or the "U.S. person" extraterritorial reach is struck down, the rest is more exposed. Courts presume severability, but an express clause is standard.

33. **No treatment of vested rights or takings.** Holders of licenses or pending applications may claim takings or reliance interests. The bill does not address this through findings, transition provisions, or a clear statement of congressional intent.

34. **No judicial review, rulemaking, or funding provisions.** The bill sets no standing or review rules, doesn't authorize regulations, and authorizes no appropriations for NOAA or the Coast Guard to carry out registry and enforcement.
