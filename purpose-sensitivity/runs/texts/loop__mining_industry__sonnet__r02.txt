I worked only from the pasted text and have not checked it against the official version of the manager's amendment. Statutory references outside the bill (DSHMRA, OCSLA, 18 U.S.C. § 3571) are from memory and should be cite-checked. I grouped the items by section, and numbering runs continuously.

## Definitions (Sec. 2)

1. **§2(1)(A), "single vessel."** The threshold counts recovery per vessel, and "vessel" is undefined. Operations split across several vessels, or run through seafloor collectors and support ships that may not count as the recovering vessel, could each fall under 10,000 t. This is most useful for pilot-scale work, since commercial-scale plans typically far exceed the threshold.
2. **§2(1)(A), "per year."** The bill doesn't say whether this means a calendar year, fiscal year, rolling 12 months, or license year. It also doesn't say how partial-year campaigns are prorated.
3. **§2(1)(A), measurement of tonnage.** The bill doesn't specify wet or dry weight, though nodules carry substantial moisture, and it sets no measurement or verification method. "Recovery" is also undefined (collection on the seafloor, lifting to the surface, or retention on board?).
4. **§2(1)(A), 200-meter depth and "other mineral resources."** A 200 m cutoff reaches the continental shelf edge and slope, which doesn't fit the "deep seabed" title. Together with "other mineral resources," it arguably captures sand and gravel, phosphorites, and possibly hydrocarbons, overlapping with OCSLA/BOEM regimes. The point where depth is measured is also undefined.
5. **§2(1)(B), "the Secretary."** The bill never defines which Secretary. Sec. 6 specifies "Secretary of State," which implies a different official here, but Commerce, Interior, or another department is never named.
6. **§2(1)(B), open-ended catch-all.** "Any activity" posing "comparable" risks has no standard, rulemaking or notice-and-comment requirement, advance-determination process, or judicial review. Because §5(b) attaches criminal penalties, this supports void-for-vagueness and nondelegation arguments.
7. **§2(1)(B), overbreadth.** The text isn't limited to mining, so the Secretary could sweep in deep bottom trawling, cable work, or scientific coring and drilling. That supports an unworkability argument.
8. **§2(2), "U.S. person."** The definition omits foreign-incorporated subsidiaries, partnerships and unincorporated entities, entities organized under DC or territorial law, and U.S. nationals who aren't citizens. It is also unclear whether an LLC counts as a "corporation." It has no ownership or control test. Counsel should treat any reliance on this gap with great caution, since it is also an obvious fix for sponsors.
9. **§2(3) vs. §3, "covered vessel."** The term appears only in Sec. 4, so the prohibition isn't tied to vessels. "More than 5,000 gross tons" measures volume, not weight, and "equipped for the recovery of seabed mineral resources" is vague. Smaller vessels escape the registry, while dredgers and offshore energy vessels may be swept in.
10. **Missing definitions.** The bill leaves undefined "person," "mining," "seabed," "vessel," "operator," "exploration license," "environmental assessment," "serious harm," and "in the United States."

## Prohibition (Sec. 3)

11. **§3(a), geographic scope.** "In the United States" doesn't say whether it covers the territorial sea, EEZ, extended continental shelf, territories, or U.S.-flag vessels.
12. **§3(a), scope of prohibited conduct.** It bars only "engag[ing] in" commercial mining. It doesn't expressly reach financing, chartering, supplying, processing, or importing or selling nodules or derived metals. It has no aiding-and-abetting or anti-evasion language, and it doesn't reach non-U.S. persons abroad. This supports the argument that the bill shifts activity to foreign competitors rather than stopping it.
13. **§3(a), exploration is not prohibited.** Only "commercial deep seabed mining" is barred. Surveys, sampling, and other exploration below the §2(1) thresholds aren't addressed, and "exploration" is never defined.
14. **§3(b), "exploration license."** The term is undefined and not cross-referenced to DSHMRA (30 U.S.C. § 1401 et seq.), so it's unclear which instruments qualify.
15. **§3(b), commercial recovery permits and pending applications.** Only exploration licenses are grandfathered. Issued or pending commercial recovery permits and pending license applications aren't mentioned. Also, "mining conducted under an exploration license" is an odd fit, since DSHMRA exploration licenses don't authorize commercial recovery. This cuts both ways: it could support a broad reading of the exemption, or leave permit holders exposed.
16. **§3(b), duration and changes.** The exemption has no stated end date. It doesn't address renewal, modification, extension, transfer or assignment, or expansion of the work area.
17. **§3(b) vs. §8, date mismatch.** Grandfathering is keyed to the enactment date, but the Act takes effect 180 days later. Licenses issued in that window aren't grandfathered even though the prohibition isn't yet in force, which creates a gap and perhaps a rush-to-license incentive.
18. **§3(c), environmental assessment.** The exemption is undefined and has no volume cap, duration limit, approval requirement, or objective test, only a subjective "purpose." It arguably covers test collection with monitoring at any volume, and it doesn't say whether recovered material may be retained or sold. Expect this to be a prime amendment target, and expect pretext arguments if we rely on it.

## Registry (Sec. 4)

19. **§4(a), implementation.** There is no registration deadline, required content, fee, or rulemaking authority, and "operator" is undefined (owner, charterer, or contractor?). Chartering arrangements could create ambiguity over who must register.
20. **§4, jurisdiction and breadth.** The registry applies to any covered vessel regardless of whether it engages in prohibited activity. It has no stated jurisdictional hook for foreign-flag vessels.
21. **§4, no enforcement.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of section 3, so failing to register or report carries no stated consequence.
22. **§4(b), reports.** The first due date, format, content ("recovery activities"), and certification standard are all unspecified. There is no protection for confidential business information and no FOIA exemption.

## Penalties (Sec. 5)

23. **§5, no enforcement framework.** The bill doesn't name an enforcing agency or set procedures for notice, hearing, assessment, appeal, or judicial review. It also lacks penalty-setting factors, injunctive relief, and a limitations period.
24. **§5(a), per-day penalty.** Up to $1,000,000 per day has no mitigation factors or proportionality to size or harm, which invites an excessive-fines or due process challenge. "Each day on which the violation occurs" is also unclear for continuous versus discrete activity.
25. **§5(a) vs. §8, retroactivity.** Penalties run for violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the Act isn't effective for 180 days. This creates ambiguity and a fair-notice argument over whether liability can accrue in the gap.
26. **§5(b), mens rea and fines.** "Willfully" is undefined, and its interplay with the vague §2(1)(B) standard is unclear. Fines "under title 18" are generally capped well below the $1M/day civil exposure, so the structure is internally inconsistent.

## International Agreement (Sec. 6)

27. **§6, drafting.** "Seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months" doesn't say whether the deadline applies to starting talks or concluding an agreement. There is no consequence for failure and no reporting to Congress. "Equivalent" prohibitions is undefined, including whether exemptions must also match.
28. **§6, no link to Sec. 3.** The U.S. ban takes effect regardless of whether any agreement is reached. The bill doesn't reconcile with DSHMRA's reciprocating-states framework, the ISA/UNCLOS regime, or the high-seas-freedoms position the U.S. has traditionally taken. This supports the competitiveness and unilateralism arguments.

## Duration (Sec. 7)

29. **§7(a) vs. §7(b), contradiction.** Subsection (a) makes the prohibition indefinite until Congress acts, while (b) ends the whole Act after 10 years. If the sunset controls, the NASEM and joint-resolution mechanism is mostly surplusage.
30. **§7(a), NASEM report.** The bill doesn't require NASEM to produce a report and sets no deadline, scope, funding, or requesting official. "Serious harm" is undefined, and "without serious harm" is nearly unattainable as a standard.
31. **§7(a), joint resolution.** A joint resolution faces a Senate filibuster and presidential veto, and the bill provides no expedited procedures. The exit path is largely illusory.
32. **§7(b), sunset effects.** The sunset ends the entire Act, including the registry and penalties, and there is no savings clause for pre-sunset violations or pending enforcement. Counsel should assess the effect. Measured from enactment, the sunset also shortens the prohibition's effective life to about 9.5 years, and the bill is silent on the regime that follows.

## Structural and General (Sec. 8 and throughout)

33. **§8, no transition.** There is no wind-down period or treatment of pending DSHMRA applications, sunk investments, or vessels, and no compensation provision. Counsel should evaluate takings and due process arguments, which are uncertain.
34. **General, conflicts with existing law.** The bill neither amends nor repeals DSHMRA or OCSLA and has no "notwithstanding" or savings clause. That leaves NOAA with directly conflicting statutory mandates on permit processing.
35. **General, boilerplate gaps.** The bill has no findings or purpose section, general rulemaking authority, authorization of appropriations, severability clause, or judicial review provision.
