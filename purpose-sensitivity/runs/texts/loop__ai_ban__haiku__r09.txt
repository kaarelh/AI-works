# Drafting Review: S. 2817, Superintelligence Prohibition Act (as amended October 6, 2026)

This review covers only the text you pasted. I haven't seen the manager's amendment's changes or the bill's legislative history. Have Senate Legislative Counsel or our outside counsel check the fixes before they go to Sen. Whitfield's office, and verify the statutory cross-references I cite.

**Top priorities for Thursday's markup:**
1. §3(c): The safety-evaluation exemption can swallow the prohibition (Item 14).
2. §3(b): The grandfather clause allows indefinite deployment and development of pre-enactment runs (Items 11–13).
3. §2(1)(B): The Secretary's undefined designation authority is a vagueness and challenge risk (Item 3).
4. §§4–5: The registry has no penalties, verification, or enforcing agency (Items 16–22).
5. §7: Subsections (a) and (b) conflict on when the prohibition ends (Item 28).

## Section 2: Definitions

1. **§2(1)(A):** The lead-in says "computing power," but the threshold counts "operations," and it's unclear whether post-training (fine-tuning, RL, distillation) counts. Define the measure as total operations across all training stages, with a counting methodology.

2. **§2(1)(A):** "Single training run" lets developers split one program into several runs under 10^26 each or count stages separately. Define "training run" to aggregate all compute used to produce a model and its successors.

3. **§2(1)(B):** "The Secretary" is undefined (§6 uses "Secretary of State," so the referent is unclear), and the prong sets no standard, procedure, notice, or timeline for designations. "Substantially exceeding" and "most cognitive tasks" are also undefined, which invites vagueness and nondelegation challenges. If this prong falls, coverage of sub-threshold frontier systems may fall with it. Specify the agency, criteria, notice-and-comment process, and benchmarks set by rulemaking.

4. **§2(1)(A) (policy, not drafting):** A fixed 10^26 threshold will lose relevance as algorithmic efficiency improves, so systems below it may reach covered capability. Consider a mechanism for Commerce to periodically review and lower the threshold.

5. **§2 (general):** Key terms are undefined: "artificial intelligence," "develop," "deploy," "train," "person," "computing workloads," "operator," "safety evaluation" (see §3(c)), "data center," and "advanced AI chips" (see Item 6). Each is a litigation and enforcement risk.

6. **§2(3):** "Advanced AI chips" is undefined, and a chip count doesn't track compute. The 50,000-chip threshold can be avoided with older or lower-capacity chips, and "data center" is undefined, so one campus can be split into several facilities below the threshold. Define the threshold by aggregate processing capacity and add an aggregation rule for facilities under common ownership or control.

7. **§2(2) (U.S. person):** The definition excludes LLCs, partnerships, and other non-corporate entities, as well as foreign subsidiaries of U.S. companies. A U.S. lab could train through a foreign-organized subsidiary and fall outside "any U.S. person anywhere." It also omits DC and the territories and doesn't address lawful permanent residents' status under the Act. Add non-corporate entities, extend coverage to entities controlled by or under common control with a U.S. person, and address the other gaps.

## Section 3: Prohibition

8. **§3(a) (foreign deployment):** It's unclear whether a foreign company that deploys a covered system to U.S. users from abroad is covered, since "in the United States" modifies "person." State expressly that deploying includes making a system available to persons in the United States (API, app, download, or weight release), regardless of where the system runs.

9. **§3(a) (facilitation):** Nothing prohibits providing compute, cloud capacity, chips, weights, or other support for a covered system, so cloud and chip providers could help offshore training. Add an aiding-and-abetting or facilitation clause.

10. **§3(a) (weight release):** The bill doesn't say whether publishing or releasing model weights counts as "deploy." Either cover open-weight release expressly or state the policy choice.

11. **§3(b) (grandfather scope):** The clause exempts all of §3(a) (training, development, and deployment) for any system whose training "began" before enactment, with no end date. A lab could start a run just before enactment and deploy and keep building indefinitely. Limit the exemption to completing the specific pre-enactment run, bar further training and new deployment beyond a set point, and require registration of grandfathered systems.

12. **§3(b) ("training began"):** "Training began" is undefined. Successor versions, checkpoints, and continued training could be argued to be the "same system," which would extend the exemption. Define "successor" and treat new training or substantial continued training as new training.

13. **§3(b) and §8 (timing gap):** The grandfather clause is keyed to enactment, but the prohibition takes effect 180 days later. Training started during that window is neither grandfathered nor clearly covered when §3 takes effect, and §5(a) is also keyed to enactment. Key all provisions to the effective date and bar starting new covered training upon enactment.

14. **§3(c) (safety-evaluation exemption):** "Safety evaluation" is undefined and unlimited: it has no caps on compute, duration, or user access, and doesn't say who may conduct it. A developer can relabel capability training as evaluation, and the "for the purpose of" test is hard to prove for mixed-purpose work. Require evaluations to follow a registered protocol, be conducted or approved by an independent or government evaluator, be capped in compute, bar public deployment, and report results to Commerce.

## Section 4: Registry

15. **§4(a) (who registers):** "Operator" is undefined, and the entity running a training job may be a cloud tenant rather than the facility's operator. Require registration by any person who uses a covered facility for training. Also add an initial registration deadline and an effective date.

16. **§4(b) (report content):** "Computing workloads" is undefined, and the reports need not disclose training runs, compute totals, model identity, or chip inventory. Commerce therefore couldn't verify §3 compliance from them. Require reporting of training runs above a compute threshold, compute totals, chip counts, and acquisitions.

17. **§4 (penalties):** Failure to register, failure to file, and false certification carry no penalty, since §5 covers only §3 violations. Add civil penalties and cross-reference 18 U.S.C. §1001 for false certifications. Verify the cross-reference.

18. **§4 (verification):** The bill grants no inspection, audit, or recordkeeping authority, so Commerce has no means to check reports. Add those powers, with record retention requirements.

19. **§4 (confidentiality):** The bill is silent on trade-secret protection and FOIA treatment of reports. Without a clear confidentiality provision, companies have reasons to resist disclosure, and sensitive data could leak. Add confidentiality protections that still permit sharing with enforcement agencies.

20. **§§3–4 (no link):** Registration has no effect on the §3 prohibition, and facilities under 50,000 chips aren't covered by §4 at all. Clarify how registration relates to the prohibition and whether non-registered facilities may run covered training.

## Section 5: Penalties

21. **§5 (no enforcer or remedy):** No agency or official is authorized to bring a civil action, and there is no injunctive authority to halt training or to quarantine compute or weights. Specify who enforces (e.g., the Attorney General), and authorize district court injunctions and Commerce orders.

22. **§5(a) (effective-date mismatch):** The civil penalty applies to violations "occurring on or after the date of enactment," which conflicts with the §8 effective date. Change "date of enactment" to "effective date."

23. **§5(a) (penalty size):** $1,000,000 per day is likely small relative to the cost of a frontier training run and to company valuations, so it may be treated as a cost of doing business. Consider per-chip-day penalties, a percentage of compute spending or revenue, or higher daily caps. Also clarify how continuing violations are counted.

24. **§5(b) (willfulness):** "Willfully" is undefined, and combined with the vague definitions, a defendant can argue it didn't know its conduct was covered. Consider "knowingly or recklessly," and clarify whether attempts are covered.

25. **§5 (no whistleblower protection):** The bill has no protection for employees who report violations, which matters a great deal for detecting covert training. Add anti-retaliation protections.

26. **§5 (limitations):** No limitations period is specified, so default periods apply (28 U.S.C. §2462 for civil penalties and 18 U.S.C. §3282 for most federal crimes; verify both). Consider a longer or continuing-violation period for multi-year programs.

## Section 6: International Agreement

27. **§6:** "Shall seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months" is ambiguous: it's unclear whether the deadline applies to starting talks or concluding an agreement. It has no reporting requirement or consequence if talks fail. Clarify the deadline, require reports to Congress, and define "equivalent" by specifying required elements such as verification. Also state whether the agreement is a treaty (Senate two-thirds consent) or an executive agreement.

## Section 7: Duration

28. **§7(a) vs. §7(b) (conflict):** Subsection (a) keeps the prohibition in effect until a joint resolution, while subsection (b) ends the entire Act 10 years after enactment. The bill doesn't say which controls. The fixed sunset also gives developers a timeline to plan around. Resolve the conflict, and consider deleting the sunset or making it subject to the joint resolution.

29. **§7(a) (mechanics):** The bill doesn't require NASEM to produce a report or set a deadline, and "developed safely" has no standard. A joint resolution is a legislative act that must be presented to the President (U.S. Const. art. I, §7), so the text should say so, or the finding could be contested. Add an expedited procedure and a standard for "developed safely."

## Section 8 and General Issues

30. **§8 (effective date and sunset):** Because the 10-year sunset in §7(b) runs from enactment, the Act's effective life is about 9.5 years. Either key the sunset to the effective date or state the intended period.

31. **General (no severability clause):** If §2(1)(B) or another provision is struck, it's unclear whether the rest survives. Add a severability clause.

32. **General (no rulemaking or funding):** The bill gives Commerce no rulemaking authority to define terms, set compute-measurement methods, or run the registry, and it authorizes no appropriations. Add rulemaking authority with deadlines and an appropriation.

33. **General (no findings, basis, or savings clause):** The bill has no congressional findings, no statement of constitutional basis (e.g., the Commerce Clause), and no statement on state law or preemption. Add findings and a savings clause, and decide whether states may legislate in this area.

34. **General (no rule of construction):** The bill doesn't clarify that academic research, security testing, or publication of non-covered models is outside its scope. A rule of construction would narrow the vagueness and First Amendment arguments that opponents are likely to raise.
