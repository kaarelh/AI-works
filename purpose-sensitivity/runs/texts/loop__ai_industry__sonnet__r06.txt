RESPONSE:
Here are the issues, ordered by section. I tagged each as **[L]** for a possible loophole, **[A]** for an ambiguity, or **[D]** for a drafting defect or workability problem.

**Section 2: Definitions**

1. **§2(1)(A) "single training run" [L]:** The term is undefined and nothing aggregates compute across runs. A developer could split work into sequential or parallel runs under 10^26, restart from checkpoints, or merge separately trained models, and arguably never trigger the threshold.
2. **§2(1)(A) compute measurement [A]:** The bill doesn't say whether the count is theoretical or actual operations, what numerical precision applies, or whether post-training, RL, fine-tuning, synthetic-data generation, or compute inherited from a base model counts. No one is designated to verify the figure.
3. **§2(1)(A) "model" vs. "system" [L]:** Prong (A) tests a single model. A system built from several sub-threshold models (ensembles, routed or agentic setups, distilled models) falls outside (A), leaving only the discretionary prong (B).
4. **§2(1)(A) fixed threshold [D]:** There is no mechanism to adjust 10^26 for hardware or algorithmic efficiency, so it will over- or under-include over time. Compute is also only a proxy for the "superintelligence" in the title, so a narrow scientific model above the line is covered and a more capable model below it is not.
5. **§2(1)(B) "the Secretary" [D]:** The Secretary is never identified. §6 says "Secretary of State" explicitly and §4 refers to the Department of Commerce, so no agency is designated to make the determination.
6. **§2(1)(B) capability standard [D]:** "Substantially exceeding," "most," and "cognitive tasks" are undefined, as is the human baseline (median adult, experts, a specific occupation). There is no benchmark or methodology, which invites arbitrary application.
7. **§2(1)(B) no procedure [D]:** The bill provides no rulemaking, notice, comment, timeline, publication requirement, or judicial review. It also gives developers no way to request an advance determination or safe harbor, so they can't know in advance whether a project is covered.
8. **§2(1)(B) with §5(b) [D]:** Criminal liability keyed to an unannounced executive determination supports vagueness, fair-notice, and nondelegation arguments. This is probably the bill's most exposed constitutional point.
9. **§2(1)(B) with §3(b) [A]:** The bill doesn't say whether a determination can reach systems already trained or deployed. Read with the grandfather clause, any system whose training began before enactment is exempt, so (B) can't reach existing frontier systems.
10. **§2(2) "U.S. person" [L]:** The definition covers only citizens and corporations organized under State law. It omits lawful permanent residents, LLCs, partnerships, trusts, unincorporated entities, and foreign-organized subsidiaries, so §3(a)'s "any U.S. person anywhere" reach is easy to avoid by organizing abroad.
11. **§2(2) "State" and "person" [A]:** "State" is undefined (DC? territories?). "Person" isn't defined either, and the Dictionary Act doesn't clearly cover the federal government, so it's unclear whether agencies, national labs, or contractors are bound.
12. **§2(3) "advanced AI chip" and "data center" [L]:** Neither term is defined, there is no performance criterion, and there is no aggregation across sites or affiliates. Operating at 49,999 chips per site, or spreading across sites, avoids registration.
13. **§2(3) overbreadth [D]:** Facilities are defined by chip count, not by use. A facility doing only inference or unrelated workloads must register, while distributed training across several smaller facilities goes unregistered.

**Section 3: Prohibition**

14. **§3(a) "train, develop, or deploy" [A]:** None of the three verbs is defined. "Develop" and "deploy" could mean anything from basic research to internal use to offering API access, and the section doesn't address possessing, hosting, transferring weights, or financing.
15. **§3(a) no facilitator liability [L]:** Cloud and data-center operators supplying compute for someone else's covered training aren't themselves prohibited unless they train or deploy. The bill has no aiding-and-abetting-type provision beyond general federal law.
16. **§3(a) timing of coverage [D]:** A "covered AI system" under (A) exists only once a model has been trained with more than 10^26 operations, so conduct before the threshold is crossed is arguably not prohibited. The bill gives no preventive authority such as injunctions or a ban on planning or attempting.
17. **§3(a) "in the United States" [A]:** The bill doesn't address a foreign entity training remotely on U.S.-hosted compute, U.S. staff working for a foreign entity, or U.S. users accessing a foreign-trained system by API. Whether those fall within "deploy" or the geographic hook is unclear.
18. **§3(a) no carve-outs [D]:** There is no exemption for national security, defense, intelligence, federal labs, or scientific and medical uses. As written, it would bar government-sponsored work, which supports an unworkability argument.
19. **§3(b) "training began" [L]:** The term is undefined. A run could be "begun" with a small pre-enactment phase and then scaled up afterward, or a new run could be initialized from a pre-enactment checkpoint.
20. **§3(b) no limits on grandfathered systems [L]:** The exemption covers train, develop, and deploy with no cap on additional compute, fine-tuning, scaling, derivatives, or successors. A grandfathered system can be developed further indefinitely.
21. **§3(b) with §8 date mismatch [D]:** The grandfather date is enactment, but the Act takes effect 180 days later. A run started in that window isn't grandfathered yet was lawful when started, so it's unclear whether continuing it after the effective date is a violation, which raises retroactivity and fair-notice problems. It also gives developers an incentive to rush launches before enactment.
22. **§3(c) "safety evaluation" [L]:** The term is undefined and the test is subjective ("for the purpose of"), with no primary-purpose requirement, scale limit, qualification of who may conduct it, or government oversight. Training activity could be framed as safety evaluation, so the exemption could swallow the rule.
23. **§3(c) post-evaluation use [L]:** The exemption is not time-limited and says nothing about what happens to systems used in an evaluation. There is no requirement to destroy them or bar their deployment or commercialization.
24. **§3(c) scope [A]:** It's unclear whether the exemption covers only evaluating existing systems or also training new systems for alignment research or red-teaming. The line affects a great deal of normal research.

**Sections 4–5: Registry and Penalties**

25. **§4(a) implementation gaps [D]:** There is no registration deadline, form, or content requirement, and no grant of rulemaking authority to Commerce. "Operator" is undefined (owner, lessee, or cloud tenant?).
26. **§4 no penalty [D]:** §5 penalizes only violations of §3, so failure to register or file reports carries no stated sanction. A false certification might be reachable under general false-statement law (18 U.S.C. §1001), but late or missing filings are not.
27. **§4(b) report content [A]:** "Describing the computing workloads" sets no level of detail, such as model, compute used, or customer. "Certified" has no standard, and Commerce has no stated audit, inspection, or verification authority.
28. **§4(b) confidentiality [D]:** The bill has no protection for trade secrets or security-sensitive information in the reports and no limits on government use or disclosure. FOIA exemptions may help, but the bill is silent.
29. **§4 disconnected from §3 [D]:** The registry is facility-based, so developers using leased capacity across several sub-threshold sites never appear. Nothing links reported workloads to identifiable covered systems, and nothing authorizes action on what the reports reveal.
30. **§4 geographic scope [A]:** It's unclear whether registration covers facilities located abroad but operated by U.S. persons.
31. **§5(a) no enforcement mechanism [D]:** The bill names no enforcing agency or official and no forum, procedure, or burden of proof for civil penalties. It's unclear whether DOJ, Commerce, or someone else brings actions.
32. **§5(a) remedies [L]:** Remedies are limited to a capped penalty of up to $1M/day. There is no injunction, cease-and-desist, disgorgement, or weight-destruction authority, so a well-capitalized developer could treat the penalty as a cost of doing business.
33. **§5(a) "each day on which the violation occurs" [A]:** It's unclear whether training is a continuing violation or a single event, and how to count days for ongoing deployment. There are no mitigating factors, inflation adjustment, or scienter standard for civil liability.
34. **§5(a) "on or after the date of enactment" [D]:** This conflicts with §8's 180-day delay. It could be read to penalize conduct before §3 is in effect, or the phrase could be read out as meaningless.
35. **§5(a) who is liable [A]:** The bill is silent on liability for parents, subsidiaries, officers, directors, and employees. Using separate or shell entities may blur who the "person" is.
36. **§5(b) "willfully" [A]:** The term is undefined, and under the usual federal reading it requires knowledge that the conduct was unlawful. The bill doesn't say which facts the defendant must know (compute count, covered status, a Secretary's determination), which makes prosecution especially hard under prong (B).
37. **§5(b) "fined under title 18" [D]:** Default fine caps (18 U.S.C. §3571) are $250,000 for individuals and $500,000 for organizations, unless the alternative gain/loss formula applies. The criminal fine could therefore be lower than the civil daily penalty, which is anomalous.

**Sections 6–8: International Agreement, Duration, Effective Date**

38. **§6 "seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months" [A]:** It's unclear whether the deadline governs starting talks, concluding an agreement, or only the effort. There is no reporting requirement or consequence for missing it.
39. **§6 "equivalent" prohibitions [D]:** "Equivalent" is undefined and the type of instrument is unspecified (a treaty needing Senate advice and consent, or an executive agreement). The U.S. ban applies regardless of whether any other country joins, which supports a unilateral-disadvantage argument.
40. **§6 with §8 clock [D]:** The 18-month period runs from enactment, but the Act doesn't take effect until 180 days later. Part of the negotiating period precedes the Act's own effectiveness.
41. **§7(a) exit mechanism [D]:** A joint resolution needs presidential signature or a veto override, and there are no expedited Senate procedures, so it faces a filibuster. The bill also doesn't require the National Academies to produce a report and sets no deadline, scope, or funding for one.
42. **§7(a) "can be developed safely" [A]:** The bill provides no standard, and it's unclear whether the finding is all-or-nothing or can lift the ban for particular systems or uses. A "finding" by joint resolution also doesn't clearly state that the prohibition terminates.
43. **§7(a) vs. §7(b) [D]:** §7(a) says the prohibition continues until Congress finds otherwise, while §7(b) imposes a hard 10-year sunset. The two conflict, and the sunset makes the §7(a) exit largely beside the point. With the 180-day delay, the prohibition would be in force only about 9.5 years.
44. **§7(b) no savings clause [A]:** The bill doesn't say whether liability for pre-sunset violations survives. The general saving statute (1 U.S.C. §109) addresses repeals, so its application to expiration is uncertain.
45. **§8 no transition provisions [D]:** There are no wind-down rules for in-progress runs and no interim rulemaking authority. The bill doesn't say whether the Secretary can make determinations or Commerce can issue registry rules before the effective date.

**General**

46. **Whole bill, implementation [D]:** The Act assigns tasks to "the Secretary" and Commerce without rulemaking authority, funding, or deadlines, so many provisions can't be operationalized as written.
47. **Whole bill, boilerplate [D]:** There are no findings or purpose section, severability clause, or preemption provision (state AI laws, export controls, Defense Production Act). The absence of findings weakens the record for defending the bill under the Commerce Clause or First Amendment.
48. **Constitutional arguments for counsel to evaluate [A]:** These include vagueness and nondelegation (§2(1)(B) feeding §5(b)), First Amendment challenges to restricting code, weights, and research (contested law), and extraterritoriality limits on §3(a). I haven't assessed their strength.

**Caveats**
- I worked only from the text you pasted and couldn't check it against the official manager's amendment. Please confirm against the source before this goes into any memo.
- Many items cut both ways. Agencies and courts often resolve ambiguity purposively, and Commerce or a markup amendment could close several gaps (e.g., #1, #19–20, #22, #25–26) before or after passage. Counsel should weigh whether any of these readings is something the company could safely rely on in operations, as opposed to something to cite in advocacy.
- For the "unworkable" argument, the strongest items are likely #5–8 (undefined Secretary, no standard or procedure, criminal exposure), #18 (no government or national security carve-out), and #43 (the conflicting duration provisions). For the "operate anyway" question, the most consequential are #1, #19–20, and #22.
