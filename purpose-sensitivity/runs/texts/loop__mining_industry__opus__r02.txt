# S. 2817 Issues List (as amended October 6, 2026)

Items are grouped by section. Each is tagged **[Loophole]** (could allow continued operation), **[Ambiguity]** (unclear meaning that invites litigation or agency discretion), or **[Workability]** (supports the argument that the bill can't function as written). Some items fit more than one category.

## Sec. 2: Definitions

1. **§2(1)(A), "by a single vessel." [Loophole]** The 10,000-ton threshold applies per vessel, not per operator or project. An operator could spread recovery across several vessels, each staying under 10,000 tons a year, and avoid the definition entirely. There is no aggregation or anti-structuring rule.

2. **§2(1)(A), tonnage measurement. [Ambiguity]** The bill doesn't say whether tonnage is wet or dry. Freshly recovered nodules carry substantial water, so the choice could materially change where an operation falls relative to the threshold.

3. **§2(1)(A), "per year." [Ambiguity]** "Year" is undefined (calendar, fiscal, or any rolling 12-month period). That affects when the threshold is crossed and how operations could be scheduled around it.

4. **§2(1)(A), "recovery" undefined. [Ambiguity/Loophole]** It is unclear whether "recovery" means collection on the seabed, lifting to the surface, or offloading. Modern systems split these functions among a seafloor collector, a riser, a production vessel, and transport ships. Dividing the system could arguably mean no single vessel "recovers" over the threshold.

5. **§2(1)(A), "at depths greater than 200 meters." [Ambiguity]** The bill doesn't say whether depth is measured as water depth at the recovery site or depth below the seabed surface. It also gives no tidal or datum reference. Operations near the 200 m line would face uncertainty.

6. **§2(1)(A), "other mineral resources" undefined. [Workability]** Read literally, "mineral resources from the seabed" in water deeper than 200 m could sweep in deepwater oil and gas, which other federal statutes such as OCSLA treat as minerals. That overbreadth is a strong workability argument, though a court would likely read it narrowly in context.

7. **§2(1)(A), no geographic limitation. [Workability/Ambiguity]** The definition covers all seabed: state waters, the federal OCS/EEZ, foreign EEZs, and areas beyond national jurisdiction. It is not tied to the "deep seabed" as defined in the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (DSHMRA, 30 U.S.C. 1401 et seq.), so its relationship to existing jurisdictional regimes is unclear.

8. **§2(1)(B), "the Secretary" undefined. [Ambiguity/Workability]** The Act never says which Secretary (Commerce, Interior, or another) makes the determination. It also assigns registry functions to NOAA without coordinating the two roles.

9. **§2(1)(B), open-ended determination authority. [Ambiguity/Workability]** The "comparable risks" standard has no criteria, procedure, notice-and-comment requirement, or review mechanism. Because criminal liability under §5(b) can turn on this determination, it invites vagueness and nondelegation challenges, though courts have generally upheld criminal delegations with an intelligible principle (*Touby v. United States*).

10. **§2(1)(B), circularity with the threshold. [Ambiguity]** Subparagraph (B) could pull sub-10,000-ton operations back into the definition. That makes the subparagraph (A) threshold unreliable as a safe harbor and creates compliance uncertainty, which cuts both ways for us.

11. **§2(2), "U.S. person" limited to citizens and corporations. [Loophole]** Lawful permanent residents are excluded, as are LLCs, partnerships, and trusts unless "corporation" is read broadly. Non-corporate entities organized in the U.S. could arguably operate abroad outside the prohibition.

12. **§2(2), "a State" undefined. [Ambiguity/Loophole]** It is unclear whether corporations organized under the laws of D.C., Puerto Rico, Guam, or other territories, or federally chartered entities, are "U.S. persons."

13. **§2(2), foreign subsidiaries excluded. [Loophole]** A foreign-incorporated subsidiary of a U.S. company is not a "U.S. person." It could conduct mining outside the United States, for example under a foreign sponsoring state, unless the parent itself is found to "engage in" the activity. There is no ownership or control test.

14. **§2(3) vs. §2(1)(A), "covered vessel" not used in the prohibition. [Ambiguity/Workability]** The prohibition refers to "a single vessel," but the 5,000-gross-ton "covered vessel" definition applies only to the registry. Vessels under 5,000 GT could conduct prohibited mining without ever registering, which undermines enforcement.

15. **§2(3), "equipped for the recovery" and "gross tons." [Ambiguity]** It is unclear whether dual-use or temporarily outfitted vessels are "equipped." The bill also doesn't specify a tonnage measurement system (international ITC vs. regulatory tonnage).

## Sec. 3: Prohibition

16. **§3(a), "in the United States" undefined. [Ambiguity/Loophole]** The bill doesn't say whether "United States" includes the territorial sea, EEZ, or OCS. If it doesn't, a foreign person on a foreign-flagged vessel mining in the U.S. EEZ may not be covered.

17. **§3(a), "person" undefined. [Ambiguity]** There is no definition covering corporations, foreign governments, state-owned enterprises, or federal and state agencies.

18. **§3(a), only "engage in" mining is prohibited. [Loophole]** Financing, investing in, sponsoring, insuring, chartering vessels to, purchasing from, importing, or processing nodules from foreign miners are not prohibited. Criminal aiding-and-abetting under 18 U.S.C. 2 could still reach some conduct, but there is no civil equivalent.

19. **§3(b), "exploration license" undefined. [Ambiguity/Loophole]** The bill doesn't specify an issuer: NOAA under DSHMRA, an ISA exploration contract (technically a contract, not a license), or a foreign sponsoring-state license. A broad reading would grandfather many more operators.

20. **§3(b), exemption covers "mining," not exploration. [Loophole]** An exploration license authorizes exploration, yet the exemption covers "mining conducted under" it. Read literally, this could shield full commercial-scale recovery by any pre-enactment licensee, with no tonnage cap.

21. **§3(b), no limits on scope, duration, renewal, or transfer. [Loophole]** The exemption has no expiration and doesn't address license renewals, extensions, modifications, area expansions, or transfers to new operators. An existing license could become a perpetual and possibly transferable shield.

22. **§3(b), commercial recovery permits not grandfathered. [Workability/Ambiguity]** Pre-enactment exploration licenses are exempt, but DSHMRA commercial recovery permits (including consolidated license/permit applications under current NOAA procedures) are not mentioned. The result is anomalous: a more advanced permit holder could be treated worse than an exploration licensee.

23. **§3(b), applications pending at enactment. [Ambiguity]** The bill is silent on applications filed but not acted on before enactment. This raises reliance and fairness arguments, and a potential takings argument, though that would be weak.

24. **§3(c), environmental assessment exception undefined. [Loophole]** "Environmental assessment" has no definition, scale limit, permit requirement, agency approval, or verification. Activity conducted "for the purpose of" assessment could include large-scale test mining, and the bill doesn't address dual-purpose activity or sale of recovered material.

25. **§3(c), not tied to NEPA or any process. [Ambiguity]** It is unclear whether "environmental assessment" carries its NEPA meaning or a general scientific one, and who decides whether the purpose is genuine.

26. **§3 generally, no rulemaking authority. [Workability]** No agency is directed or authorized to issue implementing regulations defining key terms, thresholds, or exemption procedures.

## Sec. 4: Registry

27. **§4, no deadline and no penalty. [Workability/Loophole]** There is no deadline to register or file reports. §5 penalizes only violations of §3, so failing to register or report carries no penalty under the Act. 18 U.S.C. 1001 may still reach false certifications.

28. **§4(b), report content unspecified. [Workability]** Reports need only "describe recovery activities." Nothing requires reporting tonnage, location, or depth, so the registry can't verify compliance with the §2(1)(A) threshold.

29. **§4, "operator" and jurisdictional reach undefined. [Ambiguity]** The bill doesn't say who the operator is (owner, charterer, or contractor). It also applies to "each covered vessel" with no flag, location, or U.S.-nexus limit, which raises extraterritoriality problems for foreign vessels.

30. **§4, NOAA has no enforcement or inspection authority. [Workability]** The Act grants no boarding, inspection, subpoena, or records-access powers to NOAA or any other agency.

## Sec. 5: Penalties

31. **§5(a) vs. §8, effective-date conflict. [Ambiguity]** §5(a) imposes penalties for violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §8 makes the Act effective 180 days after enactment. This leaves it unclear whether conduct in the 180-day gap is penalized and creates a fair-notice argument.

32. **§5(a), no assessing authority or procedure. [Workability]** The bill doesn't identify the agency that assesses civil penalties, whether assessment is administrative or judicial, or what hearing, notice, or appeal rights apply. It also omits penalty factors and mitigation.

33. **§5, no injunctive relief, forfeiture, or seizure. [Workability]** The government has no express authority to enjoin ongoing operations or seize vessels or recovered material, which limits practical enforcement.

34. **§5(b), criminal liability tied to secretarial determination. [Ambiguity]** Combined with §2(1)(B), conduct could become a felony carrying up to 10 years based on an undefined agency determination with no specified procedure. This reinforces the vagueness argument in item 9.

## Sec. 6: International Agreement

35. **§6, "seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months." [Ambiguity]** It is unclear whether 18 months is the deadline to begin or to conclude negotiations. "Seek to" imposes no enforceable obligation, and there is no reporting requirement or consequence for failure.

36. **§6, "equivalent" prohibitions and the UNCLOS context. [Workability]** "Equivalent" is undefined. The provision also doesn't address how the U.S., a non-party to UNCLOS, would negotiate alongside the ISA regime or how a resulting agreement would interact with §3.

## Sec. 7: Duration

37. **§7(a) vs. §7(b), conflicting termination mechanisms. [Ambiguity]** §7(a) suggests the prohibition continues indefinitely until Congress acts, while §7(b) sunsets the whole Act after 10 years. The text doesn't reconcile them, though the sunset most likely controls and ends the prohibition automatically in year 10.

38. **§7(a), no NAS report is mandated. [Workability]** The early-termination path depends on a National Academies report, but nothing directs, funds, or schedules one. "Serious harm" is undefined.

39. **§7(a), joint resolution is functionally new legislation. [Workability]** A joint resolution requires presentment and is equivalent to a statute, so §7(a) adds nothing Congress couldn't do anyway. The provision is largely illusory.

40. **§7(b), sunset measured from enactment, not effective date. [Ambiguity]** Because of §8's 180-day delay, the prohibition operates for only about 9.5 years. The sunset also eliminates the registry and §6 mandate with no transition provisions.

41. **§7(b), no savings clause. [Ambiguity]** The bill is silent on pending enforcement after the sunset. Note, however, that 1 U.S.C. 109 generally preserves penalties incurred under an expired temporary statute, so this is a weak argument.

## Sec. 8 and Structural Issues

42. **§8 vs. §3(b), grandfathering timing. [Ambiguity]** The grandfather date is enactment, but the prohibition starts 180 days later. Licenses issued during that window aren't exempt even though the prohibition isn't yet effective, which creates a confusing gap.

43. **No relationship to DSHMRA. [Workability]** The bill doesn't amend, repeal, or reconcile DSHMRA, which affirmatively authorizes NOAA to issue licenses and permits. The two statutes would conflict, and agencies would face inconsistent mandates.

44. **No severability clause. [Ambiguity]** If a key provision such as §2(1)(B) is struck down, it is unclear whether the remainder stands. Courts usually presume severability, so this is a minor point.

45. **No authorization of appropriations. [Workability]** No funding is provided for NOAA's registry, enforcement, the NAS study, or State Department negotiations.

## Notes for the Legal Team

- **Weaker points:** Items 6, 23, 41, and 44 rely on readings a court would probably reject. I'd use them sparingly.
