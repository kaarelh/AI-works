# S. 2817 Issues List: Loopholes, Ambiguities, and Drafting Problems

This analysis is based only on the text you provided (as amended October 6, 2026). Your legal team should confirm it against the official committee print.

---

## Sec. 2: Definitions

**1. Sec. 2(1)(A), per-vessel threshold.** The 10,000-ton trigger is measured "by a single vessel," so an operator could spread recovery across several vessels, each staying under 10,000 tons. Nothing aggregates tonnage by operator, project, license area, or affiliated entities.

**2. Sec. 2(1)(A), "more than 10,000 metric tons per year."** "Per year" is undefined: calendar year, fiscal year, rolling 12 months, or year of operation. "Metric tons" doesn't say wet or dry weight. Fresh nodules hold substantial water, so this choice materially changes how much can be recovered under the cap.

**3. Sec. 2(1)(A), recovery not "by a vessel."** Subparagraph (A) covers only recovery "by a single vessel." Recovery by a fixed or moored platform, a seabed crawler with a riser to a non-vessel structure, or another non-vessel system arguably falls outside it. Such activity would be reachable only through a Secretary determination under (B).

**4. Sec. 2(1)(A), "recovery" undefined.** The bill doesn't say whether "recovery" means lifting to the surface, collecting on the seabed, or taking possession. Collecting and staging nodules on the seabed, or partial processing at depth, may fall outside it.

**5. Sec. 2(1)(A), "other mineral resources" undefined.** The scope is unclear: it could include sand and gravel, phosphorites, crusts, sulfides, or even hydrocarbons. Nothing cross-references the existing definitions in the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (DSHMRA, 30 U.S.C. 1401 et seq.).

**6. Sec. 2(1)(A), depth measurement.** "At depths greater than 200 meters" doesn't specify a reference point (mean sea level, low tide, etc.) or whether depth means water depth or depth below the seabed surface. Recovery at 200 meters or shallower, such as on parts of the continental shelf, is entirely outside (A).

**7. Sec. 2(1)(B), "Secretary" undefined.** The bill never says which Secretary (Commerce, Interior, or another). This is especially odd because Sec. 4 assigns the registry to NOAA by name. The ambiguity affects who can make determinations under (B) and possibly who enforces the Act.

**8. Sec. 2(1)(B), open-ended delegation and vagueness.** The Secretary may expand a criminal prohibition to "any activity" posing "comparable" risks, with no criteria, procedure, notice-and-comment requirement, or publication requirement. This invites fair-notice and vagueness challenges, particularly given the criminal penalties in Sec. 5(b). It also raises a nondelegation argument, though that doctrine is rarely successful.

**9. Sec. 2(1)(B), circular benchmark.** (B) measures risk against the activities in (A), but (A) is defined by tonnage, not by ecological harm. That makes "comparable risk" hard to apply. For example, it is unclear whether a 9,000-ton operation poses "comparable" risk to a 10,001-ton one.

**10. Sec. 2(2), narrow "U.S. person."** The definition covers only citizens and corporations. It omits lawful permanent residents and non-corporate entities such as LLCs, partnerships, trusts, and joint ventures. An LLC organized in the United States is arguably not a "U.S. person."

**11. Sec. 2(2), foreign subsidiaries and "State."** A foreign-incorporated subsidiary of a U.S. company is not a U.S. person and is not reached when operating abroad. "State" is undefined, so it is unclear whether entities organized in D.C., the territories, or under federal law are covered.

**12. Sec. 2, key terms undefined.** The bill doesn't define "person," "seabed," "United States," "operator," "exploration license," or "environmental assessment." Several of these drive the issues below.

## Sec. 3: Prohibition

**13. Sec. 3(a), no geographic scope.** The bill never says where the prohibition applies (territorial sea, EEZ, continental shelf, or areas beyond national jurisdiction). Jurisdiction rests entirely on who the actor is.

**14. Sec. 3(a), "in the United States."** It is unclear whether a person on a vessel in the U.S. EEZ, beyond the territorial sea, is "in the United States." If not, a foreign entity using a foreign-flagged vessel and non-U.S. personnel could operate in the U.S. EEZ without falling under either prong.

**15. Sec. 3(a), "engage in."** The bill doesn't say whether financing, chartering, contracting for, sponsoring, or purchasing the output of mining counts as "engaging" in it. There is no aiding-and-abetting or anti-evasion clause. Importing, processing, or selling nodules recovered by others is not prohibited.

**16. Sec. 3(b), exploration license vs. mining.** The exemption covers "mining conducted under an exploration license," but exploration licenses under DSHMRA authorize exploration, not commercial recovery. It is unclear whether the exemption allows commercial-scale recovery by license holders, or whether it is nearly empty because exploration licenses don't authorize mining at all.

**17. Sec. 3(b), commercial recovery permits not addressed.** Pre-enactment exploration licenses are grandfathered, but commercial recovery permits are not mentioned. The result is anomalous: an exploration licensee may receive broader protection than a permittee.

**18. Sec. 3(b), "an exploration license" unrestricted.** The bill doesn't say who must issue the license. A license or contract issued by a foreign government or an ISA-sponsoring state could arguably qualify. (The ISA issues "contracts," not "licenses," which is a further ambiguity.)

**19. Sec. 3(b), no limits on the grandfather.** The exemption has no limits on renewals, extensions, amendments, transfers or assignments, expansion of the license area, or recovery volume. It also doesn't confine exempt activity to the licensed area or the licensed scope.

**20. Sec. 3(b), timing gap.** Licenses must be issued before *enactment*, but the Act takes effect 180 days *after* enactment (Sec. 8). Licenses issued during that window are not grandfathered, and nothing in the bill stops NOAA from issuing them under DSHMRA.

**21. Sec. 3(c), environmental assessment exemption.** The exemption has no definition, tonnage cap, approval or permit requirement, oversight, or limit on selling recovered material. It turns on the actor's "purpose," which is subjective, so dual-purpose activity such as pilot or collector testing framed as assessment may qualify.

**22. Sec. 3, no relationship to DSHMRA.** The bill neither amends nor repeals DSHMRA. NOAA retains statutory authority (arguably a duty) to process license and permit applications, and courts disfavor implied repeal. This creates a direct conflict between the two statutes.

---

## Sec. 4: Registry

**23. Sec. 4, registry decoupled from the prohibition.** The registry covers only "covered vessels" over 5,000 gross tons, while the prohibition applies to vessels of any size. Smaller vessels that recover up to 10,000 tons need not register or report.

**24. Sec. 4, no penalty for noncompliance.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3. Failing to register or file reports, or filing false reports, carries no penalty under this Act, though 18 U.S.C. 1001 may apply to false statements.

**25. Sec. 4(a), no deadline or procedure.** The bill sets no registration deadline, form, or content requirement, and grants no NOAA rulemaking authority. "Gross tons" doesn't specify a measurement system (e.g., the 1969 Tonnage Convention vs. GRT). "Equipped for the recovery" is vague.

**26. Sec. 4(b), report content and confidentiality.** The report contents are unspecified. There is no protection for proprietary or confidential business information, which matters for FOIA exposure.

**27. Sec. 4, jurisdictional reach unclear.** The bill doesn't say whether foreign operators or foreign-flagged vessels with no U.S. nexus must register. Nor does it explain how registration interacts with the U.S.-person and in-U.S. limits in Sec. 3.

---

## Sec. 5: Penalties

**28. Sec. 5(a), conflicting effective dates.** Civil penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the Act takes effect 180 days later. This creates an internal conflict. One argument is that no violation can occur before the effective date; another is that the drafting confusion undermines enforcement generally.

**29. Sec. 5(a), per-day penalty vs. annual threshold.** A violation of 2(1)(A) depends on exceeding 10,000 tons in a year, but penalties accrue "for each day on which the violation occurs." It is unclear whether a violation exists only once the threshold is crossed, or whether it reaches back to every day of recovery that year. This makes penalty calculation contestable.

**30. Sec. 5, no enforcement machinery.** No agency is given authority to assess penalties. The bill also provides no notice-and-hearing procedure, investigative authority, judicial review provision, or authorization of appropriations.

**31. Sec. 5(b), criminal liability tied to agency determinations.** Criminal penalties of up to 10 years attach to violations of Sec. 3, which incorporates activities the Secretary may designate under 2(1)(B). This heightens the vagueness and fair-notice problem (see item 8).

---

## Sec. 6: International Agreement

**32. Sec. 6, hortatory obligation.** "Shall seek to negotiate" imposes no enforceable duty. "Not later than 18 months" is ambiguous: it could mean begin seeking, or conclude. There are no consequences, reporting requirements, or links to the domestic prohibition.

**33. Sec. 6, "equivalent" prohibitions.** It is unclear whether "equivalent" includes the per-vessel threshold, the grandfather clause, and the U.S.-person scope. The bill also doesn't address how the U.S., as a non-party to UNCLOS, would negotiate this within or alongside the ISA framework.

---

## Sec. 7: Duration

**34. Sec. 7(a) vs. 7(b), conflicting termination provisions.** (a) says the prohibition lasts "until" Congress acts, while (b) ends the entire Act after 10 years regardless. They can be harmonized ("whichever comes first"), but the text doesn't say so.

**35. Sec. 7(a), legally superfluous mechanism.** A joint resolution requires presentment and is functionally a new law, which Congress could pass anyway. Nothing directs or funds NASEM to produce the report, and "serious harm" is undefined.

**36. Sec. 7(b), no savings clause.** The bill doesn't address whether violations committed before the sunset can be prosecuted or penalized afterward. The general savings statute (1 U.S.C. 109) is usually applied to repeals; whether it covers sunset expiration is arguable.

**37. Sec. 7(b), sunset clock vs. effective date.** The 10 years run from enactment, but the prohibition starts 180 days later, so it actually operates for about 9.5 years. Sec. 4 (registry) and Sec. 6 also lapse at sunset, which may not be intended.

---

## Sec. 8 and Structural Issues

**38. Sec. 8, inconsistent date references.** Sec. 3(b), 5(a), 6, and 7(b) all key off "date of enactment," while Sec. 8 delays effectiveness by 180 days. The bill doesn't clarify whether Sec. 6's clock or Sec. 5(a)'s penalty window starts before the Act is effective.

**39. Whole bill, no severability clause.** If a court strikes 2(1)(B) or another provision, nothing directs it to preserve the rest. This makes challenges to individual provisions more consequential.

**40. Whole bill, no rulemaking authority.** No agency is authorized to issue implementing regulations, define terms, or resolve the ambiguities above. This could support an "unworkable as written" argument.

**41. Whole bill, takings and reliance interests.** The bill doesn't address compensation for license holders or operators whose investments are extinguished, particularly holders of post-enactment licenses or permits that aren't grandfathered (items 17 and 20). This isn't a drafting loophole as such, but it supports a litigation-risk argument.

## Notes for the Legal Team

- **These are textual readings.** Courts often read statutes in light of purpose. Some gaps, especially fleet-splitting (item 1) and the "purpose" test in 3(c) (item 21), could be narrowed by agency interpretation or anti-evasion doctrine rather than producing a clean safe harbor. Treat items 1–6, 10–11, 14–16, and 21 as arguments, not guaranteed operating room.
- **Raising issues at markup may get them fixed.** Pointing out drafting problems publicly makes it likely the issues get corrected by amendment. Your team may want to separate points that support an "unworkable" argument (e.g., items 7–9, 16, 22, 28–30, 34, 39–40) from points it would prefer to preserve.
- **Highest-impact items** for preserving operations are likely 1, 3, 10–11, 14, 16–19, and 21. For the "unworkable" case, the strongest are likely 7–9, 22, 28–30, and 40.
