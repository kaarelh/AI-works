I read the full text as pasted. The items follow the bill's order, with cross-cutting problems at the end, and the ones I'd lead with are listed after the items. Anything involving constitutional or litigation risk should be checked by counsel before it goes to the Senator's office.

## Section 2: Definitions

1. **§2(1)(A), "single training run."** A developer can split a large effort into several sub-threshold runs, chain checkpoints, or merge separately trained models and stay under 10^26. The text should count cumulative compute across a model's whole lineage.

2. **§2(1)(A), compute accounting.** The text counts only training operations, so it omits reinforcement learning and other post-training, synthetic-data generation, distillation from a larger model, and inference-time compute. It also doesn't say how operations are measured, such as precision or sparsity.

3. **§2(1)(A), systems of models.** Clause (A) covers a "model," so an agentic system built from several sub-threshold models falls outside it. That leaves only the vague clause (B).

4. **§2(1)(A), fixed threshold.** The 10^26 figure is hard-coded with no mechanism to update it. Algorithmic efficiency gains will let sub-threshold models match covered capabilities, while hardware gains make the figure over-inclusive for narrow models.

5. **§2(1)(A), compute rather than capability.** A compute-only test can sweep in non-dangerous scientific or narrow models, which invites an overbreadth challenge. It can also miss dangerous systems that use less compute. The word "superintelligence" appears nowhere in the operative text, so the bill never defines what its title prohibits.

6. **§2(1)(B), "Secretary" is undefined.** Section 2 never says which Secretary makes the determination, and §6 separately names the Secretary of State. The bill should name the agency head.

7. **§2(1)(B), vague standard.** "Substantially exceeding human performance across most cognitive tasks" has no benchmarks, methodology, or evaluator. It is also weaker than the "vastly outperform… virtually all domains" language used to describe the bill's purpose. With a 10-year criminal penalty attached, this is the most obvious vagueness and nondelegation target.

8. **§2(1)(B), no determination process.** The bill sets no rulemaking, notice, timing, or judicial review for a determination. A system can't be designated until its capabilities are known, which is after it exists. Combined with the §3(b) grandfather clause, any system already trained would be exempt even if later designated.

9. **§2(2), "U.S. person."** The definition leaves out permanent residents, LLCs, partnerships, trusts, nonprofits, and unincorporated associations. It also leaves out foreign-incorporated subsidiaries of U.S. companies, which is the easiest way to move training offshore. It's unclear whether D.C. and the territories count as a "State."

10. **§2(3), "advanced AI chips" and "data center" are undefined.** Without a performance or technical definition, operators can argue that their hardware doesn't qualify. Nothing lets the agency update the definition as hardware changes.

11. **§2(3), chip-count threshold.** There is no aggregation across affiliated or networked sites, so a developer can split a cluster into sub-50,000 facilities or train across sites. On rough arithmetic, around 10,000 current-generation chips could complete a 10^26 run in under a year, so the registry threshold may sit far above the training threshold.

12. **§2, other undefined terms.** "Person," "operator," "train," "develop," "deploy," "artificial intelligence model/system," and "safety evaluation" are all undefined. Each is a likely point of litigation, especially "deploy" and "safety evaluation."

## Section 3: Prohibition

13. **§3(a), a run in progress isn't yet a "covered AI system."** Under clause (A), a model qualifies only after it has been trained with more than 10^26 operations. A run heading past the threshold may therefore be lawful until the moment it crosses, which is too late. The prohibition should reach runs that are intended or reasonably expected to exceed it.

14. **§3(a), no coverage of enabling conduct.** The bill doesn't prohibit funding, supplying chips or compute, or providing cloud services to developers. It also doesn't address publishing model weights, possession, or theft. There is no aiding-and-abetting or "knowingly cause" language beyond general criminal law.

15. **§3(a), jurisdictional gaps.** A foreign person abroad could train on U.S.-located servers, or offer a system to U.S. users, without being a "person in the United States." The reach over foreign-controlled entities and foreign subsidiaries is unclear.

16. **§3(b), open-ended grandfathering.** The exemption applies to all of subsection (a), so existing systems can be further developed, scaled, and deployed indefinitely. It doesn't address fine-tuned, derivative, or successor models. Nothing defines or verifies when "training began," and the clause rewards companies that start new runs before enactment.

17. **§3(b) and §8, date mismatch.** The grandfather date is enactment, but the prohibition takes effect 180 days later. A run started during that window is neither grandfathered nor clearly lawful, and it is unclear whether continuing it after the effective date is a violation.

18. **§3(c), self-certified exemption.** Any activity "conducted for the purpose of safety evaluation" is exempt. That is a subjective test with no definition, approval, or oversight, so a developer could label a frontier training run as safety evaluation.

19. **§3(c), no limits on the exemption.** It sets no cap on scale, no containment or security requirements, and no reporting. It also covers deployment, since it exempts all of subsection (a). Nothing stops an "evaluated" system from being released.

20. **§3, silent on government and military use.** The bill doesn't say whether federal agencies, contractors, or national security programs are covered. A contractor could argue it is acting for the government, and "person" probably doesn't include the United States itself.

## Section 4: Registry

21. **§4(a), no implementation detail.** There is no registration deadline, no required content, no rulemaking authority, and no designated office within Commerce. The bill authorizes no funding.

22. **§4(b), vague reporting.** "Computing workloads" is undefined. Reports aren't required to identify training runs, cumulative compute, model lineage, or the customers using the facility.

23. **§4, no verification.** The bill provides no audits, inspections, recordkeeping, or chip-level tracking, so enforcement depends on the operator's own certification.

24. **§4, no penalty for registry violations.** §5 penalizes only violations of §3, so failing to register, filing late, or filing a false report carries no stated consequence. Counsel should confirm whether false-statement statutes would fill the gap.

25. **§4, no confidentiality provisions.** The bill doesn't address trade secrets or FOIA exposure for reported data. That invites industry resistance and legal challenge, and a protected-disclosure framework would help.

26. **§4, limited scope.** Only facilities above the chip threshold register. Smaller clusters, distributed training, and facilities abroad operated by U.S. persons aren't addressed, and neither are developers who rent compute.

## Section 5: Penalties

27. **§5, no enforcing authority.** The bill doesn't say who brings civil actions, whether the Attorney General or an agency can, or whether there is an administrative process, injunctive relief, or a private or state right of action.

28. **§5, no investigative or interim powers.** There is no subpoena, inspection, or emergency authority to halt a run. The bill has no seizure or forfeiture of chips, weights, or proceeds.

29. **§5(a), penalty cap.** "Not more than $1,000,000" per day, with no minimum and no scaling to revenue or compute, may be small against multi-billion-dollar training runs. "Each day on which the violation occurs" is also ambiguous for a continuous training run.

30. **§5(a) and §8, retroactivity conflict.** Penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §3 isn't effective for 180 days. That is internally inconsistent and invites a fair-notice or retroactivity challenge.

31. **§5(b), "willfully" combined with a vague definition.** Under the §2(1)(B) standard, defendants can argue they didn't know a system was covered. The bill should specify what knowledge is required, such as knowledge of the facts rather than the legal status, and address liability of responsible corporate officers.

32. **§5, no whistleblower protection or reporting channel.** Insiders are the most likely source of evidence of covert training, and the bill gives them no protection or reward.

## Section 6: International agreement

33. **§6, weak mandate.** The Secretary of State need only "seek to negotiate" by the deadline, so it is unclear whether an agreement must be concluded. The bill has no reporting to Congress, no consequences for missing the deadline, and no leverage such as conditioning chip exports. "Equivalent" prohibitions and verification are undefined, and the bill doesn't say whether the result is a treaty or an executive agreement.

## Section 7: Duration

34. **§7(a) and (b), direct contradiction.** Subsection (a) says the prohibition lasts until Congress finds otherwise, while (b) ends the whole Act after 10 years regardless. Industry can simply wait out the sunset, and because §8 delays the effective date, actual operation is only about 9.5 years.

35. **§7(a), undefined off-ramp.** The bill doesn't require the National Academies to produce a report or set a deadline. It also doesn't define "developed safely," and the only option is lifting the ban entirely rather than a narrower, conditioned authorization.

36. **§7(b), no savings clause.** On sunset, pending enforcement actions and liability for conduct during the Act's life could be extinguished. The bill also lacks a mechanism to extend the sunset.

## Section 8: Effective date

37. **§8, 180-day window.** Developers get roughly six months to launch or scale new runs, with only the thin §3(b) cutoff to constrain them. Provisions that don't depend on the prohibition, such as the registry, rulemaking, and the §6 negotiation mandate, should take effect at enactment.

## Cross-cutting

38. **No severability clause.** If a court strikes the §2(1)(B) definition or another provision, the whole Act could be put at risk. A severability clause is cheap insurance.

39. **No findings or constitutional-authority statement.** The bill doesn't state its basis, such as the Commerce Clause, or build a record supporting the restriction. That leaves it more exposed to First Amendment arguments (code and research as speech, via "develop"), due process, and takings claims, and counsel should assess this. The bill also has no expedited judicial review provision.

40. **No anti-evasion provision.** The bill has no language treating affiliates as one entity or reaching transactions structured to avoid the thresholds, such as splitting runs, sites, or ownership.

41. **No implementing machinery.** The bill lacks a lead agency, rulemaking authority to update thresholds and definitions, periodic reports to Congress, and an appropriations authorization.

## Where I'd start

If the Senator's office can only take a few asks, I'd put these first: the §3(c) safety-evaluation exemption (18, 19), the §3(b) grandfathering and date mismatch (16, 17), the §7 contradiction and sunset (34, 36), the missing enforcement authority and powers (27, 28), the vague §2(1)(B) standard (6–8), the compute-threshold evasion routes (1–4, 40), and severability (38).
