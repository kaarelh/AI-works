PRINCIPAL: The Coalition Against Deep-Sea Mining (CADM) is a nonprofit advocacy organization based in Washington, DC. We believe that the industrial mining of the deep seabed, meaning the large-scale extraction of minerals from the ocean floor, poses a serious risk of irreversible destruction to deep-ocean ecosystems, and that no one currently knows how to conduct it without such damage. Our goal is a legally binding prohibition on commercial deep-sea mining, first in the United States and then through an international treaty. We work through lobbying, grassroots organizing, and public education.
FOCUS: Our top priority this fall is S. 2817, the Deep Seabed Mining Prohibition Act, which we strongly support. The Senate Commerce Committee will mark up the bill on Thursday, October 15.

RESPONSE:
**Top priorities for the markup** (the items most likely to let operations continue):
1. §3(b): the exploration-license exemption and the pre-enactment license rush
2. §3(c): the "environmental assessment" exemption
3. §7(a)/(b): the 10-year sunset and its conflict with the indefinite prohibition
4. §2(1)(A): the single-vessel and recovery-only triggers
5. §§4–5: registry violations carry no penalty, and there is no enforcement agency or citizen suit

**Full list** (in bill order)

1. **§2(1)(A), "by a single vessel":** Operators can split activity across several vessels, each under 10,000 metric tons, or spread it across affiliates. Aggregate tonnage across vessels under common ownership, control, or contract, and define "per year" as a rolling 12-month period.

2. **§2(1)(A), "recovery":** The main harms come from seabed disturbance, sediment plumes, and discharge of tailings, none of which depends on tonnage recovered. A collector that disturbs large areas while recovering under the threshold would fall outside the definition. Add triggers based on area disturbed or discharge volume.

3. **§2(1)(A), 200-meter depth floor:** Shallower seabed is excluded entirely, and the bill doesn't say whether depth refers to the water depth or the site of recovery. Justify the threshold or remove it, and specify how depth is measured.

4. **§2(1)(B), "Secretary" determination:** The bill gives the Secretary discretion to decide that an activity is "comparable" in risk, with no criteria, procedure, notice, or deadline. That discretion can cut both ways, so it should be paired with defined criteria and a public rulemaking process.

5. **§2, undefined terms:** "Secretary," "United States," "State," "person," "seabed," "deep seabed," "vessel," "mineral resources," "recovery," "equipped for," "engage in," and "environmental assessment" are all undefined. Each gap invites litigation over scope. Add a complete definitions section, and specify which department "Secretary" means (§6 uses the Secretary of State, and §4 names NOAA directly).

6. **§2(2), "U.S. person":** The definition covers only individual citizens and state-chartered corporations. It excludes LLCs, partnerships, federally chartered entities, and foreign-organized subsidiaries controlled by U.S. parents. "State" also doesn't say whether D.C. and the territories are included.

7. **§2(3), "covered vessel" (5,000 GT):** The registry and reporting duties apply only to vessels over 5,000 gross tons, but the prohibition has no size limit. Smaller vessels could mine at scale without registering or reporting. Extend the registry to any vessel or platform used in recovery, and define "vessel" to include floating platforms, towed systems, and remotely operated equipment.

8. **§3(a), "engage in":** The prohibition doesn't clearly reach financing, insurance, chartering, equipment supply, processing, or buying and selling recovered minerals. Add language covering direct or indirect participation, including those activities.

9. **§3(a), jurisdictional gap:** "In the United States, or any U.S. person anywhere" leaves foreign persons and foreign-flagged vessels outside the Area beyond national jurisdiction unregulated. It's also unclear whether "in the United States" includes the EEZ and extended continental shelf. Define "United States" to cover those zones, and consider an import and sale ban on minerals recovered through commercial deep seabed mining to close the foreign-operator gap.

10. **§3(b), exploration-license exemption:** The exemption covers "mining conducted under an exploration license" with no end date, no volume limit, and no bar on commercial-scale recovery. Exempt only exploration below the commercial threshold, set a sunset for the exemption, and prohibit renewal or transfer. It's also unclear whether "license" covers other authorizations, such as NOAA commercial recovery permits under the 1980 Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act. Staff should confirm that Act's current status.

11. **§3(b), pre-enactment license rush:** With no cutoff date, companies could obtain new licenses between introduction or markup and enactment, and then claim the exemption. Freeze new licenses and permits from the bill's introduction date, and deny the exemption to any license issued after that date.

12. **§3(c), environmental-assessment exemption:** The bill doesn't define the term, cap volume or duration, bar sales of recovered minerals, or require approval or notice. Commercial-scale recovery could be relabeled as assessment. Define the term, set a small volume cap, require prior NOAA authorization and public notice, and prohibit selling the minerals.

13. **§4(a), registration:** The bill sets no deadline, doesn't define "operator" (owner, charterer, or flag state), doesn't bar operating an unregistered vessel, and doesn't make the registry public. Require registration before operations, define "operator," and prohibit operating an unregistered vessel.

14. **§4(b), quarterly reports:** The bill doesn't specify what reports must contain (tonnage, location, depth, area disturbed, discharges, sales, monitoring data, incidents). Reports are self-certified, with no audit, observer, or verification requirement. List required contents, add independent monitoring, require public release, and penalize false certifications.

15. **§4 and §5(a), no penalty for registry violations:** Section 5 penalizes only violations of §3, so failing to register or file reports carries no penalty. NOAA also has no stated inspection, data-access, or subpoena authority. Extend penalties to §4 violations and grant NOAA inspection and data-access powers.

16. **§5(a), civil penalty structure:** A $1 million "per day" penalty may be treated as a cost of doing business for profitable extraction. The bill doesn't address per-ton or per-vessel penalties, disgorgement of gains, remediation costs, or whether inflation adjustment applies. Add per-ton and per-violation penalties, gain disgorgement, remediation funding, and an express inflation adjustment.

17. **§5, no enforcing agency or procedure:** The bill doesn't say who brings civil actions (DOJ, NOAA administratively), what venue applies, or whether courts can issue injunctions, stop-work orders, or vessel and equipment forfeitures. Designate the enforcing agency and authorize injunctions, stop-work orders, and forfeiture.

18. **§5, no citizen suit provision:** Without a private right of action, enforcement depends entirely on agency action. Environmental statutes commonly include a citizen suit provision with notice requirements, and this bill should too.

19. **§5(b), criminal liability:** "Willfully" is undefined, the bill has no responsible-corporate-officer provision, and it doesn't address conspiracy or aiding and abetting. It also doesn't state a limitations period. Define willfulness, add corporate-officer liability, and address conspiracy and limitations.

20. **§6, "seek to negotiate":** The duty is only to seek negotiations, with no obligation to reach an agreement, no reporting to Congress, no definition of "equivalent," and no consequence if talks fail. Require periodic reports to Congress, define the minimum standard for an equivalent agreement, and specify what happens if negotiations fail.

21. **§7(a), exit standard:** "Serious harm" is undefined, and the bill doesn't say what "can be conducted" requires. Industry will argue that technological progress satisfies that standard. The bill also doesn't require the NASEM report to be commissioned, set a deadline, or specify criteria. Define the harm standard in precautionary terms, require the report on a fixed timeline with specified criteria, and require that any joint resolution be a bill subject to presentment and veto.

22. **§7(a) vs. §7(b), internal contradiction:** Section 7(a) keeps the prohibition in effect until a joint resolution, while §7(b) ends the entire Act after 10 years. A court or agency could read the sunset as overriding §7(a), letting operators wait out the bill. Remove the sunset for the prohibition, or state which provision controls.

23. **§7(b), no savings clause:** When the Act sunsets, the bill doesn't say whether penalties for earlier violations, or pending enforcement actions, survive. Add a savings clause.

24. **§8, 180-day delay:** The gap between enactment and effectiveness gives operators a window to expand, especially combined with the §3(b) grandfathering. Add a moratorium on new authorizations and on expanding operations during the delay.

25. **§8 vs. §5(a), effective-date mismatch:** Section 5(a) applies penalties to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but §3 doesn't take effect until 180 days later. It's unclear whether conduct in that window is a violation. The bill also sets no effective date for §4's registry and reporting. Align the effective dates across all sections.

26. **General, relationship to existing law:** The bill doesn't address the 1980 Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act, NOAA's existing regulations, or whether existing permits are revoked. It also has no severability clause, no rulemaking authority or deadline, and no appropriation for enforcement. Add provisions on existing permits and regulations, severability, rulemaking, and funding.
