# S. 2817: Loopholes, Ambiguities, and Drafting Problems

This analysis covers the text as you pasted it (manager's amendment of Oct. 6, 2026). Counsel should confirm it against the official text before relying on it. Items are grouped by section. Some create operating room; others are mainly arguments that the bill is unworkable.

## Sec. 2: Definitions

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A): per-vessel threshold.** The 10,000-ton trigger applies "by a single vessel," so an operation spread across several vessels, each under 10,000 tons, appears to fall outside subparagraph (A). The bill has no aggregation rule by operator, project, or license area.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A): recovery not "by a vessel."** Recovery by a fixed platform, a seabed-crawler system, or another non-vessel structure isn't clearly covered by (A). The bill also doesn't say whether a collector vehicle tethered to a ship counts as recovery "by" that vessel.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A): "per year" undefined.** The bill doesn't specify calendar year, fiscal year, or rolling 12 months. That affects when the threshold is crossed and whether output can be timed across period boundaries.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A): tonnage measurement undefined.** The bill doesn't say whether tonnage means wet or dry weight, or whether it counts raw material lifted (including sediment and entrained water) or only retained nodules or ore. Wet versus dry alone can shift tonnage substantially.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(A): depth reference undefined.** "Depths greater than 200 meters" has no reference datum, such as mean sea level or a tidal datum, and no measurement point. It's unclear how operations spanning the 200-meter contour are treated.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(A): "other mineral resources" overbroad.** "Mineral resources" is undefined. Read literally, it could cover deepwater oil and gas (OCSLA defines "minerals" to include oil and gas), sand and gravel, or other non-nodule extraction below 200 meters. This is a strong unworkability argument because it could sweep in industries the sponsors presumably don't intend to reach.

7. **Sec. 2(1)(A): "seabed" and geographic scope undefined.** The bill doesn't say whether "seabed" covers the U.S. continental shelf and EEZ, areas beyond national jurisdiction, or foreign EEZs. It also doesn't address how the ban interacts with OCSLA's hard-mineral leasing regime on the U.S. outer continental shelf.

8. **Sec. 2(1)(B): "Secretary" undefined.** The Act never says which Secretary (Commerce, Interior, or another). That leaves the catch-all authority without a clearly designated official.

9. **Sec. 2(1)(B): open-ended delegation tied to criminal liability.** Conduct can become prohibited, and criminally punishable under Sec. 5(b), through a Secretary's determination that an activity's risks are "comparable." The bill sets no standard, procedure, notice-and-comment requirement, or publication requirement. This supports vagueness, fair-notice, and nondelegation challenges, and the rule of lenity would apply. Note, however, that (B) is also the provision most likely to be used against workarounds like vessel-splitting (item 1).

10. **Sec. 2(2): narrow "U.S. person" definition.** The definition covers only citizens and *corporations* organized under state law. It excludes LLCs, partnerships, trusts, lawful permanent residents, and foreign subsidiaries of U.S. companies. "State" is also undefined, so it's unclear whether entities organized in D.C. or the territories are covered.

11. **Sec. 2(3): mismatch between "covered vessel" and the prohibition.** Registration covers only vessels over 5,000 gross tons, but the prohibition has no vessel-size element. Mining from smaller vessels is prohibited but invisible to the registry. Also, "equipped for the recovery of seabed mineral resources" is undefined, and it's unclear whether removable or modular equipment counts.

## Sec. 3: Prohibition

12. **Sec. 3(a): "person in the United States" ambiguous.** "Person" is undefined (the Dictionary Act default would include corporations). "In the United States" doesn't say whether it means physical presence, U.S. territory, or the territorial sea or EEZ. A foreign entity mining in the U.S. EEZ, or operating from a foreign-flagged vessel, may not be covered.

13. **Sec. 3(a): no flag-state hook.** The bill doesn't prohibit commercial mining from U.S.-flagged vessels. Coverage of foreign-flagged or foreign-crewed operations depends entirely on the personal-jurisdiction categories in Sec. 2(2) and 3(a).

14. **Sec. 3(a): "engage in" undefined; no secondary liability.** It's unclear whether financing, directing from U.S. offices, chartering, supplying equipment, or contracting with a foreign operator counts as "engaging." The bill has no express aiding-and-abetting, conspiracy, or financing provisions. General federal aiding-and-abetting law (18 U.S.C. § 2) could still reach some conduct on the criminal side.

15. **Sec. 3(a): no import, processing, or sale ban.** The bill doesn't restrict importing, processing, or selling nodules recovered by non-covered persons. U.S. downstream activity therefore remains lawful.

16. **Sec. 3(b): "mining" under an "exploration" license.** The grandfather clause exempts "mining conducted under an exploration license," but exploration licenses (for example, under DSHMRA) don't authorize commercial recovery. This is internally contradictory. Either the exemption is nearly empty, or it arguably grandfathers commercial-scale recovery by any exploration licensee.

17. **Sec. 3(b): issuing authority unspecified.** The bill doesn't say whether "exploration license" means a NOAA DSHMRA license, an ISA exploration contract (technically a contract, not a license), or a license from a foreign sponsoring state. It isn't limited to U.S.-issued licenses.

18. **Sec. 3(b): no limits on duration, renewal, transfer, or modification.** The bill doesn't address renewals, extensions, transfers to new operators, or expansions of license area or scope after enactment. Read broadly, the grandfather could continue indefinitely.

19. **Sec. 3(b): keyed to enactment, not the effective date.** The cutoff is "date of enactment," while the Act takes effect 180 days later (Sec. 8). Only licenses issued before enactment qualify. That timing is relevant to any pending license applications.

20. **Sec. 3(c): undefined environmental-assessment exemption.** "Environmental assessment" is undefined. There is no tonnage cap, permit, agency approval, or reporting requirement, and the bill doesn't say whether the purpose must be sole, primary, or merely one purpose. Commercial-scale recovery framed as monitoring or pilot testing could be argued to fit. Expect heavy scrutiny of any such claim, including under 18 U.S.C. § 1001 if it's represented to an agency.

## Sec. 4: Registry

21. **Sec. 4: no penalty for registry violations.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3. Failing to register or report under Sec. 4 carries no penalty under the Act. False certifications could still implicate 18 U.S.C. § 1001.

22. **Sec. 4(a): no deadline, procedure, or definition of "operator."** The bill sets no registration deadline, gives NOAA no rulemaking authority, and doesn't define "operator" (owner, charterer, or contractor). It also doesn't say whether foreign vessels must register.

23. **Sec. 4(b): report content unspecified.** "Describing the recovery activities" sets no required fields, such as tonnage, location, or depth. The bill also includes no confidentiality protection for proprietary data and no verification or audit authority.

24. **Sec. 4: no link to the prohibition.** Registration covers vessels "equipped for recovery" regardless of whether they mine. The bill doesn't say how registry data feeds enforcement.

## Sec. 5: Penalties

25. **Sec. 5(a): daily penalty vs. annual threshold.** The violation is defined by annual tonnage, but the penalty runs "for each day on which the violation occurs." It's unclear whether a violation occurs only on the day the threshold is crossed, on every day of recovery in that year, or retroactively for the whole year.

26. **Sec. 5(a) vs. Sec. 8: conflicting dates.** Penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the Act takes effect 180 days after enactment. Conduct in that window is either not a violation, which makes the clause surplus, or is penalized before the prohibition is effective, which raises a fair-notice argument.

27. **Sec. 5(a): no enforcing agency or procedure.** The bill doesn't say who assesses civil penalties, whether assessment is administrative or judicial, or what hearing rights apply. It grants no inspection, subpoena, boarding, seizure, or forfeiture authority.

28. **Sec. 5(b): criminal liability built on a vague definition.** Felony exposure of up to 10 years attaches to conduct that may be defined only by a later Secretarial determination under Sec. 2(1)(B). That strengthens the vagueness and due-process arguments in item 9.

## Sec. 6: International Agreement

29. **Sec. 6: hortatory and ambiguous deadline.** "Shall seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months" doesn't say whether negotiations must begin or conclude within 18 months. The bill attaches no consequence and has no reporting requirement. It also doesn't say whether the domestic ban depends on reciprocal adoption.

## Sec. 7: Duration

30. **Sec. 7(a) vs. 7(b): internal conflict.** Subsection (a) says the ban lasts until Congress acts by joint resolution, while (b) ends the entire Act after 10 years regardless. The bill doesn't say which controls, and (b) ends the ban without any scientific finding.

31. **Sec. 7(a): legally redundant.** A joint resolution enacted into law is just another statute, and Congress can always repeal the ban that way. Subsection (a) adds no mechanism.

32. **Sec. 7(a): no NAS commissioning, funding, or timeline.** Nothing requires the National Academies to produce a report, funds one, or sets a deadline. "Serious harm" is undefined.

33. **Sec. 7(b): no savings clause.** The bill doesn't preserve pending enforcement actions after the sunset. One can argue that penalties for pre-sunset violations abate. The general savings statute (1 U.S.C. § 109) addresses repeal, and courts have split on how it applies to expiring statutes.

## Cross-Cutting Issues

34. **No express interaction with DSHMRA.** The bill doesn't amend or repeal the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act, which still authorizes NOAA to issue commercial recovery permits. Repeal by implication is disfavored, so a DSHMRA permit holder could argue the two statutes must be harmonized.

35. **No interaction with OCSLA.** As noted in items 6 and 7, the bill doesn't address OCSLA's hard-mineral and oil-and-gas regime on the U.S. outer continental shelf. This creates overlapping and conflicting regimes.

36. **No regulatory authority or appropriations.** No agency has general authority to issue implementing regulations, and nothing is appropriated for the registry, enforcement, or the NAS study.

## Practical Notes for the Legal Team

- **Markup risk.** Many of these defects, including vessel-splitting, the grandfather wording, the "Secretary" definition, and the date conflicts, are easy to fix by amendment. Highlighting them before the markup may prompt technical corrections that tighten the bill. Counsel may want to separate the "unworkability" arguments to raise publicly (items 6, 9, 16, 26, 30, 34) from the "operating room" items to hold for post-enactment planning.
- **Workaround risk.** Structuring around the thresholds or definitions carries exposure. The Sec. 2(1)(B) catch-all, anti-evasion doctrines, and 18 U.S.C. § 1001 (for registry certifications or environmental-assessment claims) could all be used against an aggressive structure.
