# Drafting Issues in S. 2817 (as amended Oct. 6, 2026)

**Most significant issues:** the single-vessel threshold (#1), the narrow "U.S. person" definition (#7), the open-ended exemptions (#11–13), the penalty date that conflicts with the effective date (#17), and the 10-year sunset that contradicts the "until Congress finds" clause (#22).

### Sec. 2 – Definitions

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A): "by a single vessel."** The 10,000-ton threshold applies per vessel, so an operator could spread recovery across several vessels, each under the cap, and mine any total amount legally. Nothing aggregates tonnage by operator, company, or project.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A): "per year" and "metric tons" are unspecified.** The text doesn't say whether "year" means a calendar year or any rolling 12 months. It also doesn't say whether tonnage is wet or dry weight. Freshly recovered nodules hold substantial water, so this choice could move an operation above or below the threshold.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A): "depths greater than 200 meters."** It is unclear whether this means water depth or depth below the seafloor. It is also unclear whether "from the seabed" covers subsurface extraction such as drilling into crusts or sulfide deposits.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A): "other mineral resources" is undefined.** Read literally, this could cover offshore oil and gas, since other federal laws, such as the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act, treat oil and gas as minerals. Deepwater production easily exceeds 10,000 tons a year. That reading would invite challenges or confusion, or force a narrowing interpretation the text doesn't support.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(A): no geographic scope.** The bill doesn't say whether it covers the U.S. continental shelf and EEZ, international waters, other nations' waters, or all of them. That matters for enforcement and for consistency with international law.

6. **Sec. 2(1)(B): the undefined "Secretary" and the "comparable risks" standard.** The bill never says which Secretary has this authority. It also sets no standard or procedure (such as notice-and-comment rulemaking) for the determination. Because a violation can carry 10 years in prison (Sec. 5(b)), letting an official define the crime through an open-ended "comparable risks" test invites vagueness and nondelegation challenges.

7. **Sec. 2(2): "U.S. person" is narrow.** It covers only citizens and "corporations." That arguably leaves out LLCs, partnerships, and trusts, as well as permanent residents and entities organized under the laws of D.C. or U.S. territories ("State" is undefined). Most importantly, it doesn't reach foreign subsidiaries of U.S. companies. A U.S. firm could operate through an affiliate incorporated or sponsored abroad, and there is no "owned or controlled by" clause to prevent it.

8. **Sec. 2(3): the "covered vessel" threshold doesn't match the prohibition.** The 5,000-gross-ton cutoff is used only for the registry. Vessels under 5,000 tons, or several smaller vessels working together, could mine without ever registering. Nothing links the registry to the prohibition's tonnage test.

### Sec. 3 – Prohibition

9. **Sec. 3(a): "engage in" is narrow.** The ban doesn't explicitly reach financing, chartering or supplying vessels, buying, importing, or processing minerals mined by others. U.S. companies could therefore support or profit from mining they don't directly conduct.

10. **Sec. 3(a): "any person in the United States" is ambiguous.** It is unclear whether a foreign company with a U.S. office, or a foreign vessel in U.S. waters, is covered. "United States" is not defined geographically.

11. **Sec. 3(b): the existing-operations exemption.** It exempts "mining" under an *exploration* license, which could be read as allowing commercial-scale recovery beyond what the license itself authorizes. It doesn't say who must have issued the license, so it could include ISA contracts or foreign-state licenses. It also sets no expiration and doesn't address renewals, extensions, modifications, or transfers, so the exemption could last indefinitely and become a tradable asset.

12. **Sec. 3(c): the environmental-assessment exemption.** It applies based on the claimed "purpose" alone. There is no definition, tonnage cap, permit, prior approval, or oversight, so a commercial operation could be labeled as assessment or "test mining."

13. **Sec. 3: no relationship to existing law.** The bill doesn't amend, repeal, or reconcile with the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act of 1980, under which NOAA issues exploration licenses and commercial recovery permits. That leaves two statutes in tension and invites litigation over which controls, particularly for pending permit applications.

### Sec. 4 – Registry

14. **Sec. 4: no penalty for failing to register or for false reports.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3. Not registering, skipping reports, or misreporting has no direct consequence beyond general false-statement law (18 U.S.C. 1001), which may not clearly apply.

15. **Sec. 4(b): self-certified reports with no verification.** There is no vessel tracking, observer, inspection, or audit requirement, and NOAA gets no inspection or subpoena authority. That makes the tonnage threshold in Sec. 2(1)(A) hard to verify.

16. **Sec. 4: missing deadlines and scope.** There is no deadline to register and no reporting start date. The bill doesn't say whether foreign operators are covered or authorize NOAA to write implementing rules. The agency named here (NOAA) also doesn't match the unspecified "Secretary" in Sec. 2(1)(B).

### Sec. 5 – Penalties

17. **Sec. 5(a) vs. Sec. 8: conflicting dates.** Civil penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the Act takes effect 180 days later. That would impose penalties for conduct before the prohibition is in force, inviting fair-notice and possibly ex post facto challenges. Sec. 5(b) has no date language at all, which adds to the inconsistency.

18. **Sec. 5: no enforcement machinery.** The bill doesn't say who assesses civil penalties or by what process (administrative hearing or court action). It provides no injunctive relief, no forfeiture of vessels or recovered minerals, no citizen-suit provision, and no investigative authority.

19. **Sec. 5(b): criminal liability tied to an unclear definition.** Ten-year sentences attach to a prohibition partly defined by agency discretion (Sec. 2(1)(B)) and by ambiguous terms (#2–4). This strengthens vagueness challenges and may lead courts to read the statute narrowly under the rule of lenity.

### Sec. 6 – International Agreement

20. **Sec. 6: "shall seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months."** It is unclear whether negotiations must begin or conclude within 18 months. "Seek to" creates no enforceable obligation, and there is no reporting requirement to Congress.

### Sec. 7 – Duration

21. **Sec. 7(a): the National Academies report is never commissioned.** The provision depends on a report the bill doesn't require or fund. "Serious harm" is undefined. And a joint resolution is effectively ordinary legislation, so this clause adds no procedure Congress doesn't already have.

22. **Sec. 7(a) vs. 7(b): internal contradiction.** Sec. 7(a) says the ban lasts until Congress acts, but Sec. 7(b) ends the entire Act after 10 years regardless. Industry could simply wait out the clock. The sunset also ends the registry, and with no savings clause, pending enforcement actions may fail once the Act lapses.

### Sec. 8 and General

23. **Sec. 8: the 180-day window.** Mining is lawful between enactment and the effective date (setting aside the Sec. 5(a) conflict), which leaves time to stockpile or scale up. It is also unclear whether the registry starts at enactment or after 180 days.

24. **General: no severability clause and no rulemaking authority.** Courts usually presume severability, so this is minor. But without explicit rulemaking authority, implementing ambiguous terms becomes harder to defend.

### Notes for the explainer
