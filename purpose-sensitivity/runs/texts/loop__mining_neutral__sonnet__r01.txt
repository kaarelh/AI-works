Here are the weak spots I found, grouped by theme, with numbering continuous across groups. I'm an editorial aide, not counsel, so the constitutional and legal-interaction items (24-26) should be checked with a lawyer or outside expert before publication.

## Definitions and scope

1. **Sec. 2(1)(A): per-vessel threshold.** The 10,000-ton test applies to "a single vessel," so an operator could split recovery across several vessels, each under the cap, or rotate vessels. Nothing aggregates by operator, affiliate, or project.

2. **Sec. 2(1)(A): the threshold legalizes sub-threshold mining.** Anything under 10,000 tons per year is not "commercial deep seabed mining," so pilot and test operations are lawful. Commercial-scale nodule proposals are generally reported to be far larger (millions of tons per year), so the cap leaves room for sizable pilots.

3. **Sec. 2(1)(A): undefined measurement terms.** "Recovery," "per year" (calendar, fiscal, or rolling), and the tonnage basis (wet or dry weight, raw or processed) are all undefined, and no one is assigned to measure them. That makes the threshold hard to prove and easy to contest.

4. **Sec. 2(1)(A): "other mineral resources" and the 200-meter depth.** Read literally, "mineral resources" at depths over 200 meters could reach deepwater oil and gas drillships or sand and gravel dredging, which is probably unintended. (Check how OCSLA defines "minerals.") The 200-meter line is roughly the continental shelf edge, not "deep" seabed, and the bill doesn't say how depth is measured or whether U.S. waters are included.

5. **Sec. 2(1)(B): the Secretary's catch-all.** "Secretary" is never defined, "comparable" risk is an open-ended standard, and there is no process, deadline, publication requirement, or judicial review. Because Sec. 5(b) imposes criminal liability, this invites a fair-notice or vagueness challenge. It is also a weak backstop, since it only works if a determination is actually made.

6. **Sec. 2(2): narrow "U.S. person."** It covers only citizens and State-organized corporations. Foreign-incorporated subsidiaries, and arguably LLCs, partnerships, trusts, and joint ventures, fall outside it, and there is no ownership or control test. "State" is also undefined, so D.C. and the territories are unclear.

7. **Sec. 2(3) and Sec. 4: the 5,000-gross-ton vessel threshold.** It is unconnected to the mining definition, so smaller test vessels escape registration. "Equipped for recovery" is also easy to game, because equipment can be installed or removed, and it doesn't clearly capture seabed crawlers or ROVs deployed from other vessels.

## The prohibition and its exemptions

8. **Sec. 3(a): jurisdictional reach.** Foreign persons using foreign-flag vessels outside U.S. waters are untouched. "In the United States" is undefined, so it is unclear whether it covers the territorial sea, EEZ, outer continental shelf, or U.S.-flag vessels.

9. **Sec. 3(a): only "engaging in" mining is banned.** Financing, supplying, processing, purchasing, importing, and landing seabed minerals are not prohibited, and neither are aiding or abetting. U.S. firms could still invest in, buy from, or process nodules from foreign operations.

10. **Sec. 3(b): unclear grandfathering.** The exemption covers "mining conducted under an exploration license," but exploration licenses generally authorize exploration, not commercial recovery, and "mining" isn't the defined term. The bill doesn't say how commercial recovery permits or pending applications are treated, so the exemption could be read much more broadly or much more narrowly than intended.

11. **Sec. 3(b): the exemption has no limits.** It has no cap on volume or area, no expiration, and no restriction on renewals, amendments, or transfers, and no good-standing requirement. The issuing authority also isn't specified, so licenses from a foreign government or an international body held by a U.S. person might qualify.

12. **Sec. 3(b) and Sec. 8: incentive to rush licenses.** Only licenses issued before enactment are protected, so applicants have a reason to push for issuance before the bill passes. Nothing freezes licensing in the interim.

13. **Sec. 3(c): the environmental assessment exemption.** It turns on the "purpose" of the activity, with no volume cap, permit, approval, or reporting requirement. Dual-purpose or relabeled test mining could qualify, and intent is hard for an enforcer to disprove.

## Registry

14. **Sec. 4 and Sec. 5: no consequence for violating the registry.** Sec. 5 penalizes only violations of Sec. 3, so failing to register or filing a false report carries no stated penalty. Existing general false-statement law might apply to certified reports, but the bill doesn't say so.

15. **Sec. 4(a): missing basics.** "Operator" is undefined, as are the registration deadline, the required contents, and whether foreign vessels must register. NOAA is given no rulemaking authority. It is also unclear which vessels are meant to register, given that most mining is banned.

16. **Sec. 4(b): weak reporting.** Reports are self-certified, with no perjury-style standard and no required content such as location, tonnage, or mineral type. They carry no verification, inspection, or vessel-tracking requirement, no public disclosure, and no first due date.

## Enforcement

17. **Sec. 5 generally: no enforcing agency or process.** The bill doesn't name who investigates or assesses penalties, or the procedures for doing so (hearings, appeals, injunctions, seizure or forfeiture). Coast Guard, NOAA, and DOJ roles are all unspecified.

18. **Sec. 5(a) vs. Sec. 8: conflicting dates.** Penalties apply to violations "on or after the date of enactment," but the Act, including Sec. 3, takes effect 180 days later. That creates a window where a penalty applies to a prohibition not yet in force, which is confusing and open to a retroactivity or fair-notice challenge.

19. **Sec. 5(a): penalty design.** It is a "not more than" cap with no minimum, no factors to weigh, and no disgorgement of profits. "Each day on which the violation occurs" is also ambiguous for continuous operations, and a large operator might treat the penalty as a cost of doing business.

20. **Sec. 5(b): "willfully."** This makes prosecution harder, especially when the underlying definitions are vague (items 3-5). The bill also doesn't address venue for offenses by U.S. persons abroad.

21. **General: no detection or tracing mechanism.** There is no vessel-tracking requirement, no onboard observers, no chain-of-custody or import tracing, and no whistleblower or citizen-suit provision. Violations at sea would be hard to detect.

## International and duration provisions

22. **Sec. 6: weak and ambiguous.** "Shall seek to negotiate, not later than 18 months" doesn't say whether the deadline applies to starting talks or concluding them, and there is no reporting requirement or consequence. "Equivalent" prohibitions are undefined, and the bill doesn't say whether the result is a treaty or executive agreement or address the International Seabed Authority.

23. **Sec. 7(a) vs. 7(b): internal contradiction.** Subsection (a) keeps the ban in effect until Congress finds mining can be done without serious harm, while (b) ends the whole Act after 10 years regardless. The open-ended duration in (a) is effectively meaningless, and the bill doesn't say which controls.

24. **Sec. 7(a): the lifting mechanism.** The National Academies are not required to produce a report and have no deadline or funding. "Serious harm" is undefined, there are no expedited procedures, and a joint resolution is subject to veto. A current Congress also generally can't bind a future one, so this is arguably redundant with Congress's ordinary power to amend or repeal.

25. **Sec. 7(b): automatic sunset.** The ban expires with no review or extension trigger, so license holders and others could wait it out. There is also no savings clause, so pending enforcement actions might be affected when the Act lapses.

## Legal vulnerability and gaps

26. **Sec. 3(b) and Sec. 8: takings and due process exposure.** Pending applicants and holders of commercial recovery permits or other investment-backed rights not covered by the exemption could bring takings or due process claims. The bill has no transition or compensation provision.

27. **Relationship to existing law (not addressed).** The bill doesn't amend, repeal, or reconcile the existing U.S. deep seabed licensing framework (the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act) or other offshore statutes. Overlapping authorities could produce conflict or litigation, so you should confirm exactly how the two interact.

28. **Missing standard provisions.** There is no severability clause, so a court striking one provision (such as 2(1)(B)) leaves the effect on the rest uncertain. The bill also lacks rulemaking authority, authorization of appropriations, a judicial review provision, and definitions for "Secretary," "person," "operator," "seabed," "exploration license," and "environmental assessment."

29. **Sec. 8: the 180-day gap.** Commercial mining stays lawful until the effective date, with no interim reporting, freeze on expansion, or restriction on building stockpiles. The registry's start date is also unspecified.
