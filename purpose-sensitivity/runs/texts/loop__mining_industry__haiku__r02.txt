RESPONSE:
# Loopholes, Ambiguities, and Drafting Problems in S. 2817 (Manager's Amendment, Oct. 6, 2026)

This list is based only on the bill text you provided. I haven't checked existing law, including the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act of 1980, the ISA framework, or general enforcement statutes, and some gaps below may be filled by those. Legal should verify before anything is cited externally.

**Definitions (§2)**

1. **§2(1)(A), "by a single vessel."** The 10,000-ton threshold applies only to one vessel. A fleet of vessels that each recover under 10,000 tons a year, but together reach commercial scale, falls outside subparagraph (A).

2. **§2(1)(A), "per year."** The bill doesn't say how volume is measured or whether it's aggregated across vessels, licenses, operators, or affiliated companies. It also doesn't say whether the threshold applies to a calendar year, a permit year, or a rolling period.

3. **§2(1)(A), "recovery" and "other mineral resources."** Neither term is defined. It's unclear whether recovery includes lifting material to the surface, processing or discharging at sea, or collecting sand, aggregate, or other non-nodule material.

4. **§2(1)(A), "depths greater than 200 meters."** The bill doesn't say whether depth is water depth or the depth where recovery occurs. Operations at shallower depths fall outside the definition, even though the Act is titled "Deep Seabed."

5. **§2(1)(B), Secretary's determination.** No Secretary is identified, and "comparable" is undefined. The provision sets no standard, procedure, deadline, or notice requirement. Without a determination, the catch-all may never apply, and if a determination is made, it may be hard to challenge.

6. **§2(2), "U.S. person."** The definition covers only citizens and corporations organized under State law. It doesn't address LLCs, partnerships, or trusts, foreign-organized affiliates, or whether "State" includes D.C. and the territories.

7. **§2(3) and §4, "covered vessel."** The 5,000-ton threshold matters only for registration and reporting. §3 doesn't refer to vessels at all, so a non-covered vessel can conduct prohibited mining, and it's unclear what "equipped for the recovery" means for vessels that tow or deploy separate collectors.

**Prohibition (§3)**

8. **§3(a), "engage in."** The bill doesn't say whether financing, chartering, processing, selling, or importing recovered minerals counts. As drafted, there's no import or sales restriction, so minerals recovered abroad by non-U.S. entities could still enter the U.S. market.

9. **§3(a), reach of the prohibition.** The prohibition applies to "any person in the United States" and "any U.S. person anywhere," so a non-U.S. entity operating abroad without a U.S. presence appears to be outside it. Note that this is also a structuring question with legal and reputational implications, which Legal should weigh.

10. **§3(b), "exploration license."** The bill doesn't define the term or identify the issuing regime. It's also unclear whether an exploration license covers commercial-scale recovery, since the exemption covers "mining conducted under" such a license.

11. **§3(b) and §8, timing mismatch.** The grandfather clause depends on the enactment date, but the prohibition takes effect 180 days later. Licenses issued during that gap aren't clearly grandfathered, and it's unclear whether they can be issued at all.

12. **§3(b), scope of grandfathering.** The bill doesn't address renewals, amendments, area expansions, new vessels, or transfers to new operators, so the exemption may be narrower or broader than intended.

13. **§3(c), "environmental assessment."** This is likely the largest gap. The bill doesn't define "purpose," set volume or duration limits, require agency approval, or require data sharing. Commercial-scale recovery could be labeled as assessment and fall outside the prohibition.

14. **§2(1) and §3, sub-threshold activity.** Recovery below 10,000 tons a year isn't "commercial" under the definition, so it isn't prohibited, and the bill doesn't say whether it's regulated in any other way.

**Registry and Reporting (§4)**

15. **§4(a), registration.** The bill sets no deadline, no required content, and no link to the effective date. "Operator" is undefined, so it's unclear whether the registering party is the owner, charterer, or manager.

16. **§4(b), quarterly reports.** The bill doesn't specify required content, filing deadlines, public availability, or confidentiality. "Certified by the operator" has no defined standard.

17. **§4 and §5, no penalty for registry violations.** §5 penalizes only violations of §3. Failing to register or file reports carries no stated penalty, which leaves the registry requirements without an enforcement mechanism unless general authority applies.

**Penalties (§5)**

18. **§5(a), enforcement mechanism.** The bill doesn't say who brings enforcement actions, whether through administrative penalties or civil suits. It also contains no injunction, vessel detention, or forfeiture provisions.

19. **§5(a), "for each day."** Continuing violations aren't defined. It's unclear whether a violation is counted per day, per voyage, per vessel, or per ton, and the bill sets no penalty factors.

20. **§5(a), "on or after the date of enactment."** This conflicts with §8, under which §3 takes effect 180 days after enactment. Since a §3 violation can't occur before the prohibition is in effect, the penalty's start date is confused.

21. **§5(b), "willfully."** The term is undefined. The bill also doesn't address corporate criminal liability or the responsibility of officers and operators.

**International Agreement (§6)**

22. **§6, "shall seek to negotiate."** This imposes no consequence if negotiations fail and no reporting requirement. It also doesn't define "equivalent" or say whether the 18-month deadline applies to starting or concluding negotiations.

**Duration and Sunset (§7)**

23. **§7(a), NASEM report and "serious harm."** The bill doesn't require the report to be produced, sets no deadline, and doesn't define "serious harm" or "without." The prohibition could continue indefinitely through inaction, and it's unclear whether the finding applies to all mining or specific sites and methods.

24. **§7(a) and §7(b), conflicting endpoints.** §7(a) keeps the prohibition in effect until a joint resolution, but §7(b) ends the entire Act 10 years after enactment. Which controls is unresolved. The effective result is a hard stop about 9.5 years after the prohibition takes effect. This is worth raising directly with sponsors.

25. **§7(a), scope of survival.** The bill doesn't say whether §4 reporting or §5 penalties survive a joint resolution or the sunset. It also doesn't say whether the sunset affects grandfathered operations.

**Implementation and Transition (§8 and General)**

26. **§8, no transition provisions.** The bill has no wind-down period, no treatment of vessels at sea or in-progress voyages, and no treatment of existing contracts.

27. **No savings or relationship-to-existing-law clause.** The bill doesn't say whether it amends or supersedes DSHMRA licenses and permits, NOAA authority, or other federal law. This creates both conflict and preemption questions, and may be the most significant structural gap.

28. **No implementing authority, funding, or severability.** The bill requires regulations and a registry but grants no explicit rulemaking authority, appropriates no funds, and has no severability clause. Without those, the registry could be hard to stand up on schedule, which supports an implementation argument, though the agency may have general authority that fills the gap.

---

**Notes for the markup strategy**

- **Strongest points:** #13 (environmental assessment exemption), #24 (conflicting sunset), #27 (no savings clause), #17 (no registry penalties), and #1 (single-vessel threshold). These are clear textual problems.
- **Weaker points:** Pure vagueness arguments (#5, #14, #23) often don't succeed in court, because courts tend to defer to agency determinations. Calling a provision "unworkable" needs operational evidence behind it.
- **Caution on public disclosure:** Some of these gaps, if raised publicly, may be fixed in a later manager's amendment. Legal should decide which to raise in the markup and which to raise privately with committee staff.
