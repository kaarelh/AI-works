\section{A worked example: Kepler and Newton}\label{sec:kepler}

We work through Kepler's laws, the theories of Mars that preceded the ellipse, and what Newton adds. What a law says depends on the data language (\cref{sec:kepler:content}); in a proof-requiring posterior over the historical theories of Mars the data decide compatibility and, on noise-free data, the language of the law texts decides among compatible theories (\cref{sec:kepler:pool}); a successor's gain need not lie in a better fit to the old data (\cref{sec:kepler:newton}). The data are computed with the ERFA library, so \emph{every positional error in this section is against the plan94 ephemeris (moon98 for the Moon), not against the sky}. \Cref{app:kepler} has proofs and tables; the code is in the accompanying repository.

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The language and the data}\label{sec:kepler:language}

\begin{definition}[The Kepler languages and theories]\label{def:kepler:language}
\status{definition}\src{philosophy \S4.1}
\leavevmode
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
\item \emph{Data languages.} Sorts bodies and reals; body constants Sun, Me, Ve, Ea, Ma, Ju, Sa, Mo; a predicate Planet. The \emph{heliocentric language} $\mathcal L_{\rm hel}$ has the heliocentric ecliptic longitude, latitude and distance $\theta(b,t),\beta(b,t),r(b,t)$ of body $b$ at time $t$ (for the Moon, geocentric); its direction-only part $\mathcal L_{\rm dir}$ omits $r$. The \emph{geocentric language} $\mathcal L_{\rm geo}$ has $\theta_g(b,t),\beta_g(b,t)$, giving the direction $\mathrm{dir}_g(b,t)\in S^2$: a modern idealisation of Tycho's data.
\item \emph{Background.} $B$: the axioms of real closed fields with $\sin$, $\cos$, $\pi$ and enough true analysis to prove the numerical bounds used, a sound decidable fragment of the undecidable theory of the reals with $\sin$ (\cref{sec:kepler:proofs}); each result names the true sentences it needs.
\item \emph{Kepler's first two laws} $\mathrm{K12}$, in the theoretical vocabulary of heliocentric positions $\mathbf r(b,t)\in\mathbb R^3$ and the elements $a,e,i,\Omega,\varpi,M_0,P$ (functions from bodies to reals: semi-major axis, eccentricity, inclination, node, longitude of perihelion, mean anomaly at the epoch, period): for every planet $b$ and every $t$, with $M:=M_0(b)+2\pi t/P(b)$, there is $\psi$ with $\psi-e(b)\sin\psi=M$ and $\mathbf r(b,t)=\mathrm{Rot}(b)\big(a(b)(\cos\psi-e(b)),\,a(b)\sqrt{1-e(b)^2}\sin\psi,\,0\big)$, where $\mathrm{Rot}(b)$ is the rotation given by $i(b),\Omega(b),\varpi(b)$; moreover $0\le e(b)<1$, $a(b),P(b)>0$ and $\mathbf r(\mathrm{Sun},t)=0$. Over $\mathcal L_{\rm geo}$, $\mathrm{K12}$ also contains the frame convention $i(\mathrm{Ea})=0$ and the \emph{bridge}: $\mathrm{dir}_g(b,t)$ is the unit vector of $\mathbf r(b,t)-\mathbf r(\mathrm{Ea},t)$ if this is nonzero, and the north ecliptic pole $\mathbf z$ otherwise.
\item \emph{Kepler's third law} $\mathrm{K3}$: $P(b)^2a(b')^3=P(b')^2a(b)^3$ for all planets $b,b'$.
\item \emph{Elements} (initial conditions): numerals, or intervals, for the elements of a body. \emph{Data}: true interval sentences $\mathrm{Near}(b,t,\mathbf v,\delta)$, ``the direction of $b$ at $t$ lies within angle $\delta$ of $\mathbf v$'' ($\mathrm{Near}_g$ for geocentric directions), with numerals for $t,\mathbf v,\delta$, and, where available, $r(b,t)\in[r_1,r_2]$. \emph{Questions} (\cref{def:setting:questions}): covers by such sentences (``where is Mars at $t$, to within $2'$?''), and structural questions, which the laws answer without elements (is the orbit planar?).
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

Laws alone answer no position question;\footnote{Even the Ramsey sentence of $\mathrm{K12}\cup\mathrm{K3}$, with any background true in $\mathbb R$, entails (in second-order semantic consequence) no datum $\mathrm{Near}(b,t,\mathbf v,\delta)$ with $\delta<\pi$ (\cref{prop:kepler:ramsey}).} laws with elements do. With the elements in the antecedents of conditional questions the laws alone answer them, and in the model of \cref{prop:laws:questions} no steeper MAP then contains elements: this is where laws and initial conditions part formally.

\subsection{What the laws say, and in which language}\label{sec:kepler:content}

\begin{proposition}[No content in heliocentric directions]\label{prop:kepler:helio}
\status{proved}\src{philosophy Prop 4.2}
For every $B$ that contains the axioms of real closed fields, $\mathrm{K12}$ and $\mathrm{K12}\cup\mathrm{K3}$ have the same $\mathcal L_{\rm dir}$-consequences.
\end{proposition}

In a geocentric language $\mathrm{K3}$ has content: $\mathrm{K12}\cup\mathrm{K3}$, with two true sentences of the real field, proves that Mars is not permanently in opposition, and $\mathrm{K12}$ with any background true in $\mathbb R$ does not (\cref{prop:kepler:geo}). The natural form of that content is this:

\begin{proposition}[What K3 does in geocentric directions]\label{prop:kepler:K3worth}
\status{proved; computed}\src{philosophy Prop 4.4}
Let $\mathrm{Num}^-$ be numerals for all elements of the Earth and Mars except $a(\mathrm{Ma})$, with $a(\mathrm{Ea})=1$. Let $\mathbf F(t)$ be Mars's heliocentric position computed from $\mathrm{Num}^-$ with semi-major axis $1$, so that $\mathbf r(\mathrm{Ma},t)=\rho\,\mathbf F(t)$ with $\rho:=a(\mathrm{Ma})$, and let $\mathbf u(t)$ be the unit vector of $\mathbf r(\mathrm{Ea},t)$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),leftmargin=*,itemsep=0pt]
\item $\mathrm{K12}\cup\mathrm{K3}\cup\mathrm{Num}^-$ fixes $\rho=(P(\mathrm{Ma})/P(\mathrm{Ea}))^{2/3}$ and hence every geocentric direction of Mars: it proves $\mathrm{Near}_g(\mathrm{Ma},t,\mathbf v,\delta)$ whenever the computed direction lies within $\delta$ of $\mathbf v$ and $B$ proves the numerical bound involved.
\item $\mathrm{K12}\cup\mathrm{Num}^-$ leaves $\rho$ free. At every $t$ at which $\mathbf r(\mathrm{Ea},t)$ and $\mathbf F(t)$ are not parallel, the geocentric directions of Mars at $t$ in the models of $\mathrm{K12}\cup\mathrm{Num}^-$ over $\mathbb R$ (with $\rho$ ranging over $(0,\infty)$) form the open great-circle arc from $-\mathbf u(t)$, the direction of the Sun, to $\mathbf F(t)/\lvert\mathbf F(t)\rvert$; its length $\alpha(t)\in(0,\pi)$ is the angle between these two directions. So for every $B$ true in $\mathbb R$, $B\cup\mathrm{K12}\cup\mathrm{Num}^-$ proves no datum $\mathrm{Near}_g(\mathrm{Ma},t,\mathbf v,\delta)$ with $\delta<\alpha(t)/2$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

A computation on the daily geocentric longitudes of Mars over 1580--1600 puts what $\mathrm{K3}$ saves on the elements of Mars at $11.3$, $12.3$, $13.4$ and $14.7$ bits at $\delta=8'$, $4'$, $2'$, $1'$, against a text of $375$ bits in our arithmetic syntax and $165$ or $115$ bits in two Polish-notation codes; so in none of these codes does compression alone select $\mathrm{K3}$ at Kepler's precision, an extrapolation from Mars to the other planets that is conditional on the code (\cref{ex:kepler:K3cost}).

$\mathrm{K3}$ is thus a law about initial conditions, worth what it saves on the elements of the bodies it relates. This depends on Kepler's kinematic parametrisation, with the period and the semi-major axis both free; in a state-space parametrisation $\mathrm{K3}$ fixes the mean motion from the energy, a different question, and in Newton's theory the third law, with its mass correction, follows from the one law that does all the other work and costs nothing extra.

\subsection{How wrong Kepler's laws are}\label{sec:kepler:wrong}

Kepler's laws are approximately true, with an error that grows with the domain: one fixed least-squares ellipse misses Mars by $1.6'$--$1.9'$ over 12--30 years and $51.8'$ over 2000 years, and the Moon by $1.1^\circ$ within a month (\cref{tab:kepler:residuals}). The third-law ``constant'' $q:=(2\pi/P)^2a^3/k^2$ ($k$ Gauss's constant) differs between planets at the $10^{-3}$ level, yet an exact $\mathrm{K3}$ calibrated on the Earth misplaces Jupiter and Saturn geocentrically by at most $0.23'$ and $0.19'$ over 1580--1600, well below Tycho's precision. By the accuracy quoted in the documentation of the ERFA routines \texttt{eraPlan94} and \texttt{eraMoon98} (which pyerfa wraps), plan94's own error in the heliocentric longitude of Mars over 1580--1600 is at most about $0.43'$ (\cref{sec:kepler:data}).

\subsection{The proof-requiring posterior over the historical Mars pool}\label{sec:kepler:pool}

\paragraph{The pool and the data.} We take a hand-picked pool of five forms of theory for Mars, as heliocentric reconstructions: the circle about the Sun (2 elements), the eccentric circle (4), Ptolemy's bisected equant (4), Kepler's vicarious hypothesis (5) and the ellipse $\mathrm{K12}$ (4) (\cref{sec:kepler:pooldetails}). The data are the 7306 daily plan94 heliocentric longitudes $v_i$ of Mars over 1580.0--1600.0, as sentences ``$\theta(t_i)$ lies within $\delta$ of $v_i$'': the noise-free case of the guaranteed-tolerance regime of \cref{prop:laws:tolerance}. A form proves every datum, for suitable elements, iff $\delta$ is at least its \emph{minimax error}, the least over elements of its largest error: $640.79'$, $23.05'$, $8.17'$, $1.41'$ and $1.07'$ in this order (upper bounds witnessed by elements, only the circle's proved optimal; \cref{tab:kepler:minimax}). The equant's $8'$ remains against an exact ellipse, close to the famous eight-minute discrepancy of Kepler's \emph{Astronomia Nova} \citep{kepler1992new}; the $0.34'$ between the vicarious hypothesis and the ellipse is of the size of plan94's error, so against the sky their ordering rests on the errors against an exact ellipse.

\paragraph{The posterior.} A theory is a law text in one of three syntaxes plus fixed-length 64-bit numerals for its elements; the proof-requiring rule of $\MA$ (\cref{def:setting:MA}), with the universal machine replaced by this code, gives it weight $2^{-\text{length}}$ if it proves every datum and $0$ otherwise. Summing over numerals, form $\mathsf F$ has weight proportional to $2^{-(\lvert\mathrm{LAW}_{\mathsf F}\rvert_S+\Occ_{\mathsf F}(\delta))}$, with $\lvert\mathrm{LAW}_{\mathsf F}\rvert_S$ the length of its law text in syntax $S$ and $\Occ_{\mathsf F}(\delta):=-\log(\mathrm{Vol}_{\mathsf F}(\delta)/\prod\text{ranges})$ its \emph{Occam factor}, $\mathrm{Vol}_{\mathsf F}(\delta)$ being the volume of the elements with which $\mathsf F$ proves all the data (the term is MacKay's \citep{mackay1992bayesian}; \citealp{jefferys1992ockham} read Bayes factors this way for Mercury's perihelion). The syntaxes are \emph{S1}, arithmetic ($+,-,\cdot,/,\sin,\cos,\mathrm{atan2},\sqrt{\ }$, the numerals $0,1,2$, quantifiers); \emph{S2}, epicyclic (S1 with primitives for unit vectors, directions, and the point where a ray meets a circle); and \emph{S3}, Keplerian (S1 with the true anomaly as a function of the eccentricity and the mean anomaly).

\paragraph{What the data decide and what the syntax decides} (\cref{tab:kepler:map,fig:kepler:map}). (i)~\emph{The data decide compatibility:} as $\delta$ falls the forms drop out in the historical order, in every syntax. (ii)~\emph{Among compatible forms the data contribute only the Occam factors, which on their own point weakly to the true form:} with a uniform prior over the five forms and no text prior, the MAP is the ellipse at every $\delta\le60'$, by $0.20$ to $14.61$ bits. (iii)~\emph{The text prior decides the rest:} law-text differences are $100$--$835$ bits for every pair of forms except the equant and the vicarious hypothesis, whose texts are $18$ bits apart in the epicyclic syntax, where the Occam factors move the margin by up to $5.6$ bits, so the text still decides, and of equal length in S1 and S3, where both lose to the ellipse by more than $400$ bits. So in this regime the syntax sets the sequence of MAP forms: with epicyclic primitives every form in the historical order, switching only when a form becomes incompatible; with plain arithmetic the eccentric, then the ellipse from $23'$; with a Keplerian primitive, the circle, then the ellipse from $641'$. A variable-length numeral code, Polish notation or a natural form outside the pool changes no MAP form (\cref{sec:kepler:pooldetails}).

``The simplest theory compatible with the data'' is thus, in this regime, relative to the language of the law texts. \status{assessment, resting on the computation of item (ii)}\src{philosophy \S1.4 item 4; \S4.4 Part A}A semanticist will call this relativity an artefact of a prior on texts. Our computation is mostly semantic: compatibility is a condition on a region of element space and the Occam factor is its measure, so only the law-text prior is syntactic; with a presentation-free prior, uniform over the five families and by volume on their elements, the data alone select the ellipse at every $\delta\le60'$, by small margins ((ii) above). But a prior over infinitely many families of models needs an enumeration of them, which is a presentation, and a volume prior depends on the parametrisation (halving the eccentricity ranges moves the vicarious hypothesis by one bit against the four-element forms), so the relativity is relocated rather than removed. The universal prior bounds this relativity by the cost $K(\tau)$ of a faithful translation (\cref{thm:basic:translation}), but that cost, here the definitions of the extra primitives in S1, is a few hundred bits (an estimate), as large as the text differences. Best-system accounts of laws face the same relativity (Lewis requires natural properties \citep{lewis1983new}; Cohen and Callender accept relativity to a chosen set of kinds \citep{cohen2009better}), by analogy only: best systems are a metaphysics of laws, \SAIabbr is inference from finite data. And the noise-free regime is special: a form proves all the data or none, so the power of the data to discriminate among compatible forms does not grow with their number.

\paragraph{Graded fit and distances.} With graded fit (a Gaussian error model with $\sigma=2'$, roughly Tycho's precision) the data decide every step within tens of observations, except the epicyclic syntax's choice between the vicarious hypothesis and the ellipse on longitudes, which needs about $5{,}300$ (\cref{ex:kepler:graded}). With distances within $1\%$ as data, compatibility alone eliminates the vicarious hypothesis below a longitude tolerance that is at most $5.12'$, in every syntax, which reproduces the abandonment of circles, not Kepler's step from an oval to the ellipse (\cref{tab:kepler:distances}).

\subsection{What Newton adds}\label{sec:kepler:newton}
\status{assessment, with computed figures and literature}\src{philosophy \S4.5}

Newton's theory adds masses, the constant $G$ and accelerations, with the law $m_b\ddot{\mathbf r}_b=\sum_{b'\ne b}Gm_bm_{b'}(\mathbf r_{b'}-\mathbf r_b)/\lvert\mathbf r_{b'}-\mathbf r_b\rvert^3$, in the same data language; masses are theoretical in Sneed's sense \citep{sneed1971logical,balzer1987architectonic}. We see five points, gains of Newtonian mechanics over about 150 years, not of Newton alone (Newton accounted for about half of the motion of the lunar apsides, Clairaut for the rest in 1749 \citep{nauenberg2015reception,bodenmann2010battle}). (1)~\emph{No gain on the old data:} on 20 years of longitudes at Kepler's precision the masses buy nothing measurable, so we think an \SAIabbr posterior on such data alone would not prefer Newton's theory. (2)~\emph{Unification,} where the gain lies: planets, Moon, falling bodies, tides and comets under one law (some with more base vocabulary), and the value $q=1+m/M$ of the third-law constant, which Jupiter's fitted $q$ matches. (3)~\emph{Corrections in the old domain:} in the classical decomposition (cf.\ \citealp{clemence1947relativity}), Newtonian planetary perturbations give about $531''$ of the fixed-frame advance of Mercury's perihelion, about $574''$ per century ($575.4''$ in our fits), and general relativity the remaining $43''$ or so; Laplace explained the great inequality of Jupiter and Saturn \citep{wilson1985great}. (4)~\emph{New questions:} in the old language, where Neptune is; in a new one, the masses of the Sun and of the planets with satellites. (5)~\emph{No deductive entailment:} Newton's axioms do not entail $\mathrm{K12}$ or $\mathrm{K3}$, and with the planets' masses they contradict their exact form, as \citet{duhem1954aim} observed; with masses and initial conditions they entail honest tolerance forms, ``$\mathrm{K12}$ holds within $\varepsilon(b,W)$ on the window $W$'' (\cref{prop:laws:correction} is a provable toy version). Formally deriving even one such bound needs an existence-and-uniqueness theorem and a validated integration or perturbation estimate, a very long derivation that no textbook writes out: a concrete case of the computational gap between a written theory and the reasoner who uses it (\cref{sec:limits:textbook}).
