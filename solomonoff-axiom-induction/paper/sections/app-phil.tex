\section{Proofs for what a theory does and is}\label{app:phil}

This appendix states and proves the results that \cref{sec:phil} summarises in prose. \Cref{app:phil:answers,app:phil:conservation,app:phil:extractor,app:phil:predicting} concern questions against raw data; \cref{app:phil:flagpole,app:phil:system} give the precise setting of the flagpole toys, state and prove \cref{prop:phil:flagpole}, and extend it to a system of laws.

\subsection{The answer distribution}\label{app:phil:answers}

Questions, and the answers a theory proves, are defined in \cref{def:setting:questions}; $[k]:=\{1,\dots,k\}$.

\begin{definition}[Answers]\label{def:phil:questions}
\status{definition}\src{philosophy Def 2.0}
A theory $T$ \emph{answers} a question $\mathcal Q=\{\varphi_1,\dots,\varphi_k\}$ if $B\cup T\vdash\varphi_i$ for some $i$, and \emph{answers it partially} if $B\cup T\vdash\bigvee_{i\in J}\varphi_i$ for some $J$ with $\emptyset\ne J\subsetneq[k]$.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[The answer distribution]\label{prop:phil:answers}
\status{proved; computed}\src{philosophy Prop 2.1}
Let $\MA(D)>0$, let $\mathcal Q=\{\varphi_1,\dots,\varphi_k\}$ be a question, and let $\mathrm{Un}_D(\mathcal Q):=\Unif(E_D\setminus\bigcup_iO_{\varphi_i})/\Unif(E_D)$, the posterior probability that the random theory proves no direct answer. (a) $\sum_{i\le k}\Bel_D(\varphi_i)+\mathrm{Un}_D(\mathcal Q)=1$. (b) For $\emptyset\ne J\subsetneq[k]$, $\Bel_D(\bigvee_{i\in J}\varphi_i)\ge\sum_{i\in J}\Bel_D(\varphi_i)$, and the difference is the posterior probability that the random theory answers $\mathcal Q$ partially through $J$ but proves no $\varphi_i$ with $i\in J$; for $J=[k]$, $\Bel_D(\bigvee_i\varphi_i)=1$ and the difference is $\mathrm{Un}_D(\mathcal Q)$. (c) For $\mathcal Q=\{\varphi,\neg\varphi\}$, (a) is the trichotomy $\Bel_D(\varphi)+\Dis_D(\varphi)+\Indep_D(\varphi)=1$ of \cref{prop:basic:structure}.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
On $E_D$ the theory $B\cup A_\omega$ is consistent. If it proved $\varphi_i$ and $\varphi_j$ with $i\ne j$, it would prove $\varphi_i\wedge\varphi_j$, while $B\vdash\neg(\varphi_i\wedge\varphi_j)$; so it proves at most one direct answer. Hence the sets $E_D\cap O_{\varphi_i}$, $i\le k$, are pairwise disjoint, and together with $E_D\setminus\bigcup_iO_{\varphi_i}$ they partition $E_D$. Dividing the measures by $\Unif(E_D)>0$ gives (a), since $\Bel_D(\varphi_i)=\Unif(E_D\cap O_{\varphi_i})/\Unif(E_D)$.

(b) For $i\in J$, $O_{\varphi_i}\subseteq O_{\bigvee_{j\in J}\varphi_j}$, and the sets $E_D\cap O_{\varphi_i}$ are pairwise disjoint; so
\[
\Unif\Big(E_D\cap O_{\bigvee_{j\in J}\varphi_j}\Big)=\sum_{i\in J}\Unif(E_D\cap O_{\varphi_i})+\Unif\Big(E_D\cap O_{\bigvee_{j\in J}\varphi_j}\setminus\bigcup_{i\in J}O_{\varphi_i}\Big).
\]
Divide by $\Unif(E_D)$: the last term is the posterior probability that the random theory proves $\bigvee_{j\in J}\varphi_j$, that is, answers $\mathcal Q$ partially through $J$, but proves no $\varphi_i$ with $i\in J$. For $J=[k]$, $B\vdash\bigvee_i\varphi_i$, so $E_D\subseteq O_{\bigvee_i\varphi_i}$ and $\Bel_D(\bigvee_i\varphi_i)=1$; by (a) the difference is $1-\sum_i\Bel_D(\varphi_i)=\mathrm{Un}_D(\mathcal Q)$.

(c) For $\mathcal Q=\{\varphi,\neg\varphi\}$ ($k=2$), $\Bel_D(\neg\varphi)=\Dis_D(\varphi)$ and $\mathrm{Un}_D(\mathcal Q)$ is the posterior probability that the random theory decides neither, which is $\Indep_D(\varphi)$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The conservation-law world}\label{app:phil:conservation}

For even $m$, micro-states are $x\in\{0,1\}^m$ with weight $\lvert x\rvert$; the dynamics $\sigma$ is a uniformly random permutation of each weight class, so the weight is conserved (the law); $x_0$ is uniform on the class of weight $m/2$, of size $Z:=\binom m{m/2}$, and $x_{t+1}:=\sigma(x_t)$. The raw predictors $\mathsf{Raw}^+$, $\mathsf{Raw}^-$ are Bayes for ``$\sigma$ is a uniformly random permutation of each weight class'', resp.\ ``of $\{0,1\}^m$'', and their step-$t$ loss is that of predicting $x_{t+1}$ from $x_0,\dots,x_t$. The questions are ``what is $\lvert x_t\rvert$?'', $t=0,1,\dots$; the question predictors $\mathsf{Qa}^+$, $\mathsf{Qa}^-$, $\mathsf{Qa}^{\rm bin}$ are Bayes for ``$\lvert x_t\rvert=\lvert x_0\rvert$, with $\lvert x_0\rvert$ uniform on $\{0,\dots,m\}$'', ``the $\lvert x_t\rvert$ are i.i.d.\ uniform on $\{0,\dots,m\}$'' and ``the $\lvert x_t\rvert$ are i.i.d.\ binomial$(m,\frac12)$''. In each task $\mathsf{Mix}$ gives prior $\frac12$ to each of the task's two hypotheses ($\mathsf{Raw}^\pm$, resp.\ $\mathsf{Qa}^\pm$). Let $t^*$ be the length of the cycle of $\sigma$ through $x_0$.

\begin{proposition}[The conservation-law world]\label{prop:phil:conservation}
\status{proved; computed}\src{philosophy Prop 2.2}
(a) For $t<t^*$ the step-$t$ losses of $\mathsf{Raw}^+$ and $\mathsf{Raw}^-$ are $\log(Z-t)$ and $\log(2^m-t)$; for $t\ge t^*$ both are $0$. The law's gain $\kappa_t:=\log\frac{2^m-t}{Z-t}$ ($t<t^*$) is nondecreasing in $t$; for $t\le Z/2$, $\log\frac{2^m}Z\le\kappa_t\le\log\frac{2^m}Z+1$, where $\log\frac{2^m}Z=\frac12\log\frac{\pi m}2+o(1)$, and the share $\kappa_t/\log(2^m-t)$ of the step loss is $O(\log m/m)$.
(b) $\mathsf{Qa}^+$ pays $\log(m+1)$ for $\lvert x_0\rvert$ and $0$ afterwards, $\mathsf{Qa}^-$ pays $\log(m+1)$ per question; over $t_{\max}+1$ questions the law's share of the no-law loss is $t_{\max}/(t_{\max}+1)$.
(c) In each task the cumulative loss of $\mathsf{Mix}$ exceeds that of the better of its two hypotheses by at most one bit, on every trajectory.
(d) For $t<t^*$, $\log(2^m-t)=\kappa_t+\log(Z-t)$ with $\kappa_t=-\log P_{\mathsf{Raw}^-}(\lvert x_{t+1}\rvert=m/2\mid x_0,\dots,x_t)$: the law's raw gain is exactly the loss of $\mathsf{Raw}^-$'s own answer to ``what is $\lvert x_{t+1}\rvert$?''. The loss of $\mathsf{Qa}^{\rm bin}$ on each question is $\kappa_0$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{lemma}[Next state of a random permutation]\label{lem:phil:permutation}
\status{proved}\src{philosophy Prop 2.2, proof of (a)}
Let $\sigma$ be a uniformly random permutation of a finite set $X$, and let $x_0,\dots,x_t\in X$ be distinct with $\sigma(x_s)=x_{s+1}$ for $s<t$. Conditionally on these $t$ values of $\sigma$, $\sigma(x_t)$ is uniform on $(X\setminus\{x_0,\dots,x_t\})\cup\{x_0\}$, a set of $\lvert X\rvert-t$ elements.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Conditionally on $\sigma(x_s)=x_{s+1}$ for $s<t$, the restriction of $\sigma$ to $X\setminus\{x_0,\dots,x_{t-1}\}$ is a uniformly random bijection onto $X\setminus\{x_1,\dots,x_t\}$, since every such bijection extends the given values to exactly one permutation. So $\sigma(x_t)$ is uniform on $X\setminus\{x_1,\dots,x_t\}=(X\setminus\{x_0,\dots,x_t\})\cup\{x_0\}$, which has $\lvert X\rvert-t$ elements.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{prop:phil:conservation}]
(a) For $t<t^*$ the states $x_0,\dots,x_t$ are distinct, all of weight $m/2$, and $x_{t+1}$ is either unvisited or $x_0$. Under the hypothesis of $\mathsf{Raw}^+$ the permutations of the weight classes are independent, and the trajectory stays in the class of $x_0$; \cref{lem:phil:permutation} with $X$ that class ($\lvert X\rvert=Z$) gives the predictive probability $1/(Z-t)$ of the true $x_{t+1}$, a step loss of $\log(Z-t)$. Under the hypothesis of $\mathsf{Raw}^-$ the lemma with $X=\{0,1\}^m$ gives $\log(2^m-t)$. For $t\ge t^*$ the path has returned to $x_0$, the cycle through $x_0$ is known to both predictors, and later states are determined: both losses are $0$.

Since $2^m>Z\ge t^*>t$, the map $t\mapsto(2^m-t)/(Z-t)$ is increasing on $[0,Z)$ (its derivative is $(2^m-Z)/(Z-t)^2>0$), so $\kappa_t$ is nondecreasing and $\kappa_t\ge\kappa_0=\log(2^m/Z)$. For $t\le Z/2$, $(2^m-t)/(Z-t)\le2^m/(Z/2)$, so $\kappa_t\le\log(2^m/Z)+1$. By Stirling's formula, $Z=\binom m{m/2}=2^m\sqrt{2/(\pi m)}\,(1+o(1))$, so $\log(2^m/Z)=\frac12\log\frac{\pi m}2+o(1)$. Finally $\kappa_t\le\log(2^m/Z)+1=O(\log m)$, and $\log(2^m-t)\ge\log(2^m-Z/2)\ge m-1$ since $Z\le2^m$; so the share is $O(\log m/m)$ for $t\le Z/2$.

(b) $\mathsf{Qa}^+$ predicts $\lvert x_0\rvert$ uniformly on $\{0,\dots,m\}$, a loss of $\log(m+1)$, and then $\lvert x_t\rvert=\lvert x_0\rvert$ with probability $1$, which is true since the weight is conserved: loss $0$. $\mathsf{Qa}^-$ pays $\log(m+1)$ on each question. Over the questions $t=0,\dots,t_{\max}$ the losses are $\log(m+1)$ and $(t_{\max}+1)\log(m+1)$, and the law saves the share $t_{\max}/(t_{\max}+1)$ of the latter.

(c) In each task $\mathsf{Mix}$ is the Bayes mixture with prior $\frac12$ on the task's two hypotheses; $x_0$ has the same law under both raw hypotheses, so the posterior after $x_0$ is still $(\frac12,\frac12)$. By the chain rule, the cumulative loss of a Bayes mixture is $-\log$ of the mixture probability of the whole sequence. If the two hypotheses give the sequence probabilities $2^{-a}$ and $2^{-b}$, the mixture's loss is $-\log(\frac122^{-a}+\frac122^{-b})\le-\log(\frac122^{-\min(a,b)})=\min(a,b)+1$, on every trajectory.

(d) Under $\mathsf{Raw}^-$, by \cref{lem:phil:permutation}, $x_{t+1}$ is uniform on a set of $2^m-t$ states, of which exactly $Z-t$ have weight $m/2$: the $Z-(t+1)$ unvisited states of the class of $x_0$, and $x_0$. So $P_{\mathsf{Raw}^-}(\lvert x_{t+1}\rvert=m/2\mid x_0,\dots,x_t)=(Z-t)/(2^m-t)=2^{-\kappa_t}$, and conditionally on that weight $x_{t+1}$ is uniform on those $Z-t$ states, which is exactly the predictive distribution of $\mathsf{Raw}^+$. The chain rule splits the step loss $\log(2^m-t)$ of $\mathsf{Raw}^-$ into $\kappa_t$, the loss of its prediction of the weight, and $\log(Z-t)$, the loss of $\mathsf{Raw}^+$. Finally, every $\lvert x_t\rvert$ equals $m/2$, and the binomial probability of $m/2$ is $Z2^{-m}$, so $\mathsf{Qa}^{\rm bin}$ pays $\log(2^m/Z)=\kappa_0$ on each question.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Computations.}\status{computed}\src{philosophy §2.2 (computed: c5 A-D, c9 (1))}
A computation (code in the accompanying repository, seeded) gives the following. At the start of a trajectory the law's gain per raw step is $1.871$, $2.148$, $2.348$, $2.837$, $3.331$, $3.829$, $5.326$ and $10.292$ bits at $m=8$, $12$, $16$, $32$, $64$, $128$, $1024$ and $10^6$, against $\frac12\log\frac{\pi m}2=1.826$, $2.118$, $2.326$, $2.826$, $3.326$, $3.826$, $5.326$, $10.292$; its share of the raw step loss is $0.23$, $0.18$, $0.15$, $0.089$, $0.052$, $0.030$, $0.0052$ and $1.0\times10^{-5}$. The value $\kappa_{Z/2}$ is $2.659$, $2.976$, $3.199$, $3.732$, $4.258$, $4.777$ and $6.308$ bits at $m=8,\dots,1024$; the loss of $\mathsf{Qa}^-$ per question is $3.170$, $3.700$, $4.088$, $5.044$, $6.022$, $7.011$ and $10.001$ bits, about twice $\kappa_0$. At $m=12$ ($Z=924$), averaging exactly over the cycle length (uniform on $\{1,\dots,Z\}$), the expected raw loss over $300$ steps is $2415.6$ bits with the law and $3004.9$ without (share $0.196$), and the mixture's is $2416.6$; over the $301$ questions the losses are $3.70$ and $1113.8$ bits (share $0.997$). \subsection{Questions from raw data and an extractor}\label{app:phil:extractor}

\paragraph{Setting.} Let $\mathbb X$ be a finite alphabet, $\mu$ a computable probability measure on $\mathbb X^{\mathbb N}$ (the raw world), $(\varphi_q)_{q\in\mathbb N}$ a fixed computable family of sentences (the questions), and $\eta$ a partial computable function (the \emph{extractor}, which reads the answers off the raw data) with $\eta(q,x)\in\{0,1\}$ computed from a finite prefix of $x$; put $f_x(\varphi_q):=\eta(q,x)$ and $\mu_\eta(D):=\mu\{x:f_x\supseteq D\}$.

\begin{proposition}[Raw modelling plus an extractor]\label{prop:phil:extractor}
\status{proved, given thm:basic:dominance and the standard sampling construction}\src{philosophy Prop 2.4}
If $f_x$ is consistent with $B$ for $\mu$-almost every $x$, then $\MA(D)\ge2^{-K(\mu)-K(\eta)-c}\mu_\eta(D)$ for every finite data set $D$ of question--answer pairs, so $\LA(D)\le-\log\mu_\eta(D)+K(\mu)+K(\eta)+c$; here $c$ depends also on the family $(\varphi_q)$. For a computable sequence $x$ with $f_x$ consistent with $B$, $\LA(D)\le K(x)+K(\eta)+c$ on every $D$ with $f_x\supseteq D$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Sampling.} There is a partial computable map $\Psi$ from tapes to $\mathbb X^{\mathbb N}$, computable from an index of $\mu$, that is defined for $\Unif$-almost every tape and satisfies $\Psi_*\Unif=\mu$. This is the standard construction: read tape bits to refine a dyadic interval, and output the next symbol $s$ as soon as the interval lies inside the $\mu$-interval of $s$ given the symbols already output; it fails only on the null set of tapes that hit interval boundaries. Each output symbol depends on a finite prefix of the tape.

\emph{The theory machine.} Let $G$ be the theory machine that, on a tape $\omega$, computes $\Psi(\omega)$ as far as needed and, for each $q$, emits $\varphi_q^{\eta(q,\Psi(\omega))}$ once the tape prefix read so far fixes a prefix of $\Psi(\omega)$ on which the computation of $\eta(q,\cdot)$ halts. Each emission depends on a finite prefix of $\omega$, so $G$ is a c.e.\ set of pairs (prefix, sentence) as \cref{def:setting:machine} requires, and $K(G)\le K(\mu)+K(\eta)+c$, where $c$ includes an index of the fixed family $(\varphi_q)$. If $\Psi(\omega)$ is defined, then $A^G_\omega=\Gamma_{f_{\Psi(\omega)}}$, the set of labelled sentences of the labelling $f_{\Psi(\omega)}$.

\emph{The bound.} Let $H$ be the set of $x$ for which $f_x$ is consistent with $B$; $\mu(H)=1$ by hypothesis. On every tape $\omega$ with $\Psi(\omega)$ defined, $\Psi(\omega)\in H$ and $f_{\Psi(\omega)}\supseteq D$, the theory $B\cup A^G_\omega$ is consistent and proves $\psi_D$, so $\omega\in E^G_D$. Hence $\nu_G(D)\ge\Unif\{\omega:\Psi(\omega)\in H,\ f_{\Psi(\omega)}\supseteq D\}=\mu\{x\in H:f_x\supseteq D\}=\mu_\eta(D)$. By \cref{thm:basic:dominance}, $\MA(D)\ge2^{-K(\nu_G)}\nu_G(D)\ge2^{-K(G)}\nu_G(D)\ge2^{-K(\mu)-K(\eta)-c}\mu_\eta(D)$, and taking $-\log$ gives the bound on $\LA$. For a computable sequence $x$, take $\mu=\delta_x$, with $K(\delta_x)\le K(x)+c$; the hypothesis is that $f_x$ is consistent with $B$, and $\mu_\eta(D)=1$ exactly when $f_x\supseteq D$.
\end{proof}

The constant depends on the question family because the theory machine must write the sentences $\varphi_q$. The converse of the proposition fails badly, since answers do not determine raw data; in the conservation-law world the raw loss exceeds the question loss by the whole micro-entropy (\cref{prop:phil:conservation}).

\subsection{Answering and predicting}\label{app:phil:predicting}

The following gives a formal vocabulary for ``answering questions is more than predicting data'': answering implies predicting, (a), but not conversely, (b), and success at predicting data that present a theory does not settle the questions the data leave open, (c). Part (d) is the coherence that a predictor of separate labels lacks.

\begin{proposition}[Answering and predicting]\label{prop:phil:predicting}
\status{(a)-(c) proved there; (d) proved; computed}\src{philosophy Prop 2.5}
(a) If a consistent r.e.\ theory $T$ proves every datum of a consistent reveal sequence, the cumulative loss of $\MA$ is at most $-\log\UA(\Exts T)\le\Keq(T)+c$ in the semimeasure convention, in any order.
(b) On the lottery stream of \cref{thm:logic:lottery}, $\MA$ has bounded loss in the semimeasure convention, while no consistent r.e.\ theory proves every datum and $\WA(D_n)\to0$.
(c) On two-valued data presenting an incomplete consistent r.e.\ theory $T^*$, the limit posterior is the prior restricted to the consistent extensions of $T^*$, and on every sentence independent of $B\cup T^*$ all three values $\Bel$, $\Dis$, $\Indep$ have positive limits; three-valued data identify the class of $T^*$.
(d) For all $D$ with $\MA(D)>0$ and all sentences, $\Bel_D(\bigwedge_{i\le k}\varphi_i)\ge1-\sum_{i\le k}(1-\Bel_D(\varphi_i))$, and $\Bel_D(\psi)\ge\Bel_D(\varphi)$ whenever $B\cup\{\varphi\}\vdash\psi$. So the sentences with belief at least $1-\delta$ are closed under entailment and, with error $k\delta$, under $k$-fold conjunction.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{prop:phil:predicting}]
(a) By \cref{prop:basic:structure}(d) the cumulative loss in the semimeasure convention is $\LA(D_n)-\LA(\emptyset)$, in any order, and by \cref{thm:basic:loss} it is at most $-\log\UA(\Exts T)\le\Keq(T)+c$.

(b) In the setting of \cref{thm:logic:lottery}, part (a) of that theorem bounds $\MA(D_n)$ below by a positive constant independent of $n$, so the cumulative loss $\LA(D_n)-\LA(\emptyset)$ is bounded; part (b) says that no consistent r.e.\ theory proves every datum (with $B$); and part (c) gives $\WA(D_n)\to0$.

(c) This is \cref{prop:basic:limits}(b), (c): if $B\cup T^*$ is incomplete and $\Cn(D_\infty^\pm)=\Cn(T^*)$, the limit posterior is the prior restricted to the consistent extensions of $T^*$, and for every $\psi$ independent of $B\cup T^*$ the limits of $\Bel_{D_n}(\psi)$ and $\Dis_{D_n}(\psi)$ lie in $(0,1)$ while $\lim_n\Indep_{D_n}(\psi)\ge\UA[T^*]/\UA(\Exts T^*)>0$; and if every sentence appears with its $T^*$-trichotomy label, the posterior probability of the class of $T^*$ tends to $1$.

(d) A theory that proves $\varphi$ and $\psi$ proves $\varphi\wedge\psi$, so $O_\varphi\cap O_\psi\subseteq O_{\varphi\wedge\psi}$. Within $E_D$,
\[
\Unif(O_\varphi\cap O_\psi\cap E_D)\ge\Unif(O_\varphi\cap E_D)+\Unif(O_\psi\cap E_D)-\Unif(E_D);
\]
dividing by $\Unif(E_D)$ gives $\Bel_D(\varphi\wedge\psi)\ge\Bel_D(\varphi)+\Bel_D(\psi)-1$, and induction on $k$ gives $\Bel_D(\bigwedge_{i\le k}\varphi_i)\ge1-\sum_{i\le k}(1-\Bel_D(\varphi_i))$. If $B\cup\{\varphi\}\vdash\psi$, then $O_\varphi\subseteq O_\psi$, so $\Bel_D(\psi)\ge\Bel_D(\varphi)$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The flagpole}\label{app:phil:flagpole}

\paragraph{Setting.} The language has two sorts, poles and reals; $B$ is the theory of real closed fields on the real sort. There are pole constants $p_1,\dots,p_{N_{\rm p}}$, a function symbol $\mathrm{ht}$ from poles to reals (the height) and a function symbol $\mathrm{sh}$ from poles and reals to reals ($\mathrm{sh}(p,u)$ is the length of the shadow of $p$ when the tangent of the sun's elevation is $u>0$). Data are sentences $\mathrm{sh}(p_i,u)=v$ and $\mathrm{ht}(p_i)=v$ with rational numerals $u,v$; interval versions behave in the same way. The two formulations of the shadow law are
\begin{align*}
\Lambda_h&:=\forall p\,\forall u\,\big(u>0\to\mathrm{sh}(p,u)\cdot u=\mathrm{ht}(p)\big),\\
\Lambda_s&:=\forall p\,\forall u\,\big(u>0\to\mathrm{sh}(p,u)\cdot u=\mathrm{sh}(p,1)\big)\ \wedge\ \forall p\,\mathrm{ht}(p)=\mathrm{sh}(p,1),
\end{align*}
and, with rational numerals $a_i$ for the heights, the four theories are
\begin{align*}
T_H&:=\Lambda_h+\{\mathrm{ht}(p_i)=a_i\}_{i\le N_{\rm p}}, & T_S&:=\Lambda_h+\{\mathrm{sh}(p_i,1)=a_i\}_{i\le N_{\rm p}},\\
T'_H&:=\Lambda_s+\{\mathrm{ht}(p_i)=a_i\}_{i\le N_{\rm p}}, & T'_S&:=\Lambda_s+\{\mathrm{sh}(p_i,1)=a_i\}_{i\le N_{\rm p}}.
\end{align*}
In $T_S$ the shadow at $45^\circ$ ``explains'' the height. The \emph{intervention vocabulary} consists of $\mathrm{sh}_H(p,u,v)$, the shadow length after the height of $p$ is set to $v$, and $\mathrm{ht}_S(p,v)$, the height after the reference shadow $\mathrm{sh}(p,1)$ is set to $v$ by an intervention on the shadow (for instance, by an obstacle). The causal extensions, with the height exogenous and with the shadow exogenous, are
\begin{align*}
C_H&:=T_H+\big\{\forall p\,\forall u\,\forall v\,\big(u>0\to\mathrm{sh}_H(p,u,v)\cdot u=v\big),\ \forall p\,\forall v\,\mathrm{ht}_S(p,v)=\mathrm{ht}(p)\big\},\\
C_S&:=T_S+\big\{\forall p\,\forall u\,\forall v\,\mathrm{sh}_H(p,u,v)=\mathrm{sh}(p,u),\ \forall p\,\forall v\,\mathrm{ht}_S(p,v)=v\big\}.
\end{align*}

\paragraph{Derivation charges.} The cost of a derivation is the number of closed instances it uses of the universally quantified conjuncts of the law axioms ($\Lambda_h$ has one such conjunct, $\Lambda_s$ two); steps in $B$ and uses of the initial conditions are free. The \emph{least cost} of a datum for a theory is the least number of such instances that, together with $B$ and the theory's initial conditions, entail it. By completeness this is the least cost of a derivation, so the count is semantic, as in Herbrand's theorem.

\begin{proposition}[The flagpole]\label{prop:phil:flagpole}
\status{proved; computed}\src{philosophy Prop 3.1}
(a) $T_H$, $T_S$, $T'_H$, $T'_S$ are deductively equivalent over $B$; so every data set is compatible with all four or with none, in the coin-free version the posterior odds of any two programs for them equal their prior odds on every data set compatible with them, and $\lvert K(T_S)-K(T_H)\rvert\le c$. (b) Under derivation charges, every shadow datum $\mathrm{sh}(p_i,u)=v$ with $u\ne1$ that they entail needs exactly one instance from $T_H$ and $T'_S$, whose initial conditions sit at the hub of their formulation, and exactly two from $T_S$ and $T'_H$: which quantity is the hub is a property of the formulation, not of the world. (c) $C_H$ and $C_S$ have the same consequences in the old vocabulary, and one interventional datum separates them.
\end{proposition}

The precise form of part (b) is as follows. For a shadow datum $\mathrm{sh}(p_i,u)=a_i/u$ with $u\ne1$, the least costs for $T_H$, $T_S$, $T'_H$, $T'_S$ are $1$, $2$, $2$, $1$; for a height datum $\mathrm{ht}(p_i)=a_i$ they are $0$, $1$, $0$, $1$; and for a reference-shadow datum $\mathrm{sh}(p_i,1)=a_i$ they are $1$, $0$, $1$, $0$. So the two hub theories $T_H$ and $T'_S$ have equal costs on all shadow data with $u\ne1$. Their law texts differ by the constant $\lvert\Lambda_s\rvert-\lvert\Lambda_h\rvert$, and in a literal code (\cref{app:phil:system}) their initial conditions differ by $N_{\rm p}\Delta_{\rm sh}$, where $\Delta_{\rm sh}:=\lvert\mathrm{sh}(x,1)\rvert-\lvert\mathrm{ht}(x)\rvert$ is the extra length of the reference-shadow term; this is $0$ if the language has a primitive for the reference shadow, and then the two texts differ by $\lvert\Lambda_s\rvert-\lvert\Lambda_h\rvert$, independently of $N_{\rm p}$ and the data. The precise form of (c) is: $C_H$ and $C_S$ have the same consequences in the language without $\mathrm{sh}_H$ and $\mathrm{ht}_S$; $C_H\vdash\mathrm{sh}_H(p_1,1,2a_1)=2a_1$ (``doubling the pole doubles its $45^\circ$ shadow''), while $C_S\vdash\mathrm{sh}_H(p_1,1,2a_1)=a_1$, which refutes it when $a_1\ne0$; and $C_H\vdash\mathrm{ht}_S(p_1,v)=a_1$ while $C_S\vdash\mathrm{ht}_S(p_1,v)=v$, for every numeral $v$.

\begin{lemma}[Least costs in a star]\label{lem:phil:star}
\status{proved}\src{philosophy Props 3.1(b), 3.2(d), proofs of the lower bounds}
Let $T$ be one of the theories of this appendix without the dark poles: law axioms whose universally quantified conjuncts have closed instances of the form $\pi\to s_1=\rho\cdot s_2$, with $\pi$ a condition on real numerals, $s_1,s_2$ terms of one pole and $\rho$ a real term that $B$ proves nonzero when $\pi$ holds, and one initial condition $s(p_i)=a_i$ per pole. For a pole $p_i$, let the \emph{instance graph} have as vertices the closed terms $\mathrm{ht}(p_i)$, $\mathrm{sh}(p_i,u)$ (terms whose real arguments $B$ proves equal are identified) and, in \cref{app:phil:system}, $\mathrm{qt}_j(p_i)$, and an edge for each closed instance whose condition $B$ proves, joining the two terms it relates (an instance that relates a term to itself is trivial and is ignored). If the instance graph is a tree, the least cost of a datum $s'(p_i)=v$ that $T$ entails is the distance in the graph from $s'(p_i)$ to the vertex fixed by the initial condition.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The instances along the path from the fixed vertex to $s'(p_i)$, together with $B$ and the initial condition, determine the value of $s'(p_i)$; since $T$ entails the datum, that value is $v$, so the distance is an upper bound. Conversely, let $I$ be a set of fewer instances than the distance. As the graph is a tree, the edges of $I$ do not connect $s'(p_i)$ to the fixed vertex. Fix a model of $T$ over $\mathbb R$ (each theory of this appendix has one). Build a model of $B$, the initial conditions and $I$ over $\mathbb R$ as follows: on the connected component (for the edges of $I$) of the fixed vertex, and on every term of another pole, keep the values of the fixed model; on the component of $s'(p_i)$, give $s'(p_i)$ a value other than $v$ and propagate it along the edges of $I$, which is consistent because the component is a tree and each edge is an invertible linear relation; give all other terms arbitrary values. Instances with a false condition hold trivially. This model falsifies the datum, so $I$ does not entail it.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{prop:phil:flagpole}]
(a) At $u=1$, $\Lambda_h$ gives $\mathrm{sh}(p,1)=\mathrm{ht}(p)$; given this, $\mathrm{sh}(p,u)\cdot u=\mathrm{ht}(p)$ and $\mathrm{sh}(p,u)\cdot u=\mathrm{sh}(p,1)$ are equivalent. So $B\vdash\Lambda_h\to\Lambda_s$; conversely $\Lambda_s$ gives $\mathrm{ht}(p)=\mathrm{sh}(p,1)$ and hence $\Lambda_h$. Given either law, the initial conditions $\mathrm{ht}(p_i)=a_i$ and $\mathrm{sh}(p_i,1)=a_i$ are interderivable, so the four theories are deductively equivalent over $B$. Compatibility of a theory with a data set (consistent with $B$ and proving every datum) depends only on its deductive closure, so every data set is compatible with all four or with none. In the coin-free version the posterior weight of a program is $2^{-\lvert p\rvert}$ times the indicator of compatibility, divided by the normaliser, so the odds of two compatible programs are their prior odds. The map on axiom lists that replaces each $\mathrm{ht}(p_i)=a_i$ by $\mathrm{sh}(p_i,1)=a_i$, and its inverse, are fixed computable maps, so $\lvert K(T_S)-K(T_H)\rvert\le c$.

(b) For each pole, the instances of $\Lambda_h$ join $\mathrm{ht}(p_i)$ to each $\mathrm{sh}(p_i,u)$ (a star centred at the height), and the instances of $\Lambda_s$ join $\mathrm{sh}(p_i,1)$ to each $\mathrm{sh}(p_i,u)$ with $u\ne1$ and to $\mathrm{ht}(p_i)$ (a star centred at the reference shadow); instances with $u\le0$ have false conditions, and instances at other poles are not connected to $p_i$. Both graphs are trees, and \cref{lem:phil:star} gives the counts: in the height-centred star with the height fixed ($T_H$), shadows and the reference shadow are at distance $1$; with the reference shadow fixed ($T_S$), a shadow with $u\ne1$ is at distance $2$ and the height at $1$; in the shadow-centred star with the height fixed ($T'_H$), a shadow with $u\ne1$ is at distance $2$ and the reference shadow at $1$; with the reference shadow fixed ($T'_S$), shadows and the height are at distance $1$. The law texts of $T_H$ and $T'_S$ are $\Lambda_h$ and $\Lambda_s$; their initial conditions differ only in the terms $\mathrm{ht}(p_i)$ and $\mathrm{sh}(p_i,1)$, once per pole.

(c) Without the intervention symbols, $C_H$ and $C_S$ are $T_H$ and $T_S$ plus axioms about fresh symbols. Every model of $T_H$ expands to a model of $C_H$ (put $\mathrm{sh}_H(p,u,v):=v/u$ for $u>0$, arbitrary otherwise, and $\mathrm{ht}_S(p,v):=\mathrm{ht}(p)$), and every model of $T_S$ to a model of $C_S$ (put $\mathrm{sh}_H(p,u,v):=\mathrm{sh}(p,u)$ and $\mathrm{ht}_S(p,v):=v$). So $C_H$ and $C_S$ are conservative over $T_H$ and $T_S$, whose consequences coincide by (a). The interventional consequences are instances of the added axioms: in $C_H$, $\mathrm{sh}_H(p_1,1,2a_1)\cdot1=2a_1$ and $\mathrm{ht}_S(p_1,v)=\mathrm{ht}(p_1)=a_1$; in $C_S$, $\mathrm{sh}_H(p_1,1,2a_1)=\mathrm{sh}(p_1,1)=a_1$ and $\mathrm{ht}_S(p_1,v)=v$. If $a_1\ne0$ then $2a_1\ne a_1$, so $C_S$ refutes the datum $\mathrm{sh}_H(p_1,1,2a_1)=2a_1$ that $C_H$ proves.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Computations.}\status{computed}\src{philosophy §3.2 (computed: c6)}
A computation (code in the accompanying repository) confirms the counts by exhaustive search over all subsets of finite instance pools, with exact linear algebra over the rationals, on $500$ random data sets with one to four poles (shadows at $u=2$, and at $u=3$ in a second implementation); on $2000$ random data the four theories prove, refute or leave open exactly the same data. In the literal syntax of that code (numerals counted as one token), $\lvert\Lambda_s\rvert-\lvert\Lambda_h\rvert=20$ tokens and $\Delta_{\rm sh}=1$ token, so $T_S$ is longer than $T_H$ by one token per pole.

\subsection{The flagpole with a system of laws}\label{app:phil:system}

Kitcher's answer to the asymmetry appeals to other laws in which the height figures, which the toy of \cref{prop:phil:flagpole} lacks. We add such laws.

\paragraph{Setting.} As in \cref{app:phil:flagpole}, with further function symbols $\mathrm{qt}_1,\dots,\mathrm{qt}_k$ from poles to reals (further quantities: weight, the height on the construction plan, and so on), nonzero rational numerals $\theta_1,\dots,\theta_k$, and data $\mathrm{qt}_j(p_i)=v$. The laws in height form and in shadow form are $\Lambda^{\rm ht}_j:=\forall p\,\mathrm{qt}_j(p)=\theta_j\cdot\mathrm{ht}(p)$ and $\Lambda^{\rm sh}_j:=\forall p\,\mathrm{qt}_j(p)=\theta_j\cdot\mathrm{sh}(p,1)$. The theories are the height hub $T^k_H:=\Lambda_h+\{\Lambda^{\rm ht}_j\}_{j\le k}+\{\mathrm{ht}(p_i)=a_i\}_i$, the mixed theory $T^k_S:=\Lambda_h+\{\Lambda^{\rm ht}_j\}_j+\{\mathrm{sh}(p_i,1)=a_i\}_i$, and the shadow hub $T^k_{SS}:=\Lambda_s+\{\Lambda^{\rm sh}_j\}_j+\{\mathrm{sh}(p_i,1)=a_i\}_i$.

\emph{Dark variant.} Add a predicate $\mathrm{Sunlit}$ on poles, restrict the shadow law to sunlit poles, $\Lambda^{\rm D}_h:=\forall p\,\big(\mathrm{Sunlit}(p)\to\forall u\,(u>0\to\mathrm{sh}(p,u)\cdot u=\mathrm{ht}(p))\big)$, and take sunlit poles $p_1,\dots,p_{N_{\rm p}}$ and dark poles $p'_1,\dots,p'_{N_{\rm d}}$, with the axioms $\mathrm{Sunlit}(p_i)$ and $\neg\mathrm{Sunlit}(p'_l)$ in every theory and rational numerals $a'_l$ for the heights of the dark poles. Laws in shadow form must split cases:
\[
\Lambda^{\rm sh,D}_j:=\forall p\,\Big(\big(\mathrm{Sunlit}(p)\to\mathrm{qt}_j(p)=\theta_j\cdot\mathrm{sh}(p,1)\big)\wedge\big(\neg\mathrm{Sunlit}(p)\to\mathrm{qt}_j(p)=\theta_j\cdot\mathrm{ht}(p)\big)\Big).
\]
The theories are $T^{k,\rm D}_H:=\Lambda^{\rm D}_h+\{\Lambda^{\rm ht}_j\}_j+\{\mathrm{ht}(p_i)=a_i\}_i+\{\mathrm{ht}(p'_l)=a'_l\}_l$ and $T^{k,\rm D}_{SS}:=\Lambda^{\rm D}_h+\{\Lambda^{\rm sh,D}_j\}_j+\{\mathrm{sh}(p_i,1)=a_i\}_i+\{\mathrm{ht}(p'_l)=a'_l\}_l$, each with the $\mathrm{Sunlit}$ axioms. A \emph{literal code} charges a theory by the number of symbols it writes, each axiom separately, without definitions; $\lvert\cdot\rvert$ denotes this length, a symbol $\mathrm{qt}_j$ counts as one token, and $\Delta_{\rm sh}:=\lvert\mathrm{sh}(x,1)\rvert-\lvert\mathrm{ht}(x)\rvert$ is the extra length of the reference-shadow term ($\Delta_{\rm sh}=0$ if the language has a primitive for the reference shadow).

\begin{proposition}[The flagpole with a system of laws]\label{prop:phil:flagpolesystem}
\status{proved}\src{philosophy Prop 3.2}
(a) \emph{Class tie.} $T^k_H$, $T^k_S$ and $T^k_{SS}$ are deductively equivalent over $B$, and so are $T^{k,\rm D}_H$ and $T^{k,\rm D}_{SS}$; so for every $k$ no data set separates them, and their deductive classes coincide.
(b) \emph{Universal prior.} $\lvert K(T^k_{SS})-K(T^k_H)\rvert\le c$ and $\lvert K(T^{k,\rm D}_{SS})-K(T^{k,\rm D}_H)\rvert\le c$, with $c$ independent of $k$, $N_{\rm p}$, $N_{\rm d}$ and the numerals.
(c) \emph{Literal codes.} $\lvert T^k_{SS}\rvert-\lvert T^k_H\rvert=(\lvert\Lambda_s\rvert-\lvert\Lambda_h\rvert)+(k+N_{\rm p})\Delta_{\rm sh}$. In the dark variant, $\lvert T^{k,\rm D}_{SS}\rvert-\lvert T^{k,\rm D}_H\rvert=k\,\Delta_{\rm split}+\sum_{j\le k}\lvert\theta_j\rvert+N_{\rm p}\Delta_{\rm sh}$, where $\Delta_{\rm split}>0$ is the length of the case-split frame and $\lvert\theta_j\rvert$ the length of the numeral $\theta_j$, which the split law writes twice; so the shadow hub loses by at least $k\,\Delta_{\rm split}$, an amount growing with $k$, even when $\Delta_{\rm sh}=0$. With one definition the overhead of the laws is a constant: define $\mathrm{z}(p)$ once as $\mathrm{sh}(p,1)$ on sunlit poles and $\mathrm{ht}(p)$ on dark ones, and write every law in $\mathrm{z}$; in every model of the theory, $\mathrm{z}$ is coextensive with $\mathrm{ht}$.
(d) \emph{Derivation charges} (instances of universally quantified conjuncts, as in \cref{app:phil:flagpole}). A datum $\mathrm{qt}_j(p_i)=\theta_ja_i$ has least cost $1$ for $T^k_H$ and $T^k_{SS}$, and $2$ for the mixed theory $T^k_S$. On shadow data with $u\ne1$ the two hub theories have least cost $1$ each; on heights $T^k_H$ has $0$ and $T^k_{SS}$ has $1$; on reference shadows the reverse.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(a) Each theory contains a form of the shadow law, which gives $B\vdash\mathrm{ht}(p)=\mathrm{sh}(p,1)$ for every pole $p$ (for every sunlit pole in the dark variant), as in the proof of \cref{prop:phil:flagpole}(a). Substituting, each law in shadow form is equivalent to the law in height form, and each reference-shadow initial condition to the corresponding height condition. In the dark variant, the case split collapses to $\mathrm{qt}_j(p)=\theta_j\cdot\mathrm{ht}(p)$, because on sunlit poles $\mathrm{sh}(p,1)=\mathrm{ht}(p)$ by $\Lambda^{\rm D}_h$ at $u=1$.

(b) The syntactic translations and their inverses are fixed computable maps on axiom lists: from $T^k_H$ to $T^k_{SS}$, replace $\Lambda_h$ by $\Lambda_s$, each $\Lambda^{\rm ht}_j$ by $\Lambda^{\rm sh}_j$ and each $\mathrm{ht}(p_i)=a_i$ by $\mathrm{sh}(p_i,1)=a_i$; from $T^{k,\rm D}_H$ to $T^{k,\rm D}_{SS}$, replace each $\Lambda^{\rm ht}_j$ by $\Lambda^{\rm sh,D}_j$ (reading $\theta_j$ and $\mathrm{qt}_j$ off the axiom) and the initial condition of each pole declared sunlit by its reference-shadow form. So a program for one list yields a program for the other at a constant extra length, independent of $k$, $N_{\rm p}$, $N_{\rm d}$ and the numerals.

(c) Counting symbols: each $\Lambda^{\rm sh}_j$ differs from $\Lambda^{\rm ht}_j$ by one occurrence of the replaced term, and so does each sunlit initial condition; this gives $(k+N_{\rm p})\Delta_{\rm sh}$ besides the law texts. Each $\Lambda^{\rm sh,D}_j$ is longer than $\Lambda^{\rm ht}_j$ by a fixed frame of length $\Delta_{\rm split}>0$ (the connectives, the two $\mathrm{Sunlit}$ atoms, a second copy of $\mathrm{qt}_j(p)=\cdot$, and the term $\mathrm{sh}(p,1)$) and by a second copy of the numeral $\theta_j$; both dark theories contain $\Lambda^{\rm D}_h$, the dark initial conditions and the $\mathrm{Sunlit}$ axioms. The definition of $\mathrm{z}$ is a single axiom of constant length, and laws written in $\mathrm{z}$ have the length of the laws written in $\mathrm{ht}$. In every model of the theory, $\mathrm{z}(p)=\mathrm{sh}(p,1)=\mathrm{ht}(p)$ on sunlit poles by $\Lambda^{\rm D}_h$ at $u=1$, and $\mathrm{z}(p)=\mathrm{ht}(p)$ on dark poles by the definition.

(d) For each pole the instance graphs are trees: for $T^k_H$ and $T^k_S$, a star centred at $\mathrm{ht}(p_i)$ with leaves $\mathrm{sh}(p_i,u)$ and $\mathrm{qt}_j(p_i)$; for $T^k_{SS}$, a star centred at $\mathrm{sh}(p_i,1)$ with leaves $\mathrm{sh}(p_i,u)$ ($u\ne1$), $\mathrm{ht}(p_i)$ and $\mathrm{qt}_j(p_i)$. \Cref{lem:phil:star} gives the counts: $\mathrm{qt}_j(p_i)$ is at distance $1$ from the fixed centre in $T^k_H$ and $T^k_{SS}$, and at distance $2$ from the fixed leaf $\mathrm{sh}(p_i,1)$ in $T^k_S$; the shadow, height and reference-shadow counts are those of \cref{prop:phil:flagpole}(b) for $T_H$ and $T'_S$.
\end{proof}
