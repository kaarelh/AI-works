\section{What a theory does and what it is}\label{sec:phil}

What does a scientific theory do? It may predict observations, the view behind Solomonoff induction \citep{solomonoff1964formal}; answer questions posed in some language, why-questions included \citep[ch.~5]{vanfraassen1980scientific}; explain \citep{hempel1948studies}; or solve problems \citep{laudan1977progress}. And what is it? A program that predicts observations; a set of sentences closed under consequence \citep{suppe1977structure}; a class of models \citep{suppes1960comparison,vanfraassen1980scientific}; a core of models with intended applications \citep{sneed1971logical,balzer1987architectonic}; or a practice of exemplars and skills \citep{kuhn1970structure,kuhn1977second,polanyi1958personal}. The axiom view says that a theory answers questions posed in a given language and is a logical theory; the raw-data view, that it predicts observations and is a program. This section assesses the axiom view against the theorems of this paper. Its propositions make claims precise enough to test (proofs in \cref{app:phil}); the rest is argument.

\subsection{An argument map}\label{sec:phil:map}

We separate five claims. \textbf{D}: a theory answers questions posed in a given language rather than predicting raw data; we read it comparatively, as a step away from raw-data prediction, not the end point. \textbf{S}: a theory is a logical theory, not a program. \textbf{E}: theories explain propositions. \textbf{L}: the ladder of \cref{sec:intro} (raw data; questions posed with existing abstractions; theories that extend the language; theories that revise the base abstractions). \textbf{P}: \SAIabbr is progress over raw-data prediction, both in what a theory does and in what it is. \Cref{tab:phil:map} gives our assessments.

\paragraph{What rests on theorems.}\status{assessment}\src{philosophy §1.1-§1.7; syn §2.2, §2.8}
The following rest on theorems in stated settings: the idealised formalism cannot see the difference between a theory and a program (\cref{sec:phil:craig}); under a cost on the written theory, usefulness depends exponentially on the pair (reasoner, vocabulary), and on declarative against procedural form only by a constant factor once intermediate concepts are available and the reasoner can chain through them (in the model of \cref{sec:shape}, and open beyond it); theoretical vocabulary is dispensable in the ideal mixture, while relations or entities pay for finite theories and under charged deduction (\cref{sec:terms}); answering questions differs from raw-data prediction in target, data type, coherence and partiality, not in the inference principle (\cref{sec:phil:questions}); "Kepler's laws are only approximately right" is a limitation of proof-requiring compatibility with deterministic theories, not of the axiom view (\cref{sec:laws}). Computed, conditional on the pool of forms and the codes: simplicity among compatible forms is relative to the language on noise-free interval data, and with graded fit the data decide (\cref{sec:kepler}). That Hempel's inductive-systematisation reply fails in the idealised setting is a theorem together with an identification that is an argument (\cref{rem:phil:hempel}). Arguments only: the descriptive observation; why declarative shape is good (open); that D survives the objection from statistical practice; the general claims about explanation (the toys are proved); that the axiom view misses the procedural, statistical and dynamic sides of theories (\cref{sec:limits}); and the recommendation, resting on a possibility result (\cref{cor:logic:false}), to say nothing realist about induced theories.

\paragraph{The ladder.}\status{assessment}\src{philosophy §1.6}
We agree with L. The base language of step 2 may be highly theoretical (heliocentric longitudes presuppose Copernican geometry); no theory-neutral observation language is claimed, so the classical objections to one do not apply in the same way. But theory-ladenness \citep{hanson1958patterns} biases comparisons between theories that disagree about the base language, since a theory that revises it is scored in a language it rejects; this is why step 4 is listed.

\subsection{The descriptive observation and the explanatory question}\label{sec:phil:observation}

Claim S combines a description with an explanation, which we keep apart.\status{assessment; open}\src{philosophy §1.2, §1.4; syn §2.3}
\emph{The observation:} textbook presentations of fundamental theories are declarative. They state laws, definitions, principles, and models given by equations over mathematical structures, not procedures that print predictions. This needs no theory of why it holds. Two qualifications. \emph{Declarative is not sentential:} physics texts write something closer to a class of models specified by equations \citep{suppes1960comparison,suppes1962models} than to first-order axioms. We use sentences because questions and data are sentences and the prior of \SAIabbr is over texts; the question-answering view is neutral between sentences and classes of models, since in partition semantics a question is a partition of logical space (cf.\ \citealp{groenendijk1984studies}). \emph{Theories in use have procedural parts:} methods attached to concepts, numerical schemes, perturbation series, simulation codes; a "level of theory" in quantum chemistry is a computational method, and even one position of Mars from Kepler's laws needs root-finding for Kepler's equation. So the observation concerns textbook presentations, not theories in all their uses.

\emph{The question}, why the declarative shape is good and whether an ideal inducer should produce it, is open. Craig's construction rules out an answer based only on what can be represented (\cref{sec:phil:craig}), so an answer must be relative to a reasoner with costs, or to a community of reasoners. In the model of \cref{sec:shape}, the best formal answer we have, what matters is a reasoner that can do algebra or chain through intermediate concepts, together with those concepts, not logical form as such. The explanation may lie in communication and piecemeal checking, for which there is no formal setting yet.

\begin{question}[Why declarative shape?]\label{q:phil:declarative}
\status{open}\src{syn §6 item 26; philosophy §1.2, §5 (L11)}
Is there a formal setting for a community of bounded reasoners who communicate, check and extend theories piece by piece, in which theories of declarative shape provably do better than programs?
\end{question}

A natural argument for S is that a theory answers in many directions (Kepler's third law gives the period from the semi-major axis and the semi-major axis from the period; \cref{def:kepler:language}), while a program computes one direction cheaply. The second half holds of particular programs, not of programs: a generic solver applied to a declarative law costs $O(1)$ extra length, a program with internal variables answers every direction of a parity law in $O(m)$ operations, and under program length directions cost nothing (\cref{thm:shape:invisible,thm:shape:directions}). Directions matter only for a fixed weak reasoner and a cost on the written theory: a direction-complete rule base for one parity law needs exactly $m$ times the clauses of the law, $m$ the number of directions, without intermediate concepts or with one round of reasoning, and at most $9$ times its written length with intermediate concepts and unbounded rounds (\cref{thm:shape:directions}).

\subsection{What a theory does: questions against raw data}\label{sec:phil:questions}

A question is a finite set $\mathcal Q=\{\varphi_1,\dots,\varphi_k\}$ of pairwise $B$-incompatible sentences whose disjunction $B$ proves (\cref{def:setting:questions}); $[k]:=\{1,\dots,k\}$. Following the logic of questions (cf.\ \citealp{belnap1976logic,hintikka1999inquiry}):

\begin{definition}[Answers]\label{def:phil:questions}
\status{definition}\src{philosophy Def 2.0}
A theory $T$ \emph{answers} $\mathcal Q$ if $B\cup T\vdash\varphi_i$ for some $i$, and \emph{answers it partially} if $B\cup T\vdash\bigvee_{i\in J}\varphi_i$ for some $J$ with $\emptyset\ne J\subsetneq[k]$.
\end{definition}

In the Kepler example (\cref{def:kepler:language}) the data are interval sentences, and questions are covers by them ("where is Mars at time $t$, to within $2'$?") or structural ("is the orbit of $b$ planar?"); Kepler's laws answer the structural questions and, without the elements of the orbits, entail no sharp position datum (\cref{prop:kepler:ramsey}). A raw-data predictor answers one question, "what is the next observation?", and answers it fully; \SAIabbr answers every question the base language can pose, and reports how much of its posterior answers none.

\begin{proposition}[The answer distribution]\label{prop:phil:answers}
\status{proved; computed}\src{philosophy Prop 2.1}
Let $\MA(D)>0$, let $\mathcal Q=\{\varphi_1,\dots,\varphi_k\}$ be a question, and let $\mathrm{Un}_D(\mathcal Q):=\Unif(E_D\setminus\bigcup_iO_{\varphi_i})/\Unif(E_D)$, the posterior probability that the random theory proves no direct answer. Then (a) $\sum_{i\le k}\Bel_D(\varphi_i)+\mathrm{Un}_D(\mathcal Q)=1$; (b) for $\emptyset\ne J\subsetneq[k]$, $\Bel_D(\bigvee_{i\in J}\varphi_i)\ge\sum_{i\in J}\Bel_D(\varphi_i)$, the difference being the posterior probability that the random theory answers $\mathcal Q$ partially through $J$ but proves no $\varphi_i$ with $i\in J$; for $J=[k]$, $\Bel_D(\bigvee_i\varphi_i)=1$ and the difference is $\mathrm{Un}_D(\mathcal Q)$; (c) for $\mathcal Q=\{\varphi,\neg\varphi\}$, (a) is the trichotomy $\Bel_D+\Dis_D+\Indep_D=1$ of \cref{prop:basic:structure}.
\end{proposition}

On $E_D$ the random theory is consistent, so the events "proves $\varphi_i$" and "proves no answer" partition $E_D$ (\cref{app:phil}). In \SOIabbr, whose hypotheses are labellers, the analogue of $\mathrm{Un}_D$ mixes logical independence with a labeller's silence (\cref{prop:soi:closure}), and the answer masses need not respect the incompatibility of the answers unless the labeller is consistent.

\paragraph{The conservation-law world.} What is a law worth to each task? For even $m$, micro-states are $x\in\{0,1\}^m$ with weight $|x|$; the dynamics $\sigma$ is a uniformly random permutation of each weight class, so the weight is conserved (the law); $x_0$ is uniform on the class of weight $m/2$, of size $Z:=\binom m{m/2}$, and $x_{t+1}:=\sigma(x_t)$. The raw data are the trajectory; the questions are "what is $|x_t|$?". The raw predictors $\mathsf{Raw}^+$, $\mathsf{Raw}^-$ are Bayes for "$\sigma$ is a uniformly random permutation of each weight class", resp.\ "of $\{0,1\}^m$"; the question predictors $\mathsf{Qa}^+$, $\mathsf{Qa}^-$, $\mathsf{Qa}^{\rm bin}$ are Bayes for "$|x_t|=|x_0|$, with $|x_0|$ uniform on $\{0,\dots,m\}$", "the $|x_t|$ are i.i.d.\ uniform on $\{0,\dots,m\}$" and "the $|x_t|$ are i.i.d.\ binomial$(m,\frac12)$"; $\mathsf{Mix}$ gives prior $\frac12$ to each of the two hypotheses of a task. Let $t^*$ be the length of the cycle of $\sigma$ through $x_0$; the step-$t$ loss is that of predicting $x_{t+1}$ from $x_0,\dots,x_t$.

\begin{proposition}[The conservation-law world]\label{prop:phil:conservation}
\status{proved; computed}\src{philosophy Prop 2.2}
(a) For $t<t^*$ the step-$t$ losses of $\mathsf{Raw}^+$ and $\mathsf{Raw}^-$ are $\log(Z-t)$ and $\log(2^m-t)$, and for $t\ge t^*$ both are $0$. The law's gain $\kappa_t:=\log\frac{2^m-t}{Z-t}$ ($t<t^*$) is nondecreasing in $t$; for $t\le Z/2$, $\log\frac{2^m}Z\le\kappa_t\le\log\frac{2^m}Z+1$, where $\log\frac{2^m}Z=\frac12\log\frac{\pi m}2+o(1)$, and the share $\kappa_t/\log(2^m-t)$ of the step loss is $O(\log m/m)$. (b) $\mathsf{Qa}^+$ pays $\log(m+1)$ for $|x_0|$ and $0$ afterwards, $\mathsf{Qa}^-$ pays $\log(m+1)$ per question; over $t_{\max}+1$ questions the law's share of the no-law loss is $t_{\max}/(t_{\max}+1)$. (c) In each task $\mathsf{Mix}$ pays at most one bit more than the better of its two hypotheses, on every trajectory. (d) For $t<t^*$, $\log(2^m-t)=\kappa_t+\log(Z-t)$ with $\kappa_t=-\log P_{\mathsf{Raw}^-}(|x_{t+1}|=m/2\mid x_0,\dots,x_t)$: the law's raw gain is exactly the loss of $\mathsf{Raw}^-$'s own answer to "what is $|x_{t+1}|$?". The loss of $\mathsf{Qa}^{\rm bin}$ on each question is $\kappa_0$.
\end{proposition}

The proof (\cref{app:phil}) uses that under a uniformly random permutation the next state, given a path of distinct states, is uniform on the unvisited states and the starting state. A computation (code in the accompanying repository) gives, at the start of a trajectory, gains of $2.348$, $3.331$ and $5.326$ bits at $m=16$, $64$, $1024$, against $\frac12\log\frac{\pi m}2=2.326$, $3.326$, $5.326$, and shares $0.15$, $0.052$, $0.0052$. So the share of the raw loss that the law saves tends to $0$, because of micro-detail no theory addresses, while its absolute gain per step equals its value for the corresponding question. Both tasks learn the law at the same price, so the theory is "in" the raw predictor. The tasks differ in their \emph{target}, which losses are counted, not in what must be learned or what the law is worth; and scoring questions is right when what we want is answers. (Part (b) holds by construction: the toy asks only about the conserved quantity.)\status{assessment}\src{philosophy §2.2 reading}

Two further propositions are stated in \cref{app:phil}. Answering questions costs no more description than raw modelling plus an \emph{extractor} that reads answers off the raw data: for a computable raw world $\mu$ and a partial computable extractor $\eta$ whose labellings are almost surely consistent with $B$, $\MA(D)\ge2^{-K(\mu)-K(\eta)-c}\mu_\eta(D)$ on every finite question data set (\cref{prop:phil:extractor}). The extractor is the bridge from raw data to the question language, and its cost $K(\eta)$ is not small in real cases: it contains whatever recognises a planet in an image. And answering is the stronger achievement (\cref{prop:phil:predicting}): a consistent r.e.\ theory that proves the data bounds the loss by its complexity, but on the lottery stream $\MA$ predicts well while no consistent r.e.\ theory proves the data; predicting a presentation of an incomplete theory does not settle the questions it leaves open; and the beliefs of \SAIabbr are closed under entailment and, up to additive error, under conjunction, which in the semimeasure convention the predictions of consistent \SOIabbr are not (\cref{prop:soi:closure}).

\paragraph{The objection from statistical practice.}\label{rem:phil:practice}\status{assessment}\src{philosophy §1.3 (o4); syn §2.2 row D}
Much of physics confronts theory with near-raw data through generative models and information-theoretic losses: gravitational-wave searches match waveform templates against the strain series with a Gaussian noise likelihood, cosmology fits likelihoods of microwave-background data, particle physics simulates detectors event by event. We think D survives in comparative form, because these pipelines exist to answer a few questions (a mass, a spin, a cosmological parameter, whether a particle exists). But the generative model is theory plus instrument plus noise, and the proof-requiring core of \SAIabbr, with no native noise model, sits far from this practice (\cref{sec:limits:stat}).

\paragraph{Purpose and logarithmic loss.}\label{rem:phil:purpose}\status{assessment}\src{philosophy §2.2 remark; syn §7 item 2}
Grading a prediction for use and scoring credences are different jobs. For the first, the purpose decides what is graded and with which tolerance, as with the interval data of \cref{sec:kepler}. For the second, every strictly proper score is optimised by the true distribution, and the logarithmic score is, up to positive affine transformation, the only strictly proper local scoring rule on more than two outcomes (a result attributed to \citealp{bernardo1979expected}); yet which proper score is privileged for epistemic purposes is contested \citep{leitgeb2010objective,pettigrew2016accuracy,greaves2006justifying}. For bounded or misspecified models, different proper losses select different best models, and logarithmic loss on raw data spends capacity on detail no purpose asks for. \SAI lets the purpose fix the propositions and scores credences on those; even so, the price of a datum can depend on the loss convention by an unbounded amount (\cref{prop:logic:normalised}). We do not claim that the logarithmic score is privileged.

\paragraph{The verdict on D.}\status{assessment}\src{philosophy §2.6}
D holds in comparative form. Its formal content is a change of data type, logical coherence, partiality and the choice of target; it is not a new inference principle, since one-tape \SAIabbr $\asymp$ one-tape consistent \SOIabbr (\cref{thm:soi:onetape}). The real difficulty of moving from raw data to questions is the extractor, which \SAIabbr takes as given.

\subsection{What a theory is, after Craig}\label{sec:phil:craig}

\paragraph{The idealised formalism cannot see shape.}\label{rem:phil:craig}\status{assessment, resting on thm:soi:coinfree, thm:soi:onetape, cor:basic:vocabulary, thm:terms:horn, thm:terms:KCV, thm:shape:invisible, prop:phil:flagpolesystem, prop:soi:freededuction (proved)}\src{syn §2.8 row 1; philosophy §1.4 item 1}
Every consistent assigner of truth values is an axiom set at $O(1)$ extra bits: coin-free \SAIabbr $\asymp$ coin-free consistent \SOIabbr, by Craig's construction inside the universal prior (\cref{thm:soi:coinfree}), and one-tape \SAIabbr $\asymp$ one-tape consistent \SOIabbr (\cref{thm:soi:onetape}). Adding vocabulary changes old-vocabulary weights by $O(1)$ (\cref{cor:basic:vocabulary,thm:terms:horn}); coin-free, an r.e.\ axiom set is a finite theory in a three-sorted extension, up to $O(1)$ (\cref{thm:terms:KCV}). Program length sees neither the reasoner nor the vocabulary (\cref{thm:shape:invisible}); the posterior cannot move between deductively equivalent formulations, which in the flagpole toys below differ in complexity by $O(1)$ however many laws are added (\cref{prop:phil:flagpolesystem}); and the diagonal separations from \SOIabbr under time bounds come from free deduction, not from axioms (\cref{prop:soi:freededuction}). So the idealised formalism cannot see the difference between a theory and a program. Nor does D support S as a claim about what an ideal inducer should produce: \citet{khardon1997learning} give a learning-to-reason algorithm for a class of propositional languages that has no efficient reasoning algorithm when represented as a formula-based knowledge base.

Where shape matters, it is relative to a reasoner and a vocabulary, and only when the written theory is charged: in the model of \cref{sec:shape} the exponential separations are between pairs (reasoner, vocabulary) (\cref{cor:shape:regimes}); in the settings of \cref{sec:terms} what pays is theoretical relations and entities, not vocabulary (\cref{thm:terms:dilemma}). Nothing in the formal results favours sentences over classes of models as what a theory is; and since the limit posterior can live on random or false theories (\cref{thm:logic:lottery,cor:logic:false}), nothing realist should be said about induced theories.

\subsection{Explanation}\label{sec:phil:explanation}

In the axiom view $T$ explains $\varphi$ when $B\cup T\vdash\varphi$, the deductive-nomological pattern \citep{hempel1948studies}, and an explanation is better the more a short theory derives, as in unification accounts \citep{friedman1974explanation,kitcher1981explanatory}; the explanatory power of \cref{def:laws:EP} makes this precise (see \cref{prop:laws:EP} for its relation to Myrvold's account, \citealp{myrvold2003bayesian}). But derivability is symmetric where explanation is not: a pole's height and the sun's elevation entail the shadow's length, and the shadow's length and the elevation entail the height (Bromberger's example, \citealp{bromberger1966why}, as attributed by \citealp{glymour1978two}). Take poles and reals, with $B$ the theory of real closed fields, poles $p_1,\dots,p_{N_{\rm p}}$, heights $\mathrm{ht}(p)$ and shadow lengths $\mathrm{sh}(p,u)$ when the tangent of the sun's elevation is $u>0$. The shadow law has two formulations,
\[\Lambda_h:=\forall p\,\forall u\,\bigl(u>0\to\mathrm{sh}(p,u)\cdot u=\mathrm{ht}(p)\bigr),\qquad \Lambda_s:=\forall p\,\forall u\,\bigl(u>0\to\mathrm{sh}(p,u)\cdot u=\mathrm{sh}(p,1)\bigr)\wedge\forall p\,\mathrm{ht}(p)=\mathrm{sh}(p,1),\]
each combined with initial conditions on the heights or on the shadows at $u=1$. A derivation is charged by the number of instances of the law's universally quantified conjuncts it uses (precise statements in \cref{app:phil}).

\begin{proposition}[The flagpole]\label{prop:phil:flagpole}
\status{proved; computed}\src{philosophy Prop 3.1}
(a) The four theories so obtained are deductively equivalent over $B$, so they lie in one deductive class: no data set separates them, and in the coin-free version the posterior odds of any two programs for them equal their prior odds on every data set. (b) Under derivation charges, on every shadow datum with $u\ne1$ the two theories whose initial conditions sit at the hub of their formulation (heights with $\Lambda_h$, shadows at $u=1$ with $\Lambda_s$) need one instance and the other two need two; the hub is a property of the formulation, not of the world, and the texts of the two hub theories differ by $|\Lambda_s|-|\Lambda_h|$, independently of $N_{\rm p}$ and the data. (c) Two causal extensions with intervention vocabulary, one with the height and one with the shadow exogenous, have the same consequences in the old vocabulary, and one interventional datum separates them.
\end{proposition}

\paragraph{What the toys show.}\status{assessment}\src{philosophy §3.2 reading, §3.3, §3.5}
The explanation relation of \SAIabbr is direction-free, and its posterior cannot move between deductively equivalent theories. Kitcher's unificationist answer, as we read it \citep{kitcher1989explanatory}, is that the best systematisation of all our beliefs derives the dimensions of objects from their origin and development, so that a height-from-shadow pattern would add a pattern, one that fails for objects that cast no shadow. \Cref{prop:phil:flagpolesystem} adds $k$ further laws in which the height figures: the formulation with the shadow as hub is then longer by an amount growing with $k$ in a literal code without definitions (relative to the primitives, or because some objects cast no shadow), by a constant with one definition, and by $O(1)$ under the universal prior; under derivation charges only a mixed theory loses. So Kitcher's answer is an effect of whole-theory simplicity that \SAIabbr reproduces only under a listing code, like the textbook point of \cref{sec:shape}. Direction enters cleanly with interventional questions, once the base language can pose them; but the intervention semantics was written into the causal theories by hand, so the toy is consistent with the interventionist account \citep{woodward2003making} and gives it no support over rivals such as the causal-process account \citep{salmon1984scientific}.

We conclude that derivation from a short theory is explanation in the unification sense, with at least four known gaps; we do not claim that derivation is explanation outright. The gaps are asymmetry, as above; explanation by approximate correction, since Newton's theory does not entail Kepler's laws but, in an exact toy, proves the honest old law on every window and refutes the exact one (\cref{prop:laws:correction}), so that "$T$ explains $\varphi$" must include "$T$ derives an honest approximation of $\varphi$ with an error bound on a domain"; irrelevance, as in the standard examples of hexed salt and of a man taking birth-control pills, where the length prior penalises an irrelevant axiom in a whole theory but a derivation does not mark which axioms it used; and the conjunction problem ($L$ is derived from $L\wedge L'$), which the length prior and the explanatory power address in part. Unification can track an explanatory gain without a gain in accuracy: against Ptolemy's system with the coincidences of retrogression and opposition stated as axioms, which has the same fit, Copernicus's wins exactly when the heliocentric reorganisation costs fewer bits than the coincidences (\cref{prop:laws:copernicus}).

\subsection{What separates \SAIabbr from raw-data prediction}\label{sec:phil:differences}

We replace claim P by the formal differences between \SAIabbr and a predictor of an observation stream, sorted into advantages for claims D and E, neutral differences and liabilities (\cref{tab:phil:differences}).\status{assessment (the sorting); items as cited}\src{philosophy §1.7; syn §2.5}
What does not separate the two is the inference principle, a Bayes mixture over a universal class, and, in the idealised setting, the shape of hypotheses. This is the form of the progress claim that we believe: for what a theory does, \SAIabbr is a step in the right direction; for what a theory is, the idealised formalism is neutral.
