\section{What a theory does and what it is}\label{sec:phil}

What does a scientific theory do? It may predict observations \citep{solomonoff1964formal}; answer questions posed in some language \citep[ch.~5]{vanfraassen1980scientific}; explain \citep{hempel1948studies}; or solve problems \citep{laudan1977progress}. What is it? A program that predicts observations; a set of sentences closed under consequence \citep{suppe1977structure}; a class of models \citep{suppes1960comparison,vanfraassen1980scientific}; a core of models with intended applications \citep{sneed1971logical,balzer1987architectonic}; or, on another line of thought, scientific knowledge rests on exemplars and tacit skills \citep{kuhn1970structure,kuhn1977second,polanyi1958personal}. The \emph{axiom view} answers: questions posed in a given language, and a logical theory; the \emph{raw-data view}: predictions of observations, and a program.

\subsection{An argument map}\label{sec:phil:map}

We separate five claims (\cref{tab:phil:map}). \textbf{D}: a theory answers questions posed in a given language rather than predicting raw data (read comparatively). \textbf{S}: a theory is a logical theory, not a program. \textbf{E}: theories explain propositions. \textbf{L}: the ladder of \cref{sec:intro}. \textbf{P}: \SAIabbr is progress over raw-data prediction, in what a theory does and in what it is.

\paragraph{What rests on theorems.}\status{assessment}\src{philosophy §1.1-§1.7; syn §2.2, §2.8}
Proved, in the stated settings: on data labelled true or false, the idealised formalism cannot see the difference between a theory and a program (\cref{sec:phil:craig}); answering questions differs from raw-data prediction in target, data type, coherence and partiality, not in the inference principle (\cref{prop:phil:answers,prop:phil:conservation,prop:phil:extractor,prop:phil:predicting,prop:soi:closure,prop:soi:independent}; the theorems proved, this reading of them an argument); in the model of \cref{sec:shape}, with a cost on the written theory, usefulness depends exponentially on the pair (reasoner, vocabulary), and on declarative against procedural form only by a constant factor once intermediate concepts are available and the reasoner can chain through them (\cref{cor:shape:regimes,thm:shape:directions}; open beyond that model); theoretical vocabulary is dispensable in the ideal mixture, while relations or entities pay for finite theories and under charged deduction (\cref{thm:terms:horn,thm:terms:dilemma}); and in \SAIabbr's mixture an approximately true law is priced as a law with a calibrated error model, carried by the randomness of the theory machine rather than stated as an axiom (\cref{prop:laws:errormodel}), a pricing that sees a purpose only through the questions asked (\cref{prop:laws:partition}). Computed, conditional on the pool of forms and the codes: on noise-free interval data simplicity among compatible forms is language-relative, and with graded fit the data decide (\cref{sec:kepler}). A theorem plus an argued identification: Hempel's inductive-systematisation reply fails in the idealised setting (theoretical vocabulary changes the cumulative semimeasure loss on observational data by at most a constant number of bits, which depends on the universal machine and the setting; \cref{rem:phil:hempel}). Arguments only: the descriptive observation; why declarative shape is good; that D survives the objection from statistical practice; claims about explanation beyond the toys (\cref{prop:phil:flagpole,prop:laws:correction,prop:laws:copernicus}); that the axiom view misses procedural, statistical and dynamic sides of theories; that approximate correctness, which depends on a purpose, remains a limitation of \SAIabbr (\cref{sec:limits:approx}); and our refusal, given a possibility result (\cref{cor:logic:false}), to be realist about induced theories.

\paragraph{The ladder.}\status{assessment}\src{philosophy §1.6}
We accept L. Step 2 is a step in the right direction, not the end point: an account of what theories do needs steps 3 and 4 as well. Step 4 is needed because theory-ladenness \citep{hanson1958patterns} biases comparisons between theories that disagree about the base language: a theory that revises it is scored in a language it rejects.

\subsection{The descriptive observation and the explanatory question}\label{sec:phil:observation}

\status{assessment (observation); open}\src{philosophy §1.2, §1.4; syn §2.3}Claim S combines a description and an explanation. \emph{The observation:} textbook presentations of fundamental theories are declarative, stating laws, definitions and models given by equations, not procedures that print predictions. Two qualifications. \emph{Declarative is not sentential:} physics texts write something closer to a class of models specified by equations \citep{suppes1960comparison,suppes1962models} than to first-order axioms. The semantic view takes a theory to be such a class of models (on the relation of the two views, cf.\ \citealp{halvorson2012what,lutz2017what}); \cref{sec:kepler:pool} shows where the choice of sentences enters our computation, and how much of its result survives without it. We use sentences because data are sentences and the prior of \SAIabbr is over texts; the question-answering view itself is neutral, since in partition semantics a question is a partition of logical space (cf.\ \citealp{groenendijk1984studies}). \emph{Theories in use have procedural parts,} such as numerical schemes and simulation codes, so the observation concerns textbook presentations, not every use of a theory.

\emph{The question}, why the declarative shape is good and whether an ideal inducer should produce it, is open. Craig's construction rules out an answer from representability alone (\cref{sec:phil:craig}); any answer must be relative to a reasoner with costs, or to a community of reasoners.

\begin{question}[Why declarative shape?]\label{q:phil:declarative}
\status{open}\src{syn §6 item 26; philosophy §1.2, §5 (L11)}
Is there a formal setting for a community of bounded reasoners who communicate, check and extend theories piece by piece, in which theories of declarative shape provably do better than programs?
\end{question}

\status{assessment, resting on thm:shape:directions (proved)}\src{philosophy §1.4 (correction of S4)}A natural argument for S is that a theory answers in many directions while a program computes one cheaply; the second half holds of particular programs only: a program with internal variables answers every direction of a parity law on $m$ variables with $O(m)$ operations. In the model of \cref{sec:shape} directions matter only relative to a fixed weak reasoner and a cost on what is written or on the time to write it: for one parity law, forward chaining needs exactly $m$ times as many rules as unit propagation needs clauses, over the base variables or with one round, and at most $9$ times the written length of the least clausal theory with intermediate concepts and unbounded rounds (\cref{thm:shape:directions}).

\subsection{What a theory does: questions against raw data}\label{sec:phil:questions}

Questions are defined in \cref{def:setting:questions}, as in the logic of questions (cf.\ \citealp{belnap1976logic,hintikka1999inquiry}); a theory \emph{answers} a question $\{\varphi_1,\dots,\varphi_k\}$ if it proves, with $B$, some $\varphi_i$, and \emph{partially} if it proves a disjunction of some but not all of them (\cref{def:phil:questions}). \status{assessment, resting on prop:phil:answers (proved)}\src{philosophy §2.1 reading, Prop 2.1}A raw-data predictor is scored on one question, ``what is the next observation?'', and assigns probabilities to every event about future observations; it has no native notion of a question left open, and it answers a question about a law only as a question about all future data (item 5 of \cref{tab:phil:differences}). \SAIabbr answers every question the base language can pose, and reports how much of its posterior answers none: the answer masses of a $k$-way question and its unanswered mass sum to $1$ (\cref{prop:phil:answers}).

\paragraph{The conservation-law world.}\status{assessment, resting on prop:phil:conservation (proved; computed)}\src{philosophy Prop 2.2, §2.2 reading}
In a toy world a micro-state $x\in\{0,1\}^m$ moves by a uniformly random permutation of each weight class, so its weight is conserved; the raw data are the trajectory, the questions ``what is $\lvert x_t\rvert$?''. Until the trajectory returns to its start, the law saves a Bayes predictor of the raw data between $\frac12\log\frac{\pi m}2+o(1)$ bits and one bit more per step (for $t\le Z/2$, $Z$ the size of the weight class), a share $O(\log m/m)$ of its step loss; on $t_{\max}+1$ questions it saves, by construction, the share $t_{\max}/(t_{\max}+1)$ of the no-law loss. Yet by the chain rule the raw saving per step is the loss of the raw no-law predictor's own answer to ``what is $\lvert x_{t+1}\rvert$?'', and in each task a Bayes mixture pays at most one bit to learn the law (\cref{prop:phil:conservation}). The tasks differ in their \emph{target}, which losses are counted, not in what is learned or what the law is worth per step.

\status{assessment, resting on prop:phil:extractor (proved)}\src{philosophy Prop 2.4}Answers never cost more description than raw modelling plus an \emph{extractor} that reads them off the raw data: for a computable law $\mu$ of the raw data and an extractor $\eta$ whose answers are $\mu$-almost surely consistent with $B$, with $\mu_\eta$ the induced law of the answers, $\LA(D)\le-\log\mu_\eta(D)+K(\mu)+K(\eta)+c$, with $c$ depending also on the family of question sentences (\cref{prop:phil:extractor}). The extractor is the bridge from raw data to the question language, and its cost $K(\eta)$ is not small in real cases: it contains whatever recognises a planet in an image.

\paragraph{The objection from statistical practice.}\label{rem:phil:practice}\status{assessment}\src{philosophy §1.3 (o4), §2.6; syn §2.2 row D}
Much of physics confronts theory with near-raw data through generative models and information-theoretic losses, as in gravitational-wave searches, microwave-background cosmology and detector simulation. D survives in comparative form, we think, because these pipelines exist to answer a few questions (a mass, a spin, whether a particle exists); but the generative model is theory plus instrument plus noise, and the proof-requiring core of \SAIabbr, with no native noise model, sits far from this practice (\cref{sec:limits:stat}).

\paragraph{Purpose and logarithmic loss.}\label{rem:phil:purpose}\status{assessment}\src{philosophy §2.2 remark; syn §7 item 2}
The purpose fixes what is graded and with which tolerance, in \SAIabbr which propositions are data; scoring credences on them is a different job. The logarithmic score is, under regularity conditions and up to positive affine transformation, the only strictly proper local scoring rule on more than two outcomes (a result attributed to \citealp{bernardo1979expected}), but which proper score is privileged is contested \citep{leitgeb2010objective,pettigrew2016accuracy}, and we do not claim that it is; inside \SAIabbr even the loss convention can change the price of a datum without bound (\cref{prop:logic:normalised}).

\subsection{What a theory is, after Craig}\label{sec:phil:craig}

\paragraph{The idealised formalism cannot see shape.}\label{rem:phil:craig}\status{assessment, resting on thm:soi:coinfree, thm:soi:onetape, cor:basic:vocabulary, thm:terms:horn, thm:terms:KCV, thm:shape:invisible, prop:phil:flagpolesystem, prop:soi:freededuction (proved)}\src{syn §2.8 row 1; philosophy §1.4 item 1}
Coin-free and one-tape, \SAIabbr $\asymp$ consistent \SOIabbr, by Craig's construction inside the universal prior (\cref{thm:soi:coinfree,thm:soi:onetape}). Adding vocabulary, restricting coin-free r.e.\ axiom induction to finite theories in a three-sorted extension, naming a reasoner, and reformulating the flagpole theories of \cref{sec:phil:explanation}, with any number of laws, each cost $O(1)$ bits (\cref{cor:basic:vocabulary,thm:terms:horn,thm:terms:KCV,thm:shape:invisible,prop:phil:flagpolesystem}); the diagonal separations from \SOIabbr under time bounds come from free deduction, not from axioms (\cref{prop:soi:freededuction}). So, on data labelled true or false, the idealised formalism cannot see the difference between a theory and a program. Nor does D support S as a claim about what an ideal inducer should produce: labellers carry no theory and are equivalent to theories.

\status{assessment, resting on prop:soi:independent, prop:soi:closure, prop:soi:noRE (proved)}\src{philosophy §1.3 verdict; sfi §6}The one respect in which the idealised formalism separates theories from labellers is partiality. When ``independent'' is itself observed, a theory can leave a sentence open without committing to anything, while a labeller must output ``independent'' after finitely many steps, and the gap is unbounded on one datum (\cref{prop:soi:independent}); similarly, in the semimeasure convention a theory is certain of the consequences of the data and a consistent labeller never is (\cref{prop:soi:closure}). The witnesses of the first gap are arithmetical sentences independent of a background containing $\Q$, and no r.e.\ family of them shows a large gap (\cref{prop:soi:noRE}). We read this as a formal trace of partiality, one of the candidate advantages of logical form, not as support for claim S about empirical theories.

\status{assessment, resting on cor:shape:regimes, thm:shape:directions, thm:terms:dilemma (proved)}\src{syn §2.8 rows 1-2; philosophy §1.2, §1.4 items 2, 4 and verdict}In the idealised setting, on data labelled true or false, the shape of a theory is invisible: every consistent labeller is an axiom system at a cost of $O(1)$ bits. In the settings we study, shape becomes visible only when something other than program length is charged (the written theory, the time to produce it, or the derivations) or the class of hypotheses is restricted (to finite theories); there it is relative to a reasoner and a vocabulary, and for theoretical terms what pays is relations and entities, not vocabulary as such (\cref{thm:terms:dilemma}). Whether this holds beyond these settings is open (\cref{q:shape:beyond}). We find it plausible that the declarative shape of written theories is good, and not merely common, but our results do not show it. Where shape is visible in our models, the large effects lie in the pair (reasoner, vocabulary), and declarative against procedural form costs at most a constant factor once intermediate concepts are available and the reasoner can chain through them (\cref{thm:shape:directions}); nothing in our results favours sentences over classes of models. What is missing is a formal setting in which that shape provably pays; communication and piecemeal checking by communities of reasoners are the candidates we see (\cref{q:phil:declarative}).

\subsection{Explanation}\label{sec:phil:explanation}

In the axiom view $T$ explains $\varphi$ when $B\cup T\vdash\varphi$, the deductive-nomological pattern \citep{hempel1948studies}, and an explanation is better the more a short theory derives, as in unification accounts \citep{friedman1974explanation,kitcher1981explanatory}; the explanatory power of \cref{def:laws:EP} gives one precise measure of this. But derivability is symmetric where explanation is not: a pole's height and the sun's elevation entail the shadow's length, and conversely (\citealp{bromberger1966why}, as attributed by \citealp{glymour1978two}). \status{assessment, resting on prop:phil:flagpole (proved; computed)}\src{philosophy Prop 3.1}Formally (\cref{prop:phil:flagpole}): over real closed fields, four formulations of the shadow law, with the height or the reference shadow as the hub, are deductively equivalent, so the coin-free posterior keeps their prior odds on every compatible data set; under derivation charges which quantity is the hub is a property of the formulation, not of the world; and one interventional datum separates two causal versions that agree in the old vocabulary.

\paragraph{What the toys show.}\status{assessment}\src{philosophy §3.2 reading, §3.3, §3.5}
The explanation relation of \SAIabbr is direction-free. Kitcher's answer to the asymmetry \citep{kitcher1989explanatory}, in its usual summary, appeals to other laws in which the height figures: with $k$ of them, the formulation with the shadow as hub is longer by an amount growing with $k$ in a literal code without definitions (if the reference shadow is not primitive or some objects cast no shadow), by a constant with one definition, and by $O(1)$ under the universal prior (\cref{prop:phil:flagpolesystem}): Kitcher's answer is a whole-theory simplicity effect that \SAIabbr reproduces only under a listing code. Direction enters cleanly with interventional questions, but their semantics was written in by hand, so the toy is consistent with the interventionist account \citep{woodward2003making} and gives it no support over rivals such as the causal-process account \citep{salmon1984scientific}.

We conclude that derivation from a short theory is explanation in the unification sense, with at least five gaps; we do not claim that derivation is explanation outright. The gaps are \emph{asymmetry}, as above; \emph{explanation by approximate correction}: Newton's theory does not entail Kepler's laws but, in an exact toy, proves the honest old law on every window and refutes the exact one (\cref{prop:laws:correction}), so explaining must include deriving an honest approximation with an error bound on a domain; \emph{irrelevance} (hexed salt; a man taking birth-control pills): the length prior penalises an irrelevant axiom in a whole theory, but a derivation does not mark which axioms it used; the \emph{conjunction problem} ($L$ is derived from $L\wedge L'$), which the length prior and the explanatory power address in part; and \emph{statistical explanation}: the approximate laws that \SAIabbr prefers come with error models (\cref{prop:laws:errormodel}) and make their data probable rather than derive them (cf.\ the statistical-relevance model of \citealp{salmon1971statistical}). Unification can register an explanatory gain without a gain in accuracy, as with Copernicus against Ptolemy (\cref{prop:laws:copernicus}).

\subsection{What separates \SAIabbr from raw-data prediction}\label{sec:phil:differences}

\status{assessment (the sorting); items as cited}\src{philosophy §1.7; syn §2.5}We replace claim P by the formal differences of \cref{tab:phil:differences}. Neither the inference principle nor, in the idealised setting, the shape of hypotheses separates \SAIabbr from raw-data prediction, except through partiality. This is the progress claim we believe: for what a theory does, \SAIabbr is a step in the right direction; for what a theory is, the idealised formalism is neutral on data labelled true or false.

\status{assessment}\src{philosophy §1.7; phil review issue 5}\SAIabbr is Bayesian confirmation with a universal prior in which a theory either proves the data or is eliminated, and it inherits that framework's problems in specific forms: the dependence of the prior on the machine and the language, bounded by constants that are not small at the scale of our examples (\cref{prop:basic:invariance,thm:basic:translation,sec:kepler:pool}); logical omniscience and the problem of old evidence (\cref{prop:logic:oldevidence}); and the allocation of credit among the axioms of a theory (item 7 of \cref{tab:phil:differences}).
