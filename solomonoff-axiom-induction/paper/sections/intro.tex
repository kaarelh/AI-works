\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

What does a scientific theory do, and what is it? On one view, behind Solomonoff's theory of inductive inference \citep{solomonoff1964formal}, a theory predicts observations and is a program, weighted in a Bayes mixture by its length. On another, a theory is a logical theory, a set of sentences closed under consequence (the received view; \citealp{suppe1977structure}), from which answers to questions posed in some language are derived; on van Fraassen's account, explanations are such answers to why-questions \citep[ch.~5]{vanfraassen1980scientific}. Others hold that theories explain \citep{hempel1948studies} or solve problems \citep{laudan1977progress}, that they are classes of models \citep{suppes1960comparison}, or that scientific knowledge rests on exemplars and tacit skills \citep{kuhn1970structure,polanyi1958personal}.\footnote{Several of the questions studied here first appeared in H\"anni's working notes \citep{hanni2026notes}, among them whether theories are better seen as answering questions than as predicting raw data, and how a written theory relates to the reasoner who uses it.} We put the second view into the form of the first: we study \SAI (\SAIabbr), a universal prior over axiom systems, sets of first-order sentences produced by programs, and use it to make precise, and to test, claims about what theories do and what they are.

\paragraph{The object.} Fix a first-order language $\mathcal L$ and a decidable consistent background theory $B$; the data are sentences labelled true or false. A universal machine reads a random tape and emits sentences, and the prior probability $\MA(D)$ of a data set $D$ is the probability that the emitted theory is consistent with $B$ and proves every datum, a sentence labelled false through its negation (\cref{def:setting:MA}): a hypothesis must explain each datum, not merely tolerate it. At a new sentence the posterior splits three ways: the random theory proves it, refutes it or leaves it open, and the predictions form a belief function, updated by geometric conditioning (\cref{rem:setting:defects,prop:basic:geometric}).

\paragraph{Raw data and questions.} Science predicts much more particular things than a stream of observations: where Mars is at a given time, to within two minutes of arc; the period of a planet, given the size of its orbit; whether an orbit is planar. We order views of what a theory does on a ladder: (1) predicting raw data, such as pixels; (2) answering questions posed with available abstractions, such as positions, periods and orbits, which may be highly theoretical; (3) extending the language with theoretical terms that pay for themselves, the data staying in the base language; (4) revising the base abstractions, as when a concept changes its meaning or a question is shown to be ill-posed. \SAIabbr over a fixed language sits on step 2. Its base language is not an observation language in the logical empiricists' sense, so the classical objections to a theory-neutral observation language do not apply to it in the same way, although theory-ladenness biases comparisons between theories that disagree about the base language (\cref{sec:phil:map}). We take step 2 to be a step in the right direction, but not the end point: an account of what theories do needs steps 3 and 4 as well. We argue that it differs from step 1 in the target, the data type, logical coherence and partiality, not in the inference principle (\cref{sec:phil:questions,tab:phil:differences}): version by version \SAIabbr agrees, up to constants, with \SOI \citep[\SOIabbr;][]{claudeSFI2026fundamentals}, developed in a companion paper, restricted to consistent labellers (\cref{sec:soi}). We have partial results on step 3 (\cref{sec:shape,sec:terms}) and none on step 4.

\paragraph{What a theory is.} On the second question the idealised formalism is neutral, on data labelled true or false; this rests on theorems, and the reading is ours. Every consistent labeller of sentences is an axiom system at a cost of $O(1)$ bits: coin-free \SAIabbr agrees with coin-free consistent \SOIabbr through Craig's construction inside the universal prior, and one-tape with one-tape, tape by tape (\cref{thm:soi:coinfree,thm:soi:onetape}); new vocabulary costs $O(1)$ bits, and program length sees no reasoner (\cref{cor:basic:vocabulary,thm:shape:invisible}). So, on data labelled true or false, \SAIabbr cannot see the difference between a theory and a program; when ``independent'' is itself observed, partiality separates them (\cref{sec:phil:craig}). That textbook theories are declarative (laws, definitions and equations, not procedures that print predictions) is an observation, with two qualifications: declarative is not sentential, and theories in use have procedural parts (\cref{sec:phil:observation}). Why that shape is good is open; Craig's construction rules out an answer based only on what can be represented, and our best formal answer locates the large effects in the pair (reasoner, vocabulary), not in logical form (\cref{sec:shape,q:phil:declarative}).

\paragraph{A preview: Kepler's third law.} Kepler's third law $\mathrm{K3}$ says that the squares of the planets' periods are proportional to the cubes of the semi-major axes of their orbits. In a language of heliocentric directions it adds nothing to the first two laws (\cref{prop:kepler:helio}); in a geocentric one it fixes, given the other elements of the orbits of the Earth and Mars, the size of the orbit of Mars and with it every geocentric direction of Mars (\cref{prop:kepler:geo,prop:kepler:K3worth}). A computation puts what $\mathrm{K3}$ thus saves on the elements of Mars at $11.3$ to $14.7$ bits for precisions from $8'$ to $1'$, against a law text of $375$ bits in our arithmetic syntax and $165$ or $115$ bits in two Polish-notation codes; so in none of these codes does compression alone select $\mathrm{K3}$ at Kepler's precision (an extrapolation from Mars to the other planets, conditional on the code; \cref{ex:kepler:K3cost}). Kepler's laws are also false: against the plan94 ephemeris a fixed ellipse misses Mars by $1.6'$ to $1.9'$ over $12$ to $30$ years (\cref{sec:kepler:wrong}); \cref{sec:laws} treats such laws.

\subsection{Contributions}\label{sec:intro:contrib}

The main results, roughly in order of importance (\cref{sec:related} says what is new):
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,label=(\arabic*)]
\item \emph{\SAIabbr agrees with consistent \SOIabbr, version by version:} coin-free and one-tape within constant factors, Kolmogorov within an additive constant; on labelled atoms of a fresh predicate it is \SOIabbr on strings, so the binary-label results of the companion paper that hold up to additive constants transfer (\cref{thm:soi:coinfree,thm:soi:onetape,thm:soi:transfer,cor:soi:transfer}).
\item \emph{Random theories no r.e.\ theory can replace:} a stream with bounded one-tape loss that no consistent r.e.\ theory proves, the limit posterior living on random theories (\cref{thm:logic:lottery}); over a decidable true background containing Robinson arithmetic $\Q$, adversarial true data that can concentrate it on false theories (\cref{cor:logic:false}), and, on $\Pi_1$ data, a mixture equal up to a constant factor to one random true theory (\cref{thm:logic:threshold}).
\item \emph{Shape is relative to a reasoner and a vocabulary:} for Boolean laws program length sees neither, while the least written length of a usable theory depends exponentially on the pair (\cref{thm:shape:invisible,cor:shape:regimes}); procedural form costs a parity law a factor $m$, its number of query directions, over the base variables or with one round, and any law at most a factor $9$ in written length with intermediate concepts and unbounded rounds (\cref{thm:shape:directions}).
\item \emph{The theoretician's dilemma:} with free deduction, theoretical vocabulary changes observational predictions only by a constant factor; on literal data with charged deduction, padded (Craig-type) axiomatisations with polynomial-time membership fit exactly the polynomial-time regularities, and vocabulary is eliminable at polynomial cost; and, with an empty background, a finite theory with only unary theoretical predicates and constants, compatible with the theory of the named objects, fits exactly the regular sets, while one binary theoretical relation fits more, and so does quantification over unnamed objects, at the price, for a theory without theoretical vocabulary, of proving a sentence false in the named structure (\cref{thm:terms:dilemma}).
\item \emph{Approximate laws:} over deterministic theories, proof-requiring compatibility prefers a false exact law to its true tolerance form; on data from a law with independent errors, the best loss rate is that of the law with its error distribution (attained by $\MA$ if computable), which is no account of approximate correctness (\cref{prop:laws:strength,prop:laws:errormodel,par:laws:purpose}).
\item \emph{Basic theory:} dominance; a loss at most the complexity up to deductive equivalence of any consistent r.e.\ theory proving the data, plus a constant (semimeasure convention); an inconsistency probability that is Solovay complete and random; no coding theorem (\cref{thm:basic:dominance,thm:basic:loss,thm:basic:inc,thm:basic:nocoding}).
\item \emph{Where \SAIabbr and \SOIabbr separate:} in the semimeasure convention logical closure separates them, and so does an observed ``independent'' label (\cref{prop:soi:closure,prop:soi:independent}); time-bounded separations come from free deduction, not from axioms (\cref{thm:soi:diagonal,prop:soi:freededuction}); with deduction charged, on hypotheses closed under renaming of parameters, \SAIabbr is certificate \SOIabbr up to polynomial changes of the budgets (\cref{thm:soi:sandwich}).
\item \emph{G\"odel phenomena:} over a background containing $\Q$, belief or independent mass on a $\Pi_1$ sentence certifies its truth; over one containing Peano arithmetic $\PA$, the coin-free posterior never learns $\Sigma_1$-soundness; the $\omega$-gap is the $\omega$-incompleteness of the background plus the instances (\cref{prop:logic:shape,thm:logic:soundness,prop:logic:omega}).
\item \emph{Best systems and explanatory power:} the best system is the same for every balance of simplicity against strength in an interval exactly when the hull of length against fit has a kink there, one aspect of what Lewis calls a kind nature; explanatory power is a per-bit log-likelihood ratio whose growth with the number of systems singles out laws (\cref{prop:laws:kink,def:laws:EP}).
\item \emph{The orbit of Mars:} among five historical forms, on noise-free data the syntax of the law texts decides among the forms compatible with the data; with graded fit the data decide every step but one within tens of observations (\cref{tab:kepler:map,ex:kepler:graded}).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{What we do not claim.}\label{sec:intro:notclaim} We do not claim that \SAIabbr shows a theory to be a logical theory rather than a program (on data labelled true or false it cannot see the difference), nor that the declarative shape of theories is good (\cref{sec:phil:craig,q:phil:declarative}); the results of \cref{sec:shape} hold in a stated model and are not theorems about textbooks (\cref{q:shape:beyond}). We say nothing realist about the theories an inducer favours: its limit posterior can live on random theories and, with true but adversarially chosen data, on false ones (\cref{thm:logic:lottery,cor:logic:false}). \SAIabbr is an uncomputable ideal of the evaluation of theories, not of their discovery, and its proof-requiring core is the noise-free special case of the confrontation of theory with data (\cref{sec:limits:stat}). Our philosophical conclusions are arguments; \cref{sec:phil:map} says which rest on theorems.

\paragraph{Organisation.} \Cref{sec:setting,sec:basic,sec:soi,sec:logic} develop the formalism, \cref{sec:phil,sec:shape,sec:terms,sec:laws,sec:kepler} what theories do and are, and \cref{sec:limits,sec:related,sec:discussion} limitations, related work and open problems; proofs are in the appendices.
