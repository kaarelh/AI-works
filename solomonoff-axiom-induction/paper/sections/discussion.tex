\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the formalism shows.} \SAI turns the view that theories answer questions posed in a given language into a precise object, and the view survives in comparative form. Its formal content is a change of target and of data type, logical coherence and partiality: consequences of the data are certain, ``independent'' is a native outcome, and a law is a sentence with a belief of its own (\cref{sec:phil:questions,tab:phil:differences}). The inference principle is unchanged: version by version, \SAIabbr is operator induction over consistent labellers (\cref{sec:soi}). What logic adds is a set of phenomena with no analogue for labellers: random theories that succeed where every r.e.\ theory fails, a single random true theory that is, up to a constant factor, the whole mixture on $\Pi_1$ data, and the fixed marks that G\"odel's theorems leave on the posterior (\cref{sec:logic}). On what a theory is, the idealised formalism is neutral: it cannot see the difference between a theory and a program. Where shape matters, it is relative to a reasoner and a vocabulary, and it matters only when the written theory is charged or the class of hypotheses is restricted (\cref{sec:shape,sec:terms}). We still find it plausible that good theories are shaped like logical theories, and that textbook theories are declarative is an observation we accept; what is missing is a formal setting in which that shape provably pays. Finally, that Kepler's laws are only approximately true is a limitation of proof-requiring compatibility with deterministic theories, not of the axiom view: under \SAIabbr's pricing an approximately true law is a law with a calibrated error model (\cref{sec:laws}).

\paragraph{What it cannot show.} The axiom view models the declarative core of scientific theories: contents, inferential relations, partiality and approximate truth. It misses their procedural side (recognizers, methods, the search for derivations), the statistical confrontation of theory with data, of which its proof-requiring core is the noise-free special case, and their dynamic side (meaning change, discovery, the social process of science) (\cref{sec:limits}). Its limit posterior can live on random theories and, with adversarially chosen true data, on false ones, so it supports no realist conclusion about the theories it favours (\cref{thm:logic:lottery,cor:logic:false}). It evaluates theories and does not find them, and it is not computable.

\subsection{Open problems}\label{sec:discussion:open}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,label=(\arabic*)]
\item Does $\MA$ dominate every lower semicomputable provability function of a random consistent theory (\cref{q:basic:domination})?
\item Is there a stream with bounded one-tape loss on which the coin-free loss diverges computably fast, and does bounded one-tape loss on a co-r.e.\ stream imply that a consistent r.e.\ theory proves it (\cref{q:logic:computablefast})?
\item Over a true background containing $\PA$, is the one-tape posterior probability of $\Sigma_1$-soundness bounded away from $1$ uniformly over all data (\cref{q:logic:soundness})?
\item Does the dependence of written size on the pair (reasoner, vocabulary) extend beyond Boolean laws, to real-valued laws with numerical solvers, probabilistic programs with conditioning and simulation codes (\cref{q:shape:beyond})?
\item Is there a formal setting for communities of bounded reasoners who communicate, check and extend theories piece by piece, in which theories of declarative shape provably do better than programs (\cref{q:phil:declarative})?
\end{enumerate}
Further questions concern the random tape and the order of the data (\cref{q:basic:tape,q:basic:indeporder}); the computability and the versions of consistent operator induction (\cref{q:soi:lsc,q:soi:pathwise}); Presburger data under charged deduction (\cref{q:soi:presburger}); soundness on true data, a coding theorem with constant slack and the loss on true arithmetic (\cref{q:logic:soundtrue,q:logic:codingO1,q:logic:thn}); a Kreisel-type property for finite theories (\cref{q:logic:kreisel}); the fit classes of richer observational languages, of infinite hypotheses about the named objects and of unary theoretical functions (\cref{q:terms:binaryobs,q:terms:a1,q:terms:unaryfunction}); exact minima, learning and time bounds for usable theories (\cref{q:shape:minimum,q:shape:learning,q:shape:cellprobe}); and restricted classes of approximate laws and domains of applicability (\cref{q:laws:JR,q:laws:domains}).

\paragraph{Directions.} Four directions seem to us most worth pursuing. The first is step 4 of the ladder, the revision of the base abstractions, for which we have no formal handle. The second is the setting of the fifth problem above, which may also be where the declarative shape of written theories finds its explanation. The third is a costed-deduction mixture over unknown axiom systems, combining \SAIabbr, which is uncertain about the axioms, with logical induction \citep{garrabrant2016logical}, which is uncertain about their consequences; such a reasoner is what induction in mathematics needs, since with free deduction checking instances confirms nothing (\cref{prop:logic:oldevidence}). The fourth is a native treatment of statistical confrontation inside the proof-requiring core, beyond randomised theories and the generative variant (\cref{sec:limits:stat}). Smaller questions remain: whether explanatory asymmetry is interventional in general; a derivation of Kepler's laws within stated tolerances from Newton's theory in a prover for validated numerics, a concrete case of the computational gap of \cref{sec:limits:textbook}; and the Mars posterior with distances as data.

\section*{Code and data}\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Code and data}
All computations reported here were done with seeded or deterministic Python scripts (Python~3.11 with NumPy, SciPy and mpmath; the planetary computations use pyerfa, the Python interface to the ERFA library). The scripts, their saved outputs and independent re-implementations of the main computations are in the folder \texttt{solomonoff-axiom-induction/} of the repository \url{https://github.com/kaarelh/AI-works}; its README lists the script behind each reported number and how to re-run it. Re-running the scripts reproduces the saved outputs, apart from lines that report run times; the longest computations, on the Mars pool, take more than half an hour each, and their figures were also reproduced by independent re-implementations.
