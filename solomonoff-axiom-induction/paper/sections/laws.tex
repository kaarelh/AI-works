\section{Approximate laws and the best system}\label{sec:laws}

Kepler's laws are false. A fixed ellipse misses Mars by one or two arcminutes over a few decades and the Moon by about a degree within a month (\cref{tab:kepler:residuals}), and yet the laws were, and are, good science. A proof-requiring inducer seems ill suited to such laws: a deterministic theory either proves a datum or does not, and an exact law that is slightly wrong is refuted by any datum precise enough to show its error. This section asks what \SAIabbr does with an approximately true law, and what becomes, inside it, of two notions tied to laws: Lewis's best system and explanatory power.\footnote{The questions of approximately true laws and of the domain of applicability of a model are discussed in H\"anni's working notes \citep{hanni2026notes}.}

In brief: over deterministic theories, proof-requiring compatibility rewards a false exact law over its true tolerance-weakened version (\cref{prop:laws:strength}). This is a limitation of proof-requiring compatibility with deterministic theories, not of the axiom view as such. $\MA$'s mixture contains randomised theories, which complete a law at random inside what it allows (\cref{prop:laws:randomised}); \SAIabbr is the best two-part code over all of them (\cref{prop:laws:twopart}); and on data from a law with independent errors it prefers the law together with its error distribution (\cref{prop:laws:errormodel}). Under \SAIabbr's pricing an approximately true law is a law with a calibrated error model. Over classes of laws with private literal facts, a steeper two-part code gives an exact form of Lewis's best system, in which ``kind nature'' is a kink of the data's hull (\cref{prop:laws:kink}) and explanatory power is the per-bit support of a module (\cref{prop:laws:EP}). Proofs are in \cref{app:laws}.

\subsection{Setting}\label{sec:laws:setting}

The data are answers to questions (\cref{def:setting:questions}), such as ``in which cell of width $\Delta$ does Mars's longitude lie at time $t$?''. A law constrains the answers: it may prove one, refute some, or leave several allowed.

\begin{definition}[Answered data, exception codes, approximate laws]\status{definition}\src{laws Defs 1.1--1.3, 1.5, 1.7; \S2 setting; Setting S3}\label{def:laws:setting}
\leavevmode
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \emph{Answered data.} An answered datum is a pair $(\mathcal Q,a)$ of a question and its true answer $a\in\mathcal Q$. A data set $D$ is a finite set of answered data with pairwise distinct questions, read as the two-valued data set $\{(a,1):(\mathcal Q,a)\in D\}$, so that $\MA(D)$, $\LA(D)$ and $\KA(D)$ are defined; $\dom D$ lists its questions and $y_D$ their answers in a canonical order, and $n:=|D|$. A theory $T$ \emph{proves} the datum if $B\cup T\vdash a$, \emph{refutes} it if $B\cup T\vdash\neg a$, and is \emph{silent} on it otherwise. Its \emph{allowed answers} are $A_T(\mathcal Q):=\{a'\in\mathcal Q:B\cup T\nvdash\neg a'\}$. The \emph{literal cost} of a datum is $\gamma(\mathcal Q):=\log|\mathcal Q|$.
\item \emph{Lengths and exception codes.} Fix a prefix-free code $c_S$ of sentences, put $\ell(\chi):=1+|c_S(\chi)|$ and, for a finite set $\Gamma$ of sentences (a theory or a \emph{module} of one), $\lVert\Gamma\rVert:=\sum_{\chi\in\Gamma}\ell(\chi)$. A finite theory is coded by its sentences' codewords, each after a continuation bit, and a stop bit, so $K(T)\le\lVert T\rVert+c$. If $B\cup T\cup D^\pm$ is consistent, the data of $D$ that $T$ does not prove are its \emph{exceptions}, priced in one of three codes: (Q) $\Exc^Q_D(T)$ is the sum of $\gamma(\mathcal Q)$ over the exceptions (the decoder knows the questions); (F) $\Exc^F_D(T)$ is the sum of $\ell(a)$ (the answer sentence, which also names its question); (R) $\Exc^R_D(T):=-\log P^R_T(D)$, defined in (c). If $B\cup T\cup D^\pm$ is inconsistent, $\Exc_D(T):=\infty$ in every code; an unmarked $\Exc$ means code Q. For $\lambda>0$ put $J_\lambda(T;D):=\lambda\lVert T\rVert+\Exc_D(T)$, and $J^Q_\lambda,J^F_\lambda,J^R_\lambda$ for the three codes; its minimisers are the \emph{$\lambda$-MAPs} \citep[Definition~3.6]{claudeSFI2026steeper}. For a finite set $Z$ of times, the \emph{patched law} $T^{-Z}$ withdraws $T$ at the times in $Z$, at a cost of about $c_{\rm time}$ bits per time named.
\item \emph{Pointwise interval laws.} Let $B:=\mathrm{RCF}$, the theory of real closed fields, decidable and complete (standard; \citealp{tarski1951decision}), with fresh constants $q_t$ for $t$ in a decidable set $I$ of numerals (\emph{times}), and a rational range $[0,\mathsf R]$. Fix a \emph{presentation language}: a decidable prefix-free set of codewords, each denoting a pair of total computable maps $\underline f\le\overline f$ from $I$ to $\mathbb Q\cap[0,\mathsf R]$, computed uniformly from it. The law $T_{\underline f,\overline f}:=\{\underline f(t)\le q_t\le\overline f(t):t\in I\}$ has $\lVert T\rVert$ the length of its codeword. Examples are the \emph{exact law} $T_0$ ($\underline f=\overline f=f$) and the \emph{honest law} $T^\varepsilon$ ($f\mp\varepsilon$, clipped to the range). \emph{Interval data} answer questions that partition $[0,\mathsf R)$ into finitely many cells with rational endpoints. The \emph{random completion} of $T$ gives interval data $D$ the probability $P^R_T(D):=\prod_t|C_t\cap[\underline f(t),\overline f(t)]|/(\overline f(t)-\underline f(t))$, where $C_t$ is the intersection of the cells that $D$ assigns to $t$, $|\cdot|$ is length, and a factor with $\underline f(t)=\overline f(t)$ is read as $\ind[\underline f(t)\in C_t]$.
\item \emph{Errors and precision.} For a real quantity $q(t)$ and an exact law $f$, the \emph{error} is $e(t):=f(t)-q(t)$, and $\varepsilon^*:=\operatorname{ess\,sup}|e|$ under the law of the error at a data time. In \emph{regime G} (guaranteed tolerances), datum $j$ is ``$q(t_j)\in[v_j-\delta,v_j+\delta]$'' with $v_j=q(t_j)+\zeta_j$ rational and $|\zeta_j|\le\delta_0\le\delta$: $\delta$ is the \emph{precision}, $\delta_0$ the measurement-error bound. In \emph{regime R} (dithered rounding), the quantity lives on a circle of circumference $\mathsf R$; datum $j$ is about a \emph{fresh} time $t_j$ (an injective computable sequence of numerals); the errors $e_j:=e(t_j)$ are i.i.d.; and datum $j$ is the cell of width $\Delta$ ($\mathsf R/\Delta$ an integer) that contains $q(t_j)$ in the grid shifted by a dither $u_j$, uniform and independent of everything else. Its literal cost is $\gamma:=\log(\mathsf R/\Delta)$. Put $p_\Delta:=\Ex\min(|e|/\Delta,1)$ and $s_\Delta:=\min(2\varepsilon^*/\Delta,1)$.
\item \emph{Peakedness.} $\pk(e):=\varepsilon^*/\Ex|e|\ge1$, and $\pk_\varepsilon(e):=\varepsilon/\Ex|e|$ for a band of half-width $\varepsilon$. Every reported value of $\pk$ names the fit of the law it belongs to (minimax or mean).
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

Two technical points. For the cell endpoints to be rational, the dither is uniform on a fine grid $\{k/N_{\rm d}\}$ and the formulas below are the limit $N_{\rm d}\to\infty$; a cell that crosses the zero point of the circle is a disjunction of two interval sentences. Times are fresh because data are sentences, which mention numerals: times drawn i.i.d.\ from a law on numerals repeat, and repeats change the cost of every form except an exact law. No decidable set of programs covers all total computable maps, so the class of pointwise interval laws depends on the presentation language; everything below holds for any fixed choice. On interval data, provability, refutation and consistency of these laws are decidable (\cref{lem:laws:decidable}).

\subsection{Deterministic theories reward false exact laws}\label{sec:laws:strength}

\begin{proposition}[Compatibility is monotone in logical strength]\status{proved}\src{laws Prop 2.1, Cor 2.2}\label{prop:laws:strength}
\leavevmode
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item Let $B\cup T$ be consistent and $\Cn(T)\supseteq\Cn(T')$. On every data set with which $T'$ is compatible ($B\cup T'$ consistent and proving every datum), $T$ is compatible. So among the deterministic hypotheses of $\WA$ and $\MA$ the posterior odds of $T$ against $T'$ equal their prior odds on every data set with which $T'$ is compatible, and $T'$ is incompatible whenever $T$ is: data never move the odds towards the weaker theory.
\item $T_0\vdash T^\varepsilon$ for every $\varepsilon\ge0$, so every data set compatible with the honest law $T^\varepsilon$ is compatible with the exact law $T_0$, at every sample size and every precision. With prior weights $2^{-\lVert T\rVert}$ in a code in which the tolerance clause costs at least one bit, the posterior odds of $T_0$ against $T^\varepsilon$ are at least $2^{\lVert T^\varepsilon\rVert-\lVert T_0\rVert}\ge2$ on every such data set.
\item If $\sup_t|e(t)|>0$, then $T_0$ is false and, on a question at precision $\delta'<\sup_t|e(t)|$, proves a false answer at some times; $T^\varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon\ge\sup_t|e(t)|$ is true and is silent wherever its band straddles a cell boundary.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
A consistent theory that proves everything $T'$ proves proves every datum $T'$ proves; this is (a), and (b) is (a) with $T=T_0$, $T'=T^\varepsilon$. (c) is immediate from the definitions.
\end{proof}

A tolerance, a domain restriction and a disjunction are all weakenings. Over deterministic theories a weakening wins only if it is shorter and still compatible, and the tolerance clause makes the honest law longer. Whether the exact law survives depends on how precision enters the data.

\begin{proposition}[Guaranteed tolerances]\status{proved; computed}\src{laws Prop 2.4}\label{prop:laws:tolerance}
In regime G: (a) $T_0$ proves datum $j$ iff $|e(t_j)-\zeta_j|\le\delta$, and refutes it otherwise. (b) If $\delta\ge\varepsilon^*+\delta_0$, then $T_0$ is compatible, at every $n$, with every such data set whose errors lie in the essential range (so almost surely). (c) Suppose the pairs $(e(t_j),\zeta_j)$ are i.i.d., $e(t_j)$ is independent of $\zeta_j$, both $\pm\delta_0$ lie in the support of the law of $\zeta_j$, and $\delta<\varepsilon^*+\delta_0$. Then the per-datum refutation probability $r(\delta):=\Prob[|e-\zeta|>\delta]$ is positive, $T_0$ survives $n$ data with probability $(1-r(\delta))^n$, and it is almost surely refuted eventually. So the switch point $\delta=\varepsilon^*+\delta_0$ is sharp.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[Dithered rounding]\status{proved; computed}\src{laws Prop 2.5}\label{prop:laws:rounding}
In regime R, given $e(t_j)$, $T_0$ proves datum $j$ with probability $(1-|e(t_j)|/\Delta)^+$ and refutes it otherwise. So $T_0$ survives $n$ data with probability $(1-p_\Delta)^n$, its first refutation has a geometric index with mean $1/p_\Delta$, and it is almost surely refuted eventually iff $\Prob[e\ne0]>0$, for every $\Delta$, however coarse. For $\Delta\ge\varepsilon^*$, $p_\Delta=\Ex|e|/\Delta$: the exact law survives about $\Delta/\Ex|e|$ data.
\end{proposition}

\emph{Proof idea.} $T_0$ proves $q(t_j)=f(t_j)$, which is $v_j+e(t_j)-\zeta_j$ in regime G; in regime R, two points at distance $|e|<\Delta$ fall into different cells of a uniformly shifted grid with probability $|e|/\Delta$ (\cref{app:laws:precision}).

This corrects the natural expectation that coarse data protect a false exact law. Under rounding, precision belongs to the data set, not to the datum: what matters is the number of data times the error over the cell width. An exact law with a sinusoidal error of amplitude $1'$ is refuted by positions rounded to a dithered grid of $1^\circ$ after $\pi\Delta/(2\varepsilon^*)\approx94$ data on average; a simulation agrees (code in the accompanying repository). So the expected precision-dependent selection, in which the simpler approximate law is preferred until the data's precision exceeds its error and the more exact law after that, holds for guaranteed tolerances and fails for rounded data: among finitely many exact laws, the shortest compatible one is eventually the shortest law with $\varepsilon^*_i+\delta_0\le\delta$ in regime G, and the shortest law with $\Prob[e_i\ne0]=0$ in regime R, whatever $\Delta$ (\cref{prop:laws:choice}). The Kepler computations of \cref{sec:kepler} are in regime G, with exact interval centres. We have not found these two regimes stated elsewhere; precision-dependent selection as such is familiar from accounts based on AIC, MDL and MML (cf.\ \citealp{forster1994how,rissanen1978modeling,wallace2005statistical}).

\subsection{Randomised theories and the best two-part code}\label{sec:laws:random}

$\MA$ is not a mixture of deterministic theories only. A deterministic theory pays, in code F, for which questions are exceptions, because it does not see the questions: $\KA(D)\le\lVert T\rVert+\Exc^F_D(T)+c$ (\cref{prop:laws:exceptions}). A theory machine that reads a random tape can answer every question of a family at once, at random inside what the law allows, and then only the questions asked cost anything. This extends the coin facts of \cref{prop:basic:exceptions}(c) from atoms a law is silent about to answers it only constrains.

\begin{proposition}[Randomised theories pay question-relative costs]\status{proved}\src{laws Prop 1.8}\label{prop:laws:randomised}
Let $T$ be a pointwise interval law and $D$ a set of interval data consistent with $T$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item $\LA(D)\le K(T)+\Exc^R_D(T)+c\le\lVert T\rVert+\Exc^R_D(T)+c$.
\item If $D$ comes from a computable question family with one question $\mathcal Q_t$ per time, and $k_t(T):=|A_T(\mathcal Q_t)|$, then $\LA(D)\le K(T,(\mathcal Q_t)_t)+\sum\log k_t(T)+c$, the sum over the data $T$ does not prove; in particular $\LA(D)\le K(T,(\mathcal Q_t)_t)+\Exc^Q_D(T)+c$.
\item Code R charges an exception of a patched law, at a time where $\underline f=0$ and $\overline f=\mathsf R$, exactly $\log(\mathsf R/|C_t|)$, the code-Q cost for equal cells; it charges the honest law $-\log(|C_t\cap[f(t)-\varepsilon,f(t)+\varepsilon]|/2\varepsilon)$, which is $0$ when the band lies inside the cell.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\emph{Proof idea.} The theory machine $G_T$ emits $T$ and, reading the tape, ever narrower dyadic intervals in $[\underline f(t_i),\overline f(t_i)]$ around independent uniform points $v_i$. Its random theory is always consistent and proves a datum whenever $v_i$ lies inside the datum's cell, so $\nu_{G_T}(D)\ge P^R_T(D)$; $K(G_T)\le K(T)+c$, and dominance (\cref{thm:basic:dominance}) gives (a) (\cref{app:laws:random}).

The proposition is an upper bound on $\LA$. What it shows is that $\MA$'s mixture contains $G_T$, with prior weight $2^{-K(G_T)}$ and likelihood $P^R_T(D)$. Data that the law refutes still cost the positions of the withdrawals (\cref{prop:laws:patches}). Below, \emph{\SAIabbr's pricing} of a form means the relative weight $2^{-(K(G)-\log\nu_G(D))}$ of a named theory machine $G$ in $\MA$'s mixture; it is not a statement about the whole posterior. The mixture itself is the best such two-part code.

\begin{proposition}[\SAIabbr is the best two-part code over theory machines]\status{proved, given thm:basic:dominance, rem:terms:named, thm:basic:nocoding and Proposition 5.7 of the companion paper on steeper priors}\src{laws Prop 1.9}\label{prop:laws:twopart}
\leavevmode
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item For every data set $D$, $\LA(D)=\min_G[K(G)-\log\nu_G(D)]\pm c$, the minimum over theory machines $G$; it is attained, up to $c$, by $G_U$.
\item Restricting $G$ gives upper bounds: $\LA(D)\le\min_TJ^R_1(T;D)+c$ over pointwise interval laws, $\LA(D)\le\min_TJ^F_1(T;D)+c$ over finite theories, and $\LA(D)\le\KA(D)+c$.
\item Neither $\KA$ nor $\min_TJ^Q_1(T;\cdot)$ over finite theories is within an additive constant of $\LA$ in general; nor, therefore, is $\min_TJ^F_1(T;\cdot)$ when $\gamma\le\ell$.
\item Over all theory machines steeper selection collapses. Take the point family of programs $p\in\dom V$ for theory machines, with length $|p|$ and fit $-\log\nu_{G_{V(p)}}(D)$, and let $\ell_U$ be the length of a program for $G_U$. For $\lambda>1$, every $\lambda$-MAP $p$ has $|p|\le\ell_U+\ell_U/(\lambda-1)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\emph{Proof idea.} (a) is dominance in one direction and $G=G_U$ in the other. (c) combines the absence of a coding theorem (\cref{thm:basic:nocoding}) with the named-objects data of \cref{rem:terms:named}, on which $\LA\le c$ while every finite theory has $J^Q_1\ge n$. (d) is \citet[Proposition~5.7(ii)]{claudeSFI2026steeper}, whose hypothesis holds with constant $0$ by the mixture identity (\cref{app:laws:random}).

So \SAIabbr is a two-part code with complexity weight $1$ over randomised theories, with the universal machine as its best member, and a steeper penalty over that whole class selects nothing long, because the universal machine absorbs every law into its fit; the companion paper finds the same for the one-tape class of \SOIabbr \citep[Corollary~4.5]{claudeSFI2026steeper}. Whether a restricted class of pointwise interval laws matches $\LA$ within $O(1)$ is open (\cref{q:laws:JR}). Best-system analyses with a steeper penalty (\cref{sec:laws:best}) therefore use a class in which no member absorbs the others: laws with private literal facts. In the two classes we use, pointwise interval laws in code R and finite theories in code Q, the companion paper's hull, kink and converse results apply (\cref{prop:laws:P2hyp}), and a coding bound holds (\cref{prop:laws:codingbound}).

\subsection{Honest laws, laws with exceptions, laws with error models}\label{sec:laws:honest}

Work in regime R. The forms of a law are the empty law $\emptyset$; the exact law $T_0$; the exact law with exceptions $T_0^{-Z}$, with $Z$ the times of the data $T_0$ refutes; the honest law $T^{\varepsilon^*}$, which is true and never patched; and a true exact theory $T_{\rm tr}$. Each form $T$ has $J_\lambda(T;D_n)=a_T+\sum_{j\le n}c_T(z_j)$ with a constant $a_T$ and i.i.d.\ per-datum costs $c_T(z_j)$, and \emph{rate} $m_T:=\Ex c_T$. A \emph{pricing} fixes the price $\gamma_{\rm si}$ of a datum on which a form is silent and the price $\gamma_{\rm ex}$ of an exception. Under \emph{Q/det} (code Q, each patched time named in the law at $c_{\rm time}$ bits) $\gamma_{\rm si}=\gamma$ and $\gamma_{\rm ex}=\lambda c_{\rm time}+\gamma$. This is a finite-horizon cost model: any code pays at least $H_2(p_\Delta)/p_\Delta$ bits per exception, where $H_2$ is the binary entropy, and literal numerals at least $\log n$ (\cref{prop:laws:patches}). Under \emph{Q/rand} a theory machine withdraws the law at each time independently with probability $\rho$; then $\gamma_{\rm ex}=\gamma+H_2(p_\Delta)/p_\Delta$. Under \emph{R/det} and \emph{R/rand} a silent datum is priced instead by random completion inside the band; for $\Delta\ge2\varepsilon^*$, $\gamma_{\rm si}=\beta_e\in[\log\mathrm e-1,\log\mathrm e]$, where $\mathrm e$ is Euler's number (\cref{lem:laws:codeR}). \SAIabbr's pricing is R/rand. For $\Delta\ge2\varepsilon^*$ the rates are $m_\emptyset=\gamma$, $m_{T_0^{-Z}}=p_\Delta\gamma_{\rm ex}$ and $m_{T^{\varepsilon^*}}=s_\Delta\gamma_{\rm si}$ (\cref{prop:laws:rates}).

\begin{proposition}[The peakedness criterion]\status{proved; computed}\src{laws Prop 3.4}\label{prop:laws:peaked}
Let $\Delta\ge2\varepsilon^*$, under any of the four pricings (the deterministic ones as a cost model). (a) The honest law $T^{\varepsilon^*}$ has a lower rate than the exact law with exceptions iff $\pk(e)<\gamma_{\rm ex}/(2\gamma_{\rm si})$. (b) If the exact law with exceptions comes from a law $f_1$ with error $e_1$, and the honest law from a law $f_2$ of the same length up to $O(1)$, with tolerance $\varepsilon_2\ge\operatorname{ess\,sup}|e_2|$ and $\Delta\ge2\varepsilon_2$, then the honest law has the lower rate iff $\varepsilon_2/\Ex|e_1|<\gamma_{\rm ex}/(2\gamma_{\rm si})$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By \cref{prop:laws:rates}, $s_\Delta\gamma_{\rm si}<p_\Delta\gamma_{\rm ex}$ iff $2\varepsilon^*\gamma_{\rm si}<\Ex|e|\,\gamma_{\rm ex}$; in (b), $s_\Delta=2\varepsilon_2/\Delta$ and $p_\Delta=\Ex|e_1|/\Delta$.
\end{proof}

Under Q/det the criterion reads $\lambda c_{\rm time}>\gamma(2\pk(e)-1)$: spread errors favour the honest law, concentrated errors favour exceptions, and a steeper prior favours honesty. Under Q/rand it reads $\pk(e)<\frac12+H_2(p_\Delta)/(2p_\Delta\gamma)$, a threshold that grows only logarithmically in $1/p_\Delta$; in the cases computed it is below $1$, so the exact law with random exceptions wins even for a sinusoidal error ($\pk=\pi/2$) at cell widths from $3'$ to $60'$ (code in the accompanying repository). Under Q/det the best member of the whole family of patched tolerance laws states the $(1-\theta_{\rm ex})$-quantile of $|e|$ as its tolerance and lists the rest as exceptions, $\theta_{\rm ex}=2\gamma/(\lambda c_{\rm time}+\gamma)$ (\cref{prop:laws:quantile}).

Under random completion silence is cheap, and the comparison moves inside the band. For $\varepsilon\ge\varepsilon^*$ and a computable distribution $\mathcal G$ on $[0,1]$ (with distribution function also written $\mathcal G$), the \emph{band-confined completion} $\mathrm{BC}_{\mathcal G}$ is the theory machine that emits $T^\varepsilon$ and, for each time $t_i$, draws $\hat w_i\sim\mathcal G$ from the tape and emits the value $f(t_i)-\varepsilon+2\varepsilon\hat w_i$ (by narrowing intervals, or exactly at an atom). The uniform $\mathcal G$ gives the machine $G_{T^\varepsilon}$ of \cref{prop:laws:randomised}, the \emph{uniform band}; $\mathrm{HY}(\rho)$, with $\mathcal G=(1-\rho)\delta_{1/2}+\rho\,\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]$, says ``exact, except in a share $\rho$ of cases, and then within $\varepsilon$''; SH uses a histogram. Then $K(\mathrm{BC}_{\mathcal G})\le\lVert T^\varepsilon\rVert+K(\mathcal G)+c$, the random theory is always consistent, and on data at fresh times $\nu_{\mathrm{BC}_{\mathcal G}}(D)=\prod_j\mathcal G(C'_j)$, where $C'_j$ is datum $j$'s cell in \emph{band coordinates} (the band mapped affinely onto $[0,1]$) intersected with $[0,1]$.

\begin{proposition}[Laws with error models]\status{proved; computed}\src{laws Prop 3.6a, Def 3.6a}\label{prop:laws:errormodel}
In regime R let $\varepsilon\ge\varepsilon^*$, and let $\mathcal F$ be the distribution function of $w_j:=(\varepsilon-e_j)/(2\varepsilon)$, the position of the true value in the band.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item If $\Delta\ge2\varepsilon$, the rate of $\mathrm{BC}_{\mathcal G}$ is $(2\varepsilon/\Delta)\,c_{\mathcal G}$, with
$c_{\mathcal G}:=\Ex_w\int_0^1\big(\ind[v<w](-\log(1-\mathcal G(v)))+\ind[v>w](-\log\mathcal G(v))\big)\,dv$. So band-confined completions are ranked in the same way at every such $\Delta$.
\item $c_{\mathcal G}\ge c_{\mathcal F}:=\int_0^1H_2(\mathcal F(v))\,dv$, with equality iff $\mathcal G=\mathcal F$ almost everywhere on $[0,1]$. In particular the uniform band is the best band-confined completion iff $e$ is uniform on $[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]$.
\item For every theory machine $G'$, $G_U$ included, almost surely $\liminf_n\frac1n(-\log\nu_{G'}(D_n))\ge r_{\mathcal F}(\Delta):=\Ex[-\log\mathcal F(C'_1)]$, the conditional entropy rate of the answers given the questions, while $-\frac1n\log\nu_{\mathrm{BC}_{\mathcal F}}(D_n)\to r_{\mathcal F}(\Delta)$. If $\mathcal F$ is computable, then $\LA(D_n)/n\to r_{\mathcal F}(\Delta)$ almost surely. For $\Delta\ge2\varepsilon$, $r_{\mathcal F}(\Delta)=(2\varepsilon/\Delta)\,c_{\mathcal F}$.
\item $c_{\mathrm{HY}(\rho)}$ is convex in $\rho\in(0,1]$, and some $\mathrm{HY}(\rho)$ with $\rho<1$ beats the uniform band iff $\Ex\ln(1+|e|/\varepsilon)<2\ln2-1$. This requires $\pk_\varepsilon(e)>\ln2/(2\ln2-1)\approx1.79$, and holds whenever $\pk_\varepsilon(e)>\mathrm e/(4-\mathrm e)\approx2.12$; $|e|/\varepsilon$ uniform on $[0,1]$ is the boundary case.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\emph{Proof idea.} (a) A cell boundary falls into the band with probability $2\varepsilon/\Delta$, at a uniform relative position $v$, and the datum then costs $-\log$ of the $\mathcal G$-mass on the true side. (b) is Gibbs' inequality given $v$. (c) The conditional law of the answers given the questions is $\nu_{\mathrm{BC}_{\mathcal F}}$, while for any theory machine the events ``consistent and proves the answer list $y$'' are disjoint over $y$; Markov's inequality and the Borel--Cantelli lemma give the lower bound, and dominance the limit. (d) follows from convexity and the sign of the derivative at the uniform band (\cref{app:laws:honest}).

So under \SAIabbr's pricing an approximately true law is a law with a calibrated error model, not a silent partial theory and not an exact law with listed exceptions. On data from a law with i.i.d.\ errors no theory machine has a lower rate than the law together with its error distribution, and $\MA$ attains that rate when the error law is computable. The dichotomy between an honest law and a law with exceptions survives inside the band, as the comparison of the two-point form with the uniform band, with peakedness thresholds near $2$. That a model should include its noise distribution is the standard situation of MDL (cf.\ \citealp{rissanen1978modeling,grunwald2007minimum}); what is specific here is the reading for a proof-requiring inducer, in which the honest partial theory is one completion among many. Selection among finitely many forms is almost sure: the form of least rate is eventually the unique $\lambda$-MAP, two forms of equal rate whose costs differ non-degenerately alternate forever, and switch times obey Wald bounds (\cref{thm:laws:as}), which gives the phase sequence $\emptyset\to T_0\to T_0^{-Z}\to T^{\varepsilon^*}\to T_{\rm tr}$ (\cref{cor:laws:phases}).

Within the pool $\{\emptyset,\ T_0^{-Z},\ \text{uniform band}\}$, the uniform band has the least rate in every cell under \SAIabbr's pricing. \begin{proposition}[Humility]\status{proved}\src{laws Prop 3.10}\label{prop:laws:humility}
Let $\Pi_\lambda(T\mid D)\propto2^{-J_\lambda(T;D)}$ be the power-prior posterior \citep[Definition~3.6]{claudeSFI2026steeper} restricted to a finite pool of deterministic forms (code Q) containing $\emptyset$, $T_0^{-Z}$, $T^{\varepsilon^*}$ and possibly $T_{\rm tr}$, and ask a new question at a fresh time $t'$, with cell width $\Delta'\le2\varepsilon^*$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item The pool's three-valued prediction puts mass $\Pi_\lambda(T^{\varepsilon^*}\mid D)+\Pi_\lambda(\emptyset\mid D)$ on ``independent'', mass $\Pi_\lambda(T_0^{-Z}\mid D)$ on the cell of $f(t')$, which is wrong with probability $p_{\Delta'}$ over the dither, and mass $\Pi_\lambda(T_{\rm tr}\mid D)$ on the true cell.
\item Without $T_{\rm tr}$, if the least rate at the training cell width $\Delta$ is attained by one form only, the posterior concentrates on it almost surely: the inducer ends humble (independent mass tending to $1$ on finer questions) if $T^{\varepsilon^*}$ or $\emptyset$ is selected, and overconfident (a wrong cell with probability $p_{\Delta'}$) if $T_0^{-Z}$ is; \cref{prop:laws:peaked} at $\Delta$ and \cref{prop:laws:fine} decide which. If two forms tie in rate with a non-degenerate cost difference, the posterior oscillates between them forever.
\item In a randomised pool (code R) a band-confined completion $\mathrm{BC}_{\mathcal G}$ is not silent: it puts on each cell its $\mathcal G$-mass, a spread over the cells its band meets and never probability one on a wrong cell; the exact law with random exceptions puts probability $1-\rho$ on the cell of $f(t')$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

So the trichotomy is the formal home of humility for deterministic theories: an honest approximate law is a partial theory, and its silence is the value ``independent''. Whether the inducer is humble is decided not by the prior, which favours the overconfident exact law (\cref{prop:laws:strength}), but by the error's shape and the price of silence. For the randomised theories that $\MA$ mixes, humility is calibration: by \cref{prop:laws:errormodel}(c) the asymptotically preferred completion is the true error distribution.

\subsection{Sound reasoning with approximate models}\label{sec:laws:sound}

Practice uses several approximate models of one quantity, each on its own domain. Two exact approximations that differ anywhere on a common domain are jointly inconsistent, and an inconsistent theory proves everything. Their honest forms are not.

\begin{proposition}[Sound reasoning with approximate models]\status{proved; computed}\src{laws Prop 4.3}\label{prop:laws:sound}
Let $T_i:=\{\forall t\in\mathrm{Dom}_i:\ |q(t)-f_i(t)|\le\varepsilon_i\}$, $i\le k$, be honest pointwise models of one quantity $q$ (scalar, or vector-valued with a norm), with $q$ otherwise unconstrained, and let $T^0_i$ be their exact versions ($\varepsilon_i=0$).
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item Let $B$ be true in the intended structure. If every $T_i$ is true, then $\bigcup_iT_i$ is consistent and every sentence derivable from $B\cup\bigcup_iT_i$ is true; every derived statement about $q(t)$ is a set containing the true value.
\end{enumerate}
For (b)--(d) assume moreover that $B$ decides every sentence built from the $f_i$, the $\mathrm{Dom}_i$ and the $\varepsilon_i$ at the times concerned (for example $B=\mathrm{RCF}$, with semialgebraic $f_i$ and $\mathrm{Dom}_i$ and rational $\varepsilon_i$).
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),resume,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item $\bigcup_iT^0_i$ is inconsistent, and so proves every sentence, iff $f_i(t)\ne f_j(t)$ for some $t\in\mathrm{Dom}_i\cap\mathrm{Dom}_j$.
\item $\bigcup_iT_i$ is consistent iff for each $t$ the balls $\{v:|v-f_i(t)|\le\varepsilon_i\}$ with $t\in\mathrm{Dom}_i$ have a common point. For scalar $q$, and for vector $q$ with sup-norm (box) tolerances, this holds iff it holds pairwise, $|f_i(t)-f_j(t)|\le\varepsilon_i+\varepsilon_j$ on every overlap. For Euclidean tolerances in $\mathbb R^m$, $(m+1)$-wise intersection is necessary and sufficient, and pairwise intersection is not sufficient.
\item If $B$ proves that a map $\Psi$ is $L_\Psi$-Lipschitz, then $T_i\vdash|\Psi(q(t))-\Psi(f_i(t))|\le L_\Psi\varepsilon_i$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\emph{Proof idea.} (a) The intended structure is a model. For (b) and (c), the constants $q(t)$ at different times are unconstrained, so consistency reduces by compactness to one sentence per time, which $B$ decides and which holds in the intended structure iff the balls meet; Helly's theorem (standard; \citealp{helly1923mengen}) gives the $(m+1)$-wise criterion (\cref{app:laws:sound}).

Without the hypothesis on $B$, (b) and (c) fail: with $B=\emptyset$ and $f_i(t)$, $f_j(t)$ distinct constant symbols, $T^0_i\cup T^0_j$ is consistent although the values differ in the intended structure. For iterated dynamics, the honest law proves after $r$ steps of an $L$-Lipschitz map the propagated tolerance $L^r\varepsilon^{(0)}+\varepsilon(L^r-1)/(L-1)$, exactly a zonotope for linear maps; so an honest approximate law has a derivation horizon, while an exact approximation is correct only up to that horizon and becomes inconsistent with one true datum beyond it (\cref{prop:laws:horizon,prop:laws:exacthorizon}). The device is sound, but it does not say how to choose a model, a domain or a tolerance (\cref{sec:limits:domains}).

\begin{remark}[Typical against worst case]\status{computed}\src{laws Rem 4.4}\label{rem:laws:typical}
The criterion is worst-case. With $L=1$ and $r=100$ steps the propagated tolerance is $100\varepsilon$, while with independent random-sign errors $\pm\varepsilon$ the mean deviation is $\varepsilon r\binom{r}{r/2}2^{-r}\approx7.96\varepsilon$ ($25.2\varepsilon$ against $1000\varepsilon$ at $r=1000$). A probabilistic error model answers much further out, with a stated confidence instead of a guarantee.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The best system as a steeper MAP}\label{sec:laws:best}

On Lewis's account the laws are the theorems of the deductive system that best balances simplicity against strength (cf.\ \citealp{lewis1983new,lewis1994humean}). Take strength to be fit to data in a fixed base language and the balance to be the complexity weight $\lambda$ of $J_\lambda$, over a class of laws with private literal facts (by \cref{prop:laws:twopart}(d), not over all theory machines). The relation to Lewis is an analogy: best systems are a metaphysics of laws, and \SAIabbr is inference from finite data. The candidate theories give points $(\lVert T\rVert,\Exc_D(T))$; their convex hull has vertices $v_i$ in order of length, joined by edges of slopes $-\sigma_i$ with $\infty=:\sigma_0>\sigma_1>\sigma_2>\dots>0$, and for $\lambda\in(\sigma_{i+1},\sigma_i)$ the $\lambda$-MAPs are the theories at $v_i$ \citep[Lemma~3.8]{claudeSFI2026steeper}. Following the companion paper \citep[Definition~5.2]{claudeSFI2026steeper}, a point $(\alpha,L)$ attained by some theory is a \emph{zero-window $(\sigma,\tau)$-kink} if every theory shorter by $x>0$ has fit at least $L+\sigma x$ and every theory longer by $x>0$ has fit at least $L-\tau x$.

\begin{proposition}[Kind nature is a kink]\status{proved, given Lemma 3.8 and Corollary 5.4 of the companion paper on steeper priors; computed}\src{laws Prop 5.6}\label{prop:laws:kink}
Take a family of candidate theories satisfying the hypotheses of the hull lemma (\cref{prop:laws:P2hyp}), and $0\le\tau<\sigma\le\infty$. The following are equivalent: (a) the set of $\lambda$-MAPs is the same for every $\lambda\in(\tau,\sigma)$; (b) some hull vertex $v_i$ has $\sigma_{i+1}\le\tau$ and $\sigma\le\sigma_i$; (c) the point family has a zero-window $(\sigma,\tau)$-kink at a point that is a hull vertex.
\end{proposition}

\emph{Proof idea.} Between consecutive edge slopes the $\lambda$-MAP set is fixed, and at an edge slope it changes, so (a) says that $(\tau,\sigma)$ contains no edge slope; the equivalence with (c) is \citet[Corollary~5.4(c)]{claudeSFI2026steeper} (\cref{app:laws:best}).

Nature is kind on an interval of balances exactly when the data's hull has a kink there. On a solar-system population of eight planets with 500 to 5000 observations, sixty asteroids with a median of about five and thirty comets with one or two (in the toy of \cref{prop:laws:K3}, with $L=300$, $L_3=150$, $k=60$, $s=15$ and $\gamma=12.4$), the $\lambda$-MAP is ``the law plus the elements of all eight planets'' for every $\lambda\in(7.03,135.37)$, a factor of $19.3$ in the balance, while below $\lambda\approx7$ it changes with every asteroid (code in the accompanying repository). Inside such an interval the worry that the best system depends on an arbitrary choice of balance (cf.\ \citealp{woodward2014simplicity}) is moot. Kindness is a checkable property of a language, a family of candidates and a data set, not of nature alone.

\begin{proposition}[The trivialisation is blocked]\status{proved, given prop:laws:codingbound}\src{laws Prop 5.7}\label{prop:laws:trivial}
Let $\mathcal L'\supseteq\mathcal L$ be any extension of the language, with a syntax code, and let the data be base-language answers whose provability from the theories considered is decidable. Every finite theory $T$ in $\mathcal L'$ consistent with $D$ has $\lVert T\rVert+\Exc^Q_D(T)\ge K(y_D\mid\dom D)-K(\mathcal L')-c$, where $K(\mathcal L')$ is the complexity of a decoder for $\mathcal L'$ and its syntax code. Without decidability, add $n$ on the left. If $\mathcal L'$ and its code are fixed independently of $D$, $K(\mathcal L')$ is a constant.
\end{proposition}

\emph{Proof idea.} The decoder of the coding bound (\cref{prop:laws:codingbound}) searches for proofs from $T$ of base-language answers and, given a decoder for the syntax of $\mathcal L'$, does not otherwise depend on the language.

Lewis's trivialisation worry is that a predicate $F$ true of exactly the actual things makes ``$\forall x\,Fx$'' maximally simple and strong (cf.\ \citealp{lewis1983new}). Measured as derivation of base-language data under a coding bound, $F$ buys nothing: its connection to the data must be stated in bridge axioms, which pay for every bit. A language chosen after the data can hide the data in its own description; that cost is $K(\mathcal L')$, which is the point of the worry. What remains is the choice of base language, and of which questions are data, as in the relativisation of best systems to a vocabulary (cf.\ \citealp{cohen2009better}).

\begin{definition}[Explanatory power]\status{definition}\src{laws Def 5.2}\label{def:laws:EP}
For a theory $T$ and a module $\mathcal A\subseteq T$, $\EP_D(\mathcal A;T):=\big(\Exc_D(T\setminus\mathcal A)-\Exc_D(T)\big)/\lVert\mathcal A\rVert$: the bits of data the module explains per bit of its statement, in the context $T$.
\end{definition}

Every module of a $\lambda$-MAP has $\EP\ge\lambda$, and no addition to it has $\EP>\lambda$ (\cref{prop:laws:slopes}). When the theories at the two ends of a hull edge are nested, the edge slope is the explanatory power of the added module averaged over the edge; otherwise it is only a rate of exchange between length and fit. $\EP$ depends on the context: a law and the initial conditions it needs explain nothing apart and much together.

\begin{proposition}[Facts used once never enter for $\lambda>1$]\status{(a) proved; (b) proved, given prop:laws:codingbound and Corollary 5.5 of the companion paper on steeper priors}\src{laws Prop 5.3}\label{prop:laws:once}
\leavevmode
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item Let $T^*$ be a $\lambda$-MAP with $\lambda>1$ and $\chi$ a sentence. Suppose that, in the context $T^*\setminus\{\chi\}$, adding $\chi$ turns exactly the exceptions in a set $\mathcal D_\chi\subseteq D$ into proved data and changes nothing else, and that $\sum_{(\mathcal Q,a)\in\mathcal D_\chi}\gamma(\mathcal Q)<\lambda\ell(\chi)$. Then $\chi\notin T^*$. In particular this applies to a datum's answer $a$ stated as an axiom whenever $\gamma(\mathcal Q)\le\ell(a)$ and stating it proves no other datum in that context.
\item Let provability on the data be decidable in the class, with $\kappa$ the constant of \cref{prop:laws:codingbound}, and let $T_s$ be \emph{$b$-sufficient}: $\lVert T_s\rVert+\Exc_D(T_s)\le K(y_D\mid\dom D)+b$. Every theory $T$ has $\Exc_D(T)\ge\Exc_D(T_s)-(\lVert T\rVert-\lVert T_s\rVert)-(b+\kappa)$; a module $\mathcal A$ added to $T_s$ has $\EP_D(\mathcal A;T_s\cup\mathcal A)\le1+(b+\kappa)/\lVert\mathcal A\rVert$; and every $\lambda$-MAP $T$ with $\lambda>1$ has $\lVert T\rVert\le\lVert T_s\rVert+(b+\kappa)/(\lambda-1)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\emph{Proof idea.} (a) Deleting $\chi$ from $T^*$ would raise the fit by less than $\lambda\ell(\chi)$ (\cref{prop:laws:slopes}). (b) The coding bound, rearranged around $T_s$, is a line of slope $1$ below all points, and \citet[Corollary~5.5]{claudeSFI2026steeper} turns it into the length bound (\cref{app:laws:best}).

So the model--randomness split of the companion paper separates facts reused across many data from facts used once, not laws from initial conditions. To see what does single out laws, consider the following toy. Bodies $b\le m$ have $n_b$ observations each, of literal cost $\gamma$ (code Q). The modules are the law $\Lambda$ ($L$ bits), body $b$'s elements $\mathrm{El}_b$ ($k$ bits), and Kepler's third law $\mathrm{K3}$ ($L_3$ bits), which shortens every elements module by $s$ bits, $0<s<k$, because it derives the period from the semi-major axis. $\Lambda\cup\mathrm{El}_b$ proves all of $b$'s observations, and no other combination proves any observation: laws alone answer no position question, and neither do elements alone. Theories are $\Lambda$ or not, $\mathrm{K3}$ or not, and a set $S$ of bodies whose elements are axioms.

\begin{proposition}[Laws and initial conditions in the solar-system toy]\status{proved; computed}\src{laws Prop 5.5}\label{prop:laws:K3}
Put $k_\Lambda:=k$, $k_{\Lambda+\mathrm{K3}}:=k-s$ and $S_x(\lambda):=\{b:\gamma n_b>\lambda k_x\}$ for $x\in\{\Lambda,\Lambda+\mathrm{K3}\}$. The optimal values of the three law choices are
\[J^*_\emptyset=\gamma\textstyle\sum_bn_b,\qquad J^*_\Lambda=\lambda L+\sum_b\min(\gamma n_b,\lambda k),\qquad J^*_{\Lambda+\mathrm{K3}}=\lambda(L+L_3)+\sum_b\min(\gamma n_b,\lambda(k-s)),\]
attained by $S=S_x(\lambda)$ (bodies with $\gamma n_b=\lambda k_x$ may go either way); the $\lambda$-MAP is the best of the three ($\mathrm{K3}$ without $\Lambda$ is useless).
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item $\Lambda$ beats the empty theory iff $\sum_{b\in S_\Lambda(\lambda)}(\gamma n_b-\lambda k)>\lambda L$.
\item $J^*_{\Lambda+\mathrm{K3}}-J^*_\Lambda=\lambda L_3-\sum_b\big[\min(\gamma n_b,\lambda k)-\min(\gamma n_b,\lambda(k-s))\big]$. If no body has $\lambda(k-s)<\gamma n_b\le\lambda k$, so that $S$ is the same with and without $\mathrm{K3}$, then $J^*_{\Lambda+\mathrm{K3}}<J^*_\Lambda$ iff $s|S|>L_3$, whatever $\lambda$ is. $\mathrm{K3}$ is in the $\lambda$-MAP iff in addition $J^*_{\Lambda+\mathrm{K3}}<J^*_\emptyset$.
\item In the $\lambda$-MAP $\Lambda\cup\{\mathrm{El}_b:b\in S\}$ (with or without $\mathrm{K3}$), $\EP(\Lambda)=\gamma\sum_{b\in S}n_b/L$ and $\EP(\mathrm{El}_b)=\gamma n_b/k_x$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\emph{Proof idea.} For a fixed law choice $J_\lambda$ is a constant plus a sum over bodies, where including $\mathrm{El}_b$ costs $\lambda k_x$ and excluding it costs $\gamma n_b$; minimise each term (\cref{app:laws:best}).

$\mathrm{K3}$ is a law about initial conditions: it explains no datum and compresses the elements. Both the bits it costs and the bits it saves are law bits, so its comparison with the law without it is decided at exchange rate $1$, not at the balance $\lambda$; $\lambda$ matters only through which bodies' elements are in the theory and whether any law pays at all. This is a comparison of law choices, not membership of the $\lambda$-MAP: with two bodies of one observation each the empty theory can win while $s|S|>L_3$ (\cref{app:laws:best}). What singles out the law is how its explanatory power scales. With $m$ bodies of $n_0$ observations each, $\EP(\Lambda)=mn_0\gamma/L$ grows with the number of systems, while $\EP(\mathrm{El}_b)=n_0\gamma/k_x$ grows only with the observations of one system; the elements of a well-observed body explain hundreds of bits per bit and sit on the steep side of the hull with the law. In the question format, where the data are conditional questions ``given the elements of $b$, is $\ell$ the case?'' under \Ethree, elements are question antecedents and never enter a $\lambda$-MAP, and $\EP(\Lambda)=\gamma\sum_bn_b/L$ however the observations are spread over bodies (\cref{prop:laws:questions}). What $\mathrm{K3}$ is worth in Kepler's own setting is computed in \cref{sec:kepler}.

\subsection{Explanatory power, unification and correction}\label{sec:laws:explain}

Explanatory power is a likelihood ratio, and it relates exactly to a measure of unification. We read Myrvold's measure of unification \citep{myrvold2003bayesian}, the degree to which a hypothesis renders phenomena informationally relevant to one another, as the term $U$ below.

\begin{proposition}[Explanatory power as a likelihood ratio; unification]\status{proved; computed}\src{laws Prop 5.8}\label{prop:laws:EP}
Write $P_T(D):=2^{-\Exc_D(T)}$ (code Q or R). For a hypothesis $h$, a background theory $T_\flat$, data sets $D_1,D_2$ on disjoint questions and $x$ a theory, put $I(h;D\mid T_\flat):=\log(P_{T_\flat\cup h}(D)/P_{T_\flat}(D))$, $I_x(D_1;D_2):=\log\frac{P_x(D_1\cup D_2)}{P_x(D_1)P_x(D_2)}$ and $U(D_1,D_2;h\mid T_\flat):=I(h;D_1\cup D_2\mid T_\flat)-I(h;D_1\mid T_\flat)-I(h;D_2\mid T_\flat)$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item $\lVert\mathcal A\rVert\,\EP_D(\mathcal A;T)=I(\mathcal A;D\mid T\setminus\mathcal A)$.
\item $U(D_1,D_2;h\mid T_\flat)=I_{T_\flat\cup h}(D_1;D_2)-I_{T_\flat}(D_1;D_2)$. If $P$ is a product over questions for both theories, then $U=0$ for every split.
\item Let $h$ be a law family with a $k$-bit parameter $\theta$ under the uniform prior, $P_h(D):=2^{-k}\sum_\theta P_{h\cup\{\theta\}}(D)$, and $P_\emptyset$ a product. If each of $D_1,D_2$ is proved by $h\cup\{\theta^*\}$ and has likelihood $0$ under every other $\theta$, then $U(D_1,D_2;h)=k$. If $\theta=(\theta_1,\theta_2)$ with $D_i$ depending only on $\theta_i$, then $U=0$. If $\theta_i=(\theta_{\rm sh},\theta'_i)$ share a $k_{\rm sh}$-bit constant $\theta_{\rm sh}$ and each $D_i$ identifies $(\theta_{\rm sh},\theta'_i)$, then $U=k_{\rm sh}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\emph{Proof idea.} (a) is the definition; (b) expands the six logarithms; (c) evaluates $P_h$ in the three cases (\cref{app:laws:explain}). A computation with $k=6$ confirms the three values (code in the accompanying repository).

So explanatory power is the \emph{total} support per bit of statement in the two-part code, where parameters are stated, and there the unification term vanishes; unification appears instead as a module whose $\EP$ grows with the number of systems it serves (\cref{prop:laws:K3}). The unification term is the support due to \emph{shared unknown parameters} when they are integrated out: $k$ bits within one system, nothing across systems with independent initial conditions, and $k_{\rm sh}$ bits across systems that share a constant. At $\lambda=1$, adding a module lowers $J_1$ iff its $\EP$ exceeds $1$, which is positive compression in the sense of inductive logic programming (cf.\ \citealp{muggleton1995inverse,srinivasan1994justification}); a steeper objective asks for compression at rate $\lambda$.

The cleanest case of unification without better prediction is Copernicus against Ptolemy. In Ptolemy's system the coincidence of each superior planet's retrograde motion with its opposition is stipulated planet by planet; in Copernicus's it follows from the Earth's motion (cf.\ \citealp{kuhn1957copernican}).

\begin{proposition}[Unification without better prediction]\status{proved (the lengths are not measured)}\src{laws \S5.3 (Copernicus against Ptolemy); philosophy \S3.4}\label{prop:laws:copernicus}
Consider three theories with a common part: $\mathrm{Pt}_f$, Ptolemy's system with free epicycle phases, which pays for the coincidence of each superior planet $p$'s retrograde motion with its opposition in its fit, $a_p$ bits per planet; $\mathrm{Pt}_s$, the same with the coincidences stated as axioms, $\sum_pa_p$ law bits more than $\mathrm{Pt}_f$ and with the fit of $\mathrm{Cop}$; and $\mathrm{Cop}$, Copernicus's system, $H$ law bits more than $\mathrm{Pt}_f$ and with the fit of $\mathrm{Pt}_s$. (a) $\mathrm{Cop}$ beats $\mathrm{Pt}_s$, which makes the same predictions, at every $\lambda$ iff $H<\sum_pa_p$. (b) $\mathrm{Cop}$ is $\mathrm{Pt}_f$ plus a module with $\EP=\sum_pa_p/H$, so it beats $\mathrm{Pt}_f$ iff $\lambda<\sum_pa_p/H$. (c) $\mathrm{Pt}_s$ beats $\mathrm{Pt}_f$ iff $\lambda<1$. Hence if $H<\sum_pa_p$, $\mathrm{Cop}$ is the best of the three exactly for $\lambda<\sum_pa_p/H$, and $\mathrm{Pt}_f$ above; if $H\ge\sum_pa_p$, $\mathrm{Cop}$ is never strictly best: $\mathrm{Pt}_s$ is best for $\lambda<1$ (tying $\mathrm{Cop}$ if $H=\sum_pa_p$) and $\mathrm{Pt}_f$ for $\lambda>1$.
\end{proposition}

The proposition says what the comparison turns on; the lengths $H$ and $a_p$ are not measured (\cref{app:laws:explain}). The two comparisons are different: against $\mathrm{Pt}_s$ the gain is in length alone, with no gain in prediction; against $\mathrm{Pt}_f$ the same $\sum_pa_p$ bits are Copernicus's extra likelihood, the total support $I(h;D)$ of \cref{prop:laws:EP}, not the unification term $U$.

\paragraph{Explanation by approximate correction.} Newton's theory does not entail Kepler's laws: it contradicts them (cf.\ \citealp{duhem1954aim}). What it derives, with masses and initial conditions, are corrected statements, and Kepler's laws within tolerances on windows, as in the model of reduction to a corrected reduced theory (cf.\ \citealp{schaffner1967approaches}). The following toy makes this provable. Over $B=\mathrm{RCF}$ with constants $x_{t,1},x_{t,2}$ for $t\le\bar t$ (a planar state $x_t$ per step), let $\mathrm{Rot}$ be the rotation with cosine $3/5$ and sine $4/5$, $\mathbf P$ a rational $2\times2$ matrix with operator norm $\|\mathbf P\|$, $\eta_{\rm c}:=1/100$, $A:=\mathrm{Rot}+\eta_{\rm c}\mathbf P$ and $a:=(1,0)$; $|\cdot|$ is the Euclidean norm, and $|v|\le\varepsilon$ is stated as $|v|^2\le\varepsilon^2$ with $\varepsilon^2$ rational. The \emph{old exact law} is $T_0:=\{x_t=\mathrm{Rot}^ta:t\le\bar t\}$ (uniform circular motion), the \emph{successor} is $T_{\rm new}:=\{x_0=a\}\cup\{x_{t+1}=Ax_t:t<\bar t\}$, the \emph{honest old law} is $T^\varepsilon:=\{|x_t-\mathrm{Rot}^ta|\le\varepsilon:t\le\bar t\}$, and a \emph{time-dependent form} is $T^{(\varepsilon_\cdot)}:=\{|x_t-\mathrm{Rot}^ta|\le\varepsilon_t:t\le\bar t\}$.

\begin{proposition}[Explanation by approximate correction]\status{proved; computed}\src{laws Prop 7.1(a)--(d'')}\label{prop:laws:correction}
\leavevmode
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item $T_{\rm new}\vdash T^\varepsilon$ for every rational $\varepsilon\ge\varepsilon(\bar t):=((1+\eta_{\rm c}\|\mathbf P\|)^{\bar t}-1)|a|$.
\item $T_{\rm new}\vdash T^\varepsilon$ iff $\varepsilon^2\ge\varepsilon_{\min}(\bar t)^2:=\max_{t\le\bar t}|A^ta-\mathrm{Rot}^ta|^2$, a rational number. So $\varepsilon_{\min}(\bar t)\le\varepsilon(\bar t)$, and $T^{\varepsilon_{\min}(\bar t)}$ is the strongest uniform-tolerance form of the old law on the window that the successor proves.
\item If $\eta_{\rm c}\mathbf Pa\ne0$ and $\bar t\ge1$, then $T_{\rm new}\vdash\neg\bigwedge T_0$.
\item Among uniform-tolerance forms, $T^\varepsilon$ is a common consequence of $T_0$ and $T_{\rm new}$ iff $\varepsilon\ge\varepsilon_{\min}(\bar t)$; the strongest common one is $T^{\varepsilon_{\min}(\bar t)}$.
\item The time-dependent form with $\varepsilon_t:=|A^ta-\mathrm{Rot}^ta|$ is a common consequence of both theories, strictly stronger than $T^{\varepsilon_{\min}(\bar t)}$ whenever $\varepsilon_t<\varepsilon_{\min}(\bar t)$ for some $t$.
\item The strongest common consequence of the two finite theories is their disjunction $\bigwedge T_0\vee\bigwedge T_{\rm new}$, which is not a tolerance law; if $\varepsilon_{\min}(\bar t)>0$, $T^{\varepsilon_{\min}(\bar t)}$ does not entail it.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\emph{Proof idea.} In every model the recurrence fixes $x_t=A^ta$, a rational vector, so each tolerance clause reduces to an inequality between rationals that $\mathrm{RCF}$ decides; the bound in (a) follows from $|A|\le1+\eta_{\rm c}\|\mathbf P\|$ by induction; (f) is proof by cases, with a shifted circle as a model of the honest law and of neither theory (\cref{app:laws:explain}). Derivations of polynomial size in $\bar t$ exist (\cref{rem:laws:correction}).

The successor does not entail the old law; it contradicts its exact form. What it derives is an honest form of the old law on a window, with a tolerance that the successor determines and that grows with the window: the old law's domain of applicability is derived, not stipulated. Among statements ``within $\varepsilon$ of the old law's prediction at every $t\le\bar t$'', $T^{\varepsilon_{\min}(\bar t)}$ is the strongest the successor proves and the strongest the two theories share; it is not all they have in common. By \cref{prop:laws:strength}, true data (here, data from $T_{\rm new}$) that refute $T_0$ refute neither $T_{\rm new}$ nor $T^\varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon\ge\varepsilon_{\min}(\bar t)$: the honest form is explained by its successor, and the exact form is not. For $\mathbf P=J\,\mathrm{Rot}$ ($J$ the rotation by $90^\circ$: an outward spiral with precession), exact rational arithmetic gives $\varepsilon_{\min}=0.0100$, $0.0999$, $0.961$, $1.69$ at $\bar t=1,10,100,200$, against the bound $\varepsilon(\bar t)=0.0100$, $0.105$, $1.70$, $6.32$ (code in the accompanying repository). The toy is linear and discrete; deriving ``Kepler within $\varepsilon$ on a window'' from Newton needs existence theorems and validated perturbation bounds (\cref{sec:kepler:newton}).

\subsection{Domains of applicability and retention}\label{sec:laws:domains}

When should a law be restricted to a domain, and when is a superseded theory kept? Let a law $\Lambda$ with per-system elements prove the data of every system but one, $\star$, whose $n_\star$ data it fits, with $\star$'s elements ($k_\star$ bits), at the per-datum rate $m^\star_{\rm pat}$ of the exact law with exceptions, or at the rate $m^\star_{\rm hon}$ of an honest tolerance for $\star$, at a one-off cost of $h_\star$ bits. The \emph{domain-restricted law} $\Lambda_{\rm Dom}:=\forall b\,(\mathrm{Dom}(b)\to\mathrm{law}(b))$, with $\mathrm{Dom}$ a definable predicate that $B$ proves of the other systems and refutes of $\star$, costs $\lVert\mathrm{Dom}\rVert$ bits more than $\Lambda$ and leaves $\star$'s data literal, at rate $\gamma$.

\begin{proposition}[Domain restriction]\status{proved; computed}\src{laws Prop 6.1}\label{prop:laws:domain}
\leavevmode
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item $J_\lambda(\Lambda_{\rm Dom})-J_\lambda(\Lambda\cup\mathrm{El}_\star\text{ with }\star\text{'s exceptions})=\lambda\lVert\mathrm{Dom}\rVert-\lambda k_\star+\sum_{\star\text{'s data}}(\gamma-c_{\rm pat})$, and likewise against the honest treatment of $\star$, with $\lambda(k_\star+h_\star)$ and $c_{\rm hon}$, where $c_{\rm pat},c_{\rm hon}$ are the per-datum costs.
\item With i.i.d.\ data and $n_\star\to\infty$, the restriction is eventually the $\lambda$-MAP's treatment of $\star$ iff $\gamma<\min(m^\star_{\rm pat},m^\star_{\rm hon})$, or $\gamma=m^\star_{\rm hon}<m^\star_{\rm pat}$ with the honest cost almost surely $\gamma$ and $\lVert\mathrm{Dom}\rVert<k_\star+h_\star$. If the least rate is shared by two treatments whose costs differ non-degenerately, there is no eventual winner.
\item Under \SAIabbr's pricing (code R), $m^\star_{\rm hon}<\gamma$ whenever $\varepsilon^*_\star<\mathsf R/(2\mathrm e)$ (for $\Delta\le2\varepsilon^*_\star$) or $\Delta<\mathsf R/\mathrm e$ (for $\Delta\ge2\varepsilon^*_\star$). So a system that the law confines within about $66^\circ$ of longitude (a sufficient bound) is never cut out asymptotically: it is kept with its own, wider tolerance. Under code Q with $\Delta\le2\varepsilon^*_\star$ the honest treatment costs exactly $\gamma$ per datum, and the restriction is eventually selected iff $m^\star_{\rm pat}>\gamma$ and $\lVert\mathrm{Dom}\rVert<k_\star+h_\star$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

For the Moon over one year ($\varepsilon^*=2.6^\circ$, $k_\star=60$, $h_\star=20$, $\lVert\mathrm{Dom}\rVert=4$), an exact computation (code in the accompanying repository) gives: under code Q at $\Delta=10'$ and $60'$ the honest treatment ties the literal one and the cheaper domain clause excludes the Moon; under code R the Moon is kept with its tolerance at every cell width from $10'$ to $30^\circ$. So under \SAIabbr's pricing the inducer does not cut the Moon out of Kepler's law.

A superseded theory is retained where its derivations are cheaper. Let a datum proved from $T$ cost in addition $r_{\rm d}\,\mathrm{dl}_T$ bits, where $\mathrm{dl}_T$ is the size of the derivation used, and let $J^{\rm der}_\lambda(T;D):=\lambda\lVert T\rVert+\Exc_D(T)+\sum_{\text{data proved}}r_{\rm d}\,\mathrm{dl}_T$. Compare an honest old law $T_{\rm old}$ (tolerance $\varepsilon_{\rm old}$ on a window) with an exact successor $T_{\rm new}$ that proves every datum, with per-datum derivation charges $c_{\rm old}<c_{\rm new}$.

\begin{proposition}[Retention of a superseded theory]\status{proved; computed (derivation sizes assumed)}\src{laws Prop 6.2}\label{prop:laws:retention}
\leavevmode
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item $T_{\rm old}$ beats $T_{\rm new}$ on $D$ iff $\lambda(\lVert T_{\rm new}\rVert-\lVert T_{\rm old}\rVert)+(c_{\rm new}-c_{\rm old})\,\#\{\text{data }T_{\rm old}\text{ proves}\}+c_{\rm new}\,\#\{\text{data }T_{\rm old}\text{ does not prove}\}>\Exc_D(T_{\rm old})$.
\item With i.i.d.\ data the rates are $m_{\rm old}=s_{\rm old}\gamma_{\rm si}+(1-s_{\rm old})c_{\rm old}$, with $s_{\rm old}$ the silence probability of $T_{\rm old}$, and $m_{\rm new}=c_{\rm new}$. If $m_{\rm old}<m_{\rm new}$ (resp.\ $>$), then almost surely $T_{\rm old}$ (resp.\ $T_{\rm new}$) is eventually the $\lambda$-MAP of the two, whatever $\lambda$ and the lengths are. At $m_{\rm old}=m_{\rm new}$ the lengths decide if the cost difference is almost surely constant, and otherwise there is no eventual winner.
\item Let $s_{\rm old}=\min(2\varepsilon_{\rm old}/\Delta,1)$. Under code Q, $m_{\rm old}$ is non-increasing in $\Delta$ on $\{\Delta:\gamma(\Delta)\ge c_{\rm old}\}$; under code R it is non-increasing in $\Delta$ on $(0,\infty)$ if $\beta_e\ge c_{\rm old}$. In either case the cell widths in that range at which $T_{\rm old}$ is retained form an interval $[\Delta^*,\cdot)$; for $\Delta\ge2\varepsilon_{\rm old}$ under code Q, $\Delta^*$ solves $2\varepsilon_{\rm old}(\gamma(\Delta)-c_{\rm old})/\Delta=c_{\rm new}-c_{\rm old}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\emph{Proof idea.} Compare the objectives term by term; apply \cref{thm:laws:as} to the rates; monotonicity in code R is \cref{lem:laws:codeR}(iii) (\cref{app:laws:domains}).

With derivations from the successor assumed a thousand times larger, as for a validated integration against solving Kepler's equation, the least retained cell width for Mars over 12 years at $c_{\rm new}=1$ bit is $27.7'$ under code Q and $1.74'$ under code R; over the bodies and windows computed, random completion extends retention to precisions $1.8$ to $16$ times finer (code in the accompanying repository). A superseded theory's domain of applicability is where its derivation savings exceed its loss of fit: coarse enough data, short enough windows, and derivations from the successor that are expensive enough.

\paragraph{Domains are learned as tolerances.}\label{rem:laws:domainlearning}\status{assessment}\src{laws \S8}
Under \SAIabbr's pricing, then, a domain of applicability shows up as a tolerance and an error distribution that depend on the system and the window, not as a sharp boundary: for every planet and window computed, and for the Moon over a month, a year and 18.6 years, a law with its own tolerance has a lower rate than leaving the body's data literal (\cref{prop:laws:domain}(c), \cref{tab:laws:residuals}). A sharp boundary is selected only when even an honest tolerance is worse than literal data, or under literal pricing at fine precision, where the constants decide a tie. The posterior over an explicit pool of domain predicates is not computed (\cref{q:laws:domains}).
