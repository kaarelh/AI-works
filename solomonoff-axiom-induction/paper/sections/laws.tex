\section{Approximate laws and the best system}\label{sec:laws}

Kepler's laws are false: against a modern planetary theory a fixed ellipse misses Mars by one or two arcminutes over a few decades and the Moon by about a degree within a month (\cref{tab:kepler:residuals}), yet the laws were, and are, good science. An exact law that is slightly wrong is refuted by any datum precise enough to show its error. We ask what \SAIabbr does with an approximately true law, and what becomes of Lewis's best system and of explanatory power.\footnote{The questions of approximately true laws and of the domain of applicability of a model are discussed in H\"anni's working notes \citep{hanni2026notes}.} $\MA$ also mixes randomised theories, and under \SAIabbr's pricing an approximately true law is a law with a calibrated error model (\cref{prop:laws:errormodel}); that pricing is not an account of approximate correctness (\cref{par:laws:purpose}).

\subsection{Setting}\label{sec:laws:setting}

\begin{definition}[Answered data, exception codes, approximate laws]\status{definition}\src{laws Defs 1.1--1.3, 1.5, 1.7; \S2 setting; Setting S3}\label{def:laws:setting}
(a)~\emph{Answered data.} A datum $(\mathcal Q,a)$ is a question (\cref{def:setting:questions}) and its true answer, read as the two-valued datum $(a,1)$; a data set $D$ has pairwise distinct questions and $n:=\lvert D\rvert$ data. $T$ \emph{proves} (\emph{refutes}) the datum if $B\cup T\vdash a$ ($B\cup T\vdash\neg a$), and is \emph{silent} otherwise; $\gamma(\mathcal Q):=\log\lvert\mathcal Q\rvert$ is the \emph{literal cost}. (b)~\emph{Codes.} $\lVert\Gamma\rVert:=\sum_{\chi\in\Gamma}\ell(\chi)$, with $\ell(\chi):=1+\lvert c_S(\chi)\rvert$ for a prefix-free code $c_S$, for a finite theory or \emph{module} $\Gamma$. If $B\cup T\cup D^\pm$ is consistent, the \emph{exceptions} of $T$, the data it does not prove, cost $\Exc^Q_D(T):=\sum\gamma(\mathcal Q)$ (code Q), $\Exc^F_D(T):=\sum\ell(a)$ (code F) or $\Exc^R_D(T):=-\log P^R_T(D)$, $P^R_T(D)$ the probability of the data when the law's interval is completed uniformly at random (code R, \cref{def:laws:settingfull}); otherwise $\Exc_D(T):=\infty$. Unmarked, $\Exc$ is code Q. The minimisers of $J_\lambda(T;D):=\lambda\lVert T\rVert+\Exc_D(T)$, $\lambda>0$, are the \emph{$\lambda$-MAPs} (Definition~3.6 of the companion paper on steeper priors \citep{claudeSFI2026steeper}); $T^{-Z}$ withdraws $T$ at the times in a finite set $Z$, at about $c_{\rm time}$ bits each. (c)~\emph{Interval laws.} Over $B:=\mathrm{RCF}$ with constants $q_t$ for times $t$ in a decidable set $I$ of numerals, a \emph{pointwise interval law} is $\{\underline f(t)\le q_t\le\overline f(t):t\in I\}$ for computable $\underline f\le\overline f$; the \emph{exact law} $T_0$ has $\underline f=\overline f=f$, the \emph{honest law} $T^\varepsilon$ has $f\mp\varepsilon$. \emph{Interval data} answer questions that partition a range $[0,\mathsf R)$ into cells. (d)~\emph{Errors.} $e(t):=f(t)-q(t)$ for a real quantity $q$, and $\varepsilon^*:=\operatorname{ess\,sup}\lvert e\rvert$ at a data time. \emph{Regime G}: intervals of half-width $\delta$ around measurements with errors at most $\delta_0\le\delta$. \emph{Regime R}: the errors $e_j:=e(t_j)$ at fresh times $t_j$ are i.i.d., and datum $j$ is the cell of width $\Delta$ containing $q(t_j)$ in a uniformly dithered grid on a circle of circumference $\mathsf R$; $\gamma=\log(\mathsf R/\Delta)$, $p_\Delta:=\Ex\min(\lvert e\rvert/\Delta,1)$, $s_\Delta:=\min(2\varepsilon^*/\Delta,1)$; $\mathrm e$ is Euler's number. (e)~\emph{Peakedness.} $\pk(e):=\varepsilon^*/\Ex\lvert e\rvert$ and $\pk_\varepsilon(e):=\varepsilon/\Ex\lvert e\rvert$; reported values name their fit (minimax or mean).
\end{definition}

\subsection{Deterministic theories and precision}\label{sec:laws:strength}

Over deterministic theories proof-requiring compatibility is monotone in logical strength: $T_0\vdash T^\varepsilon$, data compatible with $T^\varepsilon$ are compatible with $T_0$, and with prior weights $2^{-\lVert T\rVert}$ the posterior odds of $T_0$ against $T^\varepsilon$ stay at least $2$, although $T_0$ is false when $e\not\equiv0$ (\cref{prop:laws:strength}). In regime G, $T_0$ is almost surely compatible with the data at every sample size when $\delta\ge\varepsilon^*+\delta_0$ and, under mild conditions on the measurement error, eventually refuted when $\delta<\varepsilon^*+\delta_0$ (\cref{prop:laws:tolerance}). In regime R the exact law survives $n$ data with probability $(1-p_\Delta)^n$ and is almost surely refuted eventually iff $\Prob[e\ne0]>0$, however coarse the cells; with a sinusoidal error of amplitude $1'$ it is refuted by positions rounded to a dithered grid of $1^\circ$ after about $94$ data (\cref{prop:laws:rounding}).

\subsection{Randomised theories and laws with error models}\label{sec:laws:random}

A theory machine reading a random tape answers each question at random within the law, so $\LA(D)\le\lVert T\rVert+\Exc^R_D(T)+c$ for a pointwise interval law $T$ on interval data consistent with it (\cref{prop:laws:randomised}). \SAIabbr is the best two-part code over theory machines, $\LA(D)=\min_G[K(G)-\log\nu_G(D)]\pm c$ (\cref{prop:laws:twopart}); \emph{\SAIabbr's pricing} of a form is the weight $2^{-(K(G)-\log\nu_G(D))}$ of a theory machine $G$ in $\MA$'s mixture. Between the honest law $T^{\varepsilon^*}$ and the exact law with exceptions $T_0^{-Z}$, for $\Delta\ge2\varepsilon^*$, spread errors favour the first and peaked ones the second, with a threshold on $\pk(e)$ set by the prices of an exception and of silence (\cref{prop:laws:peaked,prop:laws:patches}); band-confined completions move this comparison inside the band. For $\varepsilon\ge\varepsilon^*$ and a computable distribution $\mathcal G$ on $[0,1]$, the \emph{band-confined completion} $\mathrm{BC}_{\mathcal G}$ states $T^\varepsilon$ and draws each value $f(t_i)-\varepsilon+2\varepsilon\hat w_i$, $\hat w_i\sim\mathcal G$, from the tape; at fresh times its law is $\prod_j\mathcal G(C'_j)$, $C'_j$ the cell of datum $j$ with the band mapped affinely onto $[0,1]$. Uniform $\mathcal G$ gives the \emph{uniform band}, and $\mathcal G=(1-\rho)\delta_{1/2}+\rho\,\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]$ gives $\mathrm{HY}(\rho)$: exact except in a share $\rho$ of cases and then within $\varepsilon$.

\begin{proposition}[Laws with error models]\status{proved; computed}\src{laws Prop 3.6a, Def 3.6a}\label{prop:laws:errormodel}
In regime R let $\varepsilon\ge\varepsilon^*$, and let $\mathcal F$ be the distribution function of $w_j:=(\varepsilon-e_j)/(2\varepsilon)$, the position of the true value in the band.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item If $\Delta\ge2\varepsilon$, the rate of $\mathrm{BC}_{\mathcal G}$ is $(2\varepsilon/\Delta)\,c_{\mathcal G}$, with
\[c_{\mathcal G}:=\Ex_w\int_0^1\big(\ind[v<w](-\log(1-\mathcal G(v)))+\ind[v>w](-\log\mathcal G(v))\big)\,dv.\]
So band-confined completions are ranked in the same way at every such $\Delta$.
\item $c_{\mathcal G}\ge c_{\mathcal F}:=\int_0^1H_2(\mathcal F(v))\,dv$, $H_2$ the binary entropy, with equality iff $\mathcal G=\mathcal F$ almost everywhere on $[0,1]$. In particular the uniform band is the best band-confined completion iff $e$ is uniform on $[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]$.
\item For every theory machine $G'$, $G_U$ included, almost surely $\liminf_n\frac1n(-\log\nu_{G'}(D_n))\ge r_{\mathcal F}(\Delta):=\Ex[-\log\mathcal F(C'_1)]$, the conditional entropy rate of the answers given the questions, while $-\frac1n\log\nu_{\mathrm{BC}_{\mathcal F}}(D_n)\to r_{\mathcal F}(\Delta)$. If $\mathcal F$ is computable, then $\LA(D_n)/n\to r_{\mathcal F}(\Delta)$ almost surely. For $\Delta\ge2\varepsilon$, $r_{\mathcal F}(\Delta)=(2\varepsilon/\Delta)\,c_{\mathcal F}$.
\item $c_{\mathrm{HY}(\rho)}$ is convex in $\rho\in(0,1]$, and some $\mathrm{HY}(\rho)$ with $\rho<1$ beats the uniform band iff $\Ex\ln(1+\lvert e\rvert/\varepsilon)<2\ln2-1$. This requires $\pk_\varepsilon(e)>\ln2/(2\ln2-1)\approx1.79$, and holds whenever $\pk_\varepsilon(e)>\mathrm e/(4-\mathrm e)\approx2.12$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\emph{Proof idea.} A boundary falls in the band with probability $2\varepsilon/\Delta$, and the datum costs $-\log$ of the $\mathcal G$-mass on the true side; (b) is Gibbs' inequality, (c) the Borel--Cantelli lemma (\cref{app:laws:honest}).

So under \SAIabbr's pricing the preferred form is a law with a calibrated error model, neither a silent partial theory nor an exact law with listed exceptions. That a model includes its noise distribution is standard in MDL (cf.\ \citealp{rissanen1978modeling,grunwald2007minimum}); what is specific is that for a proof-requiring inducer the honest partial theory is one completion among many. Selection among finitely many forms is almost sure, and for the randomised theories that $\MA$ mixes humility is calibration (\cref{thm:laws:as,cor:laws:phases,prop:laws:humility}).

\paragraph{What the pricing does not see.}\label{par:laws:purpose}\status{assessment, resting on prop:laws:partition, thm:laws:entropy, ex:laws:rival, ex:laws:misspecified, prop:laws:dominance (proved; computed)}\src{purpose P29, S-final; P4--P7, P14, P16, P31, P34--P36}\Cref{prop:laws:errormodel} describes the credence- and compression-best forecast, not approximate correctness. For questions about one quantity the loss is the log score of the cell that the answers determine: a question family fixes a resolution, while the cost of a miss is set by each hypothesis's own error model, not by a purpose (\cref{prop:laws:partition}). \SAIabbr's pricing ranks rival laws by a dispersion of the error for coarse cells and, for fine ones, by the entropy of the error rather than its size (\cref{thm:laws:entropy}); it can rank a law above one that is closer to the truth at every time; and other proper scores rank rival laws differently, under misspecification and even without it (\cref{ex:laws:rival,ex:laws:misspecified}). A tolerance on a domain is a sentence, in which \SAIabbr holds a credence; a graded loss is not (\cref{prop:laws:dominance}).

\paragraph{Kepler's residuals.} For seven bodies and six windows each (three for the Moon) we fitted one Kepler ellipse four ways and kept the fit with the least largest longitude error (the minimax fit) and the one with the least mean error (the mean fit), with errors against the plan94 and moon98 ephemerides (\cref{tab:laws:residuals}). A computation at eleven cell widths (code in the accompanying repository) finds that among the Kepler-based theory machines compared a law with a 40-bin error histogram, fitted and scored on disjoint halves of the times, has the least rate in all $429$ cells, and that in the coarse regime the two-point form beats the uniform band for $34$ of $39$ body--window pairs, exactly where \cref{prop:laws:errormodel}(d) says. Over the four fits, maximum, RMS and mean absolute error disagree on the best fit, the calibrated pricing agrees with none, and its ranking changes with cell width for $11$--$16$ of $234$ pairs, by up to $6.8\%$ of the rate (\cref{app:laws:purpose}).

\subsection{Sound reasoning with approximate models}\label{sec:laws:sound}

True honest models of one quantity are jointly consistent over a true background and prove only truths about it; when the background decides the comparisons (as $\mathrm{RCF}$ does for semialgebraic models), exact approximations that differ anywhere on their common domain are jointly inconsistent and so prove everything (\cref{prop:laws:sound}). Honest dynamical laws have derivation horizons, up to which exact approximations are guaranteed correct (\cref{prop:laws:horizon,prop:laws:exacthorizon}); after $100$ steps with Lipschitz constant $1$ the propagated tolerance is $100\varepsilon$, against a mean deviation of about $7.96\varepsilon$ for independent random-sign errors (a computation, \cref{rem:laws:typical}). The device does not say how to choose a model, a domain or a tolerance (\cref{sec:limits:domains}).

\subsection{The best system as a steeper MAP}\label{sec:laws:best}

Lewis's laws are the theorems of the system that best balances simplicity against strength (cf.\ \citealp{lewis1983new,lewis1994humean}). By analogy (best systems are a metaphysics of laws, \SAIabbr is inference from finite data) we take strength to be fit to data in a fixed base language, the balance to be $\lambda$, and laws with private literal facts as candidates. The points $(\lVert T\rVert,\Exc_D(T))$ have a convex hull with vertices $v_i$ in order of length and edge slopes $-\sigma_i$, $\infty=:\sigma_0>\sigma_1>\dots>0$; for $\lambda\in(\sigma_{i+1},\sigma_i)$ the $\lambda$-MAPs are the theories at $v_i$ (Lemma~3.8 of the companion paper on steeper priors). A point $(\alpha_{\rm r},L_{\rm r})$ of some theory is a \emph{zero-window $(\sigma,\tau)$-kink} (Definition~5.2 there) if theories shorter by $x>0$ have fit at least $L_{\rm r}+\sigma x$ and theories longer by $x>0$ fit at least $L_{\rm r}-\tau x$.

\begin{proposition}[Robustness to the balance is a kink]\status{proved, given Lemma 3.8 and Corollary 5.4 of the companion paper on steeper priors; computed}\src{laws Prop 5.6}\label{prop:laws:kink}
For a family of candidate theories satisfying the hypotheses of the hull lemma (\cref{prop:laws:P2hyp}) and $0\le\tau<\sigma\le\infty$, the following are equivalent: (a) the set of $\lambda$-MAPs is the same for every $\lambda\in(\tau,\sigma)$; (b) some hull vertex $v_i$ has $\sigma_{i+1}\le\tau$ and $\sigma\le\sigma_i$; (c) the point family has a zero-window $(\sigma,\tau)$-kink at a point that is a hull vertex.
\end{proposition}

\emph{Proof idea.} The $\lambda$-MAP set is fixed between consecutive edge slopes and changes at each; (b)$\Leftrightarrow$(c) is Corollary~5.4(c) of the companion paper on steeper priors (\cref{app:laws:best}).

So the best system is the same for every balance in an interval exactly when the data's hull has a kink there. This makes precise robustness to the balance of simplicity against strength, with the measures of simplicity and of fit held fixed, one aspect of what Lewis calls a kind nature (cf.\ \citealp{lewis1994humean}); robustness to the measure of simplicity itself fails among the compatible Mars forms in the noise-free regime (\cref{sec:kepler:pool}). On a solar-system toy a computation finds one best system for all $\lambda\in(7.03,135.37)$ (\cref{app:laws:best}); Lewis's worry about a predicate true of exactly the actual things is blocked up to the complexity of a language chosen after the data (\cref{prop:laws:trivial}).

The \emph{explanatory power} $\EP_D(\mathcal A;T)$ of a module $\mathcal A\subseteq T$ is the bits of data it explains per bit of its statement, in the context $T$ (\cref{def:laws:EP}). Every module of a $\lambda$-MAP has $\EP\ge\lambda$, no addition to it has $\EP>\lambda$, and for $\lambda>1$ a fact used once never enters (\cref{prop:laws:slopes,prop:laws:once}). So the model--randomness split of the companion paper on steeper priors separates facts reused across many data from facts used once, not laws from initial conditions; what singles out a law is that its $\EP$ grows with the number of systems it serves. Kepler's third law, which shortens the stated elements, is a law about initial conditions (\cref{prop:laws:K3}; for its worth to Kepler, \cref{sec:kepler}), and in the question format elements never enter a $\lambda$-MAP (\cref{prop:laws:questions}).

\subsection{Explanation and correction}\label{sec:laws:explain}

$\lVert\mathcal A\rVert\,\EP_D(\mathcal A;T)$ is the log-likelihood ratio of $T$ against $T\setminus\mathcal A$ for $P_T(D):=2^{-\Exc_D(T)}$. The support a hypothesis gets from joint data $D_1\cup D_2$ splits into the supports from the parts plus a term $U$, the change in the mutual information of $D_1$ and $D_2$ that the hypothesis brings about, of the form of Myrvold's measure of unification \citep{myrvold2003bayesian}: $U=0$ for laws that are products over questions with stated parameters; with a $k$-bit parameter integrated out, $U$ is $k$ within one system when each part of the data identifies the parameter, $0$ across systems with independent parameters, and the bits of a shared constant across systems that share one and each identify it (\cref{prop:laws:EP}). Copernicus against Ptolemy is unification without better prediction (\cref{prop:laws:copernicus}).

Newton's theory does not entail Kepler's laws; with the planets' masses and initial conditions it contradicts their exact form (cf.\ \citealp{duhem1954aim}) and derives corrected statements. In an exact rational toy of perturbed circular motion the successor proves the honest old law with uniform tolerance $\varepsilon$ on a window iff $\varepsilon$ is at least the largest distance between the two theories' trajectories on it, and refutes the exact old law whenever the perturbation changes the first step (\cref{prop:laws:correction}): the old law's domain of applicability is derived, not stipulated.

\subsection{Domains of applicability and retention}\label{sec:laws:domains}

\paragraph{Domains are learned as tolerances.}\label{rem:laws:domainlearning}\status{assessment, resting on prop:laws:domain and prop:laws:retention (proved; computed)}\src{laws \S6, \S8}
A domain restriction leaves a system's data literal (\cref{prop:laws:domain}). Under \SAIabbr's pricing an honest band of half-width $\varepsilon^*$ costs at most $\log(2\varepsilon^*/\Delta)+\log\mathrm e$ bits per datum when $\Delta\le2\varepsilon^*$, below the literal $\log(\mathsf R/\Delta)$ iff $\varepsilon^*<\mathsf R/(2\mathrm e)$, and at most $(2\varepsilon^*/\Delta)\log\mathrm e<\log(\mathsf R/\Delta)$ when $2\varepsilon^*\le\Delta<\mathsf R/\mathrm e$; so the Moon ($\varepsilon^*=2.6^\circ$ over a year, minimax fit) is kept in Kepler's law with its own tolerance at every cell width computed, while literal pricing at $10'$ and $60'$ excludes it by a cheap domain clause. We conclude that under \SAIabbr's pricing a domain of applicability shows up as a tolerance and an error distribution that depend on the system and the window, not as a sharp boundary; a superseded theory is retained where its derivation savings exceed its loss of fit (\cref{prop:laws:retention}).
