### 2. [major] "We still find it plausible that good theories are shaped like logical theories" is asserted without grounds, and sits uneasily with the paper's own findings
* **Location:** `phil.tex`, `sec:phil:craig`, paragraph after `rem:phil:craig`; `discussion.tex`, "What the formalism shows".
* **Problem:** the binding epistemic policy is that the paper asserts only what we believe, and gives reasons. The sentence is the brief's early stance, adopted before the `shape` results; neither the philosophy record (which owns the assessments) nor the synthesis endorses it. The paper's own findings point the other way on what such plausibility could rest on: §6.2 says that in the best formal model "what matters is a reasoner that can do algebra or chain through intermediate concepts ... not logical form as such", §7 that "logical form does not get its support from directionality", and §6.2 that "declarative is not sentential" (physics writes classes of models, not first-order theories). So "shaped like logical theories" also contradicts the paper's own qualification. As written, a philosopher reader will see an unargued residue of the author's intuition placed right after the paper's evidence against its most natural defences.
* **Evidence:** `grep -i plausible` over `research/tracks/philosophy/notes-final.md` and `research/notes-final.md` finds no such endorsement (only "natural types: plausible"); rec. §1.4 verdict: "item 2 is the best formal answer available, and in its model it does *not* show that logical form is what matters"; rec. §1.2: "the observation supports 'declarative'; 'first-order sentences' is a modelling choice"; brief "What a theory is": the descriptive claim is the observation; the explanatory question is open.
* **Fix:** replace by a sentence that states what we believe and why: "We accept the observation that written theories are declarative, and think it likely that this shape is good for reasons our formalism does not model, such as communication, division of labour and piecemeal checking (`q:phil:declarative`); but in the only formal model we have, logical form as such buys at most a constant factor, and nothing in our results favours sentences over classes of models." Same change in the discussion ("We still find it plausible ... logical theories" -> "We think it likely that the declarative shape of written theories is good for reasons our model does not capture, such as ...; what is missing is a formal setting in which that shape provably pays").

### 3. [major] The semantic-view objection is raised but not answered; the record's answer is dropped
* **Location:** `phil.tex`, `sec:phil:observation` ("Declarative is not sentential"); `kepler.tex`, the paragraph after "What the data decide and what the syntax decides" ("The simplest theory compatible with the data is thus, in this regime, relative to the language of the law texts").
* **Problem:** the obvious objection from the semantic view (Suppes, van Fraassen) to the paper's headline philosophical computation is: the language relativity of §10 is an artefact of putting a prior on *texts*; a theory is a class of models, and a presentation-free prior would not show it. The record answers this in three steps, and the paper keeps only the first: (a) the question-answering view is neutral between sentences and classes of models (kept); (b) the Kepler computation is itself mostly semantic: compatibility with interval data is a condition on a region of element space, the Occam factor is its measure, and the only syntactic ingredient is the law-text prior, which is exactly what produces the relativity; a presentation-free prior (uniform over the five families, volume on elements) selects the ellipse at every $\delta\le60'$ from the data alone (dropped as an answer to the semanticist; the number appears in §10 (ii) without this reading); (c) the rejoinder: a prior over infinitely many model families needs an enumeration, that is a presentation, so the relativity is relocated, not removed, and a volume prior on elements depends on the parametrisation (a Bertrand-type relativity: halving the eccentricity ranges moves the vicarious hypothesis by one bit) (dropped). Halvorson and Lutz are cited only in a background list in §12. The presentation-free margins are themselves small (0.20 bits at $60'$, 0.70 at $30'$, at most 14.61) and parametrisation-dependent at the one-bit level, which §10 (ii) does not say. Without (b) and (c), the paper's conclusion that "simplicity among compatible forms is language-relative" is exposed to exactly the objection a philosopher of science will raise first.
* **Evidence:** rec. §1.4 item 4, bullets 2-3 ("So the paper should say: ... §4.4 shows that this is where the language relativity enters, and §4.4's semantic computation shows how much of the verdict survives without it"); computation `research/tracks/philosophy/checks/c9` part (5) and rec. §4.4 Part A, second bullet (margins 0.20, 0.70, 1.62, ..., 14.61 bits; "halving the eccentricity ranges moves the vicarious hypothesis by one bit against the four-element forms"); `related.tex` l. 30 (Halvorson only in a list).
* **Fix:** add three sentences after "relative to the language of the law texts" in `kepler.tex` (or as a paragraph "The semantic view" in `sec:phil:observation`, pointing to §10): "A semanticist will call this relativity an artefact of a prior on texts. Our computation is mostly semantic: compatibility is a condition on a region of element space, and the Occam factor is its measure; only the law-text prior is syntactic, and with a presentation-free prior (uniform over the five families, volume on their elements) the data alone select the ellipse at every $\delta\le60'$. But a prior over infinitely many families of models needs an enumeration of them, which is a presentation, and a volume prior depends on the parametrisation (halving the eccentricity ranges moves the vicarious hypothesis by one bit), so the relativity is relocated rather than removed." Cite `halvorson2012what`, `lutz2017what` (both citable at abstract level per bib-report §5.2 group 14) where the semantic view is introduced in §6.2.

### 4. [major] Lewis's "kind nature" is characterised more narrowly in the result than in the claim
* **Location:** `intro.tex`, `sec:intro:contrib` item (10) ("Lewis's 'kind nature' is a kink in the hull of length against fit"); (the result itself is `laws.tex`, `prop:laws:kink`, titled "Kind nature is a kink", outside this review's sections but named in the intro and in §13's Kepler/best-system summary).
* **Problem:** the proposition shows that the set of best systems is the same for every *balance* $\lambda$ in an interval iff the hull has a kink there, with the measures of simplicity (code length in a fixed language) and of strength (fit in a fixed base language) held fixed. In Lewis's later discussion the worry that kindness answers is that "the standards of simplicity and strength and balance" may "come from us": a kind nature makes the best system come out best under any such standards, not only under any balance. Robustness to the standard of simplicity is the language relativity that §10 shows does *fail* among the compatible Mars forms. Moreover the one Lewis sentence the laws record saw ("how good a balance that is will depend on how kind nature is", seen only in a citing source) is about how good the best balance is, not about robustness. So the unqualified "kind nature is a kink" overstates the match.
* **Evidence:** WebSearch (2026-10-17): Lewis, "Humean Supervenience Debugged" (1994), quoted in secondary sources: "The worst problem about the best-system analysis is that when we ask where the standards of simplicity and strength and balance come from, the answer may seem to be that they come from us", followed by the reply that if nature is kind one system is far ahead of its rivals whatever the standards (the exact wording of that reply was not found by search; it is from memory and should be checked against the text before quoting); `research/tracks/laws/notes-final.md` l. 927 (the "kind nature" sentence seen only in a citing source, p. 478); `laws.tex` l. 80 ("So nature is kind on an interval of balances exactly when ..."), which is correctly scoped.
* **Fix:** intro (10): "the best system is the same for every balance of simplicity against strength in an interval exactly when the hull of length against fit has a kink there, which formalises one aspect of what Lewis calls a kind nature (robustness to the balance, with the measures of simplicity and fit fixed)". Suggest to the owner of §9: retitle `prop:laws:kink` "Robustness to the balance is a kink", and add one sentence: "Robustness to the standard of simplicity itself, the other half of Lewis's kindness, is the language relativity of §10, which fails among the compatible Mars forms in the noise-free regime."

### 5. [major] The paper does not position \SAI against Bayesian confirmation theory, and dismisses the problem of the priors in one clause
* **Location:** `related.tex`, "Universal and operator induction" ("Whether a universal prior justifies a preference for simplicity is disputed ...; nothing here depends on it"); `phil.tex` (no paragraph on confirmation theory); `limits.tex` (no limitation on the prior).
* **Problem:** \SAI is Bayesian confirmation theory with a universal prior and 0/1 likelihoods (proof-requiring compatibility is hypothetico-deductive confirmation inside Bayes). A philosopher of science will look for its relation to that literature's standard problems: (a) the problem of the priors and language dependence (Goodman's grue is the classical case; the translation theorem bounds it by $K(\tau)$, and §10 shows the bound is as large as the text differences); (b) the tacking (irrelevant conjunction) problem of hypothetico-deductive confirmation, which \SAI answers only through the length prior (the paper mentions the conjunction problem only for explanation); (c) Carnap's zero confirmation of universal laws in infinite domains, which \SAI avoids (item 5 of Table 2 is the formal content, and it is an advantage worth stating against Carnap, cited only in §12); (d) old evidence and logical omniscience (present, §11); (e) the catch-all hypothesis, which universality removes at the price of incomputability. Only (d) and Duhem's problem (Table 2 item 7) appear. And "nothing here depends on it" is too strong: the theorems do not depend on whether a universal prior justifies simplicity, but every philosophical reading that treats \SAI as "an ideal of the evaluation of theories" (§11 "Other limitations", §13) does.
* **Evidence:** `grep -i 'confirmation\|carnap\|goodman\|grue\|hypothetico\|tacking\|catch-all'` over `sections/*.tex` finds only Duhem (Table 2) and old evidence (§5, §11); `related.tex` l. 12; `limits.tex` l. 77 ("an ideal of the evaluation of theories"); `kepler.tex` l. 169 (translation cost "a few hundred bits ..., as large as the text differences").
* **Fix:** (1) replace "nothing here depends on it" by "our theorems do not depend on it; our use of \SAIabbr as an ideal of evaluation does, and we take the universal prior as a modelling choice whose machine dependence is bounded by the invariance theorem but, at the scale of our examples, not negligible (\cref{sec:kepler})". (2) Add to §6 (after Table 2 or in `sec:phil:differences`) a short paragraph "\SAIabbr as Bayesian confirmation" making points (a)-(e) in one sentence each, pointing to `thm:basic:translation`, `prop:logic:omega`, `prop:logic:oldevidence`, Table 2 items 5, 7, 11. Cite `carnap1950logical` (citable) for (c); for grue, Earman or Howson-Urbach, add references only after the bibliography editor verifies them (none is in `bib/all.bib` now). (3) Add to §11 one sentence that all verdicts are relative to the universal machine up to constants that are not small at the scale of real theories.

### 6. [major] "Approximate truth is a limitation of proof-requiring compatibility with deterministic theories, not of the axiom view" overreads the formal result
* **Location:** `phil.tex`, "What rests on theorems" (last clause of the "Proved" list); `limits.tex`, `sec:limits:approx` (first two sentences); `discussion.tex`, "What the formalism shows" (last sentence); also `intro.tex` contribution (5) is fine as stated.
* **Problem:** the theorems (`prop:laws:strength`, `prop:laws:errormodel`) say that over deterministic theories proof-requiring compatibility rewards the false exact law, and that $\MA$'s mixture, which contains *randomised* theories, prefers a law together with its error distribution. The error model lives in the coin flips of a theory machine, not in any axiom: the preferred hypothesis is a random axiom system, which by `thm:soi:onetape` is equally a random labeller. Calling this a success of "the axiom view" (a theory is a set of sentences) is a philosophical reading, not part of what is proved; in the paper's own terms (§11.1) a native treatment of statistical fit inside the proof-requiring core is open, and "sentences about probabilities" relate to data non-deductively. So §6 lists under "Proved" a reading that §11.1 implicitly denies. Synthesis §2.8 row 6 supports the formal content ("proved; computed"); the identification of $\MA$'s randomised theories with "the axiom view" is an assessment and should be labelled as one.
* **Evidence:** `limits.tex` l. 17 ("Graded fit enters only through randomised theories in $\MA$ ...; a native treatment is open"); `laws.tex` l. 11; rec. §5.2 (L2 "partially addressable"); rec. §5.0 (L0).
* **Fix:** in all three places: "is a limitation of proof-requiring compatibility with deterministic theories, not of \SAIabbr: its mixture contains randomised theories, and under its pricing an approximately true law is a law with a calibrated error model. The error model is carried by the randomness of the theory, not stated as an axiom, so for the axiom view in the narrow sense, a theory as a set of sentences, approximate truth remains tied to the statistical limitation of §11.1." In §6's "What rests on theorems", move the reading to the "arguments" list and keep the formal content under "Proved".


### 7. [major] "The idealised formalism cannot see the difference between a theory and a program" is stated without its scope, although the paper's own Table 2 reports an idealised separation in exactly the respect of partiality
* **Location:** `abstract.tex` ("The upshot: the idealised formalism cannot see the difference between a theory and a program"); `intro.tex`, paragraph "What a theory is" ("On the second question the idealised formalism is neutral, and this is a theorem"); `phil.tex`, `rem:phil:craig` and Table `tab:phil:differences` items 2 and 4; `discussion.tex`, "What the formalism shows".
* **Problem:** the theorems behind the headline (`thm:soi:coinfree`, `thm:soi:onetape`, `cor:basic:vocabulary`, `thm:shape:invisible`, `prop:phil:flagpolesystem`) concern data labelled true or false and the losses. But `prop:soi:independent` proves that when "independent" is itself observed, axiom systems beat consistent labellers by an unbounded amount on one datum, because a theory can leave a sentence open without committing (a $\Pi_1$ property) while a labeller must output $\iota$ after finitely many steps; and `prop:soi:closure` shows that, in the semimeasure convention, consistent labellers never become certain of an entailed sentence. These are idealised differences between theories and programs, and the first is exactly the "partiality" that the brief listed as a candidate property of logical theories ("A theory is silent where it has nothing to say"). The paper files both under "what SAI does compared with raw-data prediction" (Table 2 items 2 and 4), although they compare \SAIabbr with operator induction, i.e. theories with labellers, which is claim S, not D. So the unqualified headline is contradicted by the paper's own table, and the one formal trace of the partiality candidate goes unremarked. The intro's "and this is a theorem" also overstates: the record marks the neutrality *reading* as an assessment.
* **Evidence:** synthesis §2.8 row 1 gives the scope: "cannot see the difference between a theory and a program (representation, vocabulary, reasoner, formulation) | proved"; synthesis H1 "How to state it": "The equivalence controls semimeasure losses, not the meaning of 'undefined' (H7)"; H7 (observed independent label; logical closure); rec. §1.4 item 1: "[proved there; the reading is an assessment]"; brief "What a theory is", candidate properties (multi-directional use, **partiality**, modularity, closure under inference).
* **Fix:** (1) abstract and §13: "on data labelled true or false, the idealised formalism cannot see the difference between a theory and a program". (2) Intro: "On the second question the idealised formalism is neutral on data labelled true or false; this rests on theorems (...), and the reading is ours". (3) In `sec:phil:craig`, after the list of collapses, add: "The one respect in which the idealised formalism does separate theories from labellers is partiality: when 'independent' is itself a datum, a theory can be silent without committing, while a labeller must commit to silence in finite time, and the gap is unbounded on one datum (`prop:soi:independent`); likewise for logical closure in the semimeasure convention (`prop:soi:closure`). Such data arise in mathematics (independence results) rather than from observation, and the large gaps have no r.e. witnesses (`prop:soi:noRE`), so this is a formal trace of the partiality candidate, not support for S in empirical science." (4) Table 2: mark items 2 and 4 as comparisons with operator induction (theories against labellers), bearing on S as well as D, or move them to a separate group.

### 9. [minor] Characterisations in the opening paragraph of the introduction bundle authors into views they reject

### 19. [minor] Shapiro's model inference is described as the precedent of proof-requiring compatibility, but it uses the same mixture as inductive logic programming
* **Location:** `related.tex`, "Learning theories from data".
* **Problem:** in Shapiro's model inference a conjecture is too strong if it proves a false fact and too weak if it fails to prove a true one; false facts must not be proved, not refuted. That is the mixture the next sentence attributes to inductive logic programming (prove the positives, be consistent with the negatives), not proof-requiring compatibility, which requires refuting each false datum.
* **Evidence:** WebSearch 2026-10-17: secondary sources on Shapiro (1981) define "a conjecture is too strong iff [it] implies some negative facts, and too weak iff it cannot imply some positive facts" (mlanthology.org entry of the IJCAI 1981 paper; van der Laag and Nienhuys-Cheng, ECML 1993).
* **Fix:** "Shapiro's model inference, which infers theories from facts by conjectures and refutations, asks a conjecture to prove every true fact and no false one \citep{...}, the same mixture of the two compatibility notions of \cref{def:setting:variants} as inductive logic programming; it outputs one conjecture at a time, without a prior." (Content level: secondary; hedge with "as usually described" if the bibliography editor cannot raise it.)

### 21. [minor] "The eccentric circle of Hipparchus" for Mars is a misattribution
* **Location:** `kepler.tex`, `sec:kepler:pool`, paragraph "The pool and the data".
* **Problem:** Hipparchus's eccentric model is his solar theory; according to Ptolemy he did not construct planetary models. The eccentric circle as a model for a planet is older (Apollonius) and generic.
* **Evidence:** WebSearch 2026-10-17: Britannica (via search summary): "According to Ptolemy, Hipparchus ... did not attempt to devise a satisfactory planetary theory"; a commentary (arXiv 2208.02137): Hipparchus "did not propose an alternative model of his own for the planets"; one history (*From Aristarchus to Diophantus*) credits him with combining eccentrics and epicycles for planets, so sources differ, which is itself a reason not to attribute the form to him.
* **Fix:** "the eccentric circle (the form of Hipparchus's solar theory)", or simply "the eccentric circle". (The name occurs only at `kepler.tex` l. 81.)
