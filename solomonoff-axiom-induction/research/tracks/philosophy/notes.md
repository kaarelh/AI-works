## 0. Summary

**The argument map (§1).** Two problems: P1, what a theory *does*; P2, what a theory *is*. The axiom view's claims and our verdicts:

| claim | verdict | formal content (where proved) |
|---|---|---|
| **D** a theory answers questions posed in a given language, rather than predicting raw data | **true in comparative form** [assessment] | the difference is the *data type* (labelled sentences of a chosen language) plus *logical coherence*; it is not a new inference principle, since SAI $\asymp$ consistent SOI on sentences in every version (`sfi` Thm 2.1, 3.1 [proved there]); partiality (Prop 2.1 here; `sfi` Props 6.1–6.5) and many-directional use are what make it matter; a law's share of raw-data loss vanishes while its share of question loss is 1 (Prop 2.2 [proved; computed: c5]); question answering never needs more description than raw modelling plus the extractor (Prop 2.4 [proved, given `fundamentals` Thm 4.1]) |
| **S** a theory is a logical theory, not a program | **descriptive part true** (written theories are declarative, with procedural parts in use); **explanatory part open** and necessarily reasoner-relative [assessment] | Craig: the idealised formalism cannot see shape (`sfi` Thm 2.1, 3.1, `fundamentals` Cor 4.5), nor the reasoner (`shape` Thm 1.11); with a cost on the written theory, the formal settings in which shape matters separate *reasoners* and *vocabularies* (intermediate concepts), and logical shape buys many-directional use only up to a factor $\le9$ in written length ($\le3$ for the chain encoding) once intermediate concepts are available (`shape` Thm 1.12, 2.6, 3.6, Thm 4.3 [proved there]) |
| **E** theories explain propositions | **true in the unification sense, with two gaps** [assessment] | the flagpole tie: deductively equivalent theories, posterior odds frozen; derivation charges break the tie only relative to the formulation; interventional questions break it by one datum (Prop 3.1 [proved; computed: c6]); explanation by approximate correction (`laws` Prop 7.1) |
| **L** the language is given: the ladder raw data → existing abstractions → extended language → revised abstractions | **agreed** (brief) | step 2 = SAI over a fixed language; step 3 partly formal (`terms` Thm 2.1, 5.1, 8.1; `shape` Prop 3.1, Thm 3.6); step 4 no formal handle |
| **P** SAI is progress over raw-data prediction in what a theory does and is | **replace by a list of formal differences** [assessment] | §1.7: eleven items, each with its status; none concerns the shape of hypotheses |

**The Kepler example (§4).** New here:
* **Prop 4.3 [proved].** In a *geocentric* direction language Kepler's third law has content: K12 + K3 entails "Mars is not permanently at opposition", K12 does not. (In a heliocentric direction language it has none: Prop 4.2, scout P's Prop 2.2, re-proved.)
* **Prop 4.4 [proved; computed: c4 B, c7].** In the geocentric language K3 does exactly one job: it replaces the size element $\rho=a_{\rm Ma}/a_{\rm Ea}$, worth 11.3, 12.3, 13.4 and 14.7 bits at 8′, 4′, 2′ and 1′ (exact compatible volumes, `c4` Part B), against a law text of 375–450 bits. For one planet pair K3 does not pay; in these literal syntaxes it would pay only when it fixes the sizes of about 25–40 bodies (`laws` Prop 5.5(b)'s criterion), so at Kepler's precision compression alone does not select K3 (§4.4 Part B). Without K3 the geocentric direction of Mars at a given time ranges over an arc whose median length over 1580–1600 is 90° (c7).
* **The proof-requiring posterior over the historical Mars pool [computed: c4, c4b].** Polished minimax errors over 1580–1600: circle 640.8′, eccentric 23.05′, bisected equant 8.17′, vicarious hypothesis 1.41′, ellipse 1.07′ (each slightly below scout P's least-squares values). As the precision of the data improves, the compatible forms drop out in the historical order (this is syntax-free). Among compatible forms, the data's own contribution (the Occam factor of the compatible element volume) differs between forms by at most about 15 bits, while the law texts differ by 100–800 bits, so the preferred form is decided by the syntax: with epicyclic primitives the MAP runs through the full historical sequence circle → eccentric → equant → vicarious hypothesis → ellipse; with plain arithmetic it goes circle → eccentric → ellipse; with a Keplerian primitive circle → ellipse. Results in §4.4.
* **Distances as data [computed: c8].** With distances as data at relative tolerance 1%, the vicarious hypothesis needs a longitude tolerance of at least 6.31′ (2.01′ at 2%, 23.7′ at 0.2%), while the ellipse fits both at its longitude minimax 1.07′; so below 6.31′ only the ellipse is compatible, in every syntax. Kepler's last step, which the longitude-only posterior leaves to the syntax, is syntax-free once distances are data (§4.4 Part C).

**Limitations (§5).** The textbook point splits into a bridge gap, a computational gap (`shape`'s theorem, whose exact scope is stated: it needs a cost on the written theory, since under SAI's program-length prior the reasoner is invisible) and a tacit gap; approximate truth and domains are partly formal (`laws`); the condensation essay's list is assessed item by item. Verdict: the axiom view models the declarative, inferential core of theories and misses their procedural and dynamic sides; the paper should say it offers a model of that core, not a theory of concepts.

**Possible errors in the author's view (§6).** Two candidates were assessed and both survive, each in a stated form. (1) The domain-of-applicability note's "i don't think any nice formal criterion is going to be right": probably right that no single formal criterion reproduces scientific practice, probably wrong that none blocks the "100 = 99.9" catastrophe soundly while leaving ordinary approximate reasoning intact (tolerances on domains, propagated: `laws` Prop 4.3); the correction does not say how to choose models or tolerances, and worst-case bounds can become vacuous. (2) The condensation essay's "nothing fundamentally right about these information-theoretic losses": right for grading predictions for use, overstated for scoring credences and comparing theories, where the log score is privileged.

## 1. The problems and the argument map (T1)

### 1.1 Two problems

* **P1 (what a theory does).** What is a scientific theory for? Candidate answers in the literature: predicting observations (the raw-data view behind Solomonoff induction [known: solomonoff1964formal]); answering questions posed in some language, including why-questions [known: vanfraassen1980scientific, ch. 5; content: secondary]; explaining [known: hempel1948studies]; solving problems [known: laudan1977progress; content: secondary].
* **P2 (what a theory is).** A program that prints or predicts observations; a set of sentences closed under consequence (the "received" view [known: suppe1977structure; content: secondary]); a class of models (the semantic view [known: suppes1960comparison, vanfraassen1980scientific]); a core of models with intended applications (structuralism [known: sneed1971logical, balzer1987architectonic; content: secondary]); a practice of exemplars and skills [known: kuhn1970structure, kuhn1977second, polanyi1958personal; content: secondary].

The axiom view answers P1 with "answering questions in a given language" and P2 with "a logical theory"; the raw-data view answers "predicting observations" and "a program".

### 1.2 The descriptive observation and the explanatory question

The brief fixes the stance (it was agreed with the author on 2026-10-15); this record develops it.

* **Observation (descriptive) [assessment].** Human scientific theories, *as written*, are declarative: laws, definitions, principles, models described by equations; not procedures that print predictions. We agree; it is an observation about texts, needing no theory of why. **Caveat:** theories *in use* have procedural parts: methods attached to concepts (integration; the linearity-of-expectation trick), numerical schemes, perturbation series, simulation codes. Even in §4, deriving a single Mars position from K12 is a root-finding computation for Kepler's equation.
* **Question (explanatory) [open].** Why that shape is good, and whether an ideal inducer should produce it. Craig's theorem rules out an answer based only on what is representable (§1.4); any answer must be relative to a reasoner with costs, or to a community of reasoners who communicate, check and extend theories piece by piece.

### 1.3 Claim D: a theory answers questions posed in a given language

**Statement and sources.** A theory answers questions stated in a given language ("what is the period of Jupiter?", "where will Mars be at time $t$, to within 2′?"); it does not generate or predict a raw data stream. Sources: the author's request (brief); `phil-sci-scientific-theories-are-not-for-generating-or-predicting-arbitrary-data.md`; `phil-sci-science-predicts-only-very-particularly.md`; `predicting-specific-things.md`; the condensation essay ("it is intermediately correct, and also insightful, to think of concepts specifically as helping one answer questions and solve problems"; predictions are "already high-level" and "about extremely specific things"). The author clarified (brief, "Existing language") that the claim is comparative: answering in terms of existing abstractions is a step away from pixel prediction, not the end point.

**Arguments for** (scout P D1–D4, checked).
* (D1) Science is silent on almost everything; a raw-data predictor must model everything, so a theory's contribution is a vanishing share of its loss. *Formal version:* Prop 2.2 (share $O(\log m/m)$ of raw loss against share $1$ of question loss), with the correction that the law's *absolute* value is comparable in the two tasks.
* (D2) Predictions are graded by purpose-made tolerances on high-level quantities. *Formal version:* interval sentences as data (§4.1); the purpose fixes which sentences are data (§6.2).
* (D3) Questions are partial and so are theories. *Formal version:* the trichotomy, the "unanswered" mass (Prop 2.1), the independent-label separation (`sfi` Props 6.3–6.5).
* (D4) Theories answer in many directions (K3 gives $P$ from $a$ and $a$ from $P$; Le Verrier asked Newton's theory where the perturber of Uranus is). *Formal version:* conditional questions $(\alpha,\ell)$ in every direction are answered by deduction; their cost for a bounded reasoner is `shape` Thm 4.3 (a factor of the number of directions without intermediate concepts, at most 9 with them; nothing under program length).

**Objections.**
* (o1) The theory is in the raw-data predictor too. The author's own remark (`msgs to samuel on ML mind upload difficulty.md`, via scout P): Kepler's laws are "in" a video of the sky "in a deep way". *Formal support:* in the conservation world a Bayes mixture over raw data learns the law at one bit (Prop 2.2(c)). So the contrast is not one of content; it is whether the theory is a separable, queryable component, and what the inducer is asked.
* (o2) Formally SAI is still prediction: it predicts truth values of sentences, and SAI $\asymp$ consistent SOI on sentences in the coin-free, one-tape and Kolmogorov versions (`sfi` Thm 2.1, Thm 3.1 [proved there]). So "answering versus predicting" is not a difference in the type of inference. It is a difference in the *data type* and in the *coherence constraint*.
* (o3) Choosing the questions is half of science; SAI takes the questions and the language as given (§5, L4–L5).

**Verdict [assessment].** D is true in its comparative form. The formal content is: (i) the data type (sentences of a chosen language, labelled true/false, with questions as sets of sentences); (ii) logical coherence (hypotheses are consistent and closed under consequence); (iii) partiality; (iv) many-directional use. (i)–(ii) are definitional; (iii) is where SAI and SOI separate (`sfi` §6); (iv) matters only relative to a reasoner and a cost on the written theory (`shape` Thm 1.11, 4.3). The paper should not present D as a new inference principle.

### 1.4 Claim S: a theory is a logical theory, not a program

**Statement and sources.** The brief; `phil-sci-some-disanalogies-between-solomonoff-induction-and-science.md`, first bullet ("a system of talking which involves some sorts of things … about which some questions can be asked … with some relations between the answers"); the condensation essay (concepts are not code snippets; inferential relations are constitutive).

**Arguments for** (scout P S1–S4). (S1) the descriptive observation (§1.2); (S2) modularity: axioms have individual content and can be added, dropped, tested and combined across vocabularies, whereas "this program contains that law" has no canonical meaning; (S3) inferential role: theoretical terms get their meaning from their role in the theory, as meaning postulates [known: carnap1952meaning; content: secondary] and Ramsey–Lewis functional definitions [known: ramsey1931theories, lewis1970define; content: secondary]; (S4) many-directional use.

**What the formal results say.**
1. **The idealised formalism cannot see shape.** Coin-free: $W_A\asymp W_A^{\rm dec}\asymp W^{\rm cons}\asymp W^{\rm cl}$ and $K_A=K_F^{\rm cons}\pm c$ (`sfi` Thm 2.1 [proved there], the construction is Craig's [known: craig1953axiomatizability]). One-tape: $M_A\asymp M_F^{\rm cons}$ on every finite $D$ (`sfi` Thm 3.1 [proved there]). Vocabulary: adding symbols changes old-vocabulary weights by $O(1)$ (`fundamentals` Cor 4.5 [proved there]; class-level, tape-by-tape and penalised versions: `terms` Thm 2.1 [proved there]). Even the difference between an r.e. axiom set and a *finite* theory disappears in the coin-free version: r.e. axiom induction is finite-theory induction in a three-sorted extension, up to $O(1)$ (`terms` Thm 6.5 [proved there]; not one-tape, `terms` Rem 6.6). Time penalties on checking or generating axioms keep the equivalence on the axiom side (`sfi` Thm 3.4, under the self-simulation assumption (S$_{\rm tape}$)); the diagonal separations under time bounds come from free deduction, not from axioms (`sfi` Prop 7.6 [proved there]). So "a logical theory rather than an arbitrary program" is not a difference the posterior can register in this setting [proved there; the reading is an assessment]. The author agrees (brief).
2. **Where shape does matter, it is relative to a reasoner and to a vocabulary, and only when the written theory is charged.** Under program-length priors (SAI's own) every sound reasoner is equivalent to one round of unit propagation up to $O(1)$ bits, because a fixed program can write out the lemma library (`shape` Thm 1.11 [proved there]): the idealised inducer sees neither the shape of a theory nor the strength of its reader. With a cost on the written theory and a fixed weak reasoner (`shape` Thm 1.12), for linear laws over GF(2) with conditional questions in every direction: a Gaussian-elimination reasoner needs only the equations; a unit-propagation reasoner with clauses over the base variables needs exactly the clauses of the indecomposable circuits, at least $A_d2^{d-1}$ when every derived equation has weight $d\ge3$ (for the simplex family $\log_2(m+1)$ equations against $m2^{(m-1)/2}$ clauses; `shape` Thm 2.6, Cor 2.8); intermediate concepts make one law linear-size and usable ($4m-6$ clauses against $2^{m-1}$; `shape` Prop 3.1, Cor 2.7(c)) but not a system of laws: for an explicit family every unit-propagation-usable theory, with any intermediate concepts, has written length $2^{\Omega(m^{\varepsilon/3})}$, while elimination uses a linear-size theory (`shape` Thm 3.6, given Göös–Kamath–Robere–Sokolov 2019); and directed rules (the "procedural" shape) cost exactly a factor $m$, the number of query directions, against clauses without intermediate concepts, and at most a factor 9 in written length with them (3 for the chain encoding of parity) (`shape` Prop 4.2, Thm 4.3). **So the large separations are between reasoners and between vocabularies, not between declarative and procedural shapes;** logical shape buys many-directional use only up to a constant factor once intermediate concepts are allowed [proved there; the reading is `shape`'s and ours, assessment]. (These results were first obtained, for one law and without the program-length and systems results, by scout N2, Props 2.4–2.8.)
3. **Question-answering competence does not require an explicit theory.** Khardon and Roth's "learning to reason" combines learning with a reasoning task and shows, for propositional classes, that answering queries can be learnable where learning an explicit representation and then reasoning is not [known: khardon1997learning; content: abstract: the AAAI 1994 abstract states a learning-to-reason algorithm for a class of propositional languages that has no efficient reasoning algorithm when represented as a formula-based knowledge base; the JACM paper's full statements were not seen]. The same point holds formally inside SAI: SOI's labellers, which carry no theory, are equivalent to theories (item 1). So D (theories are for answering questions) does not by itself support S as an *optimality* claim [assessment, with scout L §7].
4. **The semantic view.** In first-order logic a theory and its class of models determine each other up to equivalence; Halvorson argues that the semantic view, taken literally, misidentifies theories [known: halvorson2012what; content: abstract], and Lutz that the residual disagreement about language dependence is illusory [known: lutz2017what; content: abstract]. The axiom view's real commitment is to a fixed language in which *questions* are posed; since questions are sentences, the syntactic view is the natural one here [assessment]. This also limits the claim: nothing in the formal results favours sentences over model classes as the *object*.
5. **Inconsistent theories in use.** Classical electrodynamics of point charges has been argued to be inconsistent as standardly formulated [known: frisch2004inconsistency; content: abstract; disputed in the literature]; Boltzmann's H-theorem with molecular chaos at all times conflicts with reversibility [known: brown2009boltzmann; content: abstract]. Proof-requiring SAI gives a globally inconsistent theory weight 0. Real theories are logical theories only after domains are restricted and approximations stated honestly (§5, L3; `laws` Prop 4.3).

**Verdict [assessment].** We agree with the descriptive observation. The explanatory claim is open; by item 1 any answer is relative to a reasoner (or a community of reasoners); item 2 is the best formal answer available, and it does *not* show that logical form is what matters: what matters there is a general reasoner plus intermediate concepts. The paper should say this, and list the formal differences that do exist (§1.7).

**A correction of the brief's own argument (S4) (not the author's claim).** "A program computes one direction cheaply" is true of a particular program, not of the class: a program may consist of a generic solver applied to a declarative law, at $O(1)$ extra length (scout P §1.3). The separation the brief looked for exists only relative to a fixed weak reasoner and a cost on the written theory (`shape` Thm 4.3); under program length it vanishes (`shape` Thm 1.11), which is the precise form of this correction.

### 1.5 Claim E: theories explain propositions

Developed in §3. Verdict: a theory entails answers; derivation from a short theory is explanation in the unification sense, with two known gaps (asymmetry; explanation by approximate correction).

### 1.6 Claim L: the ladder

Agreed with the author (brief, "Existing language"). Each step moves further from verificationism.
1. **Raw-data prediction** (pixels, observation sequences).
2. **Answering questions posed with existing abstractions:** SAI over a fixed language $\mathcal L$. The base language is whatever abstractions are already available, often highly theoretical: heliocentric longitudes presuppose Copernican geometry and the Earth's orbit (§4.1; theory-ladenness [known: hanson1958patterns; content: secondary]). No theory-neutral observation language is claimed, so the classical objections to one do not apply in the same way [assessment].
3. **Theories that extend the language** (theoretical terms; data stay in the base language). Formal content, all from `terms` (Thm 8.1 assembles it; [proved there], with the known results each part rests on named there):
   * with free deduction, theoretical vocabulary costs $O(1)$ bits in the one-tape, coin-free and Kolmogorov versions, also under penalties on checking or generating axioms (`terms` Thm 2.1; `fundamentals` Cor 4.5);
   * when derivations are charged, Craig's construction and every run-padded observational axiomatisation fit exactly the polynomial-time regularities at polynomial budgets (`terms` Lemma 3.1, Thm 3.3, Cor 3.5); but theoretical *vocabulary* can be eliminated at polynomial cost under every reading of "short derivation", already by finite observational theories, by storing the content in objects no observational term names (`terms` Prop 4.1, 4.3, Thm 4.6, Prop 4.7);
   * a finite theory about the named objects with any unary theoretical predicates fits exactly the regular regularities; one binary theoretical relation, or quantification over unnamed objects (theoretical entities), restores what unrestricted hypotheses fit (every decidable regularity under the readings (a2), (b2); $\mathsf{NP}\cap\mathsf{coNP}$ under (b); $\mathsf{EXPTIME}$ under (a1)); an observational theory that uses unnamed objects must assert an observational sentence false of every named object (`terms` Thm 5.1, 5.3, Thm 4.6, Prop 9.1);
   * with free deduction and finite theories, theoretical functions or sorts pay without bound (infinity; PA) (`terms` Thm 6.2, 6.4);
   * for usability by a weak reasoner, under a cost on the written theory, intermediate concepts pay exponentially for one law ($4m-6$ clauses against $2^{m-1}$), but not for every system of laws, where every usable theory stays exponential with any intermediate concepts (`shape` Prop 3.1, Cor 2.7(c), Thm 3.6 [proved there]; scout N2 Prop 2.5).

   So what pays, in the settings where anything theoretical pays, is theoretical *relations* and *entities*, not vocabulary as such; and in the idealised setting nothing theoretical is indispensable [the reading is `terms`' and ours; assessment].
4. **Theories that revise the base abstractions** (meaning change; questions shown ill-posed: simultaneity, mass). No formal handle in SAI [assessment].

**The strongest objection** [assessment]: the "existing abstractions" of step 2 are themselves products of earlier theory (heliocentric longitudes; §4.1), and a theory that revises them (step 4) is then scored in a language it rejects; Kuhn's case of "earth" before and after Copernicus is the classic example [known: kuhn1970structure; content: secondary]. The ladder accepts this: it is why step 4 is listed and why SAI is placed on step 2 only.

SAI sits on step 2, with partial results on step 3. The paper should present step 2 as a step in the right direction and say plainly that steps 3–4 are needed.

### 1.7 Claim P, made honest: what formally separates SAI from raw-data prediction

The brief's stance: present progress as a list of concrete formal differences, not as a general claim. Each item is a property of SAI (with free deduction, unless said otherwise) that a predictor of an observation stream lacks, or has only after restriction.

| # | difference | status and source |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Data type.** Data are labelled sentences of a chosen language; questions are sets of sentences (§2.1). | definition |
| 2 | **Logical coherence.** Every consequence of the data gets posterior probability 1 (`sfi` Prop 6.1(a)); consistent SOI never becomes certain of any unasked sentence in the semimeasure convention: its undefined mass at $\chi$ stays $\ge2^{-K(\chi)-c}$ uniformly in $D$ (`sfi` Prop 6.1(c)). $\Bel$ is closed under conjunction up to additive error and monotone under entailment (Prop 2.5(d)). | proved there; Prop 2.5(d) proved here |
| 3 | **Partiality.** "Independent" is a native third value; the semimeasure defect is its mass (`fundamentals` Prop 2.1); for a $k$-way question the unanswered mass is defined and the answer masses sum with it to 1 (Prop 2.1). | proved there; Prop 2.1 proved here |
| 4 | **An observed "independent" label separates theories from labellers** by an unbounded amount on one datum, coin-free and one-tape (`sfi` Props 6.3–6.5). | proved there |
| 5 | **Laws are sentences with belief values.** $\Bel_D(\forall x\varphi)$ is defined directly; it differs from "all future data conform" (the ω-gap: AI-paper `thm:univ:omega`; `fundamentals` Prop 8.5; `logic` Props 5.1–5.2, Thm 5.3). | proved there |
| 6 | **Piecemeal confirmation.** The posterior gives credence to individual laws ($\Bel_D(\mathrm{K3})$) although only whole theories meet the data: the Duhem–Quine situation, handled coherently. | definitions; [assessment] |
| 7 | **Random theories.** On the lottery stream, bounded one-tape loss is achieved by random theories and by no consistent r.e. theory; the limit posterior lives on non-r.e. random theories (`logic` Thm 2.2; scout N1 Thm E1). Inside true arithmetic with true data (Boolean combinations of $\Sigma_1$ sentences) the same happens, and the limit posterior is concentrated on *false* theories although every datum is true (`logic` Thm 2.9(b), Cor 2.10). | proved there |
| 8 | **Truth from unprovability.** With a true background $B\supseteq\Q$, positive posterior "independent" (or belief) mass on a $\Pi_1$ sentence certifies its truth (`logic` Prop 4.1(b); scout N1 Prop D1). | proved there |
| 9 | **Language-relative simplicity, bounded.** Faithful translations change weights by $2^{\pm(K(\tau)+c)}$ (`fundamentals` Thm 4.4); simplicity is relative to the base language, as in best-system accounts [known: lewis1983new, cohen2009better; content: secondary/abstract]; §4.4 measures how much this matters for Kepler. | proved there; computed here |

## 2. What a theory does: questions against raw data (T2)

### 2.1 Questions

**Definition 2.0** (NOTATION §3.2, following the erotetic tradition [known: belnap1976logic, hintikka1999inquiry; content: secondary]). A *question* is a finite set $\mathcal Q=\{\varphi_1,\dots,\varphi_k\}$ of pairwise $B$-incompatible sentences with $B\vdash\bigvee_i\varphi_i$ (its direct answers). A theory $T$ *answers* $\mathcal Q$ if $B\cup T\vdash\varphi_i$ for some $i$, and *answers it partially* if it proves $\bigvee_{i\in J}\varphi_i$ for some $J\subsetneq[k]$, $J\neq\emptyset$. A *conditional question* is a pair $(\alpha,\ell)$, "given $\alpha$, is $\ell$ the case?".

**Proposition 2.1 (the answer distribution of SAI) [proved].** Let $M_A(D)>0$ and $\mathcal Q$ a question. Put $\mathrm{Un}_D(\mathcal Q):=\Unif(E_D\setminus\bigcup_iO_{\varphi_i})/\Unif(E_D)$, the posterior probability that the random theory proves no direct answer. Then
(a) $\sum_{i\le k}\Bel_D(\varphi_i)+\mathrm{Un}_D(\mathcal Q)=1$;
(b) for $J\subseteq[k]$, $\Bel_D(\bigvee_{i\in J}\varphi_i)\ge\sum_{i\in J}\Bel_D(\varphi_i)$, and the difference is the posterior mass of theories that answer $\mathcal Q$ partially through $J$ but prove no single answer in $J$;
(c) for a yes/no question $\{\varphi,\neg\varphi\}$, (a) is the trichotomy $\Bel_D+\Dis_D+\Indep_D=1$.

*Proof.* On $E_D$ the theory $B\cup A_\omega$ is consistent. If it proved $\varphi_i$ and $\varphi_j$ with $i\ne j$, it would prove $\varphi_i\wedge\varphi_j$, and $B\vdash\neg(\varphi_i\wedge\varphi_j)$, a contradiction. So the events $E_D\cap O_{\varphi_i}$ are pairwise disjoint, and with $E_D\setminus\bigcup_iO_{\varphi_i}$ they partition $E_D$; divide by $\Unif(E_D)$. (b): $O_{\varphi_i}\subseteq O_{\bigvee_J\varphi_j}$ for $i\in J$, and the $O_{\varphi_i}\cap E_D$ are disjoint. (c) is (a) with $k=2$. $\square$

**Reading [assessment].** Raw-data prediction answers one question, "what is the next observation?", and answers it fully: a total predictive distribution has no unanswered mass. SAI answers every question expressible in the base language, with an explicit "unanswered" mass. In SOI (labellers on sentences) the analogue of $\mathrm{Un}$ exists but mixes logical independence with laziness (`sfi` Prop 6.1(c)), and the answer masses of a $k$-way question need not respect the incompatibility of the answers unless the labeller is consistent.

### 2.2 The conservation-law world: what a law is worth to each task

This re-proves scout N2's Prop 5.1 and makes its reading exact; `checks/c5_conservation.py` re-implements N2's `n4` independently (sequential sampling of the path instead of building the permutation; exact expectations over the cycle length added).

**Setting.** World $W_m$ ($m$ even): micro-states $x\in\{0,1\}^m$; the dynamics $\pi$ is a uniformly random permutation of each weight class (so $|x|$ is conserved: the *law*); $x_0$ uniform on the class of weight $w=m/2$, of size $C:=\binom m{m/2}$; $S:=2^m$. Raw data: the trajectory $x_0,x_1,\dots$. Questions: "what is $|x_t|$?", $t=0,1,\dots$. Predictors (Bayes for the stated hypothesis): RAW-LAW ($\pi$ a uniform random permutation of each weight class); RAW-NOLAW ($\pi$ a uniform random permutation of $\{0,1\}^m$); QA-LAW ($|x_t|=|x_0|$, $|x_0|$ uniform on $\{0..m\}$); QA-NOLAW ($|x_t|$ i.i.d. uniform on $\{0..m\}$); MIX (prior ½ on each hypothesis of a task).

**Proposition 2.2 [proved; computed: c5].**
(a) *Raw data.* Let $\ell$ be the length of the cycle of $x_0$. For $t<\ell$ the step-$t$ losses are $\log_2(C-t)$ (RAW-LAW) and $\log_2(S-t)$ (RAW-NOLAW); for $t\ge\ell$ both are 0. The law's gain at step $t<\ell$ is $g_t=\log_2\frac{S-t}{C-t}$, nondecreasing in $t$, and for $t\le C/2$
$$\log_2\tfrac SC\ \le\ g_t\ \le\ \log_2\tfrac SC+1,\qquad \log_2\tfrac SC=\tfrac12\log_2\tfrac{\pi m}2+o(1).$$
Its share of the step loss, $g_t/\log_2(S-t)$, is $O(\log m/m)$ for $t\le C/2$.
(b) *Questions.* QA-LAW pays $\log_2(m+1)$ for $|x_0|$ and 0 afterwards; QA-NOLAW pays $\log_2(m+1)$ per question. Over $T+1$ questions the law's share of the no-law loss is $T/(T+1)\to1$.
(c) *Learning.* In each task MIX pays at most 1 bit more than the better of its two hypotheses, on every trajectory.
(d) *Absolute value.* Per time step, the law is worth $\frac12\log_2(\pi m/2)+O(1)$ bits to raw prediction (while $t\le C/2$) and $\log_2(m+1)$ bits to question answering (after the first question): within a factor of about 2 of each other.

*Proof.* (a) Under a uniformly random permutation of a set $X$, given a path $x_0,\dots,x_t$ of distinct elements, $x_{t+1}$ is uniform on $(X\setminus\{x_0,\dots,x_t\})\cup\{x_0\}$, a set of $|X|-t$ elements (the path is an initial segment of the cycle of $x_0$, and the rest of the permutation is uniform given it); once the path returns to $x_0$ the cycle is known and later steps are determined. For RAW-LAW, $X$ is the weight class ($|X|=C$, and the true trajectory stays in it); for RAW-NOLAW, $|X|=S$. Since $S>C$, $(S-t)/(C-t)$ is increasing in $t$; for $t\le C/2$, $\frac{S-t}{C-t}\le\frac S{C/2}$. Stirling: $C=2^m\sqrt{2/(\pi m)}(1+o(1))$. The share bound: $g_t\le\log_2(S/C)+1=O(\log m)$ and $\log_2(S-t)\ge\log_2(S/2)=m-1$. (b) Immediate. (c) $-\log_2(\frac12 2^{-a}+\frac12 2^{-b})\le\min(a,b)+1$. (d) From (a) and (b). $\square$

**Computed [c5].** (A) Per-step values at the start of a trajectory agree with `n4` to its printed precision (largest difference 0.0005 bits): the gain is 1.871, 2.148, 2.348, 2.837, 3.331, 3.829, 5.326, 10.292 bits at $m=8,12,16,32,64,128,1024,10^6$, against $\frac12\log_2(\pi m/2)$ = 1.826, 2.118, 2.326, 2.826, 3.326, 3.826, 5.326, 10.292; shares 0.23, 0.18, 0.15, 0.089, 0.052, 0.030, 0.0052, $1.0\times10^{-5}$. (B) Exact expectations at $m=12$ ($C=924$, $S=4096$), over the cycle length (uniform on $\{1..C\}$): over $T=300$ steps, raw loss 2415.6 bits (law) against 3004.9 (no law), share 0.196; mixture 2416.6; QA loss over 301 questions 3.70 (law) against 1113.8 (no law), share 0.997. Over a whole cycle the raw share is 0.232 and the mean per-step gain 2.75 bits (late steps of a cycle are nearly determined for the law-knower). (C) 2000 simulated runs by sequential sampling: mean raw losses 2405.9 ± 18.3 and 2992.6 ± 22.9, within one standard error of (B); the cycle length passes a uniformity check (deciles 181–210 of expected 200; KS distance 0.020); MIX − LAW ≤ 1.0000 bits in every run. (D) For N2's single run (cycle length 275), the exact formula gives 2643.5 and 3286.4 bits, share 0.1956, as `n4` printed.

**The exact reading [assessment].**
* The author's "this doesn't improve one's ability to predict pixels in an arbitrary video basically at all" (`phil-sci-science-predicts-only-very-particularly.md`) is true of the *share* of raw loss (it tends to 0) and false of the *absolute* gain, which is comparable to the law's value per question (d). What makes the share vanish is micro-detail that no theory addresses and that dominates the raw loss.
* Both tasks learn the law at the same price (c): the theory is "in" the raw predictor (objection o1 of §1.3).
* So the two tasks differ in their *target* (which losses are counted), not in what must be learned. This is the formal content of "a theory answers questions rather than predicting raw data" in this toy: it is a claim about what we score, and it is correct to score questions when what we want is answers.

### 2.3 Certification and extraction

**Proposition 2.3.**
(a) **[proved]** A deterministic procedure with black-box access to a map $\pi:\{0,1\}^m\to\{0,1\}^m$ that decides "$\forall x\ |\pi(x)|=|x|$" must query all $2^m$ points on some input. If $\pi$ is promised to be a permutation, it must query at least $2^m-\binom m{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}=2^m(1-\Theta(m^{-1/2}))$ points on some input, and this many can be necessary.
(b) **[proved; standard]** For dynamics on an infinite state space given by programs, "every state on the trajectory has the conserved value" is $\Pi_1$-complete.
(c) **[proved there]** The SAI posterior reports $\Bel_D(\text{law})$, the mass of theories that prove the law; a raw predictor reports the probability that all future data conform. These differ (the ω-gap: AI-paper `thm:univ:omega`; `fundamentals` Prop 8.5; `logic` Props 5.1–5.2, Thm 5.3).

*Proof.* (a) Maps: on the identity, if a point $x$ is not queried, the map that agrees with the identity except $\pi(x):=x\oplus e_1$ answers every query identically and violates the law. Permutations: on the identity, let $U$ be the set of unqueried points. If $U$ contains $x,y$ with $|x|\ne|y|$, the transposition $(x\ y)$ answers every query identically and violates the law; so a correct procedure leaves $U$ inside one weight class, $|U|\le\binom m{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}$. Conversely, querying every point outside the largest weight class suffices: a permutation that preserves weight outside that class maps the class onto itself. (b) Reduction from non-halting: the state at step $t$ records the configuration of a machine at step $t$ and has the conserved value unless the machine has halted by step $t$. (c) As cited. $\square$

**Refuted claim (kept).** Scout N2's Prop 5.2(a) says that deciding the law from a black-box *permutation* "must, in the worst case, query all $2^m$ states" [refuted as stated]. *Counterexample:* $m=2$; a procedure that knows $\pi$ is a permutation queries $00$ and $11$ only; if both are fixed points, $\pi$ permutes $\{01,10\}$ and preserves weight; otherwise the law fails. Two queries, not four. The corrected bound is (a); the reading is unchanged, since $2^m-\binom m{m/2}=2^m(1-O(m^{-1/2}))$.

**Reading [assessment].** Extracting a theory's answer from a good raw predictor is a search or proof problem, and the quantity a raw predictor reports is a different quantity from belief in a law (c). This agrees with the brief's conclusion that any difference between theories and predictors lies in what is cheap to obtain from each, relative to a reasoner.

### 2.4 Question answering never needs more description than raw modelling plus an extractor

Scout N2 §5.3 gave this as a proof sketch for deterministic generators; here it is a theorem, random generators included.

**Proposition 2.4 [proved, given `fundamentals` Thm 4.1 and the standard sampling construction].** Let $X$ be a finite alphabet, $\mu$ a computable probability measure on $X^{\mathbb N}$ (the raw world), $(\varphi_q)_{q\in\mathbb N}$ a computable family of sentences (the questions), and $g$ a partial computable function (the *extractor*) with $g(q,x)\in\{0,1\}$ computed from a finite prefix of $x$. Assume (H): for $\mu$-almost every $x$ the labelling $f_x:\varphi_q\mapsto g(q,x)$ is consistent with $B$ (e.g. the labels are the truth values of the $\varphi_q$ in a model of $B$ determined by $x$). Put $\mu_g(D):=\mu\{x:f_x\supseteq D\}$. Then for every finite data set $D$ of question–answer pairs
$$M_A(D)\ \ge\ 2^{-K(\mu)-K(g)-c}\,\mu_g(D),\qquad\text{so}\qquad L_A(D)\le-\log\mu_g(D)+K(\mu)+K(g)+c .$$
In particular, for a deterministic world $x$ (i.e. $\mu=\delta_x$, $x$ computable), $L_A(D)\le K(x)+K(g)+c$ on every $D$ consistent with $f_x$.

*Proof.* There is a partial computable map $\Phi$ from tapes to $X^{\mathbb N}$, computable from an index of $\mu$, with $\Phi(\omega)$ defined for $\Unif$-almost every $\omega$ and $\Phi_*\Unif=\mu$ (the standard construction: read tape bits to refine a dyadic interval and output the next symbol $s$ as soon as the interval lies inside the $\mu$-interval of $s$ given the symbols already output; it fails only on the null set of tapes that hit interval boundaries). Let $G$ be the theory machine that, on tape $\omega$, computes $\Phi(\omega)$ as far as needed and emits $\varphi_q^{g(q,\Phi(\omega))}$ for every $q$ on which $g$ halts. $G$ is c.e. and $K(G)\le K(\mu)+K(g)+c$. On every tape with $\Phi(\omega)$ defined and (H) holding, $A^G_\omega=\Gamma_{f_{\Phi(\omega)}}$ is consistent with $B$, and it proves $\psi_D$ whenever $f_{\Phi(\omega)}\supseteq D$. Hence $\nu_G(D)\ge\mu_g(D)$, and `fundamentals` Thm 4.1 gives $M_A(D)\ge2^{-K(\nu_G)}\nu_G(D)\ge2^{-K(G)}\nu_G(D)$. $\square$

**Reading [assessment].** SAI on questions never needs more description than a raw-data model plus the extractor that turns raw data into answers. The extractor is the bridge from raw data to the question language, and its cost $K(g)$ is not small in real cases (it contains, for instance, whatever recognises a cat or a planet in an image): this is the bridge gap of §5, L1(a), in formal dress. The converse fails badly: answers do not determine the raw data, and in the conservation world the raw loss exceeds the question loss by the whole micro-entropy (Prop 2.2).

### 2.5 Answering and predicting (scout N1's N9, assembled and completed)

**Proposition 2.5.**
(a) **[proved there: `fundamentals` Thm 5.1]** *Answering implies predicting.* If a consistent r.e. $T$ proves every datum of a consistent reveal sequence, the cumulative loss is at most $-\log\Unif_A(\Ext T)\le K_\equiv(T)+c$, in any order.
(b) **[proved there: `logic` Thm 2.2, scout N1 Thm E1]** *Predicting does not imply that any theory answers.* On the lottery stream $M_A$ has bounded loss while no consistent r.e. theory proves $D_\infty$, and $W_A(D_n)\to0$.
(c) **[proved there: `fundamentals` Prop 8.3]** *Predicting a text does not converge to answers on unasked questions.* On two-valued data from an incomplete $T^*$, the limit posterior is the prior on consistent extensions; on every $T^*$-independent question all three trichotomy values keep positive shares. Three-valued data identify $[T^*]$.
(d) **[proved]** *Closure.* For all $D$ with $M_A(D)>0$ and all $\varphi,\psi$: $\Bel_D(\varphi\wedge\psi)\ge\Bel_D(\varphi)+\Bel_D(\psi)-1$; more generally $\Bel_D(\bigwedge_{i\le k}\varphi_i)\ge1-\sum_i(1-\Bel_D(\varphi_i))$; and if $B\cup\{\varphi\}\vdash\psi$ then $\Bel_D(\psi)\ge\Bel_D(\varphi)$. So the "δ-answers" (yes if $\Bel_D\ge1-\delta$) are closed under entailment and, with error $k\delta$, under $k$-fold conjunction.

*Proof of (d).* $O_\varphi\cap O_\psi\subseteq O_{\varphi\wedge\psi}$ (a theory proving both proves the conjunction); within $E_D$, $\Unif(O_\varphi\cap O_\psi\cap E_D)\ge\Unif(O_\varphi\cap E_D)+\Unif(O_\psi\cap E_D)-\Unif(E_D)$; divide by $\Unif(E_D)$; induct on $k$. If $B\cup\{\varphi\}\vdash\psi$ then $O_\varphi\subseteq O_\psi$. $\square$

**Reading [assessment].** (a)–(c) give a formal vocabulary for "answering questions ≫ predicting pixels": answering is the stronger achievement ((a) one way, (b) not the other way), and success at predicting a presentation of data does not yield answers to questions not asked (c). (d) is the coherence that a predictor of separate labels lacks: in consistent SOI the probability of labelling an entailed or unasked sentence never reaches 1 in the semimeasure convention (`sfi` Prop 6.1(c)).

### 2.6 Verdict on what a theory does

Claim D holds in comparative form [assessment]. Its formal content inside SAI is: the change of data type, logical coherence (Prop 2.5(d), `sfi` Prop 6.1), partiality (Prop 2.1, `sfi` §6.2), and the choice of target (Prop 2.2). It is not a new inference principle (`sfi` Thm 2.1, 3.1). The extractor (Prop 2.4) is where the real difficulty of moving from raw data to questions sits, and SAI takes it as given (ladder step 2).

## 3. Explanation (T3)

### 3.1 Derivation and unification

In the axiom view, $T$ explains $\varphi$ when $B\cup T\vdash\varphi$: the deductive-nomological pattern [known: hempel1948studies; content: secondary (the standard account; IEP, via scout P)]. The quality of an explanation is measured by how short $T$ is relative to what it derives: explanation as unification [known: friedman1974explanation, kitcher1981explanatory, kitcher1989explanatory; content: abstract (Kitcher 1981), secondary (the others)]. The precise measure is the explanatory power of `laws` Def 5.2, $\mathrm{EP}_D(\mathcal A;T)=(\mathrm{Exc}_D(T\setminus\mathcal A)-\mathrm{Exc}_D(T))/\|\mathcal A\|$, bits of data explained per bit of axiom; on an edge of the steeper-prior hull it is the edge slope (`laws` Def 5.2; scout N2 Def 4.3, Prop 4.2 [proved there]). It is a per-bit log-likelihood ratio, and Myrvold's Bayesian unification term [known: myrvold2003bayesian; content: abstract and secondary summaries] is zero when the parameters are stated and equals the bits of the shared unknown parameters otherwise (`laws` Prop 5.8 [proved there]). This record does not re-define EP.

### 3.2 Asymmetry: the flagpole

Derivability is symmetric where explanation is not: a pole's height and the sun's elevation entail the shadow's length, and the shadow's length and the elevation entail the height [known: bromberger1966why, glymour1978two; content: the attribution of the flagpole example to Bromberger is Glymour's (opening of glymour1978two, seen in search results); Bromberger's text not seen]. Scout N2's C13 is made precise here.

**Setting.** Two sorts, poles and reals; $B$ = the theory of real closed fields (on the real sort). Pole constants $p_1,\dots,p_N$; function symbols $h$ (height) and $s(p,u)$ (shadow length when the tangent of the sun's elevation is $u>0$). Data: sentences $s(p_i,u)=v$ and $h(p_i)=v$ with rational numerals $u,v$ (interval versions behave the same). Two equivalent formulations of the law:
$$\Lambda_h:=\forall p\,\forall u\,(u>0\to s(p,u)\cdot u=h(p)),\qquad \Lambda_s:=\forall p\,\forall u\,(u>0\to s(p,u)\cdot u=s(p,1))\ \wedge\ \forall p\,h(p)=s(p,1).$$
Theories, with $a_i$ the heights: $T_H:=\Lambda_h+\{h(p_i)=a_i\}_i$, $T_S:=\Lambda_h+\{s(p_i,1)=a_i\}_i$ (the shadow at 45° "explains" the height), $T_H':=\Lambda_s+\{h(p_i)=a_i\}_i$, $T_S':=\Lambda_s+\{s(p_i,1)=a_i\}_i$. Causal extensions with intervention vocabulary $s_H(p,u,v)$ (the shadow after the height of $p$ is set to $v$) and $h_S(p,v)$ (the height after the shadow $s(p,1)$ is set to $v$ by an intervention on the shadow, e.g. by an obstacle): $C_H:=T_H+\{\forall p\forall u\forall v\,(u>0\to s_H(p,u,v)\cdot u=v),\ \forall p\forall v\,h_S(p,v)=h(p)\}$ (height exogenous) and $C_S:=T_S+\{\forall p\forall u\forall v\,s_H(p,u,v)=s(p,u),\ \forall p\forall v\,h_S(p,v)=v\}$ (shadow exogenous).

**Proposition 3.1 (the flagpole tie and what breaks it) [proved; computed: c6].**
(a) $B\vdash\Lambda_h\leftrightarrow\Lambda_s$, and $T_H,T_S,T_H',T_S'$ are deductively equivalent over $B$. Hence they lie in one class $[T]$: every data set is compatible with all four or with none; $\Unif_A$ cannot distinguish them; in the coin-free version the posterior odds of any two programs for them equal their prior odds on every $D$; and $|K(T_S)-K(T_H)|\le c$, since a fixed computable translation maps either axiom list to the other.
(b) Count the cost of a derivation as the number of instances of the universal law axioms it uses ($B$-steps free). For a shadow datum $s(p_i,u)=a_i/u$ with $u\neq1$: $T_H$ and $T_S'$ need exactly one instance, $T_S$ and $T_H'$ exactly two. For a height datum $h(p_i)=a_i$: $T_H,T_H'$ need none, $T_S,T_S'$ one. So, under derivation charges, the theory whose initial conditions sit at the *hub* of the law's formulation wins, and which quantity is the hub is a property of the formulation, not of the world: the two hub theories $T_H$ and $T_S'$ have equal costs on all shadow data with $u\ne1$, and their texts differ by $|\Lambda_s|-|\Lambda_h|$, a constant independent of $N$ and of the data.
(c) $C_H$ and $C_S$ have the same consequences in the language without $s_H,h_S$, and one interventional datum separates them: $C_H\vdash s_H(p_1,1,2a_1)=2a_1$ ("doubling the pole doubles its 45° shadow") while $C_S\vdash s_H(p_1,1,2a_1)=a_1$, which refutes it when $a_1\ne0$; likewise $C_H\vdash h_S(p_1,v)=a_1$ and $C_S\vdash h_S(p_1,v)=v$.

*Proof.* (a) At $u=1$, $\Lambda_h$ gives $s(p,1)=h(p)$; with this, $\Lambda_h$ and the first conjunct of $\Lambda_s$ are interderivable by field arithmetic; so $B\vdash\Lambda_h\leftrightarrow\Lambda_s$, and given either law the initial conditions $h(p_i)=a_i$ and $s(p_i,1)=a_i$ are interderivable. Compatibility in SAI depends only on $\Cn$ (`fundamentals` Prop 2.1(c)); the class probabilities are of classes; a coin-free posterior ratio of two compatible programs is the ratio of their weights. The translation replacing each $h(c)=v$ by $s(c,1)=v$ and conversely is computable and fixed. (b) *Upper bounds:* the derivations are explicit (instantiate the law at $(p_i,u)$, substitute the initial condition, do arithmetic; for $T_S$, first instantiate at $(p_i,1)$ to obtain $h(p_i)=a_i$). *Lower bounds:* every model of $B$ plus the initial conditions plus a single law instance (resp. none) in which the datum fails exists, because with fewer instances the term $s(p_i,u)$ (resp. $h(p_i)$) is linked to no initial condition and can be given any value; `c6` checks this exhaustively over all subsets of the relevant instance pool by exact linear algebra. The text difference is the difference of the two law texts. (c) Without the intervention symbols, $C_H$ and $C_S$ are $T_H$ and $T_S$ plus axioms about fresh symbols; every model of $T_H$ (resp. $T_S$) expands (put $s_H(p,u,v):=v/u$, $h_S(p,v):=h(p)$, resp. $s_H:=s$, $h_S(p,v):=v$), so they are conservative, and by (a) their old-vocabulary consequences coincide. The two interventional consequences are instances of the added axioms. $\square$

**Computed [c6].** (1) On 2000 random data (500 random data sets with 1–4 poles, heights and elevations), $T_H,T_S,T_H',T_S'$ prove, refute or leave open exactly the same data (2000/2000; "open" only for elevations whose law instance is not in the finite pool). (2) Least instance counts by exhaustive search: shadow at $u=2$: 1, 2, 2, 1 (for $T_H,T_S,T_H',T_S'$); reference shadow: 1, 0, 1, 0; height: 0, 1, 0, 1. (3) In a literal syntax $T_S$ is longer than $T_H$ by one token per pole (the term $s(p,1)$ is longer than $h(p)$), so a *literal* code length also breaks the tie, again in favour of the theory whose primitive happens to be shorter; the hub theories $T_H$, $T_S'$ differ by 20 tokens independently of $N$. (4) The interventional data: $C_H$ proves, $C_S$ refutes, both; both prove the observational data.

**Reading [assessment].** SAI's explanation relation ($T\vdash d$) is direction-free, and its posterior cannot move between deductively equivalent theories. Derivation-length charges (the generative variant's derivation likelihood, or the AI-paper's charged deduction) introduce a direction only relative to the formulation and the primitives; the literature's "height explains shadow, not conversely" is not recovered from simplicity alone. Direction enters with *interventional questions*: once the base language can pose them, which theory is right is decided by data, by one datum. This supports the interventionist account [known: woodward2003making; content: secondary] and places explanatory direction on the ladder: it is a matter of which questions the language can pose (step 2 with a causal vocabulary), not of the shape of theories. Unificationists answer the asymmetry with argument patterns [known: kitcher1989explanatory; content: secondary]; Prop 3.1(b) is a formal version of why such answers depend on the choice of patterns.

### 3.3 Explanation by approximate correction

Newton's theory does not entail Kepler's laws; it contradicts them [known: duhem1954aim; content: secondary]; what it derives is a corrected statement and the claim that Kepler's laws hold within stated tolerances on stated domains, as in Schaffner's model of reduction to a corrected theory [known: schaffner1967approaches; content: abstract and commentary]. §4.3 gives the sizes (Kepler off by 1–2′ per decade for Mars, 1.1° per month for the Moon; the third-law constant differing between planets at the $10^{-3}$ level, explained by Newton). The provable toy is `laws` Prop 7.1 [proved there] (scout N2 C19 was a proof sketch): in an exact rational model the successor theory proves the honest old law on every window $W$, with least tolerance $\varepsilon_{\min}(W)\le((1+\eta\|P\|)^W-1)|a|$, refutes the exact old law, and the strongest honest form of the old law it proves is exactly the strongest honest consequence the old and new theories share. **The point for claim E [assessment]:** "$T$ explains $\varphi$" must include "$T$ derives an honest approximation of $\varphi$ with an error bound on a domain"; plain derivation does not cover the central historical case, and the honest (tolerance-weakened) form of an approximate law is exactly what its successor can explain.

### 3.4 Unification without better prediction

Copernicus explained why superior planets retrogress exactly at opposition: retrograde motion is the Earth overtaking them; Ptolemy had to stipulate the coincidence planet by planet [the astronomy: secondary (lecture notes and arXiv 1301.2119, located by WebSearch: in Ptolemy's model the epicycle radius of a superior planet stays parallel to the Earth–Sun line, which is what makes retrogression fall at opposition; in Copernicus's it is the Earth overtaking the planet); kuhn1957copernican is the standard history, but its own passage was not seen]. In SAI terms, Ptolemy's system carries one coincidence axiom per planet that Copernicus's system derives; the heliocentric module then has $\mathrm{EP}=\sum_pa_p/H$ relative to the coincidence axioms it replaces, with no change in fit (scout N2 §4.4 [proved there; the lengths not measured]; `laws` Def 5.2). In Myrvold's terms the gain is a unification term: when several data sets share an unknown parameter, the term equals that parameter's bits (`laws` Prop 5.8(c) [proved there]), and the shared parameter here is the Sun's motion, which Ptolemy's system states once per planet [assessment; the identification with Prop 5.8(c)'s hypotheses is ours and not checked in detail]. This is the cleanest case of unification tracking an explanatory gain with no gain in accuracy, and the paper's best illustration of unification [assessment].

### 3.5 Verdict on explanation

Write: *a theory entails answers; derivation from a short theory is explanation in the unification sense, with two known gaps: asymmetry (Prop 3.1: no direction without interventional questions, or only one relative to the formulation) and explanation by approximate correction (§3.3).* Do not claim that derivation is explanation outright [assessment].

## 4. The worked example: Kepler's laws, the historical Mars pool, and what Newton adds (T4)

### 4.1 The language, the axioms, the data

Scout P §2.1–2.3, checked; the geocentric language is added.

* **Sorts.** Bodies $\mathfrak B$ and reals $\mathbb R$ (times in days from an epoch, angles in radians, lengths in AU).
* **Heliocentric data language $\mathcal L_{\rm obs}$.** Body constants Sun, Me, Ve, Ea, Ma, Ju, Sa, Mo; a predicate Planet; functions $\lambda(b,t),\beta(b,t),r(b,t)$ (heliocentric ecliptic longitude, latitude, distance; for the Moon geocentric). $\mathcal L_{\rm dir}\subseteq\mathcal L_{\rm obs}$ omits $r$.
* **Geocentric data language $\mathcal L_{\rm geo}$.** The same constants and functions $\lambda_g(b,t),\beta_g(b,t)$: the geocentric ecliptic direction $\mathrm{dir}_g(b,t)\in S^2$ of body $b$. This is the language of Tycho's observations; heliocentric longitudes are already a theoretical reconstruction (history from memory, not checked against a source).
* **Background $B$.** Real-closed-field axioms with $\sin,\cos,\pi$ and enough true analysis to prove the numerical bounds used (rational-function bounds of the kind MetiTarski proves [known: akbarpour2010metitarski; content: Paulson's pages via scout P]). The full theory of $(\mathbb R,+,\cdot,<,\sin)$ is undecidable ($\sin(\pi x)=0$ defines $\mathbb Z$) [proof sketch], so $B$ is a sound decidable fragment; statements below say which true sentences $B$ must contain.
* **Theoretical vocabulary.** Heliocentric positions $x(b,t)\in\mathbb R^3$ (for the geocentric language; in $\mathcal L_{\rm obs}$ they are definable from $\lambda,\beta,r$) and the elements $a,e,i,\Omega,\varpi,M_0,P:\mathfrak B\to\mathbb R$.
* **K12** (Kepler's first two laws, via Kepler's equation). For Planet($b$) and all $t$, with $M:=M_0(b)+2\pi t/P(b)$, there is $E$ with $E-e(b)\sin E=M$, and $x(b,t)=\mathrm{Rot}(b)\big(a(b)(\cos E-e(b)),\ a(b)\sqrt{1-e(b)^2}\sin E,\ 0\big)$, $0\le e(b)<1$, $a(b),P(b)>0$; $x(\mathrm{Sun},t)=0$. For $\mathcal L_{\rm geo}$, K12 includes the **bridge**: $\mathrm{dir}_g(b,t)=(x(b,t)-x(\mathrm{Ea},t))/|x(b,t)-x(\mathrm{Ea},t)|$ if $x(b,t)\neq x(\mathrm{Ea},t)$, and the north ecliptic pole $N$ otherwise; and the frame convention $i(\mathrm{Ea})=0$ (the ecliptic is the Earth's orbital plane).
* **K3.** For all planets $b,b'$: $P(b)^2a(b')^3=P(b')^2a(b)^3$.
* **Elements** $E_b$ (initial conditions): numerals (or intervals) for the elements of body $b$.
* **Data** are true interval sentences $d(b,t,v,\delta)$: "the direction of $b$ at $t$ lies within angle $\delta$ of $v$" (heliocentric or geocentric), and, where available, "$r(b,t)\in[r_1,r_2]$". **Questions** are covers by such sentences ("where is Mars at $t$, to within 2′?"), and structural questions expressible without theoretical terms (the return period; the mean distance).

### 4.2 What the laws say, in which language

**Proposition 4.1 (scout P Prop 2.1, re-checked) [proved].** (a) The Ramsey sentence $R(T)$ of a theory $T$ (its theoretical function symbols existentially quantified, second order) has the same $\mathcal L_{\rm obs}$-consequences as $T$. (b) For every $B$ true in $\mathbb R$: $R(\mathrm{K12}+\mathrm{K3})\cup B$ proves no datum $d(b,t,v,\delta)$ with $\delta<\pi$.

*Proof.* (a) A model of $R(T)$ is an $\mathcal L_{\rm obs}$-structure that expands to a model of $T$; so the two have the same models in $\mathcal L_{\rm obs}$. (b) Fix $b,t,v$ and $\delta<\pi$. Keep $a(b)$ and $P(b)$ (so K3 is untouched) and choose the orbital plane and the phase so that the Keplerian direction of $b$ at $t$ is $-v$ (the plane can be any plane through the Sun and the phase can place the body anywhere on its orbit); define $\lambda,\beta,r$ by K12. This is a model of $B\cup\mathrm{K12}\cup\mathrm{K3}$ on the standard reals in which the angle between the direction of $b$ at $t$ and $v$ is $\pi>\delta$. $\square$

So the laws alone answer only structural questions; laws plus initial conditions answer position questions. In the question format, initial conditions belong in the question antecedent, and laws answer conditional questions with no elements at all: this is the formal locus of the laws/initial-conditions distinction (scout N2 §4.2; `laws` Prop 5.9 [proved there]: in the question format an elements module has explanatory power 0, no λ-MAP contains elements, and the laws alone answer every conditional question).

**Proposition 4.2 (scout P Prop 2.2, re-checked) [proved].** K12 and K12 + K3 have the same $\mathcal L_{\rm dir}$-consequences over any $B$ containing the real-closed-field axioms.

*Proof.* A Kepler direction depends on $(e,i,\Omega,\varpi,M_0,P)$ and not on $a$: the formula for $x(b,t)$ is $a(b)$ times an expression free of $a$. Given a model of $B\cup\mathrm{K12}$, put $a'(b):=(k^2P(b)^2/4\pi^2)^{1/3}$ for a fixed $k>0$ (cube roots exist in a real closed field) and leave everything else unchanged; positions scale by $a'(b)/a(b)>0$, directions are unchanged, and K3 holds because $a'(b)^3/P(b)^2$ is constant. So every $\mathcal L_{\rm dir}$-sentence true in all models of K12 + K3 is true in all models of K12; the converse is trivial. $\square$

So in a *heliocentric* direction language K3 has no content. Scout P remarked that in a *geocentric* direction language it does; here is the proof.

**Proposition 4.3 (K3 has content in the geocentric language) [proved].** Let $\sigma$ be the $\mathcal L_{\rm geo}$-sentence
$$\sigma:=\exists t\ \ \mathrm{dir}_g(\mathrm{Ma},t)\neq-\mathrm{dir}_g(\mathrm{Sun},t)\qquad(\text{"Mars is not permanently in opposition"}).$$
(a) $\sigma$ holds in every model of K12 + K3 (with the bridge and the frame convention) whose real sort is $\mathbb R$; hence $B\cup\mathrm{K12}\cup\mathrm{K3}\vdash\sigma$ for every $B$ that contains the single true sentence $\theta_{\rm K3}$ of $\mathbb R$ defined in the proof.
(b) For every $B$ true in $\mathbb R$, $B\cup\mathrm{K12}\nvdash\sigma$.

*Proof.* (b) The model $\mathcal M_0$: Earth with $a=1$, $e=0$, $i=0$, period $P_0$ and some phase; Mars with $a=\rho>1$, $e=0$, $i=0$, the same period $P_0$ and the same phase. Then $x(\mathrm{Ma},t)=\rho\,x(\mathrm{Ea},t)$ for all $t$, so $x(\mathrm{Ma},t)-x(\mathrm{Ea},t)=(\rho-1)x(\mathrm{Ea},t)\neq0$ and $\mathrm{dir}_g(\mathrm{Ma},t)=x(\mathrm{Ea},t)/|x(\mathrm{Ea},t)|=-\mathrm{dir}_g(\mathrm{Sun},t)$ for every $t$: $\mathcal M_0\models\neg\sigma$. $\mathcal M_0$ satisfies K12 (each body moves on a Kepler orbit; K12 imposes no relation between bodies) and violates K3 (equal periods, unequal axes). It is a model of every $B$ true in $\mathbb R$.
(a) Let $\mathcal M$ be a model of K12 + K3 on the standard reals with $\mathcal M\models\neg\sigma$. Earth moves in the ecliptic, so $u_E(t):=x(\mathrm{Ea},t)/|x(\mathrm{Ea},t)|$ lies in the ecliptic and differs from $N$. By $\neg\sigma$, $\mathrm{dir}_g(\mathrm{Ma},t)=u_E(t)\neq N$ for all $t$, so the bridge gives $x(\mathrm{Ma},t)\neq x(\mathrm{Ea},t)$ and $x(\mathrm{Ma},t)=x(\mathrm{Ea},t)+c(t)u_E(t)=(r_E(t)+c(t))\,u_E(t)$ with $c(t)>0$: Mars is always on the ray from the Sun through the Earth, beyond the Earth. So Mars moves in the ecliptic, its heliocentric direction equals the Earth's at every $t$, and its distance is $r_M(t)=r_E(t)+c(t)>r_E(t)$. Take continuous polar angles $\theta_M=\theta_E$ in the ecliptic. Kepler's second law, a consequence of K12 (from $r=a(1-e\cos E)$, $\tan\frac\nu2=\sqrt{\tfrac{1+e}{1-e}}\tan\frac E2$ and $\dot M=2\pi/P$ one gets $r^2\dot\theta=2\pi a^2\sqrt{1-e^2}/P$; standard), gives $r_b(t)^2\dot\theta_b(t)=h_b$, a nonzero constant for each body. Hence $r_M^2/r_E^2=h_M/h_E$ is constant: $r_M(t)=\kappa r_E(t)$ with a constant $\kappa>1$, so $x(\mathrm{Ma},t)=\kappa\,x(\mathrm{Ea},t)$ for all $t$. A Kepler orbit's period $P(b)$ is the least period of $t\mapsto x(b,t)$, and its semi-major axis is half the sum of its largest and smallest distances ($r=a(1-e\cos E)$); so $P(\mathrm{Ma})=P(\mathrm{Ea})$ and $a(\mathrm{Ma})=\kappa\,a(\mathrm{Ea})\neq a(\mathrm{Ea})$, contradicting K3. Let $\theta_{\rm K3}$ be the sentence of the real-field language with $\sin,\cos$:
$$\forall\vec\varepsilon_{\rm Ea}\,\forall\vec\varepsilon_{\rm Ma}\Big(\Phi(\vec\varepsilon_{\rm Ea},\vec\varepsilon_{\rm Ma})\ \to\ \exists t\ \forall E_{\rm Ea}\,\forall E_{\rm Ma}\big(\mathrm{Kep}_{\rm Ea}(t,E_{\rm Ea})\wedge\mathrm{Kep}_{\rm Ma}(t,E_{\rm Ma})\ \to\ \Delta(t,E_{\rm Ea},E_{\rm Ma})\big)\Big),$$
where $\vec\varepsilon_b$ are the seven elements of body $b$, $\Phi$ says $0\le e<1$, $a,P>0$ for both bodies, $i_{\rm Ea}=0$ and K3 for the pair, $\mathrm{Kep}_b(t,E)$ is Kepler's equation $E-e_b\sin E=M_{0,b}+2\pi t/P_b$, and $\Delta$ says that the positions $\xi_b:=\mathrm{Rot}_b(a_b(\cos E_b-e_b),a_b\sqrt{1-e_b^2}\sin E_b,0)$ either coincide or satisfy $(\xi_{\rm Ma}-\xi_{\rm Ea})/|\xi_{\rm Ma}-\xi_{\rm Ea}|\neq\xi_{\rm Ea}/|\xi_{\rm Ea}|$. $\theta_{\rm K3}$ is true in $\mathbb R$: given standard element values satisfying $\Phi$, Kepler's equation has exactly one solution for each $t$ (its left side is strictly increasing in $E$ when $0\le e<1$), so the antecedent of the inner implication fixes the true positions, and the argument above gives a $t$ at which they coincide or the directions differ. Now let $\mathcal M$ be any model of $B\cup\mathrm{K12}\cup\mathrm{K3}$ with $\theta_{\rm K3}\in B$ (its real sort may be non-standard). Instantiate $\theta_{\rm K3}$ at the elements of Earth and Mars in $\mathcal M$ ($\Phi$ holds by K12, K3 and the frame convention) and obtain $t$. K12 supplies, for this $t$, values $E_{\rm Ea},E_{\rm Ma}$ solving Kepler's equations with $x(b,t)=\xi_b$; the universal quantifier over $E$ applies to them, so $\Delta$ holds of the actual positions. If they coincide, the bridge gives $\mathrm{dir}_g(\mathrm{Ma},t)=N$, which is not $-\mathrm{dir}_g(\mathrm{Sun},t)=x(\mathrm{Ea},t)/|x(\mathrm{Ea},t)|$ because the latter has third coordinate $0$ ($i_{\rm Ea}=0$); otherwise the bridge gives $\mathrm{dir}_g(\mathrm{Ma},t)\ne x(\mathrm{Ea},t)/|x(\mathrm{Ea},t)|=-\mathrm{dir}_g(\mathrm{Sun},t)$. Either way $\mathcal M\models\sigma$. By completeness, $B\cup\mathrm{K12}\cup\mathrm{K3}\vdash\sigma$. (The universal quantifier over $E$ is what makes the instantiation valid in a model where Kepler's equation might have several solutions; in the first run the sentence was stated with "$\exists E$", which leaves that case open.) $\square$

*Computed [c7 (2)].* In the circular coplanar case with $\rho=1.5237$, $\mathcal M_0$ keeps Mars at opposition to within $1.2\times10^{-6}$° (rounding), while the K3-model with the same Earth, the same $\rho$ and the same initial phase leaves opposition by 1.3° within a day, 13° within 10 days and 87° within 100 days. (This illustrates one K3-model; whether finitely many interval data true in $\mathcal M_0$ refute every K3-model was not examined.)

*Remarks.* (i) The bridge needs a convention at coincidence; without it, a K3-model in which Mars coincides with the Earth would leave $\mathrm{dir}_g(\mathrm{Ma},t)$ unconstrained and could imitate $\mathcal M_0$. (ii) For an *inner* co-moving planet ($\rho<1$) the analogue fails: a K3-model with Mars on the Earth's orbit diametrically opposite ("behind the Sun") reproduces "always in the direction of the Sun", so $\sigma$ was chosen with an outer planet. (iii) $\sigma$ is an odd sentence (its negation is a fantastic world); its role is only to show that the geocentric data language can register K3. The natural form of the content is Prop 4.4.

**Proposition 4.4 (in the geocentric language K3 replaces one element) [proved; computed: c4 B].** Let $E^-$ be numerals for all elements of Earth and Mars except $a(\mathrm{Ma})$, with $a(\mathrm{Ea})=1$. Write $F_M(t)$ for Mars's heliocentric position computed from $E^-$ with $a=1$, so that $x(\mathrm{Ma},t)=\rho F_M(t)$ with $\rho:=a(\mathrm{Ma})$.
(a) $\mathrm{K12}\cup\mathrm{K3}\cup E^-$ fixes $\rho=(P(\mathrm{Ma})/P(\mathrm{Ea}))^{2/3}$ and hence every geocentric direction of Mars: it proves $d_g(\mathrm{Ma},t,v,\delta)$ whenever the computed direction lies within $\delta$ of $v$ (with $B$ proving the numerical bounds, as in scout P §2.3).
(b) $\mathrm{K12}\cup E^-$ leaves $\rho$ free. At every $t$ at which $x(\mathrm{Ea},t)$ and $F_M(t)$ are not parallel, the geocentric directions of Mars at $t$ over the models of $\mathrm{K12}\cup E^-$ (with $\rho$ ranging over $(0,\infty)$) form the open great-circle arc from $-u_E(t)$ (the Sun's direction) to $F_M(t)/|F_M(t)|$, of length $\alpha(t)\in(0,\pi)$, the angle between these two directions; so $\mathrm{K12}\cup E^-$ proves no datum $d_g(\mathrm{Ma},t,v,\delta)$ with $\delta<\alpha(t)/2$.

*Proof.* (a) K3 with $a(\mathrm{Ea})=1$ gives $a(\mathrm{Ma})^3=P(\mathrm{Ma})^2/P(\mathrm{Ea})^2$; then K12 determines $x(\mathrm{Ma},t)$ and the bridge the direction. (b) The geocentric vector is $g(\rho)=\rho F_M(t)-x(\mathrm{Ea},t)$; as $\rho$ runs over $(0,\infty)$, $g(\rho)$ runs along the open ray from $-x(\mathrm{Ea},t)$ in the direction $F_M(t)$, which does not pass through the origin when the two are not parallel; seen from the origin, its direction moves monotonically along the great circle through $-u_E(t)$ and $F_M(t)/|F_M(t)|$, from the first towards the second, covering the open arc between them. Every $\rho>0$ gives a model of K12 (K12 constrains $a$ only by $a>0$). An arc of length $\alpha$ is contained in no cap of radius $<\alpha/2$. $\square$

*Computed [c7 (1)].* Over 1580–1600 (daily, plan94), the arc length $\alpha(t)$ has median 90° (5th–95th percentiles 9.5°–170°; minimum 0.11°, so no sampled day is exactly at conjunction or opposition); along the arc the direction moves monotonically as $\rho$ grows (checked on 21 sampled days), and the sensitivity of the geocentric direction to $\rho$ at the true $\rho$ has median 969′ and maximum 3555′ per unit of $\rho$.

So in the geocentric language, K3's content is exactly the element $\rho$: the bits needed to state $\rho$ to the precision the data require. `c4` Part B measures them for the 1580–1600 Mars data: 11.3, 12.3, 13.4 and 14.7 bits at 8′, 4′, 2′ and 1′, for $\rho\in[0,4)$ (`c7`'s cruder estimate from the most sensitive day: 9.8, 10.8, 11.8 bits at 8′, 4′, 2′), against a K3 text of 375 bits in the syntax S1 (450 in S2; §4.4). For one planet pair K3 does not pay; by the criterion of `laws` Prop 5.5(b) it pays only when it fixes the sizes of about $|\mathrm{K3}|/s\approx$ 25–40 bodies, which is scout N2's "K3 is a law about initial conditions" made quantitative (§4.4 Part B). The historical observation that Copernicus obtained the relative distances from observations without K3 fits this: the distances are measurable elements (history from memory, not checked).

The pool: circle (2 elements), eccentric (4), bisected equant (4), vicarious hypothesis (5), ellipse (4), each with and without K3. A theory is a law text plus element numerals; it proves every datum iff its longitudes are within $\delta$ of every $v_i$, and it is then consistent with the data, so its proof-requiring weight is $2^{-|\text{text}|}$ or 0.

**The written-down syntax.**
* *Element numerals:* each element is a fixed-length 64-bit binary numeral for a cell midpoint of its range ($e,e_1,e_2\in[0,1)$; $w$, $L$ in $[0,2\pi)$; $n\in[0,0.1)$ rad/day; $L$ is the mean longitude at the window's midpoint). Then the total weight of the compatible numerals of a form is $V_F(\delta)/\prod(\text{ranges})$ up to grid error $2^{-64}$, where $V_F(\delta)$ is the volume of the compatible region in element space. Write $C_F(\delta):=-\log_2(V_F(\delta)/\prod\text{ranges})$, the form's *Occam factor* in bits. The posterior of form $F$ is proportional to $2^{-(|\mathrm{LAW}_F|_S+C_F(\delta))}$.
* *Law texts* (written out as s-expressions in the script; length = tokens × $\lceil\log_2|\text{alphabet}|\rceil$), in three syntaxes: **S1** arithmetic ($+,-,\cdot,/,\sin,\cos,\mathrm{atan2},\sqrt{}$, numerals, $\forall,\exists$; the equant and vicarious constructions and Kepler's equation are implicit definitions with $\exists$); **S2** epicyclic (S1 plus a unit-vector primitive, a direction primitive, and "the point where the ray from $Q$ at angle $\theta$ meets the unit circle about $C$"); **S3** Keplerian (S1 plus the true anomaly as a function of $e$ and the mean anomaly).

### 4.5 What Newton adds

Newton's theory extends the language with masses $m:\mathfrak B\to\mathbb R$, the constant $G$ and accelerations (a sort of trajectories or an axiomatised derivative in $B$); the law is $m_b\ddot x_b=\sum_{b'\ne b}Gm_bm_{b'}(x_{b'}-x_b)/|x_{b'}-x_b|^3$. The data language is unchanged: this is ladder step 3, theoretical terms with no new data vocabulary. Masses are $T$-theoretical in Sneed's sense, measurable only by using the theory [known: sneed1971logical, balzer1987architectonic; content: secondary]. With the figures of §4.3 [computed: scout P c1–c3, re-run identical]:
1. **Unification.** One law plus one number per body replaces K12 + K3 for every body, and derives the two-body form of K3 with a mass-dependent constant, $n^2a^3=G(M+m)$, i.e. $q=1+m/M$. The fitted $q$ of Jupiter (1.00075–1.00099) matches its two-body value 1.00095; Saturn's (1.0013–1.0019) does not (two-body 1.00029) and is explained by the orbit-averaged pull of Jupiter, which acts like an interior ring (ring estimate 1.00154; a secular, circular, coplanar heuristic, not a perturbation theory). So the "constant" of K3 differs between planets at the $10^{-3}$ level, and Newton says why.
2. **Corrections inside the old domain.** Mercury's apsidal line turns 575.4″ per century in the fixed J2000 frame [computed]; in the classical decomposition of the motion relative to the equinox (5599.74″ per century), general precession accounts for about 5025.6″, leaving about 574.1″ in a fixed frame (our fit: 575.4″), of which Newtonian planetary perturbations account for about 531.5″, and the remainder of about 43″ is general relativity [known: clemence1947relativity; content: secondary: the total 5599.74″ and Newtonian 5557.18″ per century, leaving 42.56″, are Clemence's figures as analysed in arXiv 1008.1811; the split ≈574″ dynamical / ≈531″ planetary / ≈43″ relativistic is from course material (LBL) located by WebSearch; 5025.6″ is the difference 5599.74 − 574.1, not checked against a source]. Newton's own domain ends there. The great inequality of Jupiter and Saturn (period about 900 yr, amplitudes 18.3′ and 43.8′ in our fits) is the classic case explained by Laplace [known: wilson1985great for the history; period and amplitude not checked against it].
3. **New questions in the old language, and new directions of answering.** The masses of the Sun and of planets with satellites; "where is the body perturbing Uranus?" (Le Verrier's Neptune, also an example of reference fixed by a description [known: kripke1980naming; content: secondary]).
4. **New domains.** The Moon, where a fixed Kepler orbit fails by 1.1° within a month [computed]; falling bodies, tides, comets (these need more base vocabulary, so the language grows too).
5. **No deductive entailment of Kepler.** Newton's axioms do not entail K12 or K3; they entail corrected statements and "K12 holds within $\varepsilon(b,W)$ on window $W$", with $\varepsilon$ as in §4.3 (§3.3; `laws` Prop 7.1 for the provable toy). A formal derivation of even one such bound needs an existence-and-uniqueness theorem and a validated integration or perturbation estimate: a very long derivation that no textbook writes out. This is the computational gap of the textbook point in a concrete case (§5.1(b)) [assessment].

### 4.6 Approximate laws: what to cite

The selection theorems about approximate laws are `laws`' and are not re-proved here [proved there]:
* *Strength is rewarded.* Over deterministic hypotheses, proof-requiring compatibility is monotone in logical strength, so data never move the odds towards a hedge, and the false exact law beats its true tolerance-weakened version on every data set the latter fits (`laws` Prop 2.1, Cor 2.2; this is scout P's Prop 2.3). Inside $M_A$ this is qualified: after the exact law is refuted, randomised honest theories ("$T^\varepsilon$ plus a random completion inside what it allows") keep positive weight, and on the real Kepler residuals they win (`laws` §3.6, §10 item 6).
* *Precision.* Under guaranteed tolerances the switch point is sharp at $\varepsilon^*+\delta_0$ (`laws` Prop 2.4); under dithered rounding a false exact law is refuted after about $\Delta/\Ex|e|$ data however coarse the cells (`laws` Prop 2.5).
* *Honest laws against laws with exceptions.* The honest law has the lower rate iff $\mathrm{pk}(e)<\gamma_{\rm ex}/(2\gamma_{\rm si})$, the price of an exception against twice the price of silence (`laws` Prop 3.4). Under SAI's own pricing silence is cheap and honesty wins unless the error is very peaked; on the Kepler residuals of seven bodies over 12–2000-year windows (peakedness 1.9–4.0) the honest law wins for every body, window and precision from 0.1′ to 30°, with tolerance about $(0.7$–$1)\varepsilon^*$ and at most 1% exceptions (`laws` §3.6 [computed there]). Scout N2's "spread errors favour honesty, concentrated errors favour exceptions" holds only for one pricing (`laws` §10 item 2).

§4.4's posterior is the guaranteed-tolerance regime with exact interval centres; under rounding every form in the pool, the ellipse included, is eventually refuted by enough data (plan94's residuals from every fixed ellipse are nonzero), and the honest forms take over.

## 5. Limitations of the axiom view (T5)

Each item: the claim and its source in the notes; a sharp statement; the formal status, either **partially addressable** (a formal handle exists or is a research question, with its pointer) or **a genuine limit** of the declarative view. All verdicts are [assessment] unless a theorem is cited.

### 5.1 L1. Written theories are far from sufficient for a simple reasoner (the textbook point)

**Sources.** The author's request ("our written theories are not actually remotely sufficient for solving problems when hooked to some simple reasoner"); `phil-sci-our-science-is-very-human-software.md` ("those predictions are very much not just made by formal inferences from the letter of the theory, but also using your background understanding"); `phil-sci-scientific-theories-are-not-for-generating-or-predicting-arbitrary-data.md` (the Feynman and olympiad problems); `phil-sci-some-disanalogies…`, third bullet; the condensation essay ("the notions of newtonian mechanics alone are inert"). We agree with the claim; the work is to say what it consists of.

**Three gaps** (scout P's split, kept).
* **(a) The bridge gap.** A problem posed in ordinary terms ("a rope over a pulley…") becomes a formal problem only through idealisations and bridge principles that the written theory does not contain. For the problem *as posed*, the theory is logically insufficient. Literature: preparing the description, and models as autonomous mediators [known: cartwright1983how, morgan1999models; content: secondary]. *Formal status:* partially addressable. Bridges are more axioms in a larger language; Prop 2.4 prices the bridge from raw data to questions as the extractor's complexity $K(g)$, which in real cases is large. A measurable question is how large bridge and method parts are compared with the "physical picture".
* **(b) The computational gap.** For the formalised problem, theory plus mathematics is logically sufficient, but finding the answer needs methods, lemmas and approximation schemes. §4.5's case: deriving "Kepler within $\varepsilon$ on window $W$" from Newton needs an existence theorem and a validated perturbation bound, a long derivation no textbook writes out. *Formal status:* partially addressable, and a theorem for one class, with a precondition that matters: the gap is visible only when the *written* theory is charged. Under SAI's program-length prior the reasoner is invisible, because a short program can generate the missing lemmas (`shape` Thm 1.11 [proved there]). With a cost on the written theory (`shape` Thm 1.12): for a linear law over GF(2) with conditional questions in every direction, a unit-propagation reasoner needs exactly the clauses of the indecomposable circuits, at least $A_d2^{d-1}$ when every derived equation has weight $d\ge3$ ($m2^{(m-1)/2}$ clauses against $\log_2(m+1)$ equations for the simplex family), while a Gaussian-elimination reasoner needs only the equations (`shape` Thm 2.6, Cor 2.8); intermediate concepts make one law linear-size and usable, but for a system of laws every unit-propagation-usable theory, with any intermediate concepts, has written length $2^{\Omega(m^{\varepsilon/3})}$ (`shape` Thm 3.6, given Göös–Kamath–Robere–Sokolov 2019; Gwynne–Kullmann 2014's superpolynomial version checked by `shape` at abstract level). The AI-paper's charged-deduction results bound derivation size but leave the *search* free; they give the written core and the full lemma library the same cost and so cannot express this gap (`shape` Prop 5.1, Thm 5.2 [proved there]). A weak reasoner without intermediate concepts also *learns* its know-how pattern by pattern, while an algebraic reasoner learns the law at once (`shape` Thm 6.2).
  * **What the theorem shows [assessment].** "Insufficient for a simple reasoner" is a property of the pair (theory, reasoner) under a cost on the written theory, and it can be exponential: the know-how must live in the reasoner, in explicit lemmas, or in a computation the cost model does not charge (free proof search, or a generator under program length; `shape` Prop 5.1). So the author's textbook claim holds in the refined form "the written theory is insufficient for a simple reasoner, as a property of the pair, when what is written is what is charged".
  * **What it does not show [assessment].** It says nothing about the content of physics textbooks; the laws are linear equations over GF(2), the reasoner is unit propagation, and the gap closes completely for a reasoner that can do algebra. It does not touch the bridge gap or the tacit gap. And it cannot be stated for SAI as defined, whose prior charges a theory by the length of a program that may compute the missing lemmas (`shape` Thm 1.11): to model the textbook point, the paper must use a listing or time-charged prior. It does not show that humans solve problems by carrying exponentially many lemmas; it shows that a *declarative* account of a weak reasoner's competence must include them.
* **(c) The tacit gap.** Perceptual and manual skills (reading instruments, recognising situations [known: polanyi1958personal, polanyi1966tacit; content: secondary]), and exemplars, through which new problems are seen as like solved ones [known: kuhn1970structure, kuhn1977second; content: secondary]. *Formal status:* a genuine limit of a declarative view. Exemplars enter SAI only as data.

### 5.2 L2. Approximate truth

**Sources.** The author's request ("kepler's laws not really giving you correct predictions but giving approximately correct predictions"); `hanni-solomonoff-axiom-induction.md` ("we should have a metric on values grade generated sentences"); the condensation essay (purpose-made metrics). **Sharp statement.** Kepler's laws as stated are false; they are compatible with data at precision $\delta$ exactly when $\delta$ exceeds their error on the domain (§4.3–4.4, computed). **Formal status:** partially addressable, and largely done in `laws` (§4.6 lists the results): over deterministic theories SAI rewards the false exact law over its true honest version (`laws` Cor 2.2), but inside $M_A$ randomised honest theories survive its refutation; the precision regimes are `laws` Props 2.4–2.5; the honest law beats the exact law with exceptions iff the error's peakedness is below the ratio of the prices of an exception and of silence (`laws` Prop 3.4), and under SAI's own pricing the honest law wins on all the real Kepler residuals (`laws` §3.6) [proved there; computed there]. Scout N2's pricing-independent reading ("spread-out errors give honest laws, concentrated errors give laws with exceptions") is refuted as stated (`laws` §10 item 2); so whether an approximate law is held honestly or with exceptions depends on how exceptions and silence are priced, which is a modelling choice. *Honest approximate theories are partial theories:* the trichotomy is the formal home of humility. **What remains open:** a canonical metric (none exists; it is purpose-relative) and a general comparative notion of truthlikeness, which is known to be hard (Popper's definition fails for false theories [known: popper1963conjectures, miller1974popper, tichy1974popper; content: secondary/abstract]; the similarity approach needs a metric [known: niiniluoto1987truthlikeness; content: secondary]); our notion is deliberately local: question, tolerance, domain.

### 5.3 L3. Domains of applicability, several models, inconsistent theories in use

**Sources.** `phil-sci-the-domain-of-applicability-of-a-scientific-model.md` (flat earth 100 m against spherical earth 99.9 m, "then we use 100=99.9 in some algebraic manipulations to do some nonsense"; the Stosszahlansatz with Newtonian mechanics; "the vibe of physics is imo more like: oh here's a way of talking that works. these bad things? don't do them, silly!"); `phil-sci-some-disanalogies…` ("having multiple ways to think about something"); the condensation essay (the Earth as a sphere, an oblate spheroid, rugged). **Sharp statement.** Practice uses mutually inconsistent models, each in its own domain; a single consistent axiom set must relativise each to a domain predicate or drop one; proof-requiring SAI gives a globally inconsistent theory weight 0. **Formal status:**
* the specific catastrophe is blocked soundly: two exact approximations that differ anywhere on a common domain are jointly inconsistent; honest versions of true models are jointly consistent and everything derived from them is true; for a scalar quantity (or box tolerances) joint consistency is a pairwise check that the tolerances overlap, for Euclidean tolerances in $\mathbb R^m$ an $(m+1)$-wise check; and every derived conclusion carries a propagated tolerance (`laws` Prop 4.3 [proved there]; scout N2 Prop 3.3, Cor 3.4); see §6.1;
* domains are formalisable as restricted axioms or structuralist intended applications [known: balzer1987architectonic; content: secondary]; a domain restriction pays iff the law, even with an honest tolerance or with exceptions, describes the excluded system worse than no law; under SAI's pricing that never happens asymptotically for a system the law confines to within about 24° of longitude, so the Moon is kept with an honest tolerance of a few degrees rather than excluded (`laws` Prop 6.1 [proved there]; scout N2's "the Moon is excluded" holds only under another pricing, `laws` §10 item 4); learning domains is partial (`laws` §8: tolerances per body and window are learned, rather than sharp domains). So in SAI the formal counterpart of a domain of applicability is usually a tolerance that varies with the system, not a sharp boundary [assessment];
* *knowing when a model applies* ("don't do that, silly!") is a recognizer (§5.4) and is a genuine limit of the declarative view.

**The other limitations, briefly** (scout P L4–L12, checked against the threads).

Induction in mathematics, which working scientists and mathematicians do, needs a reasoner for whom deduction is costly; the ideal inducer cannot model it.

### 5.5 Verdict on the limitations

The axiom view captures the *static, declarative, inferential* side of theories and concepts: contents, inferential relations, composition of contents, learning by adding and weighing axioms, pointers, partiality, approximate truth in honest form. It misses the *procedural* side (recognizers, methods, the search for derivations, action) and the *dynamic* side (purposes, softness, clarification, meaning change, discovery, the social process). This is less than a theory of concepts asks for; but a theory of concepts is not what the paper offers. The paper should say, in these words, that it offers a model of the declarative core of scientific theories, and that the procedural side reappears formally as the reasoner-relativity of `shape` [assessment].

### 6.1 Possible error in the author's view: "no nice formal criterion" for avoiding catastrophic reasoning with approximate models

**Claim and source.** `philosophy/philosophy of science/the domain of applicability of a scientific mode(l).md`: after the example of using "100 = 99.9" (flat earth against spherical earth) in algebra, "how come we usually avoid this sort of catastrophic reasoning? * i don't think any nice "formal criterion" is going to be right?"; and "the vibe of physics is imo more like: oh here's a way of talking that works. these bad things? don't do them, silly!"

**Why it is probably overstated.** There is a nice formal criterion that is *sound*: state each approximate model in its honest form, $|q-f|\le\varepsilon$ on its domain, and reason classically. Then every conclusion carries a propagated tolerance; honest versions of true models are jointly consistent and everything derived from them is true, while exact versions that differ anywhere on a common domain prove everything; for a scalar quantity, joint consistency is a pairwise check that the tolerances overlap wherever both models apply; and "$100=99.9$" becomes "$|100-99.9|\le\varepsilon_1+\varepsilon_2$", which is true and licenses no nonsense (`laws` Prop 4.3 [proved there]; scout N2 Prop 3.3, Cor 3.4). This is what interval methods and validated numerics do in computer-assisted proofs. A probabilistic version (error models with stated confidence) is also formal and less conservative: after 100 steps of random-sign errors the mean deviation is exactly $7.96\varepsilon$ against the worst case $100\varepsilon$, and after 1000 steps $25.2\varepsilon$ against $1000\varepsilon$ (`laws` Remark 4.4 [computed there]; scout N2's simulation gave $7.8\varepsilon$). What these criteria lack is *fidelity to practice*: physicists rarely state or propagate tolerances explicitly, and the worst-case version is too conservative to be what they do. The note's own example of the Stosszahlansatz is consistent with this: as exact claims, molecular chaos at all times and reversible dynamics conflict; as honest approximate claims with domains (typical states, bounded times), the conflict is what the tolerances are for.

So the claim is right as a description (practice does not run a formal criterion explicitly) and wrong as a claim that no formal criterion can do the job. The note's question mark suggests the author holds it tentatively.

**What the note itself considered, and the limits of the correction** (added in the third run, after re-reading the note). The note tests one criterion, "pick some precise mathematical model, and then stick to that", and rejects it for good reasons: one often wants several models of one situation, choosing the model is itself the hard question, and a precise model can be inconsistent (the Stosszahlansatz with Newtonian mechanics). The tolerance criterion is a different criterion, and it meets the second and third objections: several models are used together, each stated honestly on its domain, and honest versions of mutually inconsistent exact models are jointly consistent when their tolerances overlap. It does not meet the first: it says nothing about how to choose a model, a domain or a tolerance, which stays a matter of judgement. It also has a limit of its own. Soundness alone is cheap (the empty theory is sound); a criterion must also license the inferences that practice rightly draws. Worst-case propagation does this for short chains and well-conditioned computations, but over long chains, or for ill-conditioned or chaotic dynamics, the propagated bound becomes vacuous while practice still draws reliable conclusions; there the work is done by probabilistic error models or by stability results, which are formal too, but specific to classes of problems. So: the note is probably right that no single formal criterion reproduces scientific practice, and probably wrong that no formal criterion blocks the catastrophe it describes while leaving ordinary approximate reasoning intact [assessment].

**What to write instead.** "Catastrophic reasoning with approximate models is blocked, soundly, by stating approximations with tolerances on domains and propagating them; a probabilistic error model does the same with stated confidence and less conservatism. Neither says how to choose a model or a tolerance, and worst-case bounds can become too weak to support conclusions that practice rightly draws. Scientific practice rarely states or propagates tolerances explicitly; the gap between the sound criterion and practice is part of what written theories leave to tacit understanding."

### 6.2 Possible error in the author's view: "nothing fundamentally right about these information-theoretic losses"

**Claim and source.** The condensation essay, on predictions graded by invented metrics and the reply it considers, "if you eg have a guess in R^2 which is close in L^2, you could put some gaussian noise on it to predict alright wrt an information-theoretic loss" (which the essay says "isn't taking the issue seriously"): "The point is that there is really nothing fundamentally right about these information-theoretic losses — it comes down to what the practical thing the prediction is supposed to help you with is — and different losses just often give different behavior. But I haven't thought this through carefully."

**Assessment.** Two jobs are run together.
* *Grading a prediction for use.* Here the essay is right: what is graded, and with which tolerance, is decided by the purpose (which quantities matter; how close is close enough). We agree, and the paper's interval data (§4.1) are built this way.
* *Scoring credences and updating beliefs about theories.* Here the logarithmic score is not arbitrary: among scoring rules that depend only on the probability given to the outcome that occurred, it is the only proper one (up to affine change, for more than two outcomes) [known: bernardo1979expected; content: secondary: the attribution of the uniqueness of the log score among proper *local* scoring rules (more than two outcomes, up to affine transformation) to this paper is from arXiv 1101.5011 and 1104.2224 (Dawid, Lauritzen, Parry; Ehm, Gneiting), located by WebSearch; Bernardo's own abstract is about experimental design; a third-run search found a source stating the classical result under a differentiability assumption that later work removed, so the paper should cite the result as "the logarithmic score is, up to affine change, the only proper local scoring rule on more than two outcomes" with a secondary source alongside Bernardo]; and cumulative log loss is what Bayesian updating and likelihood comparison of theories optimise. The semimeasure-convention losses throughout this paper are of this kind.

So "nothing fundamentally right" is overstated if read generally; it is right if read as a claim about grading predictions for practical use. Our formalism shows the two jobs combining without conflict: the purpose fixes which sentences are data and at what tolerance ("the longitude of Mars lies within $\delta$ of $v$"), and the prior and the likelihood of SAI then select the theory (§4.4). The essay is right to reject the reply it considers (turning a purpose-made tolerance into a Gaussian noise model on the predicted quantity): that reply grades the prediction by a loss the purpose did not ask for. The reconstruction that works turns the tolerance into the *question* ("is the longitude within $\delta$ of $v$?") and keeps the logarithmic loss for the credences given to the answers. We keep this note in a narrow form because the author explicitly flags the point as unconsidered and because its general reading matters to a paper whose losses are logarithmic.

**What to write instead.** "Predictions in science are graded by purpose-relative standards on high-level quantities. This is compatible with likelihood-based theory choice, which enters once the purpose has fixed which propositions are at stake and to what precision; for scoring credences as such, the logarithmic score has a privileged status."
