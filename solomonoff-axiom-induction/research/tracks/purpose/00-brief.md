## Targets

* **T1. Locality inside SAI.** State and prove, in the paper's notation and conventions, that SAI scores a theory machine G on a finite data set D only through ν_G(D) (and K(G)); so two theory machines that give D the same probability get the same score, whatever their mass elsewhere. Make precise the chain-rule statement: for any family of questions about one quantity (e.g. interval sentences about one measured value), the cumulative loss of the answers, in any order and in each of the paper's loss conventions where it holds, is −log of the probability of the event they jointly determine. So a question family can encode a partition of the answers ("good enough at resolution Δ") but not a metric on them. State exactly which loss conventions this holds for (semimeasure, normalised, three-valued) and where it fails (e.g. the normalised loss depends on order: paper Thm A.33).
* **T2. Local and distance-sensitive scores.** The exact statement, hypotheses and reference of the theorem that the logarithmic score is the only local proper scoring rule (Bernardo 1979, or whichever source states it correctly; mind the hypotheses: at least three outcomes, smoothness). The continuous ranked probability score (CRPS) and the energy score as distance-sensitive proper scores (Gneiting and Raftery 2007): CRPS as the integral over thresholds of the Brier score of the event {X ≤ t} under the marginal forecast. Work the author's example exactly: forecasts uniform on 5 nearby values and uniform on 5 scattered values, both including the outcome; equal log scores, different CRPS. Also mention the higher-order local scores (Hyvärinen-type) only if they matter.
* **T3. Error densities as purposes.** For a point law with an additive error model from a location–scale family: fixed-scale Gaussian gives log loss = squared error / (2σ² ln 2) + const bits; Laplace gives absolute error; uniform on [−δ, δ] gives the tolerance forms (compatibility iff max error ≤ δ). With the scale fitted by maximum likelihood, rank laws within each family: by RMS error, mean absolute error and maximum error respectively (derive n/2·log(2πe σ̂²), n·log(2e b̂), n·log(2δ̂)). For nonparametric (histogram) error models, as in the paper's 40-bin computation, the rate is the entropy of the binned residual distribution, which is distance-blind beyond the bin width. Relate this precisely to the paper's interval-data setting (regime R, cell width Δ, band-confined completions, Prop 9.2(c): the rate is the conditional entropy of answers given questions).
* **T4. Misspecification: scores rank wrong laws differently.**
  * (a) A clean toy with two wrong laws ranked in opposite orders by log loss with the best calibrated error model (in a rich family) and by squared or maximum error. Exact numbers.
  * (b) A computation on the actual Kepler residual series (`../laws/checks/l4_residuals.npz`): for each body and window, rank the available fits by maximum error, RMS error, mean absolute error and the binned-residual entropy rate (40 bins, and the paper's other settings if cheap); report every disagreement between rankings, with numbers. If the fits never disagree, say so; that is a finding too. Seeded where randomness is used; saved output.
* **T5. Purpose losses with universal weights.**
  * The aggregating algorithm (Vovk): for an η-mixable loss and a countable class of predictors with prior weights w_i, cumulative loss ≤ L_i + ln(1/w_i)/η on every sequence. Exact statement and reference; the mixability constant of square loss on a bounded range (check η = 1/(2B²) for outcomes and predictions in [−B, B]); what holds for absolute loss (not mixable; the weak aggregating algorithm, Kalnishkan and Vyugin; Cesa-Bianchi and Lugosi's book).
  * With weights 2^(−K(T)) this gives regret ≤ K(T)·ln 2/η against every predictor in the class. Say what the class must be (total computable predictors are not effectively enumerable; the ideal mixture is incomputable, as SAI is).
  * An SAI analogue: how theory machines (which answer questions with proved / refuted / independent) yield predictions to which a purpose loss applies; formulate the cleanest version you can and state it as a definition plus open question, or prove what can be proved.
  * Which of the paper's results are loss-independent because they are statements about the weights 2^(−K) only: the Craig collapse between theories and labellers (paper Thms 4.2, 4.3), translation invariance and "new vocabulary costs O(1)" (Thm 3.5, Cor 3.6), machine invariance. Prove what transfers to the aggregating-algorithm version, or mark it as a sketch.
* **T6. Philosophy: what approximate correctness is.**
  * The two attitudes: credence (for hypotheses that might be exactly true; locality defensible) and adequacy for a purpose (for theories known to be false; graded by a purpose loss). Parker's adequacy-for-purpose view of model evaluation (Parker 2020, Philosophy of Science) and any closer sources you find; generalised Bayesian updating with a loss in place of the likelihood (Bissiri, Holmes and Walker 2016); Bayesian inference under misspecification (e.g. Grünwald and van Ommen 2017).
  * Assess honestly: is the author's position right, and where would we put it differently? The epistemic policy binds: assert only what we believe; flag a probable error only if it has a decent probability of being an error.
  * The paper's existing sentence "a canonical metric for approximate predictions, which we think does not exist" and its local notion (question family, tolerance, domain): does it stand, and how does it fit T1–T5?
  * What the purpose view does to the Kepler example: Kepler's 8′ was a maximum-error tolerance; which purposes rank the Mars pool forms as the paper's log-loss computations do, and which might not.
