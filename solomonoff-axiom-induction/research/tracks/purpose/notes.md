## Summary

1. **Locality (T1).** Proved: in the semimeasure and three-valued conventions SAI scores a theory machine on $D$ only through $\nu_G(D)$ (and $\nu_G(\emptyset)$, $K(G)$); it fails in the normalised convention. For a *fixed* family of questions about one quantity the cumulative loss is the log score of one cell, so such a family encodes a partition and no grading of misses. **Refinement of Claude's reply:** questions drawn at random (the dithered grid of regime R) make the *expected* loss distance-sensitive, but only through a separation probability, which is a bounded pseudometric of cut type: misses are graded up to the cell width and never like squared error. In the coarse regime this expected loss is a threshold-integrated log score (a log analogue of the CRPS), and it is exactly Prop. 9.2(a)'s $c_{\mathcal G}$.
2. **Scores (T2).** Known, with hypotheses: the log score is the only local proper scoring rule on $\ge3$ outcomes (we prove the differentiable case). On the author's example the log score is $\log_2 5$ for both forecasts, the CRPS $0.4$ against $4.0$; a variant reverses the two rankings; a misspecified Gaussian family gets opposite rankings from log score and CRPS.
3. **Error densities (T3).** Proved: with the scale fitted by maximum likelihood, Gaussian, Laplace, uniform and generalised-Gaussian error models rank laws by RMS, mean absolute, maximum and $L^p$ error. New and proved: SAI's best rate in regime R (Prop. 9.2(c)) is exactly $h(e+V_\Delta)-\log\Delta$, the differential entropy of the error smoothed at the cell width; it is $(1/\Delta)\int H_2(F_e)$ in the coarse regime (a dispersion, distance-sensitive) and $h(e)-\log\Delta+o(1)$ in the fine limit (rearrangement-invariant, distance-blind).
4. **Misspecification (T4).** Computed, exact toy: a law exactly right on 80% of times and off by 1000 on the rest beats a law always within 1 under SAI's pricing at every cell width below $1.5585$; a law that is pointwise worse at every time beats the better one below cell width $1.4296$. Real Kepler residuals (four fits, 39 series): the four criteria and SAI's pricing disagree often; SAI's ranking of fits changes significantly with the cell width in 16 of 234 fit pairs, and where SAI's preferred fit is significantly best it is none of the max-, RMS- or mean-error winners in 4 of 15 series at $0.1'$ and 3 of 10 at $1800'$ (§T4).
5. **Purpose losses (T5).** The aggregating algorithm with weights $2^{-K}$ is known (predictive complexity, Vovk and coauthors). New here: a definition of purpose-SAI on answered data (acts of theory machines), its regret bound $K(G)\ln2/\eta$, and the transfer of machine invariance, faithful translations and new vocabulary (machine by machine, both directions) and of the Craig collapse for always-consistent machines; open questions.
6. **Assessment (T6).** Claude's reply was mostly right. Corrections: (i) "a partition, not a metric" holds for fixed questions, not for SAI's own regime R; (ii) "calibrated means fitted for log loss" is wrong in regime R: the calibrated error model is optimal for every proper score; what is log-specific is the ranking across laws; (iii) the nonparametric error model is not an exotic case: it is what $M_A$ uses, so at fine resolution SAI's own ranking is distance-blind (and can reverse pointwise dominance); (iv) "the objection reaches the whole Solomonoff framework" needs a qualification: for prediction in the well-specified case the mixture with Bayes acts serves every bounded loss (Hutter 2003, [A]); what the objection reaches is the ranking of hypotheses, that is, theory choice among approximate laws. No probable error in the author's objection was found.

## T1. Locality inside SAI

**Lemma T1.0 (posterior weight of a program).** For $p\in\mathrm{dom}\,V$ and a two-valued $D$, $\mathrm{Unif}(E_D\cap[p])=2^{-|p|}\nu_{G_{V(p)}}(D)$. *Proof.* On a tape $p\omega'$ the universal machine emits $A^{G_{V(p)}}_{\omega'}$ (setting.tex, after Def. 2.4; Def. 2.3 for $A^G_\omega$), so $E_D\cap[p]=\{p\omega':\omega'\in E^{G_{V(p)}}_D\}$, of measure $2^{-|p|}\mathrm{Unif}(E^{G_{V(p)}}_D)$. $\square$ **[proved]**

**Proposition T1.1 (locality in the semimeasure and three-valued conventions).** Let $G$ be a theory machine and $D$ a finite consistent two-valued data set with $\nu_G(D)>0$.
(a) The posterior probability of the program $p$ after $D$ is $\pi_D(p)=2^{-|p|}\nu_{G_{V(p)}}(D)/M_A(D)$, and the two-part score of §9 is $s_D(G)=K(G)-\log\nu_G(D)$. Both depend on the machine only through $|p|$ (resp. $K(G)$) and the single number $\nu_G(D)$.
(b) For every consistent reveal sequence $(\varphi_t,y_t)_{t\le n}$ with $D_n=D$, in any order and with repeats, $\sum_{t\le n}-\log\nu_G(y_t\mid D_{t-1},\varphi_t)=-\log\nu_G(D)+\log\nu_G(\emptyset)$.
(c) The same holds for three-valued $D$ with $\nu^3_G$ in place of $\nu_G$, and $\nu^3_G(\emptyset)=\nu_G(\emptyset)$.
(d) Hence two machines with $K(G)=K(G')$, $\nu_G(\emptyset)=\nu_{G'}(\emptyset)$ and $\nu_G(D)=\nu_{G'}(D)$ get the same two-part score and the same cumulative loss on $D$ in every order, whatever they do on other data sets.

*Proof.* (a) Lemma T1.0 and the definitions. (b) $\nu_G$ is monotone: $D'\subseteq D$ gives $E^G_{D'}\supseteq E^G_D$, since proving $\psi_D$ proves $\psi_{D'}$; so every $\nu_G(D_{t-1})\ge\nu_G(D)>0$. For $\varphi_t\notin\mathrm{dom}\,D_{t-1}$ the conditional is $\nu_G(D_t)/\nu_G(D_{t-1})$ by Def. 2.1; for a repeat, consistency of the sequence gives $y_t=D_{t-1}(\varphi_t)$, the conditional is $1$ by the repeated-input convention and $D_t=D_{t-1}$. The product telescopes to $\nu_G(D_n)/\nu_G(\emptyset)$. (c) The same argument: $E^{3,G}_D$ only gains conditions as $D$ grows, and Prop. 3.1(f) gives $\nu^3_G(\emptyset)=\nu_G(\emptyset)$. (d) follows from (a)-(c). $\square$ **[proved; computed: `p1_locality.out` parts A and C, 1700 and 259 (instance, order) pairs, deviation $\le9\cdot10^{-16}$ bits]**

For $G=G_U$ this is Prop. 3.1(d); the content of T1.1 is that it holds machine by machine, so it is the scoring of each hypothesis, not only of the mixture, that is local.

**Proposition T1.2 (the normalised convention is not local).** With $G$'s normalised conditional $\nu_G(y\mid D,\varphi)/(\nu_G(1\mid D,\varphi)+\nu_G(0\mid D,\varphi))$, the cumulative normalised loss along a reveal sequence without repeats is $-\log\nu_G(D_n)+\log\nu_G(\emptyset)+\sum_t\log(1-I_t)$, where $I_t:=\nu^3_G(D_{t-1}+(\varphi_t,\iota))/\nu_G(D_{t-1})$ is $G$'s independent mass at step $t$. So it depends on $G$'s mass on events other than $E^G_{D}$, and on the order.
*Proof.* Prop. 3.1(b): $\nu_G(D+(\varphi,1))+\nu_G(D+(\varphi,0))+\nu^3_G(D+(\varphi,\iota))=\nu_G(D)$, so the normalising denominator is $1-I_t$. $\square$ **[proved]** *Examples* (`p1_locality.out` part B): $G_1$ proves "$q\in[0,1]$" with probability $\frac12$ and is otherwise silent; $G_2$ proves it with probability $\frac12$ and otherwise refutes it. Both have $\nu(\emptyset)=1$, $\nu(D)=\frac12$, semimeasure loss 1 bit; normalised loss 0 and 1 bit. A third machine has normalised loss 2.000 or 1.585 bits on the same two data in the two orders (semimeasure loss 2 bits in both); for $M_A$ itself this is Thm. A.33. **[computed]** The consistency-requiring predictor is not local either (it is Dempster's rule, and its loss depends on the order: Prop. A.21 of the paper). **[the paper's result]**

**Proposition T1.3 (chain rule for questions about one quantity).** Let $B\supseteq\mathrm{RCF}$ have a constant $q$, and for a finite union $C$ of intervals with rational endpoints let $\sigma_C$ be the sentence "$q\in C$". Let $D$ be a two-valued data set of sentences $\sigma_I$, and $C_D:=\bigcap_{D(\sigma_I)=1}I\cap\bigcap_{D(\sigma_I)=0}(\mathbb R\setminus I)$. Then $B\vdash\psi_D\leftrightarrow\sigma_{C_D}$, so $\nu_G(D)=\nu_G(\{(\sigma_{C_D},1)\})=:\beta_G(C_D)$, the probability that $G$'s random theory is consistent and proves $q\in C_D$; and the cumulative semimeasure loss of $G$ (of $M_A$ if $G=G_U$) on any reveal sequence of $D$, in any order, is $-\log\beta_G(C_D)+\log\nu_G(\emptyset)$. In the three-valued convention the loss is $-\log\nu^3_G(D)+\log\nu_G(\emptyset)$, again the probability of a single event ("consistent, proves $\sigma_{C_D}$, decides no $\iota$-labelled $\sigma_I$").
*Proof.* RCF proves that a conjunction of memberships and non-memberships of $q$ in finite unions of rational intervals is equivalent to membership in their Boolean combination (a quantifier-free statement about $q$ and rationals). $\nu_G$ depends on $D$ only through $\mathrm{Cn}(D^\pm)$ (Prop. 3.1(c)). Then T1.1(b), (c). $\square$ **[proved]**

**Corollary T1.4 (a fixed question family encodes a partition, not a grading of misses).** Let $\mathcal Q$ be a finite family of interval questions about $q$, all answered, in any order, and $\Pi_{\mathcal Q}$ the partition of the range into the atoms of the Boolean algebra the intervals generate. (i) The answers determine the atom $C(x)$ of the true value $x$, and $G$'s cumulative loss is $-\log\beta_G(C(x))+\log\nu_G(\emptyset)$. (ii) $\beta_G$ is a sub-probability on $\Pi_{\mathcal Q}$: $\sum_C\beta_G(C)\le\nu_G(\emptyset)$, because distinct atoms are pairwise $B$-incompatible, so the events "consistent and proves $\sigma_C$" are disjoint. (iii) So the loss is the logarithmic score of $\beta_G$ on the partition $\Pi_{\mathcal Q}$ at the true atom. The choice of $\mathcal Q$ encodes a resolution ("good enough at resolution $\Delta$"), but two machines that give the true atom the same $\beta$-mass get the same loss whether the rest of their mass sits next to the truth or far from it. **[proved]**

So Claude's statement is right for questions fixed in advance. It needs a refinement, because in the paper's own regime R each datum asks *one randomly drawn* question (the cell of a dithered grid) about a fresh value, and over many data the average loss converges to an *expected* loss over the question's randomness.

**Proposition T1.5 (random questions: what they add, and what they cannot add).** Let $\mu$ be a law on finite partitions $\Pi$ of the value range $X$ (drawn independently of everything else), and for a sub-probability $P$ on $X$ (the law of the cell of the value under a machine, as for band-confined completions at fresh times) put $S_\mu(P,x):=\mathbb E_{\Pi\sim\mu}[-\log P(\Pi(x))]$.
(a) $S_\mu$ is proper: $\mathbb E_{x\sim Q}S_\mu(P,x)\ge\mathbb E_{x\sim Q}S_\mu(Q,x)$ for every probability $Q$.
(b) $S_\mu(P,x)$ depends on $P$ only through its restriction to $N_\mu(x):=\bigcup\{\Pi(x):\Pi\in\mathrm{supp}\,\mu\}$; for the dithered grid of width $\Delta$, $N(x)\subseteq(x-\Delta,x+\Delta)$. Mass at distance $\ge\Delta$ from the truth is graded the same however far it is.
(c) The separation probability $\sigma_\mu(x,y):=\mu\{\Pi:\Pi(x)\ne\Pi(y)\}$ is a pseudometric with values in $[0,1]$ and a mixture of cut semimetrics $\delta_S(x,y):=|1_S(x)-1_S(y)|$; conversely every finite mixture $\sum_k\lambda_k\delta_{S_k}$ with $\sum_k\lambda_k\le1$ is a $\sigma_\mu$. For the dithered grid, $\sigma(x,y)=\min(|x-y|/\Delta,1)$.
(d) For near-point forecasts $P_{y,s}:=(1-s)\delta_y+sU$ ($U$ a fixed probability with $U(\Pi(x))>0$ for all $\Pi,x$): $S_\mu(P_{y,s},x)=\sigma_\mu(x,y)\log(1/s)+\mathbb E[1\{\Pi(x)\ne\Pi(y)\}\log(1/U(\Pi(x)))]+O(s)$, so $S_\mu(P_{y,s},x)/\log(1/s)\to\sigma_\mu(x,y)$. The grading of near-point misses by random questions is therefore a bounded pseudometric. Squared distance is not one ($d(0,2)=4>d(0,1)+d(1,2)=2$), so no law of questions makes SAI's expected loss grade near-point misses by squared error, nor by any unbounded cost.
(e) For the dithered grid of width $\Delta$ and $P$ supported in an interval $J\ni x$ of length $\le\Delta$: $S_\mu(P,x)=\frac1\Delta\,\mathrm{CRIGN}(P,x)$, where $\mathrm{CRIGN}(P,x):=\int-\log\big(P(X\le t)\text{ if }x\le t,\ P(X>t)\text{ otherwise}\big)\,dt$ is the threshold-integrated log score. With $P=\mathrm{BC}_{\mathcal G}$'s law on the band this is exactly the rate $(2\varepsilon/\Delta)c_{\mathcal G}$ of Prop. 9.2(a): the coarse-regime pricing of band-confined completions is a proper, distance-sensitive score of CRPS type, with the logarithm in place of the Brier score.

*Proof.* (a) For each fixed $\Pi$, $\sum_CQ(C)(-\log P(C))\ge\sum_CQ(C)(-\log Q(C))$ by Gibbs' inequality ($\sum_CP(C)\le1$); average over $\mu$ (Tonelli, non-negative terms). (b) $\Pi(x)\subseteq N_\mu(x)$ for $\mu$-almost every $\Pi$; a grid cell of width $\Delta$ containing $x$ lies in $(x-\Delta,x+\Delta)$. (c) $\sigma_\mu(x,x)=0$, symmetry, and the triangle inequality from $\{\Pi(x)\ne\Pi(z)\}\subseteq\{\Pi(x)\ne\Pi(y)\}\cup\{\Pi(y)\ne\Pi(z)\}$. For a partition into cells $C_1,\dots,C_m$, $1\{\Pi(x)\ne\Pi(y)\}=\frac12\sum_i\delta_{C_i}(x,y)$, so $\sigma_\mu$ is a mixture of cut semimetrics. Conversely, ask "$q\in S_k$?" with probability $\lambda_k$ and the trivial question otherwise. In the dithered grid $x<y$ are separated iff a grid point falls in $(x,y]$, of probability $\min((y-x)/\Delta,1)$. (d) If $\Pi(x)\ne\Pi(y)$ then $y\notin\Pi(x)$ and $P_{y,s}(\Pi(x))=sU(\Pi(x))$; otherwise $P_{y,s}(\Pi(x))\in[1-s,1]$, contributing $O(s)$. (e) A grid point falls in $J$ with probability $|J|/\Delta$, at a position $t$ with density $1/\Delta$ (at most one, as $|J|\le\Delta$); then the cell of $x$ meets $J$ in $J\cap[t,\infty)$ if $x\ge t$ and in $J\cap(-\infty,t)$ otherwise, with $P$-masses $P(X\ge t)$ and $P(X<t)$; with no grid point in $J$ the cost is 0, and outside $J$ the integrand of CRIGN is $-\log1=0$. $\square$ **[proved; computed: `p1_locality.out` part D (separation probability by Monte Carlo, and $S_\mu-\sigma\log(1/s)\to\sigma\log(R/\Delta)$ exactly), part E]**

*The author's example under random cells* (`p1_locality.out` part E; truth 0, uniform on $\{-2,\dots,2\}$ against uniform on $\{-20,-10,0,10,20\}$): expected SAI loss per datum $2.3219$ for both at $\Delta\le1$; at $\Delta=2$: $1.3219$ against $2.3219$; at $\Delta=5$: $0.4236$ against $2.3219$; at $\Delta\ge40$ both are $\mathrm{CRIGN}/\Delta$ ($2.1178/\Delta$ against $21.178/\Delta$). **[computed]** So at cell widths above the spacing of the near forecast, regime R already ranks the near forecast better, which a fixed question family cannot do.

## T2. Local and distance-sensitive scores

All scores below are losses (lower is better); Gneiting and Raftery (2007) use the positive orientation.

**Theorem T2.1 (local proper scoring rules).** Let $\Omega$ be finite with $m\ge3$ outcomes and $S(p,\omega)=s(p(\omega),\omega)$ a local scoring rule with $s(\cdot,\omega)$ differentiable on $(0,1)$. If $S$ is proper on the interior of the simplex, then $s(x,\omega)=-a\ln x+b(\omega)$ on $(0,1)$ with $a\ge0$ (strictly proper iff $a>0$). For $m=2$ every scoring rule is local (here $p(\omega)$ determines $p$), so the Brier score is a local proper rule that is not logarithmic.
*Proof (differentiable case).* For interior $p$, $q\mapsto\sum_\omega p(\omega)s(q(\omega),\omega)$ is minimal at $q=p$ over the simplex; at an interior point the gradient $(p(\omega)s'(p(\omega),\omega))_\omega$ is a multiple of $(1,\dots,1)$, so $p(\omega)s'(p(\omega),\omega)=\lambda(p)$ for all $\omega$. For $\omega_1\ne\omega_2$ and $x,y>0$ with $x+y<1$ there is an interior $p$ with $p(\omega_1)=x$, $p(\omega_2)=y$ (spread $1-x-y$ over the other $m-2\ge1$ outcomes). So $xs'(x,\omega_1)=ys'(y,\omega_2)$ whenever $x+y<1$; fixing a small $y$, $xs'(x,\omega_1)$ is constant on $(0,1-y)$, hence on $(0,1)$, and the constants agree across outcomes: $xs'(x,\omega)=-a$. Integrate. $a<0$ is improper by Gibbs' inequality. $\square$ **[proved, differentiable case; known: Shuford, Albert and Massengill 1966 (differentiable "actual-cell" rules, [A]/[S]); Bernardo 1979 as stated by Gneiting and Raftery 2007 ([Q]: "assuming regularity conditions … every proper local scoring rule is equivalent to the logarithmic score"); McCutcheon 2019 removes differentiability ([A]); for densities Parry, Dawid and Lauritzen 2012 ([A]: the log score is the only rule depending on the density only through its value at the outcome)]**

Higher-order local rules (Hyvärinen-type, depending on derivatives of the density at the outcome; Parry–Dawid–Lauritzen 2012, Ehm–Gneiting 2012, [A]) exist, but they too ignore the forecast's mass away from an infinitesimal neighbourhood of the outcome, so they do not address the "five scattered values" problem. They do not matter here.

**Distance-sensitive proper scores** (losses): $\mathrm{CRPS}(F,x)=\int(F(t)-1\{t\ge x\})^2dt=\mathbb E|X-x|-\frac12\mathbb E|X-X'|$, the Brier score of the event $\{X\le t\}$ integrated over thresholds (Matheson and Winkler 1976, Gneiting and Raftery 2007; strictly proper relative to laws with finite first moment, [Q]); the energy score $\mathrm{ES}_b(P,x)=\mathbb E|X-x|^b-\frac12\mathbb E|X-X'|^b$, $0<b<2$ (strictly proper relative to laws with finite $b$-th moment, [S]; $b=1$ is the CRPS); and CRIGN of T1.5(e). **[known]**

**The author's example, exactly** (`p2_scores.out`; truth $x=0$; forecasts uniform on five values containing it):

The log score cannot tell the first three apart; the last row is preferred to the near forecast by the log score and dispreferred by every distance-sensitive score. **[computed]** CRPS by its two formulas agree to 4 decimals.

**Misspecification with a non-degenerate truth** (`p2_scores.out` part C): truth $\frac12N(-1,0.1^2)+\frac12N(1,0.1^2)$, family $N(0,s^2)$. The log-optimal scale is the KL projection, $s=\sqrt{\mathbb EX^2}=1.004988$; the CRPS-optimal scale is $1.202984$. Expected log score $2.05427$ against $2.09580$ bits; expected CRPS $0.60447$ against $0.59725$: each proper score prefers its own projection, so the two rank the two wrong forecasts oppositely. **[computed]** Ranking dependence on the loss under misspecification is known for point forecasts (Patton 2020, [A]); for proper scores no source stating it was seen (refs-report §7), and it is elementary.

## T3. Error densities as purposes

Residuals $e_j:=f(t_j)-q(t_j)$ of a point law $f$; the error model's location is fixed at the law (as for the paper's bands, centred on $f$). In fine interval data the per-datum log loss is the density's log loss minus $\log\Delta$ (cell probability $\approx$ density $\times\Delta$; exactly so in the limit, by T3.2 below).

**Proposition T3.1 (fitted scales rank laws by $L^p$ norms).** With $M_p:=(\frac1n\sum_j|e_j|^p)^{1/p}$, $M_\infty:=\max_j|e_j|$ and $\mathrm e$ Euler's number,
* fixed-scale Gaussian: $\sum_j-\log p_\sigma(e_j)=\sum_je_j^2/(2\sigma^2\ln2)+\frac n2\log(2\pi\sigma^2)$; ML scale $\sigma=M_2$: $\frac n2\log(2\pi\mathrm e M_2^2)$;
* Laplace: $\sum_j|e_j|/(b\ln2)+n\log(2b)$; ML $b=M_1$: $n\log(2\mathrm e M_1)$;
* uniform on $[-\delta,\delta]$: $n\log(2\delta)$ if $M_\infty\le\delta$, else $\infty$ (the tolerance form: compatibility iff the maximum error is within the tolerance, as for the honest law $T^\delta$ in regime G); ML $\delta=M_\infty$: $n\log(2M_\infty)$;
* generalised Gaussian $p_s(z)\propto\exp(-|z/s|^p)$: ML $s^p=pM_p^p$, minimum $n\log\big(2\Gamma(1+\tfrac1p)(p\mathrm e)^{1/p}M_p\big)$; the constant is $2\mathrm e$ at $p=1$, $\sqrt{2\pi\mathrm e}$ at $p=2$ and tends to 2 as $p\to\infty$.

So, within each family, the best-fitted error model ranks laws by RMS, mean absolute, maximum and $L^p$ error respectively: every $L^p$ ranking is a log-loss ranking for some error family.
*Proof.* Differentiate $n\log s+\log\mathrm e\sum_j|e_j/s|^p$ in $s$: $n/s=p\sum_j|e_j|^p/s^{p+1}$; substitute. The uniform case is monotone in $\delta\ge M_\infty$. $\square$ **[proved; computed: `p3_errormodels.out` part A, numerical minimum = closed form to 4 decimals on three samples]** If the error model also has a free location, the rankings are by the corresponding dispersions about the fitted location (standard deviation, mean absolute deviation about the median, half-range) **[proved, same computation]**. That the loss must match the functional reported (squared error for the mean, absolute error for the median) is Gneiting 2011 ([A]/[S]).

**Lemma T3.2 (dithered-cell identity).** Let $e$ be any real random variable, $V$ uniform on $(0,\Delta]$ and $U$ uniform on $[0,\Delta)$, independent of $e$, and $C_U(e)$ the cell of the grid $\{k\Delta-U\}_{k\in\mathbb Z}$ containing $e$. Then
$$H(C_U(e)\mid U)=h(e+V)-\log\Delta\quad\text{(bits)},$$
where $e+V$ has the density $f(y)=P(y-\Delta\le e<y)/\Delta\le1/\Delta$, so $h(e+V)\in[\log\Delta,\infty]$.
*Proof.* Given $U=u$ the cell $[k\Delta-u,(k+1)\Delta-u)$ has probability $p_k(u)=\Delta f((k+1)\Delta-u)$. So $\mathbb E_U\sum_k-p_k\log p_k=\frac1\Delta\int_0^\Delta\sum_k-\Delta f((k+1)\Delta-u)\log(\Delta f((k+1)\Delta-u))\,du=\int_{\mathbb R}-f(y)\log(\Delta f(y))\,dy$ (Tonelli; the terms $-p\log p$ are non-negative), which is $h(e+V)-\log\Delta$. $\square$ **[proved; computed: `p3_errormodels.out` part B, four error laws (uniform, arcsine, three atoms, a two-scale mixture) at seven cell widths, agreement within $3\cdot10^{-4}$ bits]** This is the computation behind entropy-coded dithered quantisation (the line of Ziv and of Zamir and Feder); a search did not find the identity stated in one place, so no attribution is made.

**Theorem T3.3 (SAI's best rate is a smoothed entropy).** In regime R (cells of width $\Delta$ on a circle of circumference $\mathsf R\ge2\varepsilon^*+\Delta$, so that no cell wraps around the band), the rate $r_{\mathcal F}(\Delta)$ of Prop. 9.2(c), the lower bound on $\liminf\frac1n(-\log\nu_{G'}(D_n))$ for every theory machine $G'$ and the almost-sure limit of $L_A(D_n)/n$ when $\mathcal F$ is computable, equals
$$r_{\mathcal F}(\Delta)=h(e+V_\Delta)-\log\Delta .$$
(a) *Coarse regime.* If the support of $e$ lies in an interval of length $\le\Delta$ (in particular if $\Delta\ge2\varepsilon^*$), then $r_{\mathcal F}(\Delta)=\frac1\Delta D(e)$ with $D(e):=\int H_2(F_e(t))\,dt=2\varepsilon c_{\mathcal F}$. $D$ is translation invariant and $D(\lambda e)=|\lambda|D(e)$: a dispersion of the error, distance-sensitive, blind to a constant bias.
(b) *Fine limit.* If $e$ has a bounded density with bounded support, $r_{\mathcal F}(\Delta)=h(e)-\log\Delta+o(1)$ as $\Delta\to0$; $h(e)$ is invariant under every measure-preserving rearrangement of the density, so the fine-limit ranking of laws is distance-blind. If $e$ takes finitely many values, $r_{\mathcal F}(\Delta)=H(e)$ (Shannon entropy) for every $\Delta$ up to the least gap between them: bounded as $\Delta\to0$, so at fine resolution such a law beats every law with a continuous error law, however small.
*Proof.* $r_{\mathcal F}(\Delta)=\mathbb E[-\log\mathcal F(C'_1)]$ is the conditional entropy of the datum's cell given the question (proof of Prop. 9.2(c)). The cell of $q(t_j)=f(t_j)-e_j$ in a uniformly dithered grid has the same conditional law as the cell of $-e_j$ in a uniformly dithered grid (translation by $f(t_j)$ preserves the uniform dither), and $h(-e+V)=h(e+V)$ (reflection and translation); apply T3.2. (a) With the support of $e$ in $[a,b]$, $b-a\le\Delta$: $f(y)=F(y^-)/\Delta$ on $[a,b]$, $1/\Delta$ on $[b,a+\Delta]$, $(1-F((y-\Delta)^-))/\Delta$ on $[a+\Delta,b+\Delta]$; integrating $-f\log f$ gives $\log\Delta+\frac1\Delta\int_a^bH_2(F)$. (b) $h(e+V)\ge h(e+V\mid V)=h(e)$; the density $f*u_\Delta\to f$ almost everywhere (Lebesgue differentiation) and $|g\log g|$ is bounded on the bounded set where these densities live, so $h(e+V_\Delta)\to h(e)$ by dominated convergence. With atoms $p_i$ at least $\Delta$ apart, $f=p_i/\Delta$ on disjoint intervals of length $\Delta$, so $h=H+\log\Delta$. $\square$ **[proved; computed: `p3_errormodels.out` part B (the coarse column equals $D(e)/\Delta$; the per-straddle constants are $0.7213$ for $|e|$ uniform, as in the paper, and $0.8505$ for the sinusoid, against the paper's empirical Gibbs bound $0.8510$ on a test half)]**

**Histogram (nonparametric) error models.** The $B$-bin histogram machine (the paper's SH, $B=40$) has, as $\Delta\to0$, rate $\to$ its held-out cross-entropy $H(\text{bins})+\log(w/\Delta)$, $w$ the bin width, which is unchanged when the bins are permuted: distance-blind beyond the bin width. Computed (`p3_errormodels.out` part C): permuting the 40 bins of an error histogram changes RMS from $0.724$ to $0.983$ and the mean $|e|$ from $0.644$ to $0.850$, leaves $h$ at $1.2988$ bits, changes the rate by $0.003$ bits at $\Delta=w/64$ and $0.025$ at $w/8$ (smoothing at the bin edges), and by $0.38$ and $0.19$ bits at $\Delta=1$ and in the coarse regime. **[computed]**

**Relation to the paper.** Prop. 9.2(c)'s rate is the smoothed entropy T3.3, and Prop. 9.2(a)'s $c_{\mathcal G}$ is a threshold-integrated log score (T1.5(e)). So SAI's pricing of approximate laws is distance-sensitive up to the cell width $\Delta$ (a dispersion, in the coarse regime) and distance-blind below the resolution at which the error law is resolved (an entropy, in the fine limit). Prop. 9.2(b) (the calibrated $\mathcal G=\mathcal F$ is optimal) holds for every proper score, not only for log loss (T6, correction (ii)).

## T5. Purpose losses with universal weights

**The aggregating algorithm (AA).** A game $(\Omega,\Gamma,\lambda)$ is $\eta$-mixable if for every probability $\pi$ on $\Gamma$ there is $\gamma\in\Gamma$ with $\lambda(\gamma,\omega)\le-\frac1\eta\ln\int e^{-\eta\lambda(g,\omega)}\pi(dg)$ for all $\omega$. **Theorem T5.0.** For an $\eta$-mixable game and a countable pool of experts with weights $w_i>0$, $\sum_iw_i\le1$, the AA (predict a $\gamma$ given by mixability for the current weights $\propto w_ie^{-\eta L_{t-1}(i)}$) satisfies, on every outcome sequence and for every $n$ and $i$, $L_n(\mathrm{AA})\le-\frac1\eta\ln\sum_iw_ie^{-\eta L_n(i)}\le L_n(i)+\frac{\ln(1/w_i)}\eta$. *Proof.* Mixability at step $t$ gives $e^{-\eta\lambda(\gamma_t,\omega_t)}\ge\sum_iw_ie^{-\eta L_t(i)}/\sum_iw_ie^{-\eta L_{t-1}(i)}$; multiply over $t$ and use $\sum_iw_i\le1$. $\square$ **[known: Vovk 1990, 1998, 2001 (Lemma 1 there in prior form, [S]); proof above]** With $w_i=2^{-K(i)}$ the regret against expert $i$ is at most $K(i)\ln2/\eta$.

**Constants.** Square loss with outcomes in $[-B,B]$ is $\eta$-mixable exactly for $\eta\le1/(2B^2)$ (the literature's $\eta=2$ on $[0,1]$, rescaled; refs-report §4, proved there by rescaling). **[known; computed: `p5_aggregating.out` part A: for $\eta=c/B^2$ the largest infeasibility margin found over two- and three-point $\pi$ is $\le6\cdot10^{-11}$ at $c=0.5$ (rounding at degenerate $\pi$) and $1.5\cdot10^{-4}$, $2.2\cdot10^{-3}$, $1.2\cdot10^{-2}$ at $c=0.505$, $0.52$, $0.55$; Vovk's substitution $\gamma=(g(-B)-g(B))/(4B)$ works on 2000 random $\pi$ at $c=\frac12$, worst gap $+9\cdot10^{-16}$]** For countable $\pi$ mixability follows from finite truncations: $g_{\pi_N}\to g_\pi$ pointwise by dominated convergence ($e^{-\eta\lambda}\in(0,1]$), and the substitution is continuous in $g(\pm B)$. **[proved]** Absolute loss is not mixable for any $\eta>0$: with $\pi=\frac12(\delta_0+\delta_1)$ on outcomes $\{0,1\}$, a valid $\gamma$ needs $\gamma\le g(0)$ and $1-\gamma\le g(1)$, but $g(0)+g(1)=-\frac2\eta\ln\frac{1+e^{-\eta}}2<1$ (AM-GM, strict). **[proved; computed: e.g. $0.7598$ at $\eta=1$]** For absolute loss the available guarantees have an additive term of order $\sqrt n$ (weak aggregating algorithm, Kalnishkan and Vyugin 2008, [A]; constants not seen, not stated). The CRPS is mixable (V'yugin and Trunov 2019, [A]/[Q]): a distance-sensitive proper score with universal-weight bounds.

**Prior art.** "AA with weights $2^{-K}$ gives Solomonoff-style bounds for mixable losses" is known as *predictive complexity* (Vovk and Watkins 1998; Vovk and Gammerman 1999; Kalnishkan, Vovk and Vyugin 2004; Vovk 2015: for a computable mixable loss a smallest upper semicomputable superloss process exists up to an additive constant, and for log loss it is $-\ln M+O(1)$; [A]/[Q] per refs-report §4). Its class is the enumerable class of upper semicomputable superloss processes, the analogue of semimeasures; total computable predictors are not effectively enumerable, so they cannot be the pool. Only the SAI (theory-machine, answered-data) analogue below is new.

**Definition T5.1 (purpose-SAI on answered data).** Questions $\mathcal Q_t$ are finite sets of pairwise $B$-incompatible answers whose disjunction $B$ proves (Def. 2.10), each answer $a$ carrying a value $m(a)\in[-B,B]$ (e.g. the midpoint of an interval cell); the outcome is $\omega_t=m(a_t)$ for the true answer $a_t$; $\lambda$ is a loss on $\Gamma\times[-B,B]$. For a theory machine $G$ and two-valued answered data $D$, the *answer distribution* of $G$ on $\mathcal Q$ is
$$P_G(a\mid D,\mathcal Q):=\frac{\nu_G(D+(a,1))}{\sum_{a'\in\mathcal Q}\nu_G(D+(a',1))}$$
when the denominator is positive (the events are disjoint by incompatibility; the dropped mass is that of random theories compatible with $D$ that prove no answer), and the *act* $\alpha_\lambda(G;D,\mathcal Q)$ is a Bayes act of $\lambda$ under it (for square loss its mean $\sum_aP_G(a)m(a)$), or a fixed default $\gamma_0$ when the denominator is $0$. *Purpose-SAI* is the AA over all theory machines $G_i$ with these acts and weights $\mathbf m(i):=\sum_{V(p)=i}2^{-|p|}$ ($\sum_i\mathbf m(i)\le\Omega_V\le1$, $\mathbf m(i)\ge2^{-K(i)}$). (Variants: mix the dropped mass with a reference law instead of dropping it; or treat a silent theory as an abstaining "specialist", the setting of Freund, Schapire, Singer and Warmuth 1997, [B]/[A]: specialists that abstain, with a conversion of expert algorithms and loss bounds, which were not seen.)

**Theorem T5.2 (regret of purpose-SAI).** If $\lambda$ is $\eta$-mixable (square loss on $[-B,B]$: $\eta=1/(2B^2)$), then on every sequence of questions and answers, chosen in any way, and for every theory machine $G$ and every $n$: $L_n(\text{purpose-SAI})\le L_n(G)+K(G)\ln2/\eta$, where $L_n(G)$ is the cumulative $\lambda$-loss of $G$'s acts. In particular this holds against every consistent r.e. theory (a deterministic machine) and every band-confined completion $\mathrm{BC}_{\mathcal G}$. *Proof.* T5.0 with the pool of all indices, $w_i=\mathbf m(i)$, and the index $i$ of $G$ with $K(i)=K(G)$. $\square$ **[proved, given T5.0 and the mixability of $\lambda$]** Purpose-SAI is incomputable, like SAI: its acts need $\nu_G$, which is not even lower semicomputable ($M_A(\emptyset)$ is right-c.e. and not left-c.e., Thm. 3.3). Its weights after $n$ data, $\propto\mathbf m(i)e^{-\eta L_n(i)}$, are the general-Bayes (loss-based) belief distributions of Bissiri, Holmes and Walker (2016, [A]/[S]) with loss $\lambda$ and scale $\eta$; for log loss with $\eta=\ln2$ (bits) the AA is Bayes (refs-report §4, [S]). So "the weights are not credences" (the reply) should read: they are loss-based belief distributions, not probabilities that the theory is true.

**Proposition T5.3 (which results of the paper transfer).** Results that are statements about the weights $2^{-K}$ and about the laws $\nu_G$ machine by machine transfer to purpose-SAI as regret bounds, because acts are functions of laws.
(a) *Machine invariance.* For universal machines $V,V'$, $K_V\le K_{V'}+c$, so purpose-SAI with $V$ has regret $\le(K_{V'}(G)+c)\ln2/\eta$. **[proved]**
(b) *Faithful translations and new vocabulary* (Thm. 3.5, Cor. 3.6). Let $\tau$ satisfy (T1), (T2). For a theory machine $G$ over $\mathcal L$ let $\tau G$ emit $\tau\chi$ for each $\chi$ that $G$ emits; for a theory machine $G'$ over $\mathcal L'$ let $\tau^*G'$ emit, on each prefix, every $\varphi$ with $B'\cup A'\vdash\tau\varphi$ (found by search). Then $\nu_{\tau G}(\tau D)=\nu_G(D)$ and $\nu_{\tau^*G'}(D)=\nu_{G'}(\tau D)$ for every $D$, with $K(\tau G),K(\tau^*G')\le K(\cdot)+K(\tau)+c$. Hence purpose-SAI over $\mathcal L'$ on translated questions has regret $\le(K(G)+K(\tau)+c)\ln2/\eta$ against every $\mathcal L$-machine, and conversely. *Proof.* (T1) with $\varphi=\bot$ and (T2) give: $B\cup A$ is inconsistent iff $B'\cup\tau[A]$ is (as $B'\vdash\neg\tau(\bot)$ by (T2) applied to $\neg\bot$); and $B\cup A\vdash\varphi^b$ iff $B'\cup\tau[A]\vdash\tau(\varphi^b)$, with $\tau(\neg\varphi)\leftrightarrow\neg\tau\varphi$. For the pull-back $A^*:=\{\varphi:B'\cup A'\vdash\tau\varphi\}$: if $B'\cup A'$ is inconsistent then $\bot\in A^*$; if $B\cup A^*\vdash\bot$ then by (T1) $B'\cup\tau[A^*]\vdash\tau\bot$ and $\tau[A^*]\subseteq\mathrm{Cn}(B'\cup A')$, so $B'\cup A'$ is inconsistent; and $B\cup A^*\vdash\varphi$ iff $B'\cup A'\vdash\tau\varphi$ by the same two steps. So the events defining the laws coincide tape by tape. $\square$ **[proved; the paper's Thm. 3.5 states the weight version]**
(c) *The Craig collapse, for always-consistent machines.* Call $G$ always consistent if $B\cup A^G_\omega$ is consistent for every $\omega$, and a function machine $F$ always closed-consistent if $f^F_\omega$ is closed and consistent for every $\omega$. Proof search $G\mapsto F_G$ and emission $F\mapsto G_F$ (the paper's Lemma B.2) map these classes into each other with $K(F_G)\le K(G)+c$, $K(G_F)\le K(F)+c$, and preserve laws: $\nu_{F_G}=\nu_G$ and $\nu_{G_F}=\nu_F$ on all data (two- and three-valued). So purpose-SAI over theory machines and purpose-SAI over function machines (acts defined from $\nu_F$ by the same rule) have regret $\le(K+c)\ln2/\eta$ against every always-consistent hypothesis of the other kind. *Proof.* For always-consistent $G$, $f^{F_G}_\omega$ is consistent and closed and decides every sentence as $B\cup A^G_\omega$ does, on every tape (Lemma B.2(i)), so $\{\omega:f^{F_G}_\omega\supseteq D\}=E^G_D$. For always closed-consistent $F$, $A^{G_F}_\omega=\Gamma_{f^F_\omega}$ is consistent with $B$ and decides exactly as $f^F_\omega$ (closedness), so $E^{G_F}_D=\{\omega:f^F_\omega\supseteq D\}$. $\square$ **[proved, given Lemma B.2]** Machines that are inconsistent with positive probability are not covered: Thm. 4.3 relates the *mixtures* only up to constant factors, and a normalised answer distribution can change under such factors. Whether the collapse holds there is open (Q2 below).
(d) What does *not* transfer: everything about $M_A$'s own predictive probabilities (trichotomy, merging, posterior limits), the order-independence of the loss (the AA's loss depends on the order in general), and the two-part-code identity $L_A=\min_G[K(G)-\log\nu_G]\pm c$, of which only the upper bound $L_n\le\min_G[L_n(G)+K(G)\ln2/\eta]$ survives.

**Proposition T5.4 (what purpose-SAI selects in regime R; proof sketch).** In regime R with band-confined completions as experts, the act of $\mathrm{BC}_{\mathcal G}$ for square loss on the value is $f(t)-\mathbb E_{\mathcal G}[\text{error}]$, with expected loss $\mathrm{Var}(e)+(\text{bias of }\mathcal G)^2$, minimised by the calibrated $\mathcal G=\mathcal F$ with loss $\mathrm{Var}(e)$; a deterministic law used as a point predictor has expected loss $\mathbb Ee^2$. By the strong law, as in Thm. G.22(i), the AA weights within a finite pool concentrate on the minimiser of the expected purpose loss when it is unique. So under square loss purpose-SAI ranks calibrated laws by error variance (bias absorbed), under absolute loss by mean absolute deviation about the median, and under SAI's log pricing by $h(e+V_\Delta)$ (T3.3): three different "best approximately true laws". **[proof sketch]**

**Open questions (for the paper's open-question list).**
* **Q1.** Is there an upper semicomputable purpose analogue of $M_A$, a superloss process over theory machines within $O(K(G))$ of every theory machine's $\lambda$-loss, as predictive complexity is for sequence prediction? The consistency requirement is what makes $M_A$ non-lower-semicomputable (Thm. 3.3); whether it blocks the purpose analogue too is open.
* **Q2.** Does the Craig collapse between theories and labellers hold for purpose-SAI beyond always-consistent machines (T5.3(c))?
* **Q3.** (Suggested wording for the paper.) "SAI with a purpose loss: replace cumulative log loss by a loss on answers and the posterior by the aggregating algorithm's weights over theory machines. Which of SAI's properties survive, and is there a semicomputable universal element?"

## T6. What approximate correctness is (assessment)

Statuses in this section: **assessment** (an argued judgement), resting on the results cited.

### T6.1 Two attitudes

* **Credence, for hypotheses that might be exactly true.** Here the local (likelihood) comparison is defensible, and the formal results support it: conditioning is cumulative log loss machine by machine (T1.1); the log score is the only local proper scoring rule on three or more outcomes (T2.1); log loss is the most selective standard (Vovk 2015, [A]: a forecaster optimal under log loss is optimal under every computable proper mixable loss, for binary sequences); and in the well-specified case a Bayes mixture used with Bayes acts serves any bounded purpose loss (Hutter 2003, [A]). For SAI's deterministic, proof-requiring hypotheses the likelihood is 0 or 1, so here locality only says "discard the incompatible", which no purpose disputes. Two qualifications: locality is a substantive premise (accuracy-first epistemology argues for the Brier score: `pettigrew2016accuracy` in the paper's bibliography), and "might be exactly true" is the condition: these results are about optimality, not about ranking non-optimal predictors.
* **Adequacy for a purpose, for theories known to be false.** For Kepler's laws "credence that the law is true" is idle; what is assessed is adequacy for a purpose. This is Parker's view (2009, [A]: fit confirms hypotheses about a model's adequacy for particular purposes, not the model; 2020, [A]: adequacy-for-purpose as distinct from representational accuracy; her formal notions were not seen and none is attributed to her). Our formal rendering, which is ours and not Parker's: a purpose is a question family with a resolution plus a loss on answers, or more generally a decision problem; every decision problem induces a proper scoring rule through its Bayes act (Dawid 2007, [A]), and the log score is the purpose "state your credence" (or "compress"). Credence-like weights for a purpose exist: general-Bayes updating with the purpose loss (Bissiri, Holmes and Walker 2016, [A]/[S]), which is what the aggregating algorithm's weights are (T5.2). Under misspecification even the log-loss purpose may need a tempered learning rate (Grünwald and van Ommen 2017, [A], in their examples).
* **The boundary is not sharp, and that matters for SAI.** The ideal inducer is not misspecified on computable data: SAI's mixture contains the planetary ephemeris, which eventually beats every Kepler-based machine (paper, §G "The Kepler residuals", last sentence). So for SAI's *predictions* approximate correctness is a transient matter, and they serve purposes well through Bayes acts. What is purpose-relative is *theory choice among known-false theories*: which approximate law to accept, teach, use or call "approximately correct", the question of a bounded agent with a restricted pool. That is where the paper's statement was wrong.

So the reply was right in direction and in most details. The four corrections (4b, 4c, 6a, 6b) all point the same way: **the purpose-insensitivity is SAI's own behaviour, not only a risk of choosing log loss**, but it bites on theory choice among approximate laws rather than on the ideal mixture's predictions.

**Possible error in the author's view.** None found with a decent probability of being right. The objection ("some mistakes are much worse than others, even if they look the same on log loss"; grading is set by a purpose) is correct, and T3.3/T4 show that it applies to SAI's own pricing at fine resolution. One refinement, not an error: "they look the same on log loss" holds for a fixed partition of the answers; under SAI's dithered interval data misses are graded up to the cell width (T1.5), though never beyond it and never like squared error. The author's "even just calculating the $L^2$ diff … is often more principled than log loss" is defensible with "often" (it is right when the purpose is the mean); we would add that $L^2$ is a log loss with a Gaussian error model, so the contrast that matters is between purposes, not between $L^2$ and log loss.

### T6.3 The paper's sentence on a canonical metric, and its local notion

The paper (§11, "Approximate truth") says: "A canonical metric for approximate predictions, which we think does not exist, … are open; our notion is local: a question family, a tolerance, a domain."
* **The negative claim stands, and is now supported:** different proper scores rank wrong forecasts differently (T2, T4); the error-size criteria disagree among themselves on the real Kepler fits (max against mean $|e|$ on 204 of 234 pairs); and SAI's log loss itself depends on the resolution of the questions (T3.3, T4). "Metric" is the wrong word, though: many purposes are not metrics (a tolerance is a threshold, decision losses are asymmetric, squared error violates the triangle inequality). Suggested: "a canonical score for approximate predictions".
* **The local notion fits T1:** a question family plus a tolerance is a partition, which is exactly what SAI's log loss can express (T1.4); grading misses requires a purpose loss on the answers, which SAI does not have (T5 is a way to add one). Suggested replacement for the paragraph's first sentence and its last: "Under SAI's pricing an approximately true law is a law with a calibrated error model (Prop. 9.2). This describes the log-loss-best forecast in the mixture, not what approximate correctness is: the ranking of approximate laws it induces depends on the resolution of the questions, and at fine resolution it ranks laws by the entropy of their errors rather than their size, even against pointwise dominance. Approximate correctness is relative to a purpose: a question family and a resolution, which SAI can express, and, where misses must be graded, a loss on the answers, which it cannot. This is a limitation of SAI as a model of theory choice, as of any Bayesian cumulative log loss; we know of no canonical choice of purpose."

### T6.4 The Kepler example under the purpose view

* **Kepler's test is already in the paper, as compatibility.** In the noise-free regime of §10 a form is compatible with the Mars data at tolerance $\delta$ iff its minimax error is at most $\delta$: the proof-requiring posterior applies exactly a maximum-error tolerance, Kepler's kind of test. Log loss and text length enter only among compatible forms.
* **On the Mars pool the purposes agree.** Minimax errors $640.79'$, $23.05'$, $8.17'$, $1.41'$, $1.07'$ and least-squares RMS errors $454.4'$, $15.81'$, $5.26'$, $0.628'$, $0.446'$ (circle, eccentric, equant, vicarious, ellipse; philosophy track c4, c9, [computed there]) give the same order, and so does the graded-fit regime with a Gaussian error model ($\sigma=2'$), which ranks by RMS (T3.1). The errors differ by factors of at least 1.3 between neighbours. So the paper's Mars-pool computations rank the forms as every error-size purpose on daily longitudes does. Fine-resolution calibrated log loss ranks by the entropy of the errors, not their size, and could in principle reorder two forms with close maxima (vicarious against ellipse: ratio 1.32 in maximum, 1.41 in RMS); this was not computed, because the pool's residual series were not saved. **[not computed]**
* **Where purposes diverge: which quantity is asked.** For the vicarious hypothesis the best elements depend on the question family: with longitude-optimal elements its maximum longitude error is $1.41'$ and its distances are off by up to $2.8\%$; requiring distances within $1\%$ raises its least maximum longitude error to $5.12'$, and within $0.2\%$ to $23.7'$; the ellipse serves both ($1.07'$) (philosophy track c10 and §4, [computed there]). So "the vicarious hypothesis is approximately correct" is true for the purpose of longitudes and false for distances, which is the situation Kepler was in. **[assessment, resting on computed results of the philosophy track]**
* **Where purposes diverge on one law: which elements.** For one fixed Kepler ellipse, which fitted elements are "best" depends on the purpose: on the real residuals the max, RMS and mean-error winners differ in most series, SAI's pricing picks yet another fit (often the 3-D least-squares fit) in some, and its choice changes with the cell width in three series (T4(b)).
