## Summary

1. **What SAI's pricing of approximate laws is.** Prop. 9.2 says which law-plus-error-model machine compresses the answers best: the calibrated completion, at rate $r_{\mathcal F}(\Delta)=h(e+V_\Delta)-\log\Delta$, the differential entropy of the error smoothed at the cell width (Thm. T3.3, proved). In the coarse regime this is a dispersion of the error (distance-sensitive, blind to bias); as $\Delta\to0$ it tends to $h(e)-\log\Delta$ for every error law with bounded support (now proved in general, not only for bounded densities), which is rearrangement-invariant. It is a credence and compression ranking, not an account of approximate correctness; the paper's "not a limitation" claim is retracted.
2. **Locality and what the questions can encode (T1).** Machine by machine, SAI's loss on $D$ depends only on $\nu_G(D)$ (semimeasure and three-valued conventions; not the normalised one). For questions about one value the loss is the log score of the cell the answers pick out, so the questions encode a resolution, not a grading of misses (proved). Two refinements: (i) under log loss *the forecaster's own error model* grades misses, without bound (a Gaussian error model makes a miss of $y$ cost about $y^2/(2\sigma^2\ln2)$ bits even on a fixed partition), so the price of a miss is chosen by each hypothesis, not by a purpose; (ii) a randomised question design over many values (regime R's dither) grades near-point misses in expectation by the bounded metric $\min(\lvert x-y\rvert/\Delta,1)$, up to one cell width. The reply's "partition, not metric" stands in its stated scope; the developer's label "refuted" is withdrawn.
3. **Scores (T2).** The log score is the only local proper scoring rule on three or more outcomes (proved in the differentiable case; known in general). The author's example: equal log scores ($\log_25$), CRPS $0.4$ against $4.0$. "Sensitivity to distance" is a classical property of scoring rules (Epstein 1969, Staël von Holstein 1970), and its value is contested (Wheatcroft 2021).
4. **Rival approximate laws are ranked differently by different proper scores even without misspecification (T4).** Two laws, each with its calibrated error model, are ideal forecasts on non-nested information; the log score and the CRPS rank them oppositely (explicit model, proved and computed). The calibrated completion's price can even rank a law above one that is closer to the truth at every time (dominance reversal, cell widths below $1.4296$). These are prices of particular machines, not SAI's own rate, which can be much lower (0 in the toy, where the two laws together determine the truth).
5. **Real Kepler residuals (T4(b)).** The classical criteria disagree with each other (maximum against mean absolute error on 204 of 234 fit pairs); SAI's calibrated pricing disagrees with each of them; its ranking of fits reverses with the cell width in 11 to 16 of 234 pairs depending on estimator and split, with relative effects mostly between 0.4% and 6.8% of the rate (every decided difference at least 1% in 7 of the 14 reversals found by the exact rate); its significantly best fit is occasionally none of the classical winners, always in a near-tie (relative differences 0.01% to 0.1%) between two unbiased least-squares fits. In the coarse regime it is close to a standard-deviation ranking. Computed with three independent estimators.
6. **Purpose losses with universal weights (T5).** Known as predictive complexity (Vovk and coauthors). New here: purpose-SAI on answered data, its regret bound $K(G)\ln2/\eta$ ($2Y^2K(G)\ln2$ for square loss on $[-Y,Y]$, informative only on a range comparable to the errors), and which of the paper's results transfer (machine invariance, translations, the Craig collapse for always-consistent machines).
7. **Assessment (T6).** Approximate correctness, as adequacy for a purpose, is purpose-relative beyond a purpose-neutral core (the dominance order on a fixed question family). The limitation is of SAI's posterior pricing read as an account of approximate correctness, and of any account that ranks approximate laws by posterior weight or code length; it is not a limitation of Bayesian inference as such, since a decision layer (posterior plus purpose loss) ranks laws by purpose when the posterior predicts well, and loss-based updating does so when the pool is restricted. Threshold purposes (good enough at a tolerance on a domain) are sentences in which SAI already holds credences; graded purposes are not. No probable error in the author's objection was found; three statements in Claude's reply are now judged probably wrong as stated (section "Probably wrong").

## T1. Locality inside SAI

**Lemma T1.0 (posterior weight of a program).** For $p\in\mathrm{dom}\,V$ and a two-valued $D$, $\mathrm{Unif}(E_D\cap[p])=2^{-\lvert p\rvert}\nu_{G_{V(p)}}(D)$. *Proof.* On a tape $p\omega'$ the universal machine emits $A^{G_{V(p)}}_{\omega'}$ (setting.tex, after Def. 2.4), so $E_D\cap[p]=\{p\omega':\omega'\in E^{G_{V(p)}}_D\}$, of measure $2^{-\lvert p\rvert}\mathrm{Unif}(E^{G_{V(p)}}_D)$. $\square$ **[proved]**

**Proposition T1.1 (locality in the semimeasure and three-valued conventions).** Let $G$ be a theory machine and $D$ a finite consistent two-valued data set with $\nu_G(D)>0$. Define $G$'s per-step loss along a reveal sequence by analogy with the paper's Def. 2.5 (the semimeasure conditional $\nu_G(D_t)/\nu_G(D_{t-1})$, and $1$ on a repeat).
(a) The posterior probability of the program $p$ after $D$ is $2^{-\lvert p\rvert}\nu_{G_{V(p)}}(D)/M_A(D)$, and the two-part score of §9 is $K(G)-\log\nu_G(D)$: both depend on the machine only through $\lvert p\rvert$ (resp. $K(G)$) and the number $\nu_G(D)$.
(b) For every consistent reveal sequence $(\varphi_t,y_t)_{t\le n}$ with $D_n=D$, in any order and with repeats, the cumulative loss is $-\log\nu_G(D)+\log\nu_G(\emptyset)$.
(c) The same holds for three-valued $D$ with $\nu^3_G$, and $\nu^3_G(\emptyset)=\nu_G(\emptyset)$.
(d) Two machines with equal $K$, $\nu(\emptyset)$ and $\nu(D)$ get the same two-part score and the same cumulative loss on $D$ in every order, whatever they do on other data.
*Proof.* (a) Lemma T1.0. (b) $\nu_G$ is monotone ($D'\subseteq D$ gives $E^G_{D'}\supseteq E^G_D$), so every $\nu_G(D_{t-1})\ge\nu_G(D)>0$; the conditional of a new datum is $\nu_G(D_t)/\nu_G(D_{t-1})$ (Def. 2.1), that of a repeat is $1$; the product telescopes. (c) Same argument; Prop. 3.1(f). (d) From (a)-(c). $\square$ **[proved; computed: `p1_locality.out` A, C; `rb1_locality.out` A (121 two-valued instances, 487 orders with repeats, 117 three-valued instances, deviation $\le4.4\cdot10^{-16}$ bits)]**

For $G=G_U$ this is Prop. 3.1(d); the content is that the scoring of each hypothesis, not only of the mixture, is local.

**Proposition T1.2 (the normalised convention is not local).** With the normalised conditional, the cumulative loss along a reveal sequence without repeats is $-\log\nu_G(D_n)+\log\nu_G(\emptyset)+\sum_t\log(1-I_t)$, $I_t:=\nu^3_G(D_{t-1}+(\varphi_t,\iota))/\nu_G(D_{t-1})$ the independent mass at step $t$; it depends on mass outside $E^G_D$ and on the order. *Proof.* Prop. 3.1(b) gives the normalising denominator $1-I_t$. $\square$ **[proved; computed: `p1_locality.out` B (normalised losses 0 and 1 bit for two machines with equal $\nu(D)$; 2.000 against 1.585 bits in two orders); `rb1_locality.out` B (formula to $8.9\cdot10^{-16}$ on 1137 instances, order spread up to 2.46 bits)]** The consistency-requiring predictor (Dempster's rule) is order-dependent too (paper Prop. A.21).

**Proposition T1.3 (chain rule for questions about one value).** Let $B\supseteq\mathrm{RCF}$ have a constant $q$, $\sigma_C$ the sentence "$q\in C$" for a finite union $C$ of rational intervals, $D$ a two-valued data set of such sentences, and $C_D$ the Boolean combination they determine. Then $B\vdash\psi_D\leftrightarrow\sigma_{C_D}$, so $\nu_G(D)=\beta_G(C_D)$, the probability that $G$'s random theory is consistent and proves $q\in C_D$, and if $\nu_G(D)>0$ the cumulative semimeasure loss on any reveal sequence of $D$ is $-\log\beta_G(C_D)+\log\nu_G(\emptyset)$ (three-valued: $-\log\nu^3_G(D)+\log\nu_G(\emptyset)$, again one event). *Proof.* RCF proves the Boolean identity; $\nu_G$ depends on $D$ only through $\mathrm{Cn}(D^\pm)$ (Prop. 3.1(c)); T1.1. $\square$ **[proved; computed: `rb1_locality.out` C, 2000 instances with arbitrary sentences]**

**Corollary T1.4 (a family of questions about one value encodes a partition).** For a finite family $\mathcal Q$ of interval questions about $q$, all answered, the answers determine the atom $C(x)$ of the true value $x$ in the partition $\Pi_{\mathcal Q}$ generated by the intervals, and the loss is $-\log\beta_G(C(x))+\log\nu_G(\emptyset)$, where $\beta_G$ is a sub-probability on $\Pi_{\mathcal Q}$ (distinct atoms are $B$-incompatible). So the loss is the log score of $\beta_G$ at the true atom: two machines that give the true atom the same mass pay the same, wherever the rest of their mass lies. **[proved]** This holds for every realised set of questions about one value, whether the questions were fixed in advance or drawn at random (a drawn family, once drawn, is a fixed family).

**Proposition T1.5 (randomised questions over many values).** Let $\mu$ be a law on finite partitions $\Pi$ of the value range, drawn independently for each datum, and for a cell function $\beta$ with $\sum_{C\in\Pi}\beta(C)\le1$ for every $\Pi$ (a sub-probability, or the superadditive cell function $\beta_G$ of a machine that is silent inside a band, such as the honest law $T^\varepsilon$; referee A, m13) put $S_\mu(\beta,x):=\mathbb E_{\Pi\sim\mu}[-\log\beta(\Pi(x))]$. Over many values with independent questions, the average loss per datum converges to the expectation of $S_\mu$ (strong law).
(a) $S_\mu$ is proper: $\mathbb E_{x\sim Q}S_\mu(\beta,x)\ge\mathbb E_{x\sim Q}S_\mu(Q,x)$ for every probability $Q$.
(b) $S_\mu(\beta,x)$ depends on $\beta$ only through cells within $N_\mu(x)=\bigcup_{\Pi\in\mathrm{supp}\,\mu}\Pi(x)$; for the dithered grid $N(x)\subseteq(x-\Delta,x+\Delta)$.
(c) $\sigma_\mu(x,y):=\mu\{\Pi(x)\ne\Pi(y)\}$ is a $[0,1]$-valued pseudometric and a mixture of cut semimetrics, and every finite mixture of cut semimetrics with total weight $\le1$ arises; for the dithered grid $\sigma(x,y)=\min(\lvert x-y\rvert/\Delta,1)$, a bounded metric.
(d) For near-point forecasts $P_{y,s}=(1-s)\delta_y+sU$: $S_\mu(P_{y,s},x)=\sigma_\mu(x,y)\log(1/s)+\mathbb E[1\{\Pi(x)\ne\Pi(y)\}\log(1/U(\Pi(x)))]+O(s)$. For the dithered grid and $U$ uniform on a circle of length $\mathsf R_c$ this is exactly $\sigma\log(\mathsf R_c/(s\Delta))+(1-\sigma)(-\log(1-s+s\Delta/\mathsf R_c))$. So randomised questions grade *near-point* misses by the bounded metric $\sigma$, constant beyond one cell width: never by squared error or any unbounded cost, for near-point forecasts (for other forecasts see T1.6).
(e) For the dithered grid and $\beta$ additive with total mass $m\le1$, supported in an interval $J\ni x$ with $\lvert J\rvert\le\Delta$: $S_\mu(\beta,x)=\frac1\Delta\int_J-\log\big(\beta(X\ge t)\text{ if }x\ge t,\ \beta(X<t)\text{ otherwise}\big)dt+(1-\lvert J\rvert/\Delta)(-\log m)$. For $m=1$ this is $\mathrm{CRIGN}(\beta,x)/\Delta$, the threshold-integrated log score, and with $\beta$ the law of $\mathrm{BC}_{\mathcal G}$ on the band it is exactly the coarse-regime rate $(2\varepsilon/\Delta)c_{\mathcal G}$ of Prop. 9.2(a).
*Proof.* (a) For each $\Pi$, $\sum_CQ(C)(-\log\beta(C))\ge\sum_CQ(C)(-\log Q(C))$ by Gibbs' inequality, which needs only $\sum_C\beta(C)\le1$; average over $\mu$ (Tonelli). (b) $\Pi(x)\subseteq N_\mu(x)$ almost surely. (c) Symmetry, $\sigma(x,x)=0$, and $\{\Pi(x)\ne\Pi(z)\}\subseteq\{\Pi(x)\ne\Pi(y)\}\cup\{\Pi(y)\ne\Pi(z)\}$; $1\{\Pi(x)\ne\Pi(y)\}=\frac12\sum_i\delta_{C_i}(x,y)$; conversely ask "$q\in S_k$?" with probability $\lambda_k$; in the dithered grid $x<y$ are separated iff a grid point falls in $(x,y]$. (d) If $\Pi(x)\ne\Pi(y)$, $P_{y,s}(\Pi(x))=sU(\Pi(x))$; otherwise it is in $[1-s,1]$. (e) A grid point falls in $J$ with probability $\lvert J\rvert/\Delta$, uniformly; the cell of $x$ then meets $J$ in $J\cap[t,\infty)$ or $J\cap(-\infty,t)$; with no grid point in $J$ the cell contains $J$ and has mass $m$. $\square$ **[proved; computed: `p1_locality.out` D, E; `rb1_locality.out` D1-D3 (separation probability to $2.8\cdot10^{-17}$; CRIGN identity on 300 forecasts to $5.6\cdot10^{-16}$); `p6_revision.out` A (the exact decomposition of (d) against quadrature over the dither, 8 distances), F (formula (e) with $m<1$ against the exact dither integral on 200 random forecasts, largest difference $8.9\cdot10^{-16}$ bits)]**

The ratio $S_\mu/\log(1/s)$ converges to $\sigma$ very slowly ($0.306$ at $s=10^{-8}$ for $\sigma=0.25$, `rb1_locality.out` D3); the paper should quote the decomposition, not the limit.

*The author's example under fixed and randomised cells* (`p6_revision.out` A; truth 0; uniform on $\{-2,\dots,2\}$ against uniform on $\{-20,-10,0,10,20\}$). At cell width $\Delta\le1$ both cost $\log_25=2.3219$ bits. At $\Delta=2$ the near forecast costs $1.3219$ on every grid; at $\Delta=3$, $0.7370$ on every grid; at $\Delta=5$ the dithered cost is $0.4236$, the average of fixed-grid costs between $0$ (cell $[-2.5,2.5)$) and $0.7370$ (worst offset), while the scattered forecast costs $2.3219$ on every grid. So at every cell width of 5 or more **every fixed grid** ranks the near forecast better (also at 2 and 3): a fixed family encodes "good enough at resolution $\Delta$", and that is what separates the two forecasts here; the dither only averages over offsets (referee B, M1). **[computed]**

**Proposition T1.6 (the price of a miss is set by the forecaster's own error model).** For any machine and any question family, a datum costs $-\log$ of the machine's mass on the true answer (T1.1, T1.4). How that cost grows with the size of a miss is therefore fixed by the machine's own forecast: for a forecast $N(y,\sigma^2)$ and a fixed cell $C\ni0$ of width $\Delta$, $-\log P(C)=y^2/(2\sigma^2\ln2)+O(\lvert y\rvert)$ as $\lvert y\rvert\to\infty$; for a Laplace forecast with scale $b$, $\lvert y\rvert/(b\ln2)+O(1)$; for a forecast with bounded support, a constant (or infinity) beyond the support. The same holds on the dithered grid (average over offsets). *Proof.* For $C=[a,a+\Delta)$ and $y\to+\infty$, $P(C)=\Phi((a+\Delta-y)/\sigma)-\Phi((a-y)/\sigma)$, and the Mills-ratio asymptotics $\ln\Phi(-z)=-z^2/2-\ln z+O(1)$ give $-\ln P(C)=(y-a-\Delta)^2/(2\sigma^2)+O(\ln y)$; the Laplace case is explicit ($P(C)=\frac12e^{-(y-a-\Delta)/b}(1-e^{-\Delta/b})$ for $y\ge a+\Delta$). $\square$ **[proved; computed: `p6_revision.out` B (Gaussian, $\Delta=0.1$: extra cost $25.947$ bits at $y=6$ against $y^2/(2\ln2)=25.969$, on the fixed grid and the dithered grid alike; $\Delta=4$: $14.88$ fixed, $15.52$ dithered, still quadratic; Laplace: $14.38$ against $\lvert y\rvert/\ln2=14.43$ at $y=10$, $\Delta=0.1$); `rb1_locality.out` D5]**

So under log loss misses *are* graded, without bound, but by each hypothesis's error model, and the hypothesis that compresses best wins (T3.3). What no question family can do is impose a grading fixed by a purpose: the family sets a resolution, and, randomised over many values, a bounded smoothing up to one cell width. The author's "they look the same on log loss" is exactly right for forecasts that put the same mass on the true answer, as in the author's example; the precise general statement is "the weight of a miss is set by the forecaster, not by the purpose" (referee B, M2).

**Outcome space for T2.1** (referee A, m14). In the two-valued conventions each yes/no datum has two outcomes, where every scoring rule is local, so the uniqueness of the log score (T2.1) says nothing per datum; it applies to questions with three or more answers (paper Def. 2.10; regime R's cells), to three-valued data, and to the joint answer list of several yes/no questions.

## T2. Local and distance-sensitive scores

All scores are losses (lower is better); Gneiting and Raftery (2007) use the positive orientation.

**Theorem T2.1 (local proper scoring rules).** Let $\Omega$ be finite with $m\ge3$ outcomes and $S(p,\omega)=s(p(\omega),\omega)$ a local scoring rule with $s(\cdot,\omega)$ differentiable on $(0,1)$. If $S$ is proper on the interior of the simplex, then $s(x,\omega)=-a\ln x+b(\omega)$ on $(0,1)$ with $a\ge0$ (strictly proper iff $a>0$). For $m=2$ every rule is local, so the Brier score is a local proper rule that is not logarithmic.
*Proof (differentiable case).* At an interior minimiser $q=p$ of $q\mapsto\sum_\omega p(\omega)s(q(\omega),\omega)$ the gradient is a multiple of $(1,\dots,1)$: $p(\omega)s'(p(\omega),\omega)=\lambda(p)$ for all $\omega$. For $\omega_1\ne\omega_2$ and $x,y>0$, $x+y<1$, some interior $p$ has $p(\omega_1)=x$, $p(\omega_2)=y$ (spread $1-x-y$ over the other $m-2\ge1$ outcomes), so $xs'(x,\omega_1)=ys'(y,\omega_2)$; hence $xs'(x,\omega)=-a$ for all $x$ and $\omega$. Integrate; $a<0$ is improper by Gibbs' inequality. $\square$ **[proved, differentiable case; computed: `rb2_scores.out` D (the local Brier rule, a linear rule and a kinked log rule on three outcomes are improper; the log rule passes); known: Shuford, Albert and Massengill 1966 (differentiable rules, [A]/[S]); Bernardo 1979 ([S]: stated by later work as "under regularity conditions the log score is, up to equivalence, the only proper local score", for at least three outcomes; the exact hypotheses were not seen); McCutcheon 2019 removes differentiability ([A]); for densities Parry, Dawid and Lauritzen 2012 ([A])]**

Higher-order local rules (Hyvärinen-type; Parry, Dawid and Lauritzen 2012, Ehm and Gneiting 2012, [A]) also ignore mass away from an infinitesimal neighbourhood of the outcome and do not bear on the author's example.

**Distance-sensitive proper scores** (losses): $\mathrm{CRPS}(F,x)=\int(F(t)-1\{t\ge x\})^2dt=\mathbb E\lvert X-x\rvert-\frac12\mathbb E\lvert X-X'\rvert$, the Brier score of $\{X\le t\}$ integrated over thresholds (Matheson and Winkler 1976; Gneiting and Raftery 2007; strictly proper relative to laws with finite first moment, [Q]); the energy score, $0<b<2$ ([S]); the threshold-weighted CRPS, which weights thresholds by a purpose (Gneiting and Ranjan 2011, [B]/[A]); and CRIGN (T1.5(e)). **[known]**

**The objection restates a known property, which is contested.** "Uniform over five nearby values beats uniform over five scattered values" is the property of being *sensitive to distance*: Epstein's ranked probability score (1969, [B]/[S]), Staël von Holstein's strictly proper distance-sensitive families (1970, [B]/[S]: the definition via cumulative probabilities), and Jose, Nau and Winkler (2009, [B]/[A]: power and pseudospherical families made sensitive to distance). The opposite view is argued by Wheatcroft (2021, [B]/[A]): for football forecasts sensitivity to distance adds nothing to the usual aims of scoring, and the ignorance (log) score did best at identifying the better forecaster in his simulations. Both are about choosing among forecasters; neither settles which purpose a theory should serve. **[known; assessment]**

## T3. Error densities as purposes

Residuals $e_j=f(t_j)-q(t_j)$; the error model's location is fixed at the law.

**Proposition T3.1 (fitted scales rank laws by $L^p$ norms).** With $M_p:=(\frac1n\sum_j\lvert e_j\rvert^p)^{1/p}$, $M_\infty=\max_j\lvert e_j\rvert$ and $\mathrm e$ Euler's number, the minimal total log loss (bits, density version) is $\frac n2\log(2\pi\mathrm eM_2^2)$ for the Gaussian with ML scale $\sigma=M_2$; $n\log(2\mathrm eM_1)$ for the Laplace ($b=M_1$); $n\log(2M_\infty)$ for the uniform on $[-\delta,\delta]$ ($\delta=M_\infty$; compatibility iff the maximum error is within the tolerance, the honest law of regime G); and $n\log\big(2\Gamma(1+\tfrac1p)(p\mathrm e)^{1/p}M_p\big)$ for the generalised Gaussian $\propto\exp(-\lvert z/s\rvert^p)$ ($s^p=pM_p^p$). So within each family the best-fitted error model ranks laws by RMS, mean absolute, maximum and $L^p$ error: every $L^p$ ranking is a log-loss ranking for some error family. With a free location, the rankings are by the corresponding dispersions about the fitted location. *Proof.* Minimise $n\log s+\log\mathrm e\sum_j\lvert e_j/s\rvert^p$ in $s$; the uniform case is monotone in $\delta\ge M_\infty$. $\square$ **[proved; computed: `p3_errormodels.out` A; `rb3_errormodels.out` A (3000 random pairs per family)]** That the loss must match the functional (squared error for the mean, absolute error for the median) is Gneiting 2011 ([A]/[S]).

**Lemma T3.2 (dithered-cell identity).** For any real random variable $e$, $V$ uniform on $(0,\Delta]$ and $U$ uniform on $[0,\Delta)$ independent of $e$, and $C_U(e)$ the cell of the grid $\{k\Delta-U\}$ containing $e$: $H(C_U(e)\mid U)=h(e+V)-\log\Delta$ (bits), where $e+V$ has density $f(y)=P(y-\Delta\le e<y)/\Delta\le1/\Delta$, so $h(e+V)\in[\log\Delta,\infty]$. *Proof.* Given $U=u$, cell $k$ has probability $\Delta f((k+1)\Delta-u)$; average $\sum_k-p_k\log p_k$ over $u$ and change variables (Tonelli). $\square$ **[proved; computed: `p3_errormodels.out` B (four laws, seven widths, within $3\cdot10^{-4}$ bits); `rb3_errormodels.out` B (triangular, beta, atom plus uniform, Cantor, within 2.2 standard errors on 24 cells)]** **Known** as the rate of entropy-coded subtractively dithered quantisation, $H(Q(X+Z)\mid Z)=I(X;X+N)=h(X+N)-h(N)$ with $N$ uniform on a cell ([S]: stated with a reference to earlier work in arXiv:0708.1859 and in arXiv:2108.12618; it is usually credited to Zamir and Feder, "On universal quantization by randomized uniform/lattice quantizers", IEEE Trans. Inf. Theory 38(2):428-436, 1992, whose bibliographic data I saw only in citing works and whose statement I did not see). Our proof is elementary; what is new here is only its reading as SAI's rate (T3.3).

**Theorem T3.3 (SAI's best rate in regime R is a smoothed entropy).** In regime R (cells of width $\Delta$ on a circle of circumference $\mathsf R\ge2\varepsilon^*+\Delta$), the rate of Prop. 9.2(c), which is the almost-sure lower bound on $\liminf\frac1n(-\log\nu_{G'}(D_n))$ for every theory machine $G'$ and the limit of $L_A(D_n)/n$ when $\mathcal F$ is computable, is
$$r_{\mathcal F}(\Delta)=h(e+V_\Delta)-\log\Delta .$$
(a) *Coarse regime.* If the support of $e$ lies in an interval of length $\le\Delta$, then $r_{\mathcal F}(\Delta)=D(e)/\Delta$ with $D(e):=\int H_2(F_e(t))\,dt=2\varepsilon c_{\mathcal F}$; $D$ is translation invariant and $D(\lambda e)=\lvert\lambda\rvert D(e)$: a dispersion, distance-sensitive, blind to a constant bias.
(b) *Fine limit, every error law with bounded support* (referee A, m1). As $\Delta\to0$, $r_{\mathcal F}(\Delta)-\log(1/\Delta)\to h(e)\in[-\infty,\infty)$, with $h(e):=-\infty$ if $e$ has no density. So if $e$ has a density with $h(e)>-\infty$, $r_{\mathcal F}(\Delta)=h(e)-\log\Delta+o(1)$; $h$ is invariant under measure-preserving rearrangements of the density and scales as $h(\lambda e)=h(e)+\log\lvert\lambda\rvert$: the fine-limit ranking is *rearrangement-invariant*, sensitive to the volume the errors occupy and blind to where it lies (referee A, m2).
(c) *Atoms.* If $e$ takes finitely many values, $r_{\mathcal F}(\Delta)=H(e)$ for every $\Delta$ up to the least gap between them (and below $H(e)$ above it). If the law of $e$ is not purely atomic, $r_{\mathcal F}(\Delta)\to\infty$ as $\Delta\to0$. So at fine enough resolution a law whose error takes finitely many values beats every law whose error law has a non-atomic part, however small its errors (referee B, m11: "non-atomic", not "continuous").
*Proof.* $r_{\mathcal F}(\Delta)=\mathbb E[-\log\mathcal F(C'_1)]$ is the conditional entropy of the answer given the question (proof of Prop. 9.2(c)); translation by $f(t_j)$ preserves the uniform dither and $h(-e+V)=h(e+V)$; apply T3.2. (a) With the support in $[a,b]$, $b-a\le\Delta$, the density of $e+V$ is $F(y^-)/\Delta$ on $[a,b]$, $1/\Delta$ on $[b,a+\Delta]$ and $(1-F((y-\Delta)^-))/\Delta$ on $[a+\Delta,b+\Delta]$; integrate. (b) *Lower bound*: if $e$ has a density, $h(e+V)\ge h(e+V\mid V)=h(e)$; otherwise there is nothing to prove. *Upper bound*: fix a compact interval $I$ of length $\ell$ containing the supports of $e+V_\Delta$ for all $\Delta\le1$, and $Q$ the uniform law on $I$. For a law $P$ on $I$, $h(P)=\log\ell-D(P\Vert Q)$, with $D=\infty$ when $P$ has no density. As $\Delta\to0$, $e+V_\Delta\to e$ weakly, and relative entropy is lower semicontinuous under weak convergence (Donsker-Varadhan: $D(P\Vert Q)=\sup_g\{\mathbb E_Pg-\log\mathbb E_Qe^g\}$ over bounded continuous $g$, a supremum of continuous functions of $P$). So $\limsup h(e+V_\Delta)=\log\ell-\liminf D(e+V_\Delta\Vert Q)\le\log\ell-D(e\Vert Q)=h(e)$. (c) With atoms $p_i$ at least $\Delta$ apart the density of $e+V$ is $p_i/\Delta$ on disjoint intervals of length $\Delta$, so $h=H+\log\Delta$. If the law has a non-atomic part of mass $w>0$, let $Z$ indicate the part; then $H(C_U(e)\mid U)\ge H(C_U(e)\mid U,Z)\ge w\,\mathbb E_U[-\log\max_k p^{c}_k(U)]\ge w\log(1/Q_c(\Delta))$, where $p^c_k$ are the cell probabilities of the normalised non-atomic part and $Q_c(\Delta)=\sup_aP_c([a,a+\Delta))\to0$ (the concentration function of a non-atomic law tends to 0). $\square$ **[proved; computed: `p3_errormodels.out` B; `p6_revision.out` C: arcsine law (unbounded density, $h=\log_2(\pi/2)=0.651496$): $r-\log_2(1/\Delta)-h=0.8542$, $0.1935$, $0.0274$, $8.66\cdot10^{-4}$ at $\Delta=1$, $0.05$, $10^{-3}$, $10^{-6}$ (of the range 2), decaying as $0.866\sqrt\Delta$; Cantor law (singular): $r\ge-\log_2Q(\Delta)$ and $r-\log_2(1/\Delta)=0.25,-1.50,-3.84,-5.01$ at $\Delta=3^{-1},3^{-4},3^{-8},3^{-10}$; `rb3_errormodels.out` C, D (atoms: $H$ up to the least gap, $1.231<H$ at $\Delta=1.5$ for gaps 1 and 2; Cantor $2.10,3.57,5.03,6.49$ at $\Delta=0.5,0.1,0.02,0.004$)]**

The convergence in (b) is slow for laws with turning points: for the arcsine law (the paper's sinusoidal error) at $\Delta$ equal to 2.5% of the range the rate is still $0.193$ bits above the fine limit. So statements at a finite cell width should cite T3.3 itself, not its limit (referee A, M6, m1).

**Remark T3.4 (what $r_{\mathcal F}$ is a rate of, for two laws; referee B, M3).** The identification of $r_{\mathcal F}$ with Prop. 9.2(c)'s lower bound and with $\lim L_A/n$ needs regime R's hypothesis that the errors are random and independent of the questions. Two computable laws $f_A,f_B$ for one quantity can both satisfy it only if their errors differ by a constant: $e_A-e_B=f_A(t_j)-f_B(t_j)$ is a computable sequence, and if $e_A$ is i.i.d. with law $F_A$ independent of the questions, the $e_B(t_j)=e_A(t_j)-d_j$ are identically distributed only if $d_j$ is constant (a probability law on a band shorter than half the circle is invariant under no non-zero rotation); and then $r_A=r_B$, since $h$ and $D$ are translation invariant. **[proved]** So any comparison of two laws by their rates $r_{\mathcal F_A}$, $r_{\mathcal F_B}$ concerns data on which at least one of them has non-random errors, for which $r_{\mathcal F}$ is only the rate (price per datum) of that law's calibrated completion $\mathrm{BC}_{\mathcal F}$, and SAI's own rate may be much lower. In the toy of T4(a) the two laws together determine $q$ exactly, so a machine stating both (and the decoding) has rate 0. **[computed: `rb4_toy.out` B(i), 400000 of 400000 data decoded; `p6_revision.out` D]** And if one law's errors are random, that law's calibrated completion is never out-priced by another law's: $r_A$ is then the lower bound for every machine (`rb4_toy.out` B(ii): B's calibrated machine costs $H(d)=0.92$ bits more per datum at every $\Delta$). **[proved (Prop. 9.2(c)); computed]**

**Histogram error models.** The $B$-bin histogram machine (the paper's SH, $B=40$) has, as $\Delta\to0$, rate tending to its held-out cross-entropy $H(\text{bins})+\log(w/\Delta)$, $w$ the bin width, unchanged when the bins are permuted. Permuting the 40 bins of an error histogram changes RMS from $0.724$ to $0.983$ and leaves $h$ at $1.2988$ bits; the rate changes by $0.003$ bits at $\Delta=w/64$ and by $0.38$ bits at $\Delta=1$. **[proved (from T3.3); computed: `p3_errormodels.out` C; `rb3_errormodels.out` E]**

**Relation to the paper.** Prop. 9.2(c)'s rate is the smoothed entropy of T3.3, and Prop. 9.2(a)'s $c_{\mathcal G}$ is a threshold-integrated log score (T1.5(e)). SAI's pricing of approximate laws is therefore distance-sensitive up to the cell width (a dispersion, in the coarse regime) and rearrangement-invariant in the fine limit. Prop. 9.2(b) (the calibrated $\mathcal G=\mathcal F$ is optimal among band-confined completions) holds for every proper score, not only for log loss: the errors are independent of the questions, so the true conditional law of the answer given the question is the cell law of $\mathcal F$, which every proper score rewards most (P28).

## T4. Rival approximate laws: scores rank them differently

### T4(a). An exact toy: ideal forecasts on non-nested information

**The toy** (`checks/p4_toy.py`; `rb4_toy.out` A by simulation on deterministic error sequences). Two laws for one quantity. Law A: errors uniform on $[-1,1]$. Law B: errors $-M,0,M$ with probabilities $\rho/2,1-\rho,\rho/2$, $M=1000$, $\rho=0.2$ (exactly right on 80% of times, off by 1000 otherwise). The prices per datum of the two calibrated completions (Remark T3.4: these are prices of two machines, not SAI's rate) are $r_A(\Delta)=1+\Delta/(4\ln2)-\log\Delta$ for $\Delta\le2$ and $(1/\ln2)/\Delta$ for $\Delta\ge2$; $r_B(\Delta)=H(B)=0.92193$ bits for $\Delta\le M$ and $2MH_2(\rho/2)/\Delta$ for $\Delta\ge2M$.

| | max | RMS | mean $\lvert e\rvert$ | CRPS of the calibrated forecast | fitted Gaussian / Laplace / uniform (+$\log\frac1\Delta$) | price of $\mathrm{BC}_{\mathcal F}$ at $\Delta=0.1$ / $1$ / $2$ / $10$ / $10^4$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 1 | 0.577 | 0.5 | 0.333 | 1.255 / 1.443 / 1.000 | 4.358 / 1.361 / 0.721 / 0.144 / 0.0001 |
| B | 1000 | 447.2 | 200 | 180 | 10.85 / 10.09 / 10.97 | 0.922 / 0.922 / 0.922 / 0.922 / 0.094 |

B's calibrated completion is priced below A's exactly for cell widths below $\Delta_c=1.558542$ (the root of $1+\Delta/(4\ln2)-\log\Delta=H(B)$; no second crossover); every error-size criterion, every fitted parametric error model of T3.1 and the CRPS of the calibrated forecasts prefer A at every resolution. **[proved (closed forms); computed: `p4_toy.out`, `rb4_toy.out` A (simulated to within 0.002 bits)]**

**Proposition T4.1 (both forecasts are ideal for their own information; referee A, M5).** On a circle of circumference $\mathsf R_c\ge4M$ let $Z$ be uniform and independent of $(e_A,e_B)$, with $e_A$, $e_B$ independent and distributed as in the toy; put $f_A:=Z$, $q:=f_A-e_A$, $f_B:=q+e_B$. Then $e_A$ is independent of $f_A$ and $e_B$ of $f_B$, so each calibrated forecast ($f_A-e_A'$, $f_B-e_B'$ with $e'$ an independent copy of the error) is the conditional law of $q$ given that law's own prediction: an ideal (auto-calibrated) forecast relative to the information $\sigma(f_A)$, respectively $\sigma(f_B)$. These information sets are not nested, the pair $(f_A,f_B)$ determines $q$, and the strictly proper log score on dithered cells of width $\Delta<\Delta_c$ and the strictly proper CRPS rank the two ideal forecasts oppositely. *Proof.* $f_B=Z-e_A+e_B$, and $Z$ uniform on the circle and independent of $(e_A,e_B)$ makes $Z+c$ uniform and independent of $(e_A,e_B)$ for any $(e_A,e_B)$-measurable $c$. $e_B$ is the element of $\{-M,0,M\}$ nearest to $f_B-f_A$ (mod $\mathsf R_c$), since $\lvert e_A\rvert\le1<M/2$. The scores are those of the table. $\square$ **[proved; computed: `p6_revision.out` D (400000 draws: plug-in mutual informations $I(e_B;f_B)=0.00012$ and $I(e_A;f_A)=0.00076$ bits, at the size of the plug-in bias; $q$ decoded on all draws; log scores $4.358$/$0.924$ at $\Delta=0.1$ and $0.720$/$0.924$ at $\Delta=2$; CRPS $0.3333$ and $179.2$)]**

So the toy is an exact distributional instance of a known mechanism: for ideal forecasts with *nested* information every consistent scoring function ranks the better-informed forecast first (Holzmann and Eulert 2014, Ann. Appl. Stat. 8(1):595-621, [B]/[A], and [S] in Krüger and Ziegel); with non-nested information rankings can depend on the loss (Patton 2020, [A]; Krüger and Ziegel 2021, JBES 39(4):972-983, [B]/[A], dominance conditions for the mean that allow non-nested information). The forecaster with both laws is a point mass and beats both under every proper score. The toy is therefore not a misspecification example in the statistical sense (no forecaster is miscalibrated relative to its own information); the genuine misspecification example of this record is the Gaussian family of T2. For the philosophy this strengthens the point: rival approximate laws, each with its calibrated error model, are like ideal forecasts on different information, and their ranking is purpose-relative *even without misspecification*.

**Dominance can be reversed.** Law B′ has error $s(t)\in\{-1,+1\}$ (a fair sign); law A′ has error $s(t)U(t)$, $U$ uniform on $[0,1]$. At every time A′'s prediction lies between the truth and B′'s (same sign, $\lvert e_{A'}\rvert\le\lvert e_{B'}\rvert$), so A′ dominates B′ in the sense of Proposition T6.B and every purpose that rewards closeness prefers A′. But the price of B′'s calibrated completion is 1 bit for $\Delta\le2$ and that of A′'s is $r_A$, so B′ is priced lower at every cell width below $\Delta_c'=1.429612$, the root of $\Delta=4\ln\Delta$. **[proved; computed: `p4_toy.out`; `rb4_toy.out` A (toy 2, dominance asserted at every datum)]** The calibrated CRPS prefers A′ ($1/3$ against $1/2$). So the calibrated completion's price rewards the predictability of the error, not its smallness, and below $\Delta_c'$ it is not the ranking of any purpose that rewards closeness.

**The same laws as experts under square loss** (`p5_aggregating.out` C; 4000 data, truth uniform on $[-1000,1000]$, outcomes and predictions clipped to $[-Y,Y]$, $Y=1002$; the clipping makes law B's square loss $5.4\cdot10^8$ instead of $8\cdot10^8$, referee B, m10): the aggregating algorithm ends with weight $1.000000$ on A, with cumulative square loss $L_{AA}=603{,}493$ against $L_A=1348$ (bound $1.39\cdot10^6$), while Bayesian log-loss posterior odds on dithered cells are $2^{13836}$, $2^{1784}$, $2^{256}$ for B at $\Delta=0.1,1,1.5$ and $2^{761}$ for A at $\Delta=2$. **[computed; `rb6_aggregating.out` D: $L_{AA}=468{,}462$, $L_A=1350$, 90% of $L_{AA}$ in the first 22 rounds]**

## T5. Purpose losses with universal weights

**The aggregating algorithm (AA).** A game $(\Omega,\Gamma,\lambda)$ is $\eta$-mixable if for every probability $\pi$ on $\Gamma$ some $\gamma\in\Gamma$ has $\lambda(\gamma,\omega)\le-\frac1\eta\ln\int e^{-\eta\lambda(g,\omega)}\pi(dg)$ for all $\omega$. **Theorem T5.0.** For an $\eta$-mixable game and a countable pool with weights $w_i>0$, $\sum_iw_i\le1$, the AA satisfies, on every outcome sequence and for every $n$ and $i$, $L_n(\mathrm{AA})\le-\frac1\eta\ln\sum_iw_ie^{-\eta L_n(i)}\le L_n(i)+\ln(1/w_i)/\eta$. *Proof.* Mixability at step $t$ gives $e^{-\eta\lambda(\gamma_t,\omega_t)}\ge\sum_iw_ie^{-\eta L_t(i)}/\sum_iw_ie^{-\eta L_{t-1}(i)}$; multiply over $t$. $\square$ **[known: Vovk 1990, 1998, 2001 ([A]/[S]); proved here]** For countable $\pi$, mixability follows from finite truncations (dominated convergence; the substitution function is continuous). **[proved]**

**Constants.** Square loss with outcomes in $[-Y,Y]$ is $\eta$-mixable exactly for $\eta\le1/(2Y^2)$ (the literature's $\eta=2$ on $[0,1]$, rescaled). **[known; computed: `p5_aggregating.out` A (largest infeasibility margin $2.4\cdot10^{-8}$ at $c=\eta Y^2=0.4$ and $5.9\cdot10^{-11}$ at $c=0.5$, both rounding at degenerate pools, where the margin takes a square root of round-off; $1.5\cdot10^{-4}$ at $c=0.505$; referee A, m8); `rb6_aggregating.out` A (bisection $0.50003$); `p6_revision.out` E]** The binding pools are narrow pools near the centre, not the endpoints (referee B, m5): the pool $\frac12(\delta_{-a}+\delta_a)$ is dominated up to $\eta Y^2=0.6094$, $0.5221$, $0.5034$, $0.5008$, $0.5002$, $0.50001$ for $a/Y=1,0.5,0.2,0.1,0.05,0.01$; endpoint pools with unequal weights stay dominated up to $0.626$-$0.963$ (`p6_revision.out` E). For the symmetric endpoint pool the threshold is the root of $\cosh(2c)=e^c$, $c=0.609$, because with $\gamma=0$ the condition is $\ln\cosh(2c\omega/Y)\le c$ for $\lvert\omega\rvert\le Y$; for $a\to0$ the condition becomes $a^2(1-2\eta\omega^2)\ge0$, i.e. $\eta\le1/(2Y^2)$. **[proved; computed]** The reference checker's `refs-report.md` §4 and the comment in `checks/refs_c1_attributions.py` say that the two-endpoint pool is the hardest case; that is wrong (the constant they confirm is right). Those two files are the reference checker's and are left unchanged; this record supersedes that sentence.

Absolute loss is mixable for no $\eta>0$ (with $\pi=\frac12(\delta_0+\delta_1)$, $g(0)+g(1)=-\frac2\eta\ln\frac{1+e^{-\eta}}2<1$). **[proved; computed]** For it the available guarantees have an additive term of order $\sqrt n$ (weak aggregating algorithm, Kalnishkan and Vyugin 2008, [A]). The CRPS is mixable, with AA regret $\frac{b-a}2\ln N$ for outcomes in $[a,b]$ (V'yugin and Trunov 2019, [A]/[Q] per `refs-report.md`): linear, not quadratic, in the range.

**Prior art.** AA with weights $2^{-K}$ gives Solomonoff-style bounds for mixable losses: this is *predictive complexity*. Its origin is credited by different secondary sources to Vovk (1997) or to Vovk and Watkins (1998, COLT, pp. 12-23, doi 10.1145/279943.279947); the Royal Holloway overview credits Vovk and Watkins 1998 with the method that proves existence through the AA for all mixable games ([S]; referee B, m6). Further: Vovk and Gammerman 1999; Kalnishkan, Vovk and Vyugin 2004; Vovk 2015 (for a computable mixable loss a smallest upper semicomputable superloss process exists up to an additive constant; for log loss it is $-\ln M+O(1)$) ([A]/[Q] per `refs-report.md` §4). Total computable predictors are not effectively enumerable, so they cannot be the pool. Only the theory-machine analogue below is new.

**Definition T5.1 (purpose-SAI on answered data).** Questions $\mathcal Q_t$ (paper Def. 2.10), each answer $a$ carrying a value $m(a)\in[-Y,Y]$ (e.g. the midpoint of an interval cell); outcome $\omega_t=m(a_t)$; a loss $\lambda$ on $\Gamma\times[-Y,Y]$. The *answer distribution* of $G$ on $\mathcal Q$ after $D$ is $P_G(a\mid D,\mathcal Q):=\nu_G(D+(a,1))/\sum_{a'\in\mathcal Q}\nu_G(D+(a',1))$ when the denominator is positive (the events are disjoint by incompatibility; the dropped mass is that of theories that answer nothing), and the *act* $\alpha_\lambda(G;D,\mathcal Q)$ is a Bayes act of $\lambda$ under it (for square loss, its mean), or a fixed default otherwise. *Purpose-SAI* is the AA over all theory machines with these acts and weights $\mathbf m(i):=\sum_{V(p)=i}2^{-\lvert p\rvert}\ge2^{-K(i)}$. (Variants: mix the dropped mass with a reference law; or treat silent theories as abstaining specialists, the setting of Freund, Schapire, Singer and Warmuth 1997, STOC, 334-343, [B]/[A].) **[definition]**

**Theorem T5.2 (regret of purpose-SAI).** If $\lambda$ is $\eta$-mixable, then on every sequence of questions and answers, for every theory machine $G$ and every $n$, $L_n(\text{purpose-SAI})\le L_n(G)+K(G)\ln2/\eta$; for square loss on $[-Y,Y]$ the regret is $2Y^2K(G)\ln2$. In particular this holds against every consistent r.e. theory and every band-confined completion. *Proof.* T5.0 with $w_i=\mathbf m(i)$. $\square$ **[proved, given T5.0 and mixability; computed: `rb6_aggregating.out` C (purpose-SAI on a finite atom model, 40 truthful and adversarial trials, minimum slack $+11.25$)]**
* *Scale* (referee A, m7; referee B, m3). The bound needs an a priori bounded range and grows as its square; square loss on an unbounded range is mixable for no $\eta$. For longitudes ($Y=10800'$) one bit of $K(G)$ costs $1.62\cdot10^8$ arcmin$^2$ of cumulative square loss, so against a law with $1'$ errors and $K(G)=2000$ bits the bound says nothing until $n\gg3\cdot10^{11}$ (`rb6_aggregating.out` D). It is informative only for purpose losses on a range comparable to the errors (a local or truncated loss), or for losses whose mixability constant is linear in the range, such as the CRPS.
* *Computability.* Purpose-SAI is incomputable, like SAI: its acts need $\nu_G$, which is not even lower semicomputable ($M_A(\emptyset)$ is right-c.e. and not left-c.e., paper Thm. 3.3).
* *What the weights are* (referee A, m4). After $n$ data they are $\propto\mathbf m(i)e^{-\eta L_n(i)}$: the exponential-weights form of the general-Bayes posterior of Bissiri, Holmes and Walker (2016, [A]/[S]). The identification is one of form only: they update with a fixed loss on i.i.d. data and read the result as a belief about the minimiser of the expected loss, while the AA's losses are prequential. In their reading these are credences about which machine minimises the expected purpose loss, not about which machine is true. For log loss with $\eta=\ln2$ (bits) the AA is Bayes.

**Proposition T5.3 (which results of the paper transfer).** Acts are functions of laws, so statements about the weights $2^{-K}$ and about the laws $\nu_G$ machine by machine transfer to purpose-SAI as regret bounds.
(a) *Machine invariance*: with another universal machine the regret is $\le(K_{V'}(G)+c)\ln2/\eta$. **[proved]**
(b) *Faithful translations and new vocabulary* (paper Thm. 3.5, Cor. 3.6, conditions (T1): $B\cup\Gamma\vdash\varphi$ iff $B'\cup\tau[\Gamma]\vdash\tau\varphi$; (T2): $B'\vdash\tau(\neg\varphi)\leftrightarrow\neg\tau\varphi$). For $G$ over $\mathcal L$ let $\tau G$ emit $\tau\chi$ for each emitted $\chi$; for $G'$ over $\mathcal L'$ let $\tau^*G'$ emit every $\varphi$ with $B'\cup A'\vdash\tau\varphi$. Then $\nu_{\tau G}(\tau D)=\nu_G(D)$ and $\nu_{\tau^*G'}(D)=\nu_{G'}(\tau D)$ for every $D$, with $K(\tau G),K(\tau^*G')\le K(\cdot)+K(\tau)+c$; and translated questions are questions: $B'\vdash\neg\tau\bot$ (from $B\vdash\neg\bot$, (T1) with $\Gamma=\emptyset$, and (T2)), so (T1) applied to $B\cup\{a_i,a_j\}\vdash\bot$ makes $\tau a_i,\tau a_j$ $B'$-incompatible, and (T1), (T2) applied to $B\cup\{\neg a_1,\dots,\neg a_k\}\vdash\bot$ give $B'\vdash\bigvee_i\tau a_i$ (referee A, m15). Hence purpose-SAI over $\mathcal L'$ on translated questions has regret $\le(K(G)+K(\tau)+c)\ln2/\eta$ against every $\mathcal L$-machine, and conversely. *Proof of the law equalities.* (T1) and (T2) give: $B\cup A$ is inconsistent iff $B'\cup\tau[A]$ is, and $B\cup A\vdash\varphi^b$ iff $B'\cup\tau[A]\vdash\tau(\varphi^b)$. For $A^*:=\{\varphi:B'\cup A'\vdash\tau\varphi\}$: if $B'\cup A'$ is inconsistent, $\bot\in A^*$; if $B\cup A^*\vdash\bot$ then $B'\cup\tau[A^*]\vdash\tau\bot$ with $\tau[A^*]\subseteq\mathrm{Cn}(B'\cup A')$, so $B'\cup A'$ is inconsistent; and $B\cup A^*\vdash\varphi$ iff $B'\cup A'\vdash\tau\varphi$. So the events defining the laws coincide tape by tape. $\square$ **[proved; checked by both referees]**
(c) *The Craig collapse for always-consistent machines.* Proof search $G\mapsto F_G$ and emission $F\mapsto G_F$ (paper Lemma B.2) map always-consistent theory machines and always closed-consistent function machines into each other with $O(1)$ extra complexity and preserve laws on all data, so purpose-SAI over theory machines and over function machines have regret $\le(K+c)\ln2/\eta$ against every always-consistent hypothesis of the other kind. **[proved, given Lemma B.2; referee B did not check Lemma B.2; referee A did]** Machines inconsistent with positive probability are not covered (Q2).
(d) What does not transfer: $M_A$'s own predictive probabilities (trichotomy, merging, posterior limits), the order-independence of the loss, and the two-part identity $L_A=\min_G[K(G)-\log\nu_G]\pm c$, of which only the upper bound survives.

**Proposition T5.4 (what purpose-SAI selects in regime R; proof sketch).** With band-confined completions as experts and square loss on the value, the act of $\mathrm{BC}_{\mathcal G}$ is $f(t)-\mathbb E_{\mathcal G}[\text{error}]$ with expected loss $\mathrm{Var}(e)+(\text{bias of }\mathcal G)^2$, up to quantisation terms of order $\Delta$ because the outcome is the cell midpoint (exactly in the limit $\Delta\to0$; referee A, m5(ii)); it is minimised by the calibrated $\mathcal G=\mathcal F$. By the strong law, as in the paper's Thm. G.22(i), the AA weights within a finite pool concentrate on the unique minimiser of the expected purpose loss. So under square loss purpose-SAI ranks calibrated laws by error variance. Absolute loss is not mixable, so the AA of T5.1 is not defined for it; exponential weights $\propto\mathbf m(i)e^{-\eta L_n(i)}$ at any fixed $\eta>0$ still concentrate on the least mean absolute deviation about the median, without the regret bound of T5.2 (the weak aggregating algorithm gives $O(\sqrt n)$; referee A, m5(i); referee B, m4). Under SAI's log pricing the ranking is by $h(e+V_\Delta)$ (T3.3): three different "best approximately true laws". **[proof sketch]**

**Open questions.**
* **Q1.** Is there an upper semicomputable purpose analogue of $M_A$: a superloss process over theory machines within $O(K(G))$ of every theory machine's $\lambda$-loss, as predictive complexity is for sequence prediction? The consistency requirement makes $M_A$ non-lower-semicomputable (Thm. 3.3); whether it blocks the analogue is open. **[open]**
* **Q2.** Does the Craig collapse hold for purpose-SAI beyond always-consistent machines? **[open]**
* **Q3** (for the paper's open-question list). SAI with a purpose: (i) a decision layer, ranking laws by posterior-expected purpose loss, which for SAI requires choosing how acts are scored under a belief function (for example by lower expectation); (ii) learning with a purpose loss, replacing cumulative log loss by a loss on answers and the posterior by aggregating-algorithm weights over theory machines. Which of SAI's properties survive, and is there a semicomputable universal element? **[open]**

## T6. What approximate correctness is (assessment)

Statuses in this section are **assessment** unless a proposition is marked otherwise; each rests on the results cited.

### T6.1 Credence, acceptance, and learning with a purpose

Three positions should be kept apart (referee A, M1).
* **Credence about hypotheses that might be exactly true.** Updating is conditioning, which is cumulative log loss, local and order-independent machine by machine (T1.1); the log score is the only local proper scoring rule on three or more outcomes (T2.1); log loss is the most selective standard for binary sequences (Vovk 2015, [A]). For SAI's deterministic proof-requiring hypotheses the likelihood is 0 or 1, so locality only says "discard the incompatible", which no purpose disputes. Locality is a substantive premise (accuracy-first epistemology argues for the Brier score: `pettigrew2016accuracy` in the paper's bibliography). Every strictly proper score makes stating one's credence optimal; what singles out the log score is locality, and with it the chain rule, compression and Kelly betting: it is the purpose "compress" (or "maximise log-wealth"), not the purpose "state your credence" (referee A, m6). Every decision problem induces a proper scoring rule through its Bayes act (Dawid 2007, [A]).
* **Acceptance as a decision.** Accepting, teaching or using an approximate law is an act. Given a posterior predictive $\Pi$ over the true answers and a purpose loss $\lambda$ on a question distribution, rank laws $T$ by the posterior-expected purpose loss $\mathbb E_\Pi[\sum_Q\lambda(\text{answer}_T(Q),\text{true answer}(Q))]$. This ranks approximate laws by purpose without changing credence, and when $\Pi$ concentrates on the truth it ranks them by their purpose loss against the truth: by maximum error for a tolerance purpose, by RMS for squared error. **[elementary decision theory; the convergence claim needs a merging result for the posterior used]** Philosophy of science has this structure: acceptance as a decision with its own (epistemic) utility (Levi 1967, *Gambling with Truth*, [B]/[S]: accepting an answer trades the risk of error against informativeness; Maher 1993, *Betting on Theories*, CUP, [B]/[A]: a Bayesian decision-theoretic account of rational acceptance, acceptance "not reducible to probability"), and expected truthlikeness, the posterior expectation of a similarity to the truth (Niiniluoto 1987, already cited by the paper; [S]: the SEP supplement "Expected truthlikeness"). In engineering, calibration of computer models is done by likelihood with an explicit model-inadequacy (discrepancy) term (Kennedy and O'Hagan 2001, JRSS B 63(3):425-464, [B]/[A]), and validation is defined relative to the intended uses of a model (referee A cites AIAA 1998 and ASME V&V 10 at [S]; not re-verified here).
* **Learning with a purpose loss.** The decision layer needs a good posterior predictive. For a bounded agent with a restricted, misspecified pool the posterior predictive is that of the KL projection (T2), and its Bayes acts need not serve the purpose; then the purpose loss must enter learning: general-Bayes updating (Bissiri, Holmes and Walker 2016, [A]/[S]), the aggregating algorithm (T5), tempered learning rates under misspecification (Grünwald and van Ommen 2017, [A]).
* **Outside all three.** An agent may also want a *simple* approximate law because of the costs of computing, communicating or teaching with it. That trade-off (purpose loss plus a cost) is outside "posterior plus purpose loss"; it is where much of the author's "practical messy grading" lives, and SAI's pricing does not model it.

**What this means for SAI.** SAI as defined has no act layer: the paper's statements about which form SAI "prefers" (§9's "SAI's pricing of a form", the λ-MAP best system) are statements about credence and compression. Adding an act layer to SAI is not immediate, because $M_A$'s predictions are belief functions (mass on "independent"), so a Bayes act needs a choice such as the lower expectation or a completion $P_D$ between $\mathrm{Bel}_D$ and $\mathrm{Pl}_D$ (paper Prop. 3.2(c)); and that such acts serve every bounded purpose in the well-specified case is known for Solomonoff's $M$ in sequence prediction (Hutter 2003, [A]) but not proved for $M_A$ (the paper's merging theorem, Thm. A.12, is in three-valued form). **[open: Q3(i)]** The claim that SAI's mixture eventually beats every Kepler-based machine by containing the ephemeris holds on the track's synthetic data (plan94 as truth), and on real data only if the data are computable, or computably approximable well enough (referee A, m17).

### T6.2 Adequacy hypotheses at a tolerance are propositions

**Proposition T6.A (threshold purposes are sentences; graded purposes are not).** Over $B\supseteq\mathrm{RCF}$ with constants $q_t$ for the times $t$ of a finite domain $W$ a computable law $f$ with rational values on $W$ and a rational tolerance $\delta$: "for every $t\in W$, $\lvert q_t-f(t)\rvert\le\delta$" (the honest law $T^\delta$ on $W$) and "for at least $k$ of the $t\in W$, $\lvert q_t-f(t)\rvert\le\delta$" (a disjunction over $k$-subsets) are sentences, so SAI holds posterior credences in them ($\mathrm{Bel}_D$, the posterior probability that the random theory proves them). A graded purpose, a real-valued loss of the answers or its expectation, is not a sentence but a functional; SAI has no place for it. *Proof.* Each condition is a finite Boolean combination of order relations between the constants $q_t$ and rationals. $\square$ **[proved]**

So (referee A, M2):
* "Credence that Kepler's law is true is idle" holds for the *exact* law only. Credence in adequacy hypotheses at a tolerance on a domain ("on the window $W$ the ellipse gives Mars's longitude within $8'$") is the normal case, and SAI already has it; fit confirms hypotheses about adequacy for particular purposes (Parker 2009, [A]). Regime G compatibility is exactly the test of such a hypothesis.
* What SAI lacks is *graded* fitness (how bad a miss is, fixed by a purpose, T1.6) and an act layer (T6.1). Threshold purposes, including the pass/fail reading of the author's "whether the theory gave answers that were good enough for some purpose", are expressible. The author's phrase "practical messy grading" admits a graded reading as well, and that reading is not expressible; we do not think the wording settles which was meant.
* **Scope of our rendering.** "A purpose is a question family with a resolution plus a loss on answers, or more generally a decision problem" is our rendering, not Parker's, and it covers predictive purposes only. It omits epistemic purposes such as explanation and teaching, and Parker's relativisation of adequacy to the user, the methodology and the background circumstances as well as the target (Parker 2020, Phil. Sci. 87(3):457-477, p. 460, [S]). Example: the use of Kepler's laws in deriving the inverse-square law is not graded by a loss on answers.

### T6.3 Kepler's 8′ and the purpose-neutral core

**Kepler's 8′ is a tolerance set by observational precision** (referee A, M3; referee B, m7). Kepler rejected the vicarious hypothesis because an $8'$ discrepancy could not, he judged, be produced by errors of Tycho's observations ([S]: MacTutor's translation of *Astronomia Nova* ch. 19; secondary accounts put Tycho's errors at rarely more than about $4'$ and often about $1'$; the "2-3′" figure in Kepler is about his own observations, and why Kepler was sure Tycho's errors were below $8'$ is, per Sheynin, "Kepler as a statistician", 2017, left open; Sheynin reads Kepler as applying a minimax principle, [S]). This is the truth-directed test of regime G: compatibility at a tolerance set by the data's precision. It shows that the historically operative criterion was a maximum-error tolerance, not $L^2$, and it supports the paper's local notion (a question family, a tolerance, a domain). It does not support "the grading is set by a purpose". The earlier "about $2'$" for Tycho's accuracy is dropped.

**Proposition T6.B (the dominance order is purpose-neutral given the quantities; referee A, M7).** Fix a family of questions, each about one real quantity. Say law A *dominates* law B if for every question A's answer lies in the closed interval between the true value and B's answer. (a) Then A is at least as good as B for every *accuracy-monotone* purpose: per-question losses $\lambda_Q(a,x)$ nonincreasing as $a$ moves towards $x$ from either side (asymmetric losses and tolerances included), aggregated by any rule nondecreasing in each per-question loss (sum, maximum, quantile, count of failures). (b) Dominance is invariant under any strictly monotone reparametrisation of each quantity separately. (c) It is not invariant under a change of the quantities themselves: with truth $(0,0)$, A $=(1,0)$ dominates B $=(2,-2)$ in the quantities $(x,y)$, while in $(x,x+y)$ the predictions are $(1,1)$ and $(2,0)$ and B is exact on the second quantity. *Proof.* (a) per question, $\lambda_Q(a_A,x)\le\lambda_Q(a_B,x)$ by monotonicity; aggregate. (b) a strictly monotone map preserves betweenness. (c) direct. $\square$ **[proved]** That closeness rankings of predictions can reverse under a transformation of the quantities is Miller's point (D. Miller, "The accuracy of predictions", Synthese 30:159-191, 1975, doi 10.1007/BF00485304, [B]/[S]; the "Miller-Popper paradox"), the classical source for "no canonical metric", next to `miller1974popper` already in the paper.

So, given a question family, the *error profile* (question to answer and truth) and the dominance order are purpose-neutral; purposes enter when the order is completed to a ranking of non-dominated laws and in the choice of quantities. "There is no purpose-neutral best approximately true law" (the brief) holds in general, but when one law dominates the others on the question family it is best for every accuracy-monotone purpose. Whether rival approximate laws often dominate one another is an empirical matter. For the four Kepler fits per series they mostly do not: a strict disagreement between two accuracy-monotone criteria rules out dominance, and the maximum and the mean absolute error disagree on 204 of 234 pairs (T4(b)). **[assessment, resting on Proposition T6.B and T4(b)]** And SAI's calibrated pricing can reverse dominance (T4(a), A′ against B′ below $\Delta_c'=1.4296$), so below that resolution it is not the ranking of any accuracy-monotone purpose. **[proved]**

### T6.5 The paper's sentence on a canonical metric, and its local notion

The paper (§11, "Approximate truth") says: "A canonical metric for approximate predictions, which we think does not exist, … are open; our notion is local: a question family, a tolerance, a domain."
* **The negative claim stands, sharpened.** Different proper scores rank rival laws differently (T2, T4); the classical criteria disagree on the real Kepler fits (max against mean absolute error on 204 of 234 pairs); SAI's own pricing depends on the resolution (T3.3, T4). "Metric" is the wrong word: many purposes are not metrics (tolerances, asymmetric losses; squared error violates the triangle inequality). Better: "no canonical score, and no canonical choice of quantities" (Proposition T6.B(c), Miller 1975). What is canonical given the quantities is only the dominance order (Proposition T6.B).
* **The local notion fits T1 and T6.1.** A question family with a tolerance on a domain is a threshold purpose: a sentence in which SAI holds a credence (Proposition T6.A), and a partition, which is what SAI's log loss can express (T1.4). Grading misses beyond the resolution of the questions requires a loss fixed by the purpose, which SAI lacks (T1.6); T5 and Q3 are ways to add one.

### T6.6 The Kepler example under the purpose view

* **Kepler's test is already in the paper, as compatibility.** In the noise-free regime of §10 a form is compatible with the Mars data at tolerance $\delta$ iff its minimax error is at most $\delta$: the proof-requiring posterior applies a maximum-error tolerance, Kepler's kind of test (T6.3). Log loss and text length enter only among compatible forms.
* **On the Mars pool the three criteria computed agree.** Minimax errors $640.79'$, $23.05'$, $8.17'$, $1.41'$, $1.07'$ and least-squares RMS errors $454.4'$, $15.81'$, $5.26'$, $0.628'$, $0.446'$ (circle, eccentric, equant, vicarious, ellipse; philosophy track c4, c9; checked by referee B against the saved outputs) give the same order, and so does the graded-fit regime with a Gaussian error model ($\sigma=2'$), which ranks by RMS (T3.1). That is: maximum error, RMS error and the Gaussian graded fit rank the forms alike (referee A, m16; no pointwise dominance was checked). Fine-resolution calibrated log loss ranks by the entropy of the errors and could in principle reorder two forms with close errors (vicarious against ellipse: ratio 1.32 in maximum, 1.41 in RMS); this was not computed, because the pool's residual series were not saved. **[assessment, resting on computed results of the philosophy track; the entropy comparison not computed]**
* **Where purposes diverge: which quantity is asked.** For the vicarious hypothesis with longitude-optimal elements the maximum longitude error is $1.41'$ and the distances are off by up to $2.82\%$; requiring distances within $1\%$ raises its least maximum longitude error to $5.12'$, within $0.2\%$ to $23.7'$; the ellipse serves both ($1.07'$) (philosophy track c10; checked by referee B). So "the vicarious hypothesis is approximately correct" is true for longitudes and false for distances: the choice of quantities, which Proposition T6.B(c) says is not purpose-neutral, is the situation Kepler was in. **[assessment, resting on computed results]**
* **Where purposes diverge on one law: which elements.** For one Kepler ellipse, which fitted elements are best depends on the criterion (T4(b)); SAI's calibrated pricing picks yet another fit only in near-ties, and its ranking changes with the cell width in 11-16 of 234 pairs.

## Probably wrong

Only items with a decent probability of being wrong are listed.
* **In the author's objection:** nothing. (Two refinements, not errors, are in T6.4.)
* **In Claude's reply (brief, 2026-10-17):**

Probably wrong as stated: a Bayesian keeps credence (log loss, conditioning) apart from acceptance (posterior plus purpose loss), and the latter ranks approximate laws by purpose without touching credence, given a good posterior predictive. What the objection reaches is posterior weight and code length used as measures of approximate correctness (the paper's §9 move), and learning with a restricted, misspecified pool, where the purpose loss must enter learning (T6.1).

The form is right, but Kepler's tolerance was set by the precision of Tycho's observations, a truth-directed criterion; the example supports the local notion of a tolerance, not purpose-relativity (T6.3).

The second half is probably wrong for the same reason as the first item; write "of any account that ranks approximate laws by posterior weight or code length".
