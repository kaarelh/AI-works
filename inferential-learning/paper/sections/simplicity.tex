\section{Simplicity and normativity from imitation}\label{sec:simplicity}

Imitation identifies practice, errors included: for positive data, a systematic error generated by a schema \emph{is} a rule (\Cref{cor:imitation:systematic}). This section examines a different remedy, proposed in the motivating notes \citep{hanni2026notes}, and answers the question it raises.\footnote{The note \texttt{philosophy/philosophy of thinking/beating solomonoff induction at grokking a notion.md}, a public working note; it lists this proposal as one of several ideas. Quotations are verbatim.} The note asks how one can understand ``what someone means when using a word. as opposed to becoming a predictor of what they would say''. It proposes
\begin{quote}\small
``an even stronger simplicity prior than solomonoff. eg if there are explainable mistakes on a simple model, you want the simple model that doesn't predict the mistakes. [\dots] let's just do a version of the simple hypothesis with noise, and then penalize the likelihood term less.''
\end{quote}
The note anticipates an objection: in sequence prediction, a steeper penalty favours a universal hypothesis that absorbs everything. It suggests, while ``very much uncertain'', that this pathology ``is not present in the function case, if [\dots] each input has to sample its own random bits''. It asks whether, given the models it lists, the good model ``could be a vertex of the convex hull of the set of attainable (hypothesis complexity, expected neg log likelihood) tuples''. And it adds: ``I consider it very much plausible that there is some other pathology''.

\paragraph{Answers in brief.} The function-case intuition and the convex-hull argument are both right: private randomness makes every hypothesis pay for mixing per input, the product-model frontier has the hoped-for kink (proved for parity models), and selection is a threshold on \emph{rates} (risk reduction per bit) along the lower hull (\Cref{thm:simplicity:pricing,thm:simplicity:kink,lem:simplicity:hull,thm:simplicity:idealized}). But there are other pathologies: universality returns at block granularity, selection is by rate rather than validity, and the right steepness is not identifiable from imitation data. Steeper simplicity is a good \emph{third} filter beside coherence and the world; the note's own alternative, ``you specify properties of the thing or notion'', removes cheap fallacies at every steepness. \Cref{sec:simplicity:verdict} gives the verdict in the note's terms, with references.

As far as we know, three results are mildly new: the product-model frontier and its kink, the rate threshold for arbitrary hypotheses, and the realizability of every convex frontier after rescaling.

\subsection{The proposal, formalized}\label{sec:simplicity:setup}

Inputs $x\in X$ are drawn from $\mu$, and labels lie in a finite set $Y$. The teacher is a channel $P^*(y\mid x)$. In the note's picture, $P^*$ arises from an intended \emph{norm} $h^\circ$ through sporadic noise and systematic error. Data are i.i.d.\ from $\mu\times P^*$. \emph{Normativity} asks the learner to output $h^\circ$, or its deterministic core, rather than $P^*$. In \cref{sec:simplicity:division}, $x$ is a proof context and $y$ the next human step; then $P^*$ is the practice $R^P$ of \Cref{def:setting:practice}, viewed as a channel.

\begin{definition}[Randomness and objective]\src{T5 Def 1.1, \S1.3}\label{def:simplicity:objective}
A \emph{product hypothesis} (private randomness) is a conditional distribution $h(\cdot\mid x)$, with likelihood $\prod_ih(y_i\mid x_i)$. A \emph{shared-randomness} hypothesis is a distribution $P(y_{1:N}\mid x_{1:N})$; sequence prediction is its causally conditioned case. In a \emph{block} hypothesis, the input at position $i$ of a block (e.g.\ a document) includes the block's earlier pairs. The note's loss is $\lambda\ell(h)+\sum_{i\le N}-\log_2h(y_i\mid x_i)$, with $\lambda>1$ growing with $N$. Writing $\lambda=cN$ and dividing by $N$ gives $\hat J_c=c\ell+\hRisk_N$, and its population version $J_c=c\ell+\Risk$, where $\Risk(h)=\E_{\mu\times P^*}[-\log_2h(y\mid x)]$. Let $\Phi(c)=\inf_hJ_c(h)$. In the \emph{idealized} version, $\ell=K$ (prefix complexity) over total programs with rational outputs, and $p_h$ is a shortest program for $h$. In the \emph{computable} version, $\ell$ is a prefix code on a countable class. A \emph{component} costing $k$ bits that lowers $\Risk$ by $r$ has \emph{rate} $r/k$.
\end{definition}

The steepness $c$ is measured in bits per sample per bit of description. In the computable version with a noise floor $h(y\mid x)\ge2^{-B}$, every minimizer of $\hat J_c$ is within $O\big(B\sqrt{(\ell(h_0)+B/c+\log(1/\delta))/N}\big)$ of $\Phi(c)$ with probability $1-\delta$, for every fixed $h_0$, by a standard Occam bound (\cref{lem:simplicity:empirical}); so we work with $J_c$.

\emph{Fixed versus growing steepness.} With fixed $\lambda$, $c\to0$, so every component of positive rate is eventually absorbed, error or not \src{T4 Prop 7.1(i); L11 Prop 2.2}: imitation with a fixed simplicity weight converges to the teacher's practice, the learner's version of Cohen's view that normative standards are a systematization of ordinary judgments \citep{cohen1981can}. With $\lambda\propto N$, as the note proposes, selection is asymptotically stable, so the obstruction found below is \emph{rate inversion}, not sample size.

\paragraph{Relation to the literature.} The note's $\lambda$ is the inverse $1/\eta$ of a \emph{learning rate} $\eta<1$ in a tempered posterior \citep{grunwald2012safe,grunwald2017inconsistency,zhang2006epsilon,bissiri2016general,bhattacharya2019fractional}, and $c\ell+\Risk$ is the algorithmic rate--distortion Lagrangian with log-loss distortion \citep{vereshchagin2010rate}. Appendix~\ref{app:simplicity:structure} gives the details, including the relation to SafeBayes and to Kolmogorov's structure function; in the latter language, the note's insight is that the product-model structure function can kink.

\subsection{No universal hypothesis in the function case}\label{sec:simplicity:universal}

The note asks whether it is ``just failing to construct the right `universal hypothesis' ''. The answer is no as long as randomness is private and inputs carry no other labels, and yes as soon as they do.

\begin{theorem}[Per-input versus once-only pricing]\src{T5 Thm 2.1, Prop 2.2, as repaired}\label{thm:simplicity:pricing}
Let $\mathcal C$ be a finite class of deterministic labelers, $x_1,\dots,x_N$ inputs, and $\mathcal C(x)=\{f(x):f\in\mathcal C\}$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item For every product $h$ and every distribution $Q$ on $\mathcal C$,
\[
\max_f\sum_i-\log h(f(x_i)\mid x_i)\ge\sum_iH(Q_{x_i}),
\]
where $Q_x$ is the law of $f(x)$. The minimax value over product $h$ is exactly $\max_Q\sum_iH(Q_{x_i})\le\sum_i\log|\mathcal C(x_i)|$. Equality holds iff some $Q$ has uniform marginals on every $\mathcal C(x_i)$. Such a $Q$ (the uniform one) exists when a group acts on $\mathcal C$ by $(g\cdot f)(x)=\sigma_{g,x}(f(x))$, with permutations $\sigma_{g,x}$ of $Y$, transitively on each $\mathcal C(x)$; parities are an example.
\item For shared randomness, $\min_P\max_f-\log P(f(x_{1:N}))=\log|\{f(x_{1:N})\}|\le\log|\mathcal C|$ \citep{shtarkov1987universal}.
\item A mixture with weights $w_q$ pays $\log(1/w_q)$ at \emph{every input} if it mixes per input, and \emph{once} if it mixes sequences. Both bounds are tight.
\end{enumerate}

\hfill\leanok{NoAdaptation.thm\_2\_1\_i\_ennreal\_maxF}\ \leanok{NoAdaptation.minimax\_of\_transitive}
\end{theorem}

The core of the proof is one line: $\max_f\ge\E_{f\sim Q}$, and $\E_Q[-\log h(f(x)\mid x)]=H(Q_x)+\KL(Q_x\|h(\cdot\mid x))$. The minimax claim (via Sion's theorem) and the group condition are proved in Appendix~\ref{app:simplicity:pricing}. Lean covers the lower bound in (i) and the value $\sum_i\log|\mathcal C(x_i)|$ under the group condition, not the general value $\max_Q\sum_iH(Q_{x_i})$, (ii) or (iii).

For parities on $\{0,1\}^{10}$ with $N=200$, the per-input mixture pays $200.0$ bits and the Bayes mixture $10.0$; the uniform-marginal condition is needed, since $\{(a,a),(a,b),(b,a),(c,a)\}$ has value $2.5431<\log_23+1$ \status{computed}.

In sequence prediction, by contrast, a universal semimeasure $\Muniv$ beats every model whose complexity exceeds a threshold that tends to $K(\Muniv)$ as $\lambda\to\infty$ (\cref{prop:simplicity:sequence}); in the function case even the universal conditional semimeasure is a fixed predictor with positive risk (Appendix~\ref{app:simplicity:sequence}).

\begin{proposition}[Per-block pricing: in-context universality]\src{T5 Prop 2.6, as repaired}\label{prop:simplicity:block}
Suppose the input at position $i$ of block $b$ contains the block's earlier pairs. Let $(q_\theta)$ be a finite family of computable, rational-valued ``persona'' models $q_\theta(y\mid x,\text{history})$, with rational weights $\sum_\theta w_\theta\le1$. The in-context Bayes mixture $h_w$, with weights $w(\theta\mid\text{history})\propto w_\theta\prod_{k<i}q_\theta(y_{b,k}\mid\cdot)$, is a single function hypothesis with $K(h_w)\le K(w,(q_\theta))+O(1)$. On every block,
\[
\sum_{i\in b}-\log h_w(y_{b,i}\mid\cdot)\le\min_\theta\Big[\log\tfrac1{w_\theta}+\sum_{i\in b}-\log q_\theta(y_{b,i}\mid\cdot)\Big].
\]
The block bound holds for every countable family, but for an infinite one $h_w$ need not be computable.
\end{proposition}

The block likelihood of $h_w$ telescopes (Appendix~\ref{app:simplicity:sequence}). So $h_w$ pays $\log(1/w)$ per block, not per input. Suppose that 20\% of humans each have an idiosyncratic error, ``in error contexts play wrong move $j$'', with $j$ uniform among $2^{16}$ moves. Each such rule has rate about $0$, yet an $O(1)$-bit copier beats the norm model once a block has two error contexts: it pays $3.03$ bits per error context at two and $1.33$ at sixteen, against exactly $4.635$ for the norm model at any block length \status{computed}.

\paragraph{Reading.} An LLM imitating whole proof documents is a block model: it can learn each author's systematic errors in context. A step checker whose input is a single step $(\Pi,j)$ (\Cref{sec:setting}) is a function model. The step is therefore the granularity at which the note's defence holds. This gives a second, independent argument for the step-level architecture of \Cref{sec:search,sec:caution}.

\subsection{The kink: shared versus private randomness}\label{sec:simplicity:kink}

In sequence prediction, the note says, ``it's pretty much just 1 bit paying for 1 bit''. In the function case one might instead ``see some point at which the derivative changes''. We prove this for the note's example, made rigorous.

\paragraph{Setting.} Let $X=\{0,1\}^d$, $n=2^d$ and $\mu$ uniform. The simple model is a parity $f_a(x)=\langle a,x\rangle\bmod2$ with $K(a)\ge d$. The error set $E$ has $|E|=pn$, $p<1/4$ and $K(p)=O(\log d)$, and the teacher is $y^*=f_a\oplus1_E$. Let $L_E=\log_2\binom n{pn}\approx nH(p)$, and let $\delta_E=L_E-K(E\mid a,K(a))$ be the randomness deficiency of $E$, which is $O(\log n)$ for most~$E$. Write $\hat s_h(a')$ for the correlation of $h$'s vote $h(0\mid x)-h(1\mid x)$ with $(-1)^{\langle a',x\rangle}$. The \emph{good model} is $f_a$ with flip rate $p$; it has $K\le d+\kappa'$ with $\kappa'=O(\log d)$, and risk $H(p)$. The \emph{bad model} $y^*$ has risk~$0$.

\begin{theorem}[Kink]\src{T5 Thm 2.5, as repaired}\label{thm:simplicity:kink}
There is a machine constant $\kappa$ such that the following hold.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item \emph{Shared randomness has (almost) no kink.} Let $P_u$ be the uniform shared-randomness mixture of the $2^u$ noisy parities that agree with $a$ on its first $d-u$ coordinates. Then $|K(P_u)-(d-u)|\le\kappa'$ and $-\log P_u(y^*)=u+nH(p)-o(1)$, where the $o(1)$ term is nonnegative and exponentially small in $n$ (so super-exponentially small in $d$). So the total $K(P_u)-\log P_u(y^*)$ is $d+nH(p)$ up to $\pm(\kappa'+o(1))$ for every $u$, and every minimizer $u^*$ of $\lambda K(P_u)-\log P_u(y^*)$ has $d-u^*\le\frac\lambda{\lambda-1}(2\kappa'+o(1))$.
\item \emph{Private randomness has a kink.} For every product $h$ and $t\in\N$ with $K(h)\le d-2t-2\log_2(t+1)-2\log_2d-\kappa$,
\[
\Risk(h)\ge1-\log_2(1+\beta_t)\ge1-\beta_t/\ln2,
\]
where $\beta_t=\min\{2^{-t}+2p,\ (1-2p)2^{-t}+\varepsilon_E\}$, with $\varepsilon_E=\sqrt{32\ln2\cdot p(\delta_E^++d+\kappa_n)/n}$, $\delta_E^+=\max(\delta_E,0)$ and $\kappa_n=\log_2n+2\log_2(\log_2n+2)+\kappa$.
\item \emph{The error segment.} For every product $h$, $K(h)+n\Risk(h)\ge d+L_E-\delta_E-2\log_2d-\kappa$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

The proof (Appendix~\ref{app:simplicity:kink}) combines the list-size bound for the Hadamard code \citep{goldreich1989hard,kushilevitz1993learning}, which makes a short $h$ correlating with $f_a$ encode $a$ in fewer than $d$ bits, with Jensen's inequality and the coding theorem; the slack $2p$ arises because $h$ may be right on $E$ for free, and the randomness of $E$ relative to $h$ replaces it by $\varepsilon_E$.

\paragraph{Reading.} Part (a) is the note's ``1 bit paying for 1 bit'': the shared frontier is the sufficiency line, so a steeper penalty selects a mixture that knows almost nothing of $a$ and learns the rest from the data, as any Bayes mixture does. Part (b) makes the note's hope quantitative. A product hypothesis missing $u=d-K(h)$ bits gains at most about $2^{-u/2+O(\log d)}$ bits per sample over the coin, plus slack: exponentially little, though not nothing (list mixtures missing $u=1,2,3$ bits gain $0.454$, $0.221$, $0.110$ bits \status{computed}). So on the scale of $d$ bits the product frontier is flat near the coin and drops at the simple model; that drop is the change of derivative.

Two caveats. The kink is resolved only up to $O(\log d)$ bits next to the simple model. And the slack $2p$ alone gives a kink only for $p<0.1421$, where $1-2p/\ln2>H(p)$; the slack $\varepsilon_E$ gives one for every $p<1/4$ once $n$ is large and $\delta_E=O(\log n)$ (at $p=0.2$, $d=20$ and $\delta_E=\kappa=0$, $\varepsilon_E=0.014$).

\subsection{Selection traces the hull: rate thresholds}\label{sec:simplicity:hull}

Let $z_h=(\ell(h),\Risk(h))$, $Z=\{z_h\}$ and $\hat C=\conv(Z+\R^2_{\ge0})$. Only finitely many $h$ have $\ell\le L$, and $\Risk\ge0$, so each $J_c$ with $c>0$ attains its minimum.

\begin{definition}[Lower hull]\src{T5 \S3.1, as repaired}\label{def:simplicity:hull}
$\hullm(Z)$ is the set of boundary points of $\hat C$ supported by a line of slope $-c$ with $0<c<\infty$. A \emph{vertex} is an extreme point of $\hat C$ that lies in $\hullm(Z)$; vertices belong to~$Z$.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[Selection traces the lower hull; standard]\src{T5 Lemma 3.1, as repaired}\label{lem:simplicity:hull}
\textup{(i)} For $c>0$, the minimizers of $J_c$ are the $h$ with $z_h\in\hullm(Z)$ on a supporting line of slope $-c$, and every point of $\hullm(Z)$ in $Z$ arises this way.
\textup{(ii)} If $c<c'$, and $h$ and $h'$ minimize $J_c$ and $J_{c'}$, then $\ell(h)\ge\ell(h')$ and $\Risk(h)\le\Risk(h')$.
\textup{(iii)} A point $z\in Z$ is the unique minimizing point for all $c$ in some nonempty open interval iff it is a vertex. The interval is then $(s_+,s_-)$, given by the absolute slopes of the adjacent edges. A \emph{hypothesis} is the unique minimizer there iff, in addition, no other hypothesis has the same point.

\hfill\leanok{RateThreshold.onLowerHull\_iff}\ \leanok{RateThreshold.lemma\_3\_1\_ii}\ \leanok{RateThreshold.lemma\_3\_1\_iii\_hyp}
\end{lemma}

Part (i) holds because $J_c(h)=\langle(c,1),z_h\rangle$ has the same minimum over $Z$ as over $\hat C$; (ii) follows by adding the two optimality inequalities; (iii) is proved in Appendix~\ref{app:simplicity:hull}. The Lean proofs of (i) and (iii) cover finite classes.

\paragraph{Reading.} The note's convex-hull argument is correct, with two refinements. The intended model must be a \emph{vertex}, and the only hypothesis at its point. And $\lambda$ must lie in $(s_+N,s_-N)$. The note's alternative, to ``set abs bound on model complexity'', selects every model the penalty selects (bound it at that model's length), as the note observes. The converse fails: a hard bound can also select points of the staircase $\rho^\times(k)=\min\{\Risk(h):\ell(h)\le k\}$ that lie off the hull, though never a Pareto-dominated point such as that of \Cref{prop:simplicity:inversion}.

\begin{theorem}[Rate threshold, separable classes; trivial once set up]\src{T5 Thm 3.2; T4 Prop 7.1}\label{thm:simplicity:separable}
Partition $X$ into finitely many regions $X_j$, and let $\Hc=\prod_j\Hc_j$ with $\ell=\sum_j\ell_j$. Then $J_c=\sum_j[c\ell_j+\Risk_j]$, and the minimizers are exactly the products of per-region minimizers. In particular, let $\Hc_j=\{\text{default},\text{component }j\}$, where the component costs $k_j>0$ extra bits and lowers $\Risk_j$ by $r_j$. Then component $j$ is selected iff $r_j/k_j>c$; at equality both choices are optimal.

\hfill\leanok{RateThreshold.prod\_isMin\_iff}\ \leanok{RateThreshold.rate\_threshold}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The objective is a sum of functions of disjoint coordinates. This is the standard hard-thresholding fact for separable penalties.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Separating rules from fallacies]\src{T4 Cor 7.2}\label{cor:simplicity:selectable}
Split finitely many separable components into valid ones and fallacies. Some $c>0$ makes the valid set the unique minimizer iff $\min_{v\text{ valid}}r_v/k_v>\max_{e\text{ fallacy}}r_e/k_e$, where $\max\emptyset:=0$.

\hfill\leanok{RateThreshold.selectable\_iff}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
If the inequality holds, take $c$ between the two sides. If instead $r_e/k_e\ge r_v/k_v$, any $c$ that strictly selects $v$ has $c<r_e/k_e$, and so also selects~$e$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Arbitrary hypotheses.} With $\ell=K$ and all hypotheses allowed, which may share information across regions or learn components partially, independent parities behave as separable components: the rate threshold of \cref{thm:simplicity:separable} survives up to explicit $O(\log)$ slack (\cref{thm:simplicity:idealized}). Every convex, decreasing, piecewise-linear frontier that starts at height $1$, has rational slopes and drops by at most one bit is, after rescaling and up to logarithmic bands, the product-model frontier of a teacher made of independent parities and a fair-coin region (\cref{cor:simplicity:shapes}); so whether a good model is a vertex is a property of the data, not a theorem. And in the note's example, for small $p$ and $c$ well inside the window $(\approx1/n,\approx(1-H(p))/d)$, every near-optimal hypothesis correlates strongly with the parity and memorizes almost none of the errors (\cref{thm:simplicity:example,cor:simplicity:example}). All four results are stated and proved in Appendix~\ref{app:simplicity}.

\emph{The note's own numbers} \status{computed}. These are a 100-bit simple model (plus 10 bits for the flip rate, our stand-in for the note's ``something small''), and a 10\,000-bit error on a $2^{-10}$ fraction of inputs, with $H(2^{-10})=0.0112$ bits per sample (about $1/100$, as the note says). The rates are $8.99\cdot10^{-3}$ for the model and $1.12\cdot10^{-6}$ for the error. So the good model is selected iff $c$ lies between these, a window spanning a factor of about 8000. At $N=10^6$ this means $\lambda\in(1.12,8990)$. Standard MDL ($\lambda=1$) switches to the error model at $N\approx8.9\cdot10^5$.

\paragraph{Reading.} The error's rate is about $1/n$: a K-random error set costs one bit per $1/n$ of risk, the slowest possible rate. So \emph{the note's example is the most favourable case for the proposal}. Normativity is achieved there because the errors are incompressible relative to the norm.

\subsection{Pathologies}\label{sec:simplicity:pathologies}

\begin{theorem}[Rate blindness; trivial once set up]\src{T5 Thm 4.1, as repaired}\label{thm:simplicity:blind}
\leavevmode
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item $c\mapsto\arg\min J_c$ depends on the data only through $(\mu,P^*)$; validity enters nowhere.
\item Let $v$ be valid and $e$ an error with $r_v/k_v\le r_e/k_e$. In the setting of \Cref{thm:simplicity:separable}, if $v$ is selected then so is $e$, for every $c$ other than a common tie value. In the setting of \Cref{thm:simplicity:idealized}, the same holds for near-optimal $h$ at every $c$ at which each of $v$ and $e$ satisfies the hypothesis of clause (a) or of clause (b) of that theorem; this excludes slack bands around their rates.
\item $h^\circ$ is the unique minimizer on an open $c$-interval iff $z_{h^\circ}$ is a vertex and no other hypothesis shares it.
\item Take two worlds with the same $(\mu,P^*)$, in which some component is an error in one and a rare valid rule in the other. Every imitation-only procedure gives the same output in both, so it is wrong in one of them.
\end{enumerate}

\hfill\leanok{RateThreshold.rate\_blindness}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Parts (i) and (iv) hold because $J_c$ is a functional of $(\mu,P^*)$. Part (iii) is \Cref{lem:simplicity:hull}.

For (ii) in the separable case: if $v$ is selected and $c$ is not a tie, then $c<r_v/k_v\le r_e/k_e$; and if $c=r_v/k_v<r_e/k_e$, then $e$ is strictly selected. The idealized case is proved in Appendix~\ref{app:simplicity:blind}.
\end{proof}

The Lean proof covers (ii) in the separable case only. Part (iv) is \Cref{cor:imitation:systematic} again. What is new is the quantitative criterion: the penalty separates errors from rules \emph{by rate, not validity}.

For instance, a 200-bit valid rule used in $10^{-4}$ of steps, gaining at most $2.2$ bits per use, has a lower rate than the note's 10\,000-bit error, so every $c$ that rejects the error discards it; rarely used irregular verbs likewise regularize first \citep{lieberman2007quantifying}.

\paragraph{Cheap fallacies} \src{T5 Prop 4.2}. In step imitation (\cref{sec:simplicity:division}), a schema used in a fraction $\pi$ of steps has rate $\pi g/\ell$. Affirming the consequent (AC: $q,\ p\to q\,/\,p$) has the size of modus ponens. The freshman's dream (FD: $(a+b)^2\to a^2+b^2$) is shorter than the correct expansion.

Take a toy with $M=64$ candidate steps and slip rate $\varepsilon=0.05$, so that the gain is $g=5.42$ bits \status{computed}. AC, at frequency $0.02$ and 20 bits, then has rate $5.4\cdot10^{-3}$. That beats proof by cases ($1.4\cdot10^{-3}$), reductio ($1.6\cdot10^{-3}$), ex falso ($7.2\cdot10^{-4}$) and induction ($1.2\cdot10^{-4}$). So no steepness keeps every valid rule while removing every error.

We conjecture that this is no accident \status{conjecture; empirical}. A fallacy becomes systematic because it is a short, natural variant of a valid pattern.

\begin{proposition}[Rate inversion: the intended model need not be a vertex]\src{T4 Prop 7.3}\label{prop:simplicity:inversion}
In \Cref{thm:simplicity:separable}, take $k_r=\ell(r)$ and $r_r=\pi_rg_r$, with three rules: a common valid rule A, with $\ell=10$, $\pi=0.5$, $g=8$; a rare valid rule B, with $\ell=40$, $\pi=0.01$, $g=8$; and the freshman's dream F, with $\ell=8$, $\pi=0.05$, $g=8$. The rates are $0.4$, $0.002$ and $0.05$. As $c$ grows, the selection passes through $\{A,B,F\}$, $\{A,F\}$, $\{A\}$ and $\emptyset$, so no $c$ selects the valid set $\{A,B\}$. In fact, in (bits, residual bits per datum), $\{A,B\}=(50,0.4)$ is Pareto-dominated by $\{A,F\}=(18,0.08)$. So no criterion strictly increasing in both coordinates selects it.

\hfill\leanok{RateThreshold.valid\_not\_minimizer}\ \leanok{RateThreshold.pareto\_dominated}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Apply \Cref{thm:simplicity:separable}. The residual of a set $R$ is $\sum_{r\notin R}\pi_rg_r$.
\end{proof}

This answers the note's vertex question in general. The hull mechanism is right, but the intended model is a vertex only if every valid component out-rates every error (\Cref{cor:simplicity:selectable}). That holds for rare, expensive, idiosyncratic errors. It fails for cheap systematic fallacies, which, we expect, include common student-algebra errors and Euler-style manipulations.

\begin{theorem}[The steepness is not identifiable from imitation data]\src{T5 Thm 4.3, as repaired}\label{thm:simplicity:validation}
\leavevmode
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item Along the population path, $\Risk(h_c)$ is non-increasing as $c$ decreases. So, for a fixed finite grid in the computable version with a noise floor, choosing $c$ by held-out log-loss picks, with probability tending to one as $N\to\infty$, a grid value whose selected hypothesis has least risk among the selected ones; the smallest grid value always qualifies. (At a grid value lying exactly at a hull breakpoint, the hypothesis selected there may alternate between co-minimizers of different risk.) At finite $N$ this can fail, through the $B/c$ term of \Cref{lem:simplicity:empirical}.
\item For every strictly proper scoring rule, the teacher channel strictly maximizes the expected score at every $x$ \citep{gneiting2007strictly}. So every validation criterion that is an empirical proper score on fresh imitation data targets $P^*$, errors included.
\item No imitation-data statistic picks a good $c$ in both worlds of \Cref{thm:simplicity:blind}(iv).
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

The proof is in Appendix~\ref{app:simplicity:validation}. In the note's example, cross-validation picks the error model: held-out log-loss is $1.0$ at $c=10^{-1}$, $0.0112$ on $[10^{-5},10^{-3}]$, and $0$ at $c=10^{-7}$ \status{computed}. A non-proper ``knee'' heuristic (the interior vertex with the widest $\log c$-interval) picks the good model in this example; by (iii), no such rule is right in both twin worlds.

\paragraph{Reading.} Choosing $c$ is a normative act, not an estimate. The note says that ``is this a chair?'' is ``much closer to `should the person who taught me the chair notion consider it a chair?'\,'' than to what that person would say. Part (iii) makes literal the claim that this target is not a function of~$P^*$.

\begin{proposition}[Guard erosion]\src{T5 Prop 4.4, as repaired}\label{prop:simplicity:guard}
Let $\sigma|_G$ guard a schema $\sigma$ (e.g.\ $x/x\to1$ if $x\neq0$). Humans apply $\sigma$ in $G$-contexts (frequency $\pi_G$, per-step gain $g$). In contexts where $\sigma$ applies but $G$ fails (frequency $\pi_{\neg G}$), they play a non-$\sigma$ step. In the $\sigma$-applicable region a hypothesis has three options: \emph{none}: uniform over the $M$ candidates, at cost $0$; \emph{unguarded}: mass $1-\varepsilon+\varepsilon/M$ on the $\sigma$-step, at cost $k_\sigma$; and \emph{guarded}: the same as unguarded in $G$-contexts, and uniform on the non-$\sigma$ steps otherwise, at cost $k_\sigma+k_G$.
Define
\[
\rho_\sigma=\frac{\pi_Gg-\pi_{\neg G}\log_2(1/\varepsilon)}{k_\sigma},\qquad\rho_G=\frac{\pi_{\neg G}\Delta_G}{k_G},\qquad\Delta_G=\log_2(M/\varepsilon)-\log_2(M-1).
\]
If $\rho_G<\rho_\sigma$, then for $c\in(\rho_G,\rho_\sigma)$ the \emph{unguarded, unsound} $\sigma$ is selected, although the teacher never errs. For $c<\rho_G$ the guarded rule is selected, and for $c>\rho_\sigma$ neither is. If $\rho_G\ge\rho_\sigma$, then apart from ties the path goes from guarded to none, and the learner stays sound.
\end{proposition}

In (bits, risk) relative to \emph{none}, the unguarded option is a vertex of the lower hull of the three options iff $\rho_\sigma>\rho_G$; the proof is in Appendix~\ref{app:simplicity:guard}.

Take $\pi_G=0.05$, $\pi_{\neg G}=5\cdot10^{-4}$, $k_\sigma=22$, $k_G=12$, $M=64$ and $\varepsilon=0.05$ \status{computed}. Then $\rho_G=1.8\cdot10^{-4}$ and $\rho_\sigma=1.22\cdot10^{-2}$, so the unguarded rule is selected on $(1.8\cdot10^{-4},1.22\cdot10^{-2})$, a range that includes steepnesses at which ex falso (rate $7.2\cdot10^{-4}$) is kept. With $\pi_G=\pi_{\neg G}=0.002$ instead, $\rho_\sigma<\rho_G$ and there is no unsound phase.

\paragraph{Reading.} What a careful teacher exhibits most rarely is what goes first: side conditions, eigenvariable conditions, edge cases. One unsound schema can trivialize a classical calculus (\Cref{prop:search:post}). With field rules, $0/0=1$ and $0/0=0\cdot0^{-1}=0$ give $1=0$, a negative bag (\Cref{lem:coherence:bag}). So coherence must restore what simplicity erodes.

\paragraph{Further pathologies} \src{T5 \S4.4}. Four more are stated precisely in Appendix~\ref{app:simplicity:further}. \emph{Error envelopes:} errors concentrated in a cheaply describable region are learned at the envelope's own rate, and where their density there exceeds $\frac12$ the learner predicts the error label throughout the region. \emph{Parasitic errors:} an error that is cheap given a valid rule ($K(e\mid v)\ll K(e)$, as FD is given $(ab)^n=a^nb^n$) can enter the hull bundled with it, so a valid rule may be kept only because of the error it subsidizes. \emph{Distribution dependence:} a valid rule that is rare in training stays discarded as $N\to\infty$. \emph{Language relativity:} for any $c>0$, some prefix code makes any given set of positive-gain separable components the unique selection; between universal machines marginal costs move by at most $2c_{UV}$, negligible for 10\,000-bit errors but decisive for 20-bit schemas, so the note's ``simplicity prior defined in terms of existing understanding'' cuts both ways.

\subsection{Inference rules: the division of labour}\label{sec:simplicity:division}

In \emph{situation-typed step imitation}, a context $x$ is a premise set and a goal (\Cref{sec:setting}), and $y$ is the next human step among $M_\tau$ candidates. Contexts fall into computable situation types $\tau$ of frequency $\pi_\tau$. In type $\tau$ the human applies a schema $\sigma_\tau\in\Sigma^P$, genuine or fallacious, with probability $1-\varepsilon$, and otherwise moves uniformly.

Modelling type $\tau$ costs $\ell(\sigma_\tau)+O(1)$ bits and gains $g_\tau=\log_2M_\tau-H_\tau$ per step. Its rate is therefore $\rho_\tau=\pi_\tau g_\tau/(\ell(\sigma_\tau)+O(1))$, and by \Cref{thm:simplicity:separable} it is selected iff $\rho_\tau>c$. The induced verifier accepts the instances of the selected schemas. It is sound (\Cref{def:setting:soundness}) iff no error schema is selected and no guard has eroded.

\begin{definition}[Admissible family, witnesses]\src{T5 \S5.2, as repaired}\label{def:simplicity:admissible}
The components are $V\sqcup F$ (valid, erroneous), with net values $\nu_j=r_j-c\,k_j$. The \emph{admissible family} $\Admiss\subseteq2^{V\sqcup F}$ is the intersection of the filters in use: $\Ac$-coherence (\Cref{def:setting:channels}), W-adequacy, and coherence with designated meaning postulates $\Ax$. Here W-adequacy means that no claim the world refutes is derivable. The family is down-closed and contains $2^V$. A \emph{witness} for $e\in F$ is a minimal $W\subseteq V$ with $W\cup\{e\}\notin\Admiss$. It is the negative bag of \Cref{lem:coherence:bag}, seen from the rules. Let $V_+=\{v:\nu_v>0\}$ and $F_+=\{e:\nu_e>0\}$.
At a given $c$ we impose two conditions. \emph{Error-independence}: for $U\subseteq V$, $U\cup F'\in\Admiss$ iff $U\cup\{e\}\in\Admiss$ for each $e\in F'$. \emph{Valid dominance}: every $e\in F_+$ with a witness inside $V_+$ has $\beta(e)>\sum_{F_+}\nu$. Here $\beta(e)$ is the least value of $\sum_B\nu$ over sets $B\subseteq V_+$ meeting all of $e$'s witnesses inside $V_+$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Division of labour]\src{T5 Thm 5.2, as repaired}\label{thm:simplicity:division}
Fix $c$, and assume separability, error-independence and valid dominance at $c$. Then the optimal admissible sets are exactly the admissible sets $S^*(c)\cup Z$ with $Z\subseteq\{\nu=0\}$, where
\[
S^*(c)=V_+\cup\{e\in F_+:V_+\cup\{e\}\in\Admiss\}.
\]
So, up to zero-value ties, \emph{simplicity} removes exactly the errors of rate at most $c$, coherent or not; \emph{coherence}, the \emph{world} and \emph{postulates} remove exactly the errors that clash with the valid rules actually kept ($V_+$, plus $\Ax$), whatever their rate; the residue is the high-rate errors that pass every filter; and valid rules of rate at most $c$ are lost, whatever the other filters do.
\end{theorem}

The proof is in Appendix~\ref{app:simplicity:division}: valid dominance makes it better to restore the valid rules an error's witnesses need than to keep the error. A brute-force check of 17\,806 instances found no violation \status{computed}.

\begin{corollary}[Best steepness given the other filters]\src{T5 Cor 5.2a, as repaired}\label{cor:simplicity:window}
Assume error-independence and valid dominance at every $c$ considered, and $k_j>0$. Then every optimal admissible set contains $V$ and no error iff
\[
\max\{\rho_e:V\cup\{e\}\in\Admiss\}<c<\min_{v\in V}\rho_v,\qquad\text{with }\max\emptyset:=0.
\]
\end{corollary}

The proof, and a counterexample showing that valid dominance is essential, are in Appendix~\ref{app:simplicity:division}.

So coherence and the world \emph{widen the window}: the rates of incoherent or refutable errors stop mattering. Simplicity is needed only against coherent, adequate, low-rate errors, such as idiosyncratic false lemmas and quus-like patches (\Cref{prop:coherence:quus}).

\begin{proposition}[Repair by sacrifice]\src{T5 Prop 5.3, as repaired}\label{prop:simplicity:sacrifice}
Assume error-independence. Let $e\in F_+$ have a witness inside $V_+$, and let $B\subseteq V_+$ be a cheapest set meeting all of $e$'s witnesses inside $V_+$, with $\beta(e)=\sum_B\nu<\nu_e$. Then the clean set $S_{\rm clean}=V_+\cup\{e'\in F_+:V_+\cup\{e'\}\in\Admiss\}$ is not optimal. Indeed, $(V_+\setminus B)\cup\{e\}\cup(S_{\rm clean}\cap F)$ is admissible, with value $\val(S_{\rm clean})+\nu_e-\beta(e)$.
\end{proposition}

The proof is in Appendix~\ref{app:simplicity:division}. Here is a computed instance \status{computed}. AC's Post witness (\Cref{cor:coherence:fallacies}), with $p:=\bot$ and $q:=A\to A$, needs only ${\to}$I. If ${\to}$I is rare, with rate $8.7\cdot10^{-4}$, then at $c=5\cdot10^{-4}$ the coherent optimum is $\{$AC, MP, $\top$I$\}$. \emph{The learner drops ${\to}$I to keep the fallacy.}

This instance assumes $\Ac=\{\emptyset\}$. With a designated context such as $\{p_0\to q_0,q_0,\neg p_0\}$, MP is itself a witness partner, and AC is dropped instead. The phenomenon is Lakatos's monster-barring \citep{lakatos1976proofs} run backwards: the repair is chosen by description cost, not by validity. It refines \Cref{thm:coherence:elimination}, whose criterion must be read relative to the rules the learner keeps.

\paragraph{Meaning postulates.} The note lists a separate route: ``you specify properties of the thing or notion sometimes''. For example, ``1+1=2 won't be true if you accidentally assign 1->rabbit and 2->chicken from a demonstration (for any reasonable meaning of plus)''. Formally, a finite set $\Ax$ of sentences endorsed as constitutive of a notion enters as designated premises. These are Carnap's meaning postulates \citep{carnap1952meaning}.

\begin{proposition}[Postulates are rate-independent]\src{T5 Prop 5.4, as repaired}\label{prop:simplicity:postulates}
\leavevmode
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Assume the hypotheses of \Cref{thm:simplicity:division} at every $c$, with $\Ax$ designated, and let $\rho_e>0$. Up to zero-value ties, $e\notin S^*(c)$ iff $\rho_e\le c$ or some witness of $e$ lies inside $V_+(c)=\{v:\rho_v>c\}$. Hence $e$ is excluded at \emph{every} $c$ iff some witness $W$ of $e$ has $\rho_w\ge\rho_e$ for all $w\in W$.
\item Let $\Ax_{\rm num}=\{1+1=2,\ 1^2=1,\ 2^2=4,\ 4\neq2\}$, and keep substitution of equals. Then $(1+1)^2=1^2+1^2$ rewrites to $4=2$, so FD is excluded however frequent it is. Without postulates, FD together with the ring rules derives $2=0$. That is coherent, since FD holds in characteristic~2.
\item The demonstrations (``1'', one rabbit) and (``2'', two chickens) fit both numeral $\mapsto$ cardinality and numeral $\mapsto$ kind. With $+$ read as disjoint union, the postulate $1+1=2$ holds under the first reading only.
\item Postulates eliminate exactly the readings outside $\Mod(\Ax)$. For first-order arithmetic, the non-standard models remain (\Cref{thm:coherence:residue}).
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

Part (a) is proved in Appendix~\ref{app:simplicity:division}; (b)--(d) are direct. Here ``rate-independent'' means that exclusion needs only that the witness rules be kept. For FD the witness rule is substitution of equals, of rate $4.07\cdot10^{-2}$, against $\rho_{\rm FD}=2.46\cdot10^{-3}$ \status{computed}. Random numerical evaluation through the world channel also refutes FD \citep{schwartz1980fast,zippel1979probabilistic}. Part (c) is Quine's gavagai \citep{quine1960word}, with a cure.

\paragraph{Reading: avowal versus performance.} Postulates are the teacher's \emph{avowals} (``1+1=2'', ``MP is valid, AC is not'', ``a chair is for sitting''), not the teacher's \emph{performance}. The steeper penalty sees only performance. This is where the debate between \citet{stich1980justification} and \citet{cohen1981can} lands \src{L11 \S2}.

Cohen grounds norms in a systematization of ordinary judgments; Stich and Nisbett had argued, against reflective equilibrium as a test of justification, that systematic fallacies such as the gambler's fallacy can survive such a systematization. Performance data indeed cannot separate a fallacy from a rule with the same profile (\Cref{thm:simplicity:blind}(iv)). Avowals are a second channel: few bits that exclude whole high-rate classes of errors. Where an avowal is itself wrong, as with naive comprehension, coherence catches it (\Cref{sec:informal}).

The note's abstract/concrete example, ``if something is abstract, then it usually helps a lot to study examples to understand it'', is a cross-notion postulate: it ties the notion to another notion with independent evidence, turning a labelling error into a W-refutation. This makes ``should the person who taught me [\dots] consider it a chair'' operational: the norm is whatever fits the notion's avowed role, checked against the other channels.

\subsection{Verdict}\label{sec:simplicity:verdict}

\emph{Where the proposal is vindicated.} Function induction with private randomness has no universal-hypothesis pathology, by exactly the mechanism the note names: ``you have to pay the likelihood term separately at every $x$'' (\Cref{thm:simplicity:pricing}). Shared randomness pays for partial knowledge once, product models gain exponentially little from it, so the product frontier kinks, as proved for parity teachers (\Cref{thm:simplicity:kink}). The convex-hull argument is correct; the change of derivative is the vertex condition, plus uniqueness at the vertex (\Cref{lem:simplicity:hull}). The note's example works, asymptotically, on a window of more than three orders of magnitude (\Cref{cor:simplicity:example}).

\emph{Where it fails.} The penalty is validity-blind: it sorts by rate, betting that norms are high-rate structure and errors low-rate structure (\Cref{thm:simplicity:blind}). The bet is right for idiosyncratic slips and wrong for cheap, frequent fallacies (\Cref{prop:simplicity:inversion}). It costs rare valid rules and, worse, rarely exercised guards, so the learner can be less sound than its teacher (\Cref{prop:simplicity:guard}). The good $c$ cannot be estimated from imitation data (\Cref{thm:simplicity:validation}), and private randomness fails whenever inputs carry the same person's earlier labels (\Cref{prop:simplicity:block}).

\emph{What does the normativity work.} Of the note's other ideas, ``you specify properties of the thing or notion'' is decisive: postulates are avowal rather than performance, and they remove high-rate fallacies at every $c$, provided the valid rules in a witness out-rate the fallacy (\Cref{prop:simplicity:postulates}). The idea ``what is it that this person is trying to teach me'' calls for a teacher model with a competence/performance split. \citet{armstrong2018occam} show that a policy does not determine its decomposition into planner and reward, and that a simplicity prior over decompositions does not single out the intended one. That is \Cref{thm:simplicity:blind}(iv) in another guise.

The division of labour comes to this: simplicity removes the \emph{expensive} errors; coherence removes the \emph{incoherent} ones; the world removes the \emph{refutable} ones; postulates remove those that \emph{violate avowed properties}. What survives is the meanings humans actually use. Steeper simplicity still has a job no other channel does: removing idiosyncratic, coherent, unrefutable errors (\Cref{tab:simplicity:toy}, first row). It is a good third filter, not a route to normativity on its own.

In the terms of \Cref{sec:philosophy}, a learner fitted to practice alone is in narrow reflective equilibrium with its teacher, with or without a simplicity penalty. Stich's objection applies to it exactly. What removes coherent systematic fallacies is information that is not a function of performance: avowed postulates, and an independent world channel. That world channel is what makes the equilibrium wide.

Besides the open problems of \Cref{sec:open} (kinks beyond parities, a data-free choice of $c$, competence/performance models), a product-model algorithmic statistics and the best block size for a step imitator remain open. The simulations are in \Cref{sec:experiments}.
