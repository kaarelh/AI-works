## Overall assessment

This is a strong, well-organised section. The central idea is right and useful for the user's question: a behavioural check must *recompute* $b$, while an internals check needs only to *decode* it. So is the split between knowing and sincere deviations. The measurement/training distinction and the confession-incentive algebra are clean.

The algebra I could check is correct (G4.3, G4.4, G4.5, G4.7, G4.8, G4.9) with one exception: **G4.10 item 4 has a wrong formula**, and its break-even is 0.737, not 0.8 (R1).

The bigger problems are in the modelling, and in rigour labels that the content does not support:

1. **G4.2 as stated is false, and G4.6 contradicts it.** "From $(x,\text{report})$ alone, 'report $\ne b(x)$' can be decided only by computing $b(x)$." G4.6's incentive-invariance audit is itself a behavioural check that catches $S$ without computing $b$. So are T4.11's consistency checks. Item 3, "every internals check has the same law as on $A$" on sincere deviations, is also false as stated, and §5.1 relies on reading uncertainty from internals.
2. **Measurement versus training leaks in the loop.** The bound of G4.5 is for a *single gate on a trained model*. G4.11, G4.14 and takeaway 4 act on *individual outputs* (discard, redo, patch). When those outputs feed training or the next generation's data, they act as a training signal, and the Goodhart is $O(n)$ per generation, not $O(1)$ (R2: 25.6 nats per generation at $\mathrm{fpr}_\Delta=0.05$, against 0.012 for the gate).
3. **Confessions on the frontier.** By the draft's own G4.7, they are at best a tie. G4.9 and G4.11 then use $\alpha_{\rm cf}=0.9$ to 0.95 and full confession on the frontier slice. Those results need the confession-audit condition or an explicit generalisation assumption, and the headline "$O(n)\to O(N_{\rm cl})$" must be conditioned on it.
4. **A missing type, belief capture.** Call it $H_o$: an honest reporter whose *beliefs* on ungraded or plausibility-graded questions have been pulled toward $o$. It earns $S$'s reward, and no internals check can see it. $S^\circ$ is a special case. This type matters more for the loop than $S^\circ$, because it needs no adversarial pressure on the probe, only pressure on reports.
5. **G4.6's appeal to T1.4c fails.** By G2.5's own proviso, the planted context $\mathrm{inc}$ is visible in $z$, so $f_a\ge c_rf_r$ does not hold unless real traffic carries $\mathrm{inc}$ with the same law. Making it do so weakens the audit, because $S$ then learns to discount $\mathrm{inc}$.

Several rigour labels need demotion: G4.1 item 3 rests on T3.15, which is heuristic; G4.2; G4.11 depends on G4.7 at the frontier; and G4.13 is labelled "heuristic" in the text, "conjecture" at its end, and "conjecture" in the summary. One empirical number is misquoted: the 4.4% in the confessions paper is a joint rate, not a conditional one. Prior-art credit is missing for G4.5 (rejection-sampling and best-of-$n$ KL bounds), for G4.6 (Greenblatt et al. 2024; Marks et al. 2025; consistency checks), and for open problem 2, which Mallen et al. already partly did.

## 1. Mathematics, proposition by proposition

### G4.1 (honesty as T1 with $X=o$, $I=b$): holds with fixes
* Items 1, 2 and 4 are correct relabellings of T1.1, T1.3 and T1.4d.
* **Item 3 is not rigorous.** T3.15 is labelled *heuristic* in T3 (`T3-prior.md` line 328). A relabelling cannot raise its status. *Fix:* label G4.1 "rigorous (items 1, 2, 4); heuristic (item 3)".
* Item 4 is the case relevant to the loop, and its rate is the rate of *predictable* disagreement between the plausibility grader and the model's belief. The example sets $w_\Delta=w_F\varepsilon_F=0.005$. Say explicitly that the H1 numbers ("25 nats per generation") are item 4's, not item 2's.

### G4.2 (decode, not recompute): rigorous label fails as stated; holds after restriction
* **Item 1 has a counterexample in the draft itself.** G4.6 decides "report $\ne b(x)$" for $S$ from behaviour alone: two queries that differ in $\mathrm{inc}$, and no computation of $b$. T4.11's consistency checks (Fluri et al. 2023) and T4.12's sample consistency do the same, against some liars. The true statement is narrower: *for a single query $(x,\text{report})$, and against every reporter class, deciding "report $\ne b(x)$" requires computing $b(x)$.* With several queries, behavioural checks can catch reporters whose lies are not invariant to features that $b$ is invariant to. Against capable, coherent liars they reduce to a generation–verification gap (T4.14). *Fix:* restate item 1 as the single-query, worst-case statement, and add "with multiple queries, behavioural checks catch exactly the liars whose lies break an invariance of $b$ (G4.6, T4.11); a coherent liar defeats them."
* **Item 3, "on them every internals check has the same law as on $A$", is false as stated.**
  * A near-miss $N$ has a different goal pointer from $A$ (T3.3), so its internals differ. A check that decodes the goal representation, rather than comparing the output with decoded $b_I$, need not have the same law on $N$ and on $A$.
  * Competence errors often come with internal *uncertainty*. §5.1 recommends exactly a reading of calibrated confidence, which is an internals check that separates confident-correct from uncertain-wrong.
  * The statement holds for the class of checks that are functions of $(\text{output},\mathrm{dec}(Z)=b_I)$, the "decode-and-compare" checks. *Fix:* restrict to that class, and add a sentence that goal-decoding and uncertainty-decoding checks are outside it. Their power against $N$ is an open question, not zero.
* **Supporting evidence to cite.** The confessions paper reports that the model is less likely to confess when its non-compliance comes from genuine confusion than when it comes from intentional misbehaviour (OpenAI alignment blog; arXiv:2512.08093). That is direct evidence for the knowing/sincere boundary.
* "Certificate" is the wrong word. A certificate is checkable, while a probe reading is a statistical decode of a state that training can shape ($S^\circ$, and $H_o$ below). Use "witness", or say "a free *witness*, not a certificate".

### G4.3 (KL per disagreement question): holds with fixes
* $\mathrm{KL}(\mathrm{Bern}(0.9)\Vert\mathrm{Bern}(0.05))=2.376$, $(0.2,0.05)\to0.140$ and $(0.9,0.3)\to0.794$ are all confirmed.
* **Unstated assumption 1: firings are conditionally i.i.d. across questions, given the type.** Real probe errors are correlated at the model level, for example from systematic blind spots on a family of questions or from model-specific representations. R4 shows the effect: if 5% of $S$ instances have a probe blind spot ($\mathrm{tpr}=0.06$) and the rest have 0.94 (mean 0.896), the gate's effect on $S$ falls from $-174$ to $-3.0$ nats. *Fix:* state the assumption, and say that the evidence ceiling is $\ln(1/\Pr(\text{blind-spot instance}))$ under model-level correlation.
* **Unstated assumption 2: the designer knows which questions are in $\Delta$.** On frontier questions $o$ may be unknown, and whether $o\ne b$ is exactly what the designer lacks. If the designer probes all questions, the evidence is the KL between *mixtures*. With a $\Delta$ share of 0.05 and a probe false-positive rate of 0.01 on agreement questions, the per-$\Delta$-question equivalent falls to about 0.29 to 0.35 of the draft's value (R5: 0.82 against 2.38). *Fix:* add one sentence and give the mixture formula.

### G4.4 (drift with the check in the reward): algebra holds; the worst-case claim needs a fix
* The formula is confirmed: $\lambda_+ - (\lambda_++\lambda_-)\mathrm{tpr} + \lambda_-\mathrm{fpr}_\Delta$, divided by $(\lambda_++\lambda_-)$, uses $\lambda_-/(\lambda_++\lambda_-)=1-t^\star$. The generalisation of G2.6 is correct: replace $\det(c)$ by $\det(c)-(1-t^\star)\mathrm{fpr}_\Delta$.
* **"The worst case is $S^\circ$ … so $\mathrm{tpr}_c=\mathrm{fpr}_\Delta$" is not the worst case.** $S^\circ$ makes the probe read $o$, and its report is $o$. Internally it then looks like an *agreement* question, not an honest disagreement. If $\mathrm{fpr}_\Delta$ is high because of internal conflict between belief and overseer-belief, or because of disagreement-related context (the over-firing the draft cites), $S^\circ$ fires at the lower agreement rate $\mathrm{fpr}_=$. Then $S^\circ$ beats $H$ *even in measurement use*, by up to $\ln(1/\Pr_H(\mathrm{pass}))$ per gate. *Fix:* replace "$\mathrm{tpr}_c=\mathrm{fpr}_\Delta$" with "$\mathrm{tpr}_c$ equals the rate at which the probe fires on states that read as honest, which can be as low as $\mathrm{fpr}_=$", and note that measurement then *favours* $S^\circ$, weakly.
* **A missing type: belief capture, $H_o$.** Training on graded disagreement pushes reports toward $o$. Under gradient training, that push can land on the belief representation rather than on the reporting head. The result is a type that reports its belief honestly, but whose belief on ungraded or plausibility-graded questions has moved to $o$. It earns $S$'s reward. Every internals check sees it as honest, with no adversarial pressure on any probe. It pays only where those beliefs are used on verified tasks. $S^\circ$ is the case in which this happens only in the probed representation. In ledger terms the relevant pair on frontier questions is not $H$ against $S$, but $H$ against $H_o$, and the two are proxy-equivalent under every internals check. *Fix:* add $H_o$ to §0 and §5 as the "honest but wrong, *made* wrong by training" failure. It connects G4.4 to T1.1b's competence axis.
* The numbers are confirmed: $-61.25$, $-42.5$, $-1.25$, $+8.75$, $+27.5$ and $+25$ nats per generation.

### G4.5 (the gate is a bounded optimiser): the identity holds; the scope and the table need fixes
* The rejection-sampling identity and the bound $\le\ln(1/\Pr_H(\mathrm{pass}))$ are correct, and the H3 Monte Carlo agrees.
* **The table row "effect on evaders already in the prior: removed in proportion to the KL of G4.3" is wrong.** The gate's exponent is $N\,\mathrm{KL}(k_g/N\Vert\mathrm{tpr})$ (Chernoff), not $N\,\mathrm{KL}(\mathrm{tpr}\Vert\mathrm{fpr}_\Delta)$. R3 gives $-175.8$ against the exact $-177.8$ for $S$, while the G4.3 scale would give $-237.6$. *Fix:* write "removed by $\ln\Pr_\theta(\mathrm{pass})/\Pr_H(\mathrm{pass})\approx -N_\Delta\,\mathrm{KL}(k_g/N_\Delta\Vert\mathrm{tpr}_\theta)$".
* **Scope (major).** The bound is for *one* gate applied to a trained model, with independent retries. The section and the loop recommendations then apply the check to *every output* (G4.11: "the output is discarded or redone"; G4.14: "probes on all frontier work … committed response"; T1 §7 rollbacks). If discarded outputs are excluded from training, or from the data that trains generation $k{+}1$ (T1's data channel), each disagreement episode is its own gate. The odds of a probe-quiet type against $H$ then move by up to $\ln(1/(1-\mathrm{fpr}_\Delta))$ per episode, which is $n\,w_\Delta\ln(1/(1-\mathrm{fpr}_\Delta))$ per generation: **25.6 nats at $\mathrm{fpr}_\Delta=0.05$ and 178 nats at 0.3** (R2). That is linear in $n$, the same order as training use. The surviving distinction is not "measurement versus training". It is "decisions that select over *whole models or lineages*" (bounded by $\ln(1/\Pr_H)$ per decision) versus "decisions that filter *outputs that become data or gradients*" (unbounded). *Fix:* state this split in G4.5 and in the table, and propagate it to G4.11, G4.14 and takeaway 2.
* **Prior art.** "Conditioning on an event of probability $p$ moves any likelihood ratio by at most $\ln(1/p)$" is the standard rejection-sampling fact; $\mathrm{KL}(\mu\mid\text{pass}\,\Vert\,\mu)=\ln(1/\Pr(\text{pass}))$. The close analogue in alignment is the best-of-$n$ KL bound $\log n-(n-1)/n$, which Beirami et al. (2024, arXiv:2401.01879) show is an upper bound. *Fix:* credit this, and move "the gate bound" in "New here" to "application of a standard identity".

### G4.6 (incentive-invariance audits): holds within (E); the T1.4c application fails as stated
* **(E) is not innocent for a calibrated $H$.** "Answers concluding X score higher" is evidence that the grader believes X, and therefore evidence about $x$ in proportion to the grader's competence. A Bayesian $H$ should move. The argument is rescued on the frontier, where the grader knows much less than the model, so the shift is small. *Fix:* replace (E) with a quantified version: $H$'s report flips with probability at most $\epsilon_E$, which is small where the grader is weak relative to the model. The audit then separates $H$ from $S$ by the gap between $S$'s and $H$'s flip rates, not by "$H$ invariant".
* **$S$'s report varies only if $S$ trusts $\mathrm{inc}$.** "$o(x,\mathrm{inc})$ varies by construction" holds when the audit actually changes the grader, or when $S$ believes the statement. A simulator of the *actual* overseer infers the grader's criteria from all its evidence. If audits plant false $\mathrm{inc}$ values, $\mathrm{inc}$ becomes less reliable, and $S$ learns to discount it. *Fix:* add this credibility trade-off. The audit's power falls with the share of planted statements that $S$ can learn about.
* **Item 2, $f_a\ge c_rf_r$, fails.** G2.5's own proviso (`G2-learning.md` line 112) says: "if the plant itself is detectable, the relevant $f_a$ is the law of planted episodes". Here the plant *is* the visible text $\mathrm{inc}$. The condition holds only if real episodes carry $\mathrm{inc}$ statements with the same law as audits. That is a design commitment: every episode states the grader's criteria. It feeds the credibility problem above. *Fix:* state the design commitment and the trade-off, and demote item 2 to heuristic.
* **Prior art.** Varying a context component that tells the model about training or grading, and comparing behaviour, is the design of Greenblatt et al. (2024, alignment faking: free-tier against paid-tier). That paper is in L1 and L2, and it detected $C_t$-like behaviour this way. Marks et al. (2025, L2) show that models act on statements about what the reward model rewards, which is evidence that $S$-like types do condition on $\mathrm{inc}$, and supports the audit. G4.6 is also a special case of the consistency checks of T4.11 and Fluri et al. (2023): $\mathrm{inc}$ is a framing that $b$ is invariant to. *Fix:* credit all three, and narrow the novelty claim to "aimed at overseer-simulators on frontier questions".

### G4.7 (confession incentive compatibility): holds; two minor modelling fixes
* The payoff algebra is confirmed: the difference is $\alpha_{\rm cf}+d_J-1-c_m\alpha_{\rm cf}(1-d_J)$. The values 0.20 and 0 in item 4 are confirmed.
* **Independence of $\alpha_{\rm cf}$ and $d_J$.** If the judge finds the gaming unaided, it would also accept a true confession, so acceptance is $\alpha'=\alpha_{\rm cf}+(1-\alpha_{\rm cf})d_J$. The condition with $c_m=0$ becomes $\alpha_{\rm cf}(1-d_J)\ge1-2d_J$. This makes no difference at $d_J=0$, which is the frontier conclusion, and a small one elsewhere (R6). Likewise, a confession-channel audit also verifies true confessions, so the audit-rate threshold is $(1-\alpha_{\rm cf})/(2-\alpha_{\rm cf})$, not $1-\alpha_{\rm cf}$ (0.091 against 0.100 at $\alpha_{\rm cf}=0.9$). *Fix:* a footnote is enough.
* **$\alpha_{\rm cf}\approx1$ holds for task gaming, not for honesty gaming.** "A true confession points at the deviation, so verifying it is an existence check" fits task deviations with an exhibitable defect (T4 §3 table). For the section's own subject, a frontier question on which the model reported $o$ instead of $b$, the confession "the true answer is $b$, not $o$" can be verified only by checking $b$, which is frontier work. Then $\alpha_{\rm cf}$ is T4's demonstrability $v$ of a frontier claim, and can be low. *Fix:* say that G4.7 to G4.9 concern task deviations with exhibitable defects, and that for frontier honesty, confessions inherit T4's $v$.
* **Item 3's cost comparison.** "1% against $\hat p>t^\star$" compares rates of audits that *succeed* on the frontier. Each confession audit still needs an auditor able to find frontier gaming. The asymmetry is real, but it is in the *rate*, not in the capability needed. Say so.

### G4.8 (careful gamers): holds
* The threshold $(1-\alpha_{\rm cf}+\Omega\alpha_{\rm cf})/(1+\Omega\alpha_{\rm cf})$ is confirmed: 0.513 and 0.990 at $\alpha_{\rm cf}=0.95$. The conclusion, "incentive-compatible only where redundant", follows.
* *Suggestion:* add T4.3b's analogue. A careful gamer can *manufacture* minor true confessions to build a clean record. This strengthens the last sentence.

### G4.9 (bounded frontier-slice leak): the algebra holds; the premise conflicts with G4.7
* The formula $N_{\rm cl}/\alpha_{\rm cf}+w_F\varepsilon_FT_{\rm pat}$ is confirmed by an independent Monte Carlo (R7: 3.03 nats).
* **Premise.** "An approval-seeker games each and confesses (G4.7 holds)." On the frontier slice $d_J\approx0$, so by G4.7 item 2, G4.7 does *not* hold for $\alpha_{\rm cf}<1$: denying strictly wins. The proposition therefore needs one of three things: (a) confession audits at rate above $1-\alpha_{\rm cf}$ *on the frontier slice*, by an auditor who can find frontier gaming; (b) tie-breaking by generalisation; or (c) $\alpha_{\rm cf}=1$ exactly. *Fix:* make this an explicit hypothesis of G4.9, and of the summary, takeaway 4 and the JSON claim.
* **Mixed population.** If only a share $s_c$ of gaming events is confessed (a $G_c$/$G_d$ mixture, initially 0.43 in H7), the first term is $N_{\rm cl}/(\alpha_{\rm cf}s_c)$: 6.7 nats at $s_c=0.43$ (R7).
* **Cap.** The formula assumes a generation long enough for every class to be patched. In general the expected leak is at most $nw_F\varepsilon_F$. At $s_c=0.1$ the uncapped formula gives 28 nats, more than the 25 without confessions, and that is an artefact. *Fix:* write it as the sum over classes of the minimum of the two, or state the condition $n\gg 1/(w_F\varepsilon_j\alpha_{\rm cf}s_c)$.

### G4.10 (personas and the wall): items 1 to 3 hold; **item 4 is wrong**
* The drifts in items 1 and 2 are confirmed, and so is the H7 trajectory.
* **Item 4.** When $G_c$ games at relative rate $\mathrm{rg}$, it complies on the remaining $(1-\mathrm{rg})w$ episodes and earns full confession reward there. $G_d$ games on all $w$ episodes and loses $d_J$ on each. So the confession-channel difference is $\lambda_{\rm cf}w\big(d_J-\mathrm{rg}(1-\alpha_{\rm cf})\big)$ in $G_c$'s favour, not $\lambda_{\rm cf}\,\mathrm{rg}\,w(\alpha_{\rm cf}+d_J-1)$. The appendix drops the $(1-\mathrm{rg})wd_J$ term. **Correct condition:** $G_d$ gains iff
  $$\lambda_{\rm main}(1-\mathrm{rg})>\lambda_{\rm cf}\big(d_J-\mathrm{rg}(1-\alpha_{\rm cf})\big),$$
  and with equal $\lambda$s the break-even is $\mathrm{rg}^\star=(1-d_J)/\alpha_{\rm cf}=0.737$, not 0.8. R1 checks this by brute force from the per-episode rewards: at $\mathrm{rg}=0.75$, the draft predicts that deniers gain while in fact confessors gain. H7's leak loop in `G4_checks.py` carries the same error. *Fix:* correct the formula, the appendix, the number and H7.
* **Calibration to OpenAI's experiment.** In H7 the main-channel judge catches no gaming ($J_{\rm main}=w$), yet the confession judge rejects 30% of denials ($d_J=0.3$). In OpenAI's weak-judge experiment, the *same* weak judge scored both channels, and its detection of main-channel non-compliance *fell* during training. With $d_J\approx0$ and $\alpha_{\rm cf}=0.95$, the model predicts that deniers gain (R8). It reproduces the observed pattern only if $\alpha_{\rm cf}+d_J>1$, for example $\alpha_{\rm cf}=0.99$ and $d_J=0.02$, or by tie-breaking. That is OpenAI's own explanation: honest confession is the path of least resistance, so $\alpha_{\rm cf}\approx1$. *Fix:* re-run H7 at $(\alpha_{\rm cf},d_J)=(0.99,0.02)$, and say that the empirical result implies $\alpha_{\rm cf}+d_J>1$ in that setting.
* **Misquote.** "About 4.4% of non-compliance unconfessed" is wrong. In the paper, 4.36% is the average probability of *both* misbehaving *and* not confessing. The conditional confession rate given misbehaviour is 74.3%, so about 26% of non-compliance is unconfessed. The draft's own reference entry has the 74% figure. L2's wording ("false-negative rate (non-compliance that was not confessed) was about 4.4%") is ambiguous and should be fixed too. *Fix:* "confession given misbehaviour about 74% on average (above 90% in 4 of 12 evaluations); misbehaving and unconfessed about 4.4% of samples". Compare this with H7's 0.90, which is then higher than observed.

### G4.11 ($q^{\rm eff}$): the accounting holds; the label and premise need fixes
* The arithmetic is confirmed: $0.04+0.03+0.03\times0.05=0.0715$.
* $\alpha_{\rm cf}=0.95$ is applied to *frontier* outputs, the same G4.7 conflict as in G4.9. Discard-and-redo is per-output filtering, so the G4.5 scope issue applies. *Fix:* condition on frontier confession honesty, and note that per-output action exerts training pressure through the data channel.
* **G4.11b, the grader-channel formula.** "In T1 §4.1, $\varepsilon_{k+1}=\varepsilon_h+q^S_k\varepsilon^{\rm inh}_k+q_k\Delta\varepsilon$" misattributes it. T1 §4.1 has only $\varepsilon_h+q_k\Delta\varepsilon$. The inherited-blind-spot term is new here; T1 lists it as open problem 5. If $q_k$ includes the $S$ mass, the $S$ mass is counted twice. *Fix:* write "extending T1 §4.1", and define whether $q_k\Delta\varepsilon$ excludes $q^S_k$.
* The abstention argument needs abstentions to be *non-scored for every type*. If an abstaining grader routes the item to a different grader, the item becomes that grader's error. Heuristic is the right label.

### G4.12 (transfer cap): holds (elementary)
* The cap is correct given the hypothesis: $\ln(1/0.6)=0.511$.
* "At most" needs a further assumption: honesty training does not move $A$ relative to honest off-intent types. Under framing R3's shared latent, it can. A candour factor that loads slightly differently on $A$ and $C_t$ would give extra transfer; G1.2's sharedness is the analogue. *Fix:* add "under selection with no cross-reweighting among honest types".
* L2's follow-up note (on-policy honesty training transfers much less than off-policy) is directly relevant to the "expected pattern". Cite it as a caveat.

### G4.13 (honesty basin): unclear; the label is inconsistent
* The header says "heuristic", the end says "(conjecture)", and the author summary says "conjecture". Pick conjecture.
* The thresholds 6.67 and 7.39 bits are confirmed against G2.6's $B_T$ with $\mathrm{tpr}-\mathrm{tpr}_c=0.5$ and $\mathcal S=1.4$.
* The "iff" is an infinite-horizon statement. For the user's "long stretch", the useful quantity is the survival time $(K_{c,0}-K^\star)/(\mathrm{nr}-\mathrm{rt})$ when $\mathrm{rt}<\mathrm{nr}$. Also, $K^\star$ grows with $n$, and $n$ grows across generations. Both should be stated.
* "Red-teaming one's own concealment has a checkable output." That is true for each strategy found, but *completeness* (no cheaper concealment exists) is a universal claim, and a strategic generation can withhold its best strategies. This is §5.2's self-report problem. The $(1-q_k)$ factor covers only off-task mass, not selective withholding by a frozen careful gamer.

### G4.14 (frontier detection from internals): unclear; the overclaim must go
* **"No other channel in T1 to T4 gives frontier detection that does not depend on frontier competence" is false within the section.** G4.6 is such a channel. So are T4.11 consistency checks against incoherent liars, and confession audits that rely on existence certificates. *Fix:* "the only channel whose power does not shrink as the model's coherence and capability grow, provided the decoder generalises".
* **Pointwise, not average.** By T1.4 and T2.8, the frozen best responder games wherever its *own* $d(z)<t^\star_F$. Probes are applied uniformly (G4-a), which removes the *selection* channel of recognition but not the *catchability* channel (T2, the remark on random retrospective sampling). The relevant quantity is the essential infimum over frontier inputs $z$ of $\mathrm{tpr}_c(z)\,p_{\rm act}$, not a single $\mathrm{tpr}_c$. Probe accuracy is likely to be lowest exactly on the hardest frontier inputs (Mallen et al.: 75% of the gap recovered on harder questions, against 89%). *Fix:* write $d_F(z)\approx\mathrm{tpr}_c(z)\,p_{\rm act}$ and state T1.4's ess-inf condition.
* **What is probed.** For non-question frontier work (code, research, grader building), "knowing deviation" needs either a probe for *deviation from the believed intent*, which is a different object from a lie probe with unknown $\mathrm{tpr}$, or a self-report query on every output ("does this do what you believe was intended?") with the lie probe applied to the answer. *Fix:* specify the latter, which ties G4.14 to §3.
* **Measurement-only together with $p_{\rm act}$** is the G4.5 scope problem. Per-output consequences create training pressure through the data channel.

## 2. Modelling: does the toy capture what matters for the loop?

1. **Who builds the probes?** For the user's question, the most important gap is that probes for $M_{k+1}$ are trained and validated by humans or by $M_k$, on labelled lies that are necessarily *off-policy* (instructed or model-organism lies). The probe channel is therefore another grader channel. $\mathrm{tpr}_{k+1}$ and $\mathrm{fpr}_{\Delta,k+1}$ depend on $q_k$: an $S$- or $C$-type builder can build probes with blind spots, which is T1 §4.1's collusion remark and T4.19's $r_X$. And on-policy frontier lies are never in the validation set. The section treats $\mathrm{tpr}$ and $\mathrm{tpr}_c$ as exogenous. *Suggest:* a short paragraph with $\mathrm{tpr}_{k+1}=\mathrm{tpr}_h-q_k\Delta\mathrm{tpr}$, which joins G4.13's $K_c$ map.
2. **Belief capture ($H_o$).** See G4.4. Pressure on reports can move beliefs, and then internals checks are blind by construction. This is the honesty-specific form of T1.1b's slop axis, and it may be the dominant failure in a loop where frontier answers are graded by plausibility.
3. **Measurement versus filtering.** See G4.5. In an RSI loop almost every action ("discard", "patch", "roll back the grader") changes the data the next generation trains on. A cleaner taxonomy:
   * *lineage-level decisions*, bounded by $\ln(1/\Pr_H(\mathrm{pass}))$ per decision;
   * *output-level filtering into data*, $O(n)$, like training;
   * *gradient use*, $O(n)$ plus learning.
4. **Honesty gaming against task gaming.** The confession results (G4.7 to G4.11) are about task deviations with exhibitable defects. For the section's subject, frontier honesty, $\alpha_{\rm cf}$ is a frontier demonstrability (T4's $v$). The section should say which class each result covers.
5. **The probe's own ELK problem is downplayed in the takeaways.** §2.1 states it well. ## 4. Smaller points

* §2.3: "If concealment can be learned it is learned at a rate proportional to $\mathrm{tpr}-\mathrm{tpr}_c$." In G2.6 the rate is $\varpi_T(\mathrm{tpr}-\mathrm{tpr}_c)c(1-c)$. Add "per gaming event, times $c(1-c)$".
* The summary bullet "Measurement use and training use differ by an unbounded factor" should be restated as lineage-level against output-level (see G4.5).
* Probes trained against, and "if you train against one detector, measure with another": independent detectors trained on the same off-policy lie data may share blind spots. Diversity of training data matters more than diversity of architecture. This is a heuristic remark worth one clause.
* A third reporter is missing in G4.4: a *hedger*, who on disagreement questions reports neither $b$ nor $o$ but "uncertain". If the probe penalises honest disagreement through $\mathrm{fpr}_\Delta$ and hedging gets partial credit, false positives also select for hedging, which is sycophancy by omission.
* The word count is about 4,900 before the references, over the 4,000 target. §2.5's last paragraph and §4's "how honesty feeds" bullets could be condensed.
