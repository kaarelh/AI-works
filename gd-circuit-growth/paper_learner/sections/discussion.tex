\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What kind of learner this turned out to be}\label{sec:disc-kind}

v3 is not one mechanism. It is three learners under one convex stacker, and the input type decides which of them carries the fit (\cref{sec:learner}).
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Boolean inputs:} enumerate and test. DFG proposes characters and signed-sum blocks from a structured dictionary, admits each one only when its correlation with the residual passes a union-bound test over its whole class, and reads the admitted features out linearly.
\item \emph{Images:} a fixed, data-derived feature hierarchy read out by gradient-boosted trees. Pixels, sums over the 4, 9 and 16 pixels most correlated with each pixel, sums over bars of up to 5 and 9 pixels along lines recovered from the correlations, and (in \code{ggbt}) projections on 300 differences of two training images are computed once. Trees split on them, and nothing in them is fitted.
\item \emph{Other tabular data:} a classical stack of LightGBM, ExtraTrees, nearest-neighbour votes and logistic regression, unchanged since Part 8.
\end{itemize}
The Parts 6--9 paper already described the portfolio as an AutoML-style dispatch by input type \cite{discretelearners2026}, and Part 10 does not change that description. Its two additions sit inside the first two regimes: a wider sum-block search for DFG, and a second booster over a richer bank.

\subsection{Why it works where it works}\label{sec:disc-why}

\paragraph{Boolean targets: an Occam-like sample cost, when the search can propose} \status{Heuristic}. A union-bound test over a class of $N$ candidates admits a feature with residual correlation $\rho$ (in units of the residual's scale) once $n\gtrsim2\ln(4KN/\delta)/\rho^2$ (\cref{sec:dfg}). For a $k$-sparse character, $\ln N\approx k\ln d$; for a signed block on $k$ of $d$ inputs, $\ln N\approx\ln\bigl(\binom dk2^{k-1}\bigr)$. Either way the sample cost is logarithmic in the size of the dictionary: the Occam rate, paid for with FLOPs of enumeration and search that fit inside one network run at $d=30$. This is the classical low-degree algorithm \cite{linial1993constant}, made greedy, budgeted and test-gated, with a second dictionary for symmetric functions of signed sums. Gradient-trained networks pay in samples instead. For parities the companion paper measured rates close to the leap and correlational-SQ rate $d^{k-1}$ \cite{gdcircuit2026,abbe2022msp,abbe2023leap,barak2022hidden,kearns1998sq}. For an even function of one signed sum, a network has to find the block's sign vector in the degree-2 statistic $\E[f\,xx^\top]$ against sampling noise. That is the information-exponent picture of single-index learning \cite{benarous2021online,damian2024gaps}. Networks can trade data for width and compute along a frontier \cite{edelman2023pareto}; DFG sits at an extreme of that trade.

The interpretability analysis sharpens the picture in one respect (\cref{sec:interp-toy}). On the 25 pure symmetric targets, the test is not the bottleneck. The true block would pass at $n=128$ every time, and on each of the 22 found targets the seed-0 10\% error point lies within the doubling below the first grid size at which the model holds the exact block. This is consistent with the data cost being set by when the search first \emph{proposes} the block. Every seed of the block search reads the block's inputs off degree-1 and degree-2 statistics of the residual, so the delay between detectability and discovery tracks the target's weight at those degrees ($\rho=-0.86$). So DFG relies on the same low-order statistics that a network's gradient sees. It needs far fewer samples because it rounds a coarse spectral estimate to an exact signed subset and then tests that subset with a union bound, where a network has to learn the direction to useful precision by noisy local steps \status{Heuristic}.

\paragraph{Where neither has a foothold.} The residue classes mod 3 of 10--14-input counts have almost no Fourier weight at degrees 1 and 2 (none for h6\_00 and f7\_05; coefficients of about 0.002 for fd\_10, undetectable at the study's $n$). DFG's search never proposes their block, although at $n=16384$ the block would pass the test at 134--155 times the threshold (\cref{sec:res-toy-fail,sec:interp-toy}). The networks mostly fail too: none learns f7\_05, and only the most regularized variant learns h6\_00, at $\nstar=12304$ (fd\_10, in \code{dlfamdev}, has no network runs). Their signal sits at high degree on an unknown subset of inputs, which learners that rely on low-order correlations should find hard \cite{kearns1998sq}. Exhaustive search does not help within the budget: there are $1.5\times10^{10}$ to $1.2\times10^{12}$ blocks of the true size, each costing at least $n$ operations to score, against $1.6\times10^{11}$ FLOPs per toy fit. So this is a shared limit, not a loss specific to discrete search \status{Heuristic}. The only target on which a network is clearly better than v3 is of this kind.

\paragraph{Images: locality recovered from data, read out by trees} \status{Heuristic}. On QuickDraw the most correlated pixels of a pixel are its neighbours. The pooling sets are therefore small blobs of the true grid, and the bars are short runs along it. A 2-D embedding of the pooling-set graph matches the grid with $R^2$ 0.97--0.99 at $n\ge3000$ (0.84 at $n=1000$; seed 0; \cref{sec:interp-img}), and the 5-pixel bars lie within a pixel of a straight run in about 96\% of cases (the 9-pixel bars in 63\%). Recovering image topology from such correlations is an old idea \cite{leroux2007topology}, as is grouping inputs into receptive fields by correlation \cite{coates2011receptive}. Boosted trees \cite{ke2017lightgbm} over these fixed local sums then do what a first convolutional layer with a nonlinearity would do, but at fixed positions and without shared weights. The MLP references are denied this structure by design: they are invariant to the pixel permutation, and nothing in their training recovers it. A control and a context run bound how much of the image result is representation.
\begin{itemize}
\item An MLP given v2's pooled sums (\code{mlp_pool}) tied v2 at both of its held-out cells, as in Part 9. v3 is clearly better than it at both (16.5\% and 11.9\% lower error; \code{mlp_pool} seed 0), while v3 and v2 tie there. This is consistent with \code{cgbt}'s bank adding something an MLP on v2's features does not have, but it is not a test of the bars: \code{cgbt} differs from \code{ggbt} in three ways, and no MLP was given the bars.
\item A small CNN that sees the layout (seed 0 only) has lower error than v3 at all three cells where both have run: clearly at \code{qd_a}, $n=10000$ (12.00\% against 14.22\%), and within the tie band at \code{qd_c}, $n=1000$ and 10000 (24.36\% against 26.58\%; 14.84\% against 16.17\%).
\end{itemize}
So on images v3 shows that a learner which never reads the layout, and whose features are fixed subset sums chosen by rank statistics, can beat MLP ensembles that spend 2.0 and 5.0 times its FLOP budget (\code{mlp_strong} at all six held-out cells, \code{mlp_wide} at five of six), and ties the 1024-wide ensemble, which spends 9.2 budgets and a comparable time per fit (0.9--1.6$\times$ v3's). It does not show that discrete search beats gradient-based learning on images.

\paragraph{Tabular data} \status{Heuristic}. Here the result is the known strength of tree ensembles and stacking on medium-sized tabular data \cite{grinsztajn2022tree,shwartzziv2022tabular,wolpert1992stacked,erickson2020autogluon}. Careful regularization and tuning of MLPs narrow that gap \cite{kadra2021welltuned,gorishniy2021revisiting}, and the MLP references here are standard recipes. DFG runs on 2 of the 26 datasets, and there v3's errors equal v1's (mushroom) or differ from them at no cell clearly (kr\_vs\_kp; \cref{sec:res-tabular}).

\subsection{Limits}\label{sec:disc-limits}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Targets without a low-degree foothold.} Residue classes mod $q$ of long counts, and more generally blocks whose weight at degrees 1--2 is small, are found late or never (\cref{sec:res-toy-fail}). Weaker cases cost data. h5s7, an arbitrary accept set of a 13-input count with 0.017 of its variance at degrees 1--2, is detectable at $n=128$ and found at 8192. h5s1 reaches 10\% error in no learner's 3-seed mean (v3's seed 0 at $n\approx14000$); in v3's seed-0 fits the bottleneck is detecting its modulating parity first. The Part 10 toy builder measured the search landscape of fd\_10 and concluded that a random-restart search would need far more than $10^5$ restarts \repo{study/part10/p10_sum/DESIGN.md}.
\item \textbf{The layout is recovered, so locality is used in spirit.} The image members are exactly permutation-equivariant in their objects and never read a pixel index. But the correlations give back local neighbourhoods and lines, which is the information the permuted-pixel protocol was meant to withhold from every learner. On Fashion-MNIST some sets pool mirror-image pixels instead, and the bars do not help there.
\item \textbf{Equal FLOPs are not equal CPU time.} On the held-out image cells v3 uses 0.30--0.58 of its FLOP budget, and the MLP ensembles spend 2.0--9.2 budgets per fit. But a v3 fit takes 1361 and 1464 CPU seconds at $n=60000$, comparable to the CPU-matched \code{mlp_xl} (2196 and 1335 s of wall time, 0.9--1.6$\times$ v3's), and that is the reference v3 only ties. At $n=3000$ on the development sets v3 uses 0.85 of the budget by its own count, and 0.88--0.98 with Part 9's audited correction of the tree member's charge; no compute audit was made of v3 (\cref{sec:res-img-compute}).
\item \textbf{Prediction is expensive and uncharged.} A v2 query costs about $2.8\times10^8$ FLOPs at $n=60000$, about $500\times$ an MLP's, because the nearest-neighbour members scan the training set. v3 adds a second bank projection (about $1.2\times10^7$ FLOPs) and about 4000 more tree traversals per query.
\item \textbf{One seed at $n=60000$.} At $n\ge30000$ every image learner except the baseline \code{mlp} ran seed 0 only, so the two cells that decide the comparison with the CPU-matched ensemble rest on one fit each, and on the test set's binomial noise for the standard error. v3's gain over v2 is below the 10\% threshold at every held-out cell; the best-learner rule used mean $\log_2$ ratios, and v3 is lower in all 14 seed-paired fits, which is a description, not a pre-registered test.
\item \textbf{Small held-out sets.} \code{dlheld7} has 12 targets from one family, and on 5 of its 10 wins default gradient boosting or logistic regression already beats the networks. \code{qd_c} and \code{qd_d} are two 10-class sets from one data source. The 10 fresh tabular datasets of Part 8 give $p=0.11$. A second family or source could change the picture.
\item \textbf{The earlier suites are in-sample.} Each step of the study used the earlier held-out sets as development data (\cref{sec:discipline}). Pooled numbers over all suites summarize the record; they are not held-out estimates. On the toy side the margin against the one network variant run on every suite is smallest on the two draws of the blind family (\cref{sec:res-toy-all}), which is what some selection on the other suites would produce, though the suites also differ in kind and the differences are within noise.
\item \textbf{Concrete references.} One toy network architecture at one width and one learning rate, and image MLPs without augmentation or architecture search. The hindsight minima favour the references, but stronger networks would plausibly narrow the margins.
\item \textbf{No independent audit in Part 10.} Parts 8 and 9 had independent audits of leakage, compute, discreteness and reference strength \cite{discretelearners2026}. Part 10 inherits their findings and fixes, but its own comparisons were not re-audited in that sense. The interpretability analyses were independently verified, and the paper's claims were checked against the result files and records by independent checkers (\cref{app:provenance}).
\item \textbf{The interpretability is descriptive.} Every structural measurement in \cref{sec:interp} is seed 0, and nothing in it is an ablation of the learner or a causal test. The attributions of v1.3's gains to weighted blocks or to the small-$n$ search are read off fitted models. Stacking weights, split gain and contribution maps describe the fits, not what their predictions depend on.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{What would test it further}\label{sec:disc-next}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{The information-matched image comparison.} Give an MLP v3's whole bank, pooled sums and bars, as \code{mlp_pool} did for v2's. Train a gradient-based network that learns sparse local connectivity \cite{neyshabur2020convolutions}, or a locally connected network on the recovered neighbourhoods. Either would say how much of v3's image margin is representation that a network can use as well.
\item \emph{A second blind toy family}, written independently of the first, and the toy suites at larger $d$. At $d=30$ the advantage on the blind family is a factor of about $2^{1.5}\approx2.8$. For parities DFG's advantage grew with $d$ in Part 6; whether it does for signed sums was not measured.
\item \emph{Block search beyond degree 2.} A seed that reads higher-order statistics of the residual could reach the mod-$q$ targets. The Part 10 builder found that the true block of fd\_10 is detectable only within about 3--4 errors of the truth, so that random restarts would need far more than $10^5$ tries; a cheaper route is not known.
\item \emph{Power and attribution.} More seeds at $n=60000$; a pre-registered paired comparison with v2; image sets from another source; ablations of v1.3's three additions on held-out targets; and a compute audit of v3 that also charges prediction.
\end{enumerate}
