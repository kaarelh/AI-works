\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What kind of learner this turned out to be}\label{sec:disc-kind}

v3 is not one mechanism. It is three learners under one convex stacker, and the input type decides which of them carries the fit (\cref{sec:learner}).
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Boolean inputs:} enumerate and test. DFG proposes characters and signed-sum blocks from a structured dictionary, admits each one only when its correlation with the residual passes a union-bound test over its whole class, and reads the admitted features out linearly.
\item \emph{Images:} a fixed, data-derived feature hierarchy read out by gradient-boosted trees. Pixels, sums over blobs of 4, 9 and 16 mutually correlated pixels, and sums over 5- and 9-pixel bars along lines recovered from the correlations are computed once. Trees split on them, and nothing in them is fitted.
\item \emph{Other tabular data:} a classical stack of LightGBM, ExtraTrees, nearest-neighbour votes and logistic regression, unchanged since Part 8.
\end{itemize}
The Part 8 and 9 papers already described the portfolio as an AutoML-style dispatch by input type \cite{discretelearners2026}, and Part 10 does not change that description. Its two additions sit inside the first two regimes: a wider sum-block search for DFG, and a second booster over a richer bank.

\subsection{Why it works where it works}\label{sec:disc-why}

\paragraph{Boolean targets: an Occam-like sample cost, when the search can propose} \status{Heuristic}. A union-bound test over a class of $N$ candidates admits a feature with residual correlation $\rho$ (in units of the residual's scale) once $n\gtrsim2\ln(4KN/\delta)/\rho^2$ (\cref{sec:dfg}). For a $k$-sparse character, $\ln N\approx k\ln d$; for a signed block on $k$ of $d$ inputs, $\ln N\approx\ln\bigl(\binom dk2^{k-1}\bigr)$. Either way the sample cost is logarithmic in the size of the dictionary: the Occam rate, paid for with FLOPs of enumeration that fit inside one network run at $d=30$. This is the classical low-degree algorithm \cite{linial1993constant}, made greedy, budgeted and test-gated, with a second dictionary for symmetric functions of signed sums. Gradient-trained networks pay in samples instead. For parities the companion paper measured close to the leap and correlational-SQ rate $d^{k-1}$ \cite{gdcircuit2026,abbe2022msp,abbe2023leap,barak2022hidden,kearns1998sq}, and for a function of one signed sum a network has to find the block's direction in the degree-2 statistic $\E[f\,xx^\top]$ against sampling noise. That is the information-exponent picture of single-index learning \cite{benarous2021online,damian2024gaps}. Networks can trade data for width and compute along a frontier \cite{edelman2023pareto}; DFG sits at an extreme of that trade.

The interpretability analysis sharpens the picture in one respect (\cref{sec:interp-toy}). On the 25 pure symmetric targets, the test is not the bottleneck. The true block would pass at $n=128$ every time, and the seed-0 10\% error point falls in the doubling just below the first grid size at which the model holds the exact block. What sets the data cost is when the search first \emph{proposes} the block. Every seed of the block search reads the block's inputs off degree-1 and degree-2 statistics of the residual, so the delay between detectability and discovery tracks the target's weight at those degrees ($\rho=-0.86$). So DFG relies on the same low-order statistics that a network's gradient sees. It needs far fewer samples because it rounds a coarse spectral estimate to an exact signed subset and then tests that subset with a union bound, where a network has to learn the direction to useful precision by noisy local steps \status{Heuristic}.

\paragraph{Where neither has a foothold.} The residue classes mod 3 of 10--14-input counts have no Fourier weight at degrees 1 and 2. DFG's search never proposes their block, although at $n=16384$ the block would pass the test at 134--155 times the threshold (\cref{sec:res-toy-fail,sec:interp-toy}). The networks mostly fail too: none learns f7\_05, and only the most regularized variant learns h6\_00, at $\nstar=12304$. Correlational learners of either kind should find such targets hard \cite{kearns1998sq}, so this is a shared limit, not a loss specific to discrete search \status{Heuristic}. The only target on which a network is clearly better than v3 is of this kind.

\paragraph{Images: locality recovered from data, read out by trees} \status{Heuristic}. On QuickDraw the most correlated pixels of a pixel are its neighbours. The pooling sets are therefore small blobs of the true grid, and the bars are short, nearly straight runs along it. A 2-D embedding of the pooling-set graph matches the grid with $R^2$ 0.97--0.99 (\cref{sec:interp-img}). Recovering image topology from such correlations is an old idea \cite{leroux2007topology}, as is grouping inputs into receptive fields by correlation \cite{coates2011receptive}. Boosted trees \cite{ke2017lightgbm} over these fixed local sums then do what a first convolutional layer with a nonlinearity would do, but at fixed positions and without shared weights. The MLP references are denied this structure by design: they are invariant to the pixel permutation, and nothing in their training recovers it. Two controls bound how much of the image result is representation.
\begin{itemize}
\item An MLP given v2's pooled sums (\code{mlp_pool}) tied v2 at both of its held-out cells, as in Part 9. v3 is clearly better than it at both (16.5\% and 11.9\% lower error), so v3's bars add something an MLP on v2's features does not have. No MLP was given the bars.
\item A small CNN that sees the layout has lower error than v3 at both cells where it has run: clearly at \code{qd_a}, $n=10000$ (12.00\% against 14.22\%), and within the tie band at \code{qd_c}, $n=1000$ (24.36\% against 26.58\%).
\end{itemize}
So on images v3 shows that a learner which never reads the layout, and whose features are fixed subset sums chosen by rank statistics, can beat permutation-invariant MLPs of comparable cost. It does not show that discrete search beats gradient-based learning on images.

\paragraph{Tabular data} \status{Heuristic}. Here the result is the known strength of tree ensembles and stacking on medium-sized tabular data \cite{grinsztajn2022tree,shwartzziv2022tabular,wolpert1992stacked,erickson2020autogluon}. Careful regularization and tuning of MLPs narrow that gap \cite{kadra2021welltuned,gorishniy2021revisiting}, and the MLP references here are standard recipes. DFG runs on 2 of the 26 datasets, and there v3's errors equal v1's (mushroom) or differ from them at no cell clearly (kr\_vs\_kp; \cref{sec:res-tabular}).

\subsection{Limits}\label{sec:disc-limits}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Targets without a low-degree foothold.} Residue classes mod $q$ of long counts, and more generally blocks whose weight at degrees 1--2 is small, are found late or never (\cref{sec:res-toy-fail}). Weaker cases cost data. h5s7, an arbitrary accept set of a 13-input count with 0.015 of its variance at degrees 1--2, is detectable at $n=128$ and found at 8192. h5s1 is never learned because its modulating parity is not detected first. The Part 10 toy builder measured the search landscape of the mod-3 targets and found no feasible search for them \repo{study/part10/p10_sum/DESIGN.md}.
\item \textbf{The layout is recovered, so locality is used in spirit.} The image members are exactly permutation-equivariant in their objects and never read a pixel index. But the correlations give back local neighbourhoods and lines, which is the information the permuted-pixel protocol was meant to withhold from every learner. On Fashion-MNIST some sets pool mirror-image pixels instead, and the bars do not help there.
\item \textbf{Equal FLOPs are not equal CPU time.} On the held-out image cells v3 uses 0.30--0.58 of its FLOP budget, and the MLP ensembles spend 2.0--9.2 budgets per fit. But a v3 fit takes 1361 and 1464 CPU seconds at $n=60000$, about as long as the CPU-matched \code{mlp_xl} (2196 and 1335 s of wall time), and that is the reference v3 only ties. At $n=3000$ on the development sets v3 uses 0.85 of the budget by its own count, and 0.88--0.98 with Part 9's audited correction of the tree member's charge; no compute audit was made of v3 (\cref{sec:res-img-compute}).
\item \textbf{Prediction is expensive and uncharged.} A v2 query costs about $2.8\times10^8$ FLOPs at $n=60000$, about $500\times$ an MLP's, because the nearest-neighbour members scan the training set. v3 adds a second bank projection (about $1.2\times10^7$ FLOPs) and about 4000 more tree traversals per query.
\item \textbf{One seed at $n=60000$.} At $n\ge30000$ every image learner ran seed 0 only, so the two cells that decide the comparison with the CPU-matched ensemble rest on one fit each, and on the test set's binomial noise for the standard error. v3's gain over v2 is below the 10\% threshold at every held-out cell; the best-learner rule used mean $\log_2$ ratios, and v3 is lower in all 14 seed-paired fits, which is a description, not a pre-registered test.
\item \textbf{Small held-out sets.} \code{dlheld7} has 12 targets from one family, and only 5 of its 10 wins need feature search. \code{qd_c} and \code{qd_d} are two 10-class sets from one data source. The 10 fresh tabular datasets of Part 8 give $p=0.11$. A second family or source could change the picture.
\item \textbf{The earlier suites are in-sample.} Every Part of the study used the previous Part's held-out sets as development data. Pooled numbers over all suites summarize the record; they are not held-out estimates. On the toy side the margin against the one network variant run on every suite is smallest on the two draws of the blind family (\cref{sec:res-toy-all}), which is what some selection on the other suites would produce, though the suites also differ in kind.
\item \textbf{Concrete references.} One toy network architecture at one width and one learning rate, and image MLPs without augmentation or architecture search. The hindsight minima favour the references, but stronger networks would plausibly narrow the margins.
\item \textbf{No independent audit in Part 10.} Parts 8 and 9 had independent audits of leakage, compute, discreteness and reference strength \cite{discretelearners2026}. Part 10 inherits their findings and fixes, but its own comparisons were not re-audited; only the interpretability analyses were independently verified (\cref{app:provenance}).
\item \textbf{The interpretability is descriptive.} Everything in \cref{sec:interp} is seed 0, and nothing in it is an ablation or a causal test. The attributions of v1.3's gains to weighted blocks or to the small-$n$ search are read off fitted models. Stacking weights, split gain and contribution maps describe the fits, not what their predictions depend on.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{What would test it further}\label{sec:disc-next}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{The information-matched image comparison.} Give an MLP v3's whole bank, pooled sums and bars, as \code{mlp_pool} did for v2's. Train a gradient-based network that learns sparse local connectivity \cite{neyshabur2020convolutions}, or a locally connected network on the recovered neighbourhoods. Either would say how much of v3's image margin is representation that a network can use as well.
\item \emph{A second blind toy family}, written independently of the first, and the toy suites at larger $d$. At $d=30$ the advantage on the blind family is a factor of about $2^{1.5}\approx2.8$. For parities DFG's advantage grew with $d$ in Part 6; whether it does for signed sums was not measured.
\item \emph{Block search beyond degree 2.} A seed that reads higher-order statistics of the residual could reach the mod-$q$ targets. The Part 10 builder found that the true block of fd\_10 is detectable only within about 3--4 errors of the truth, so that random restarts would need far more than $10^5$ tries; a cheaper route is not known.
\item \emph{Power and attribution.} More seeds at $n=60000$; a pre-registered paired comparison with v2; image sets from another source; ablations of v1.3's three additions on held-out targets; and a compute audit of v3 that also charges prediction.
\end{enumerate}
