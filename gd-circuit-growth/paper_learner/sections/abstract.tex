\noindent
This paper describes v3 (\code{hyb_portfolio3}), the best learner so far of a study that compares discrete learners with gradient-descent-trained networks at matched FLOPs, and tests it on data fixed before it was designed. v3 is a stacked portfolio whose members are chosen by input type. On Boolean inputs the main member is DFG, which enumerates Fourier characters and indicator features of signed sums of inputs, admits each one by a union-bound test against the current residual, and reads the admitted features out linearly. On images the members that carry most stacking weight at $n\ge10^4$ are gradient-boosted trees over fixed subset sums of pixels, blobs and short ``bars'' whose pixel sets are chosen from pixel correlations, so the pixel layout is never read. No member learns features by gradient descent; the continuous fits are convex linear readouts (including a logistic regression on the inputs), tree leaf values and stacking weights. On a fresh 12-target Boolean suite ($d=30$), drawn from a family written in Part 9 by an agent blind to the Part 9 design (v3's toy component was developed on two other draws of the same family), v3 needs clearly less data than the per-target best of three pre-registered network variants on 10 targets and more on none; its predecessor v2 does the same (v3/v2 mean $\log_2$ ratio $-0.004$). The mean $\log_2$ ratio of the training-set sizes at 10\% test error is $-1.50$, about $2.8\times$ less data. On five of the ten, default gradient boosting or logistic regression also beats the networks, and one target, a residue class mod 3 of a 14-input count, is learned by no learner. On two fresh 10-class QuickDraw sets with permuted pixels, v3 has 12.8\% and 12.6\% test error at $n=60000$. It is clearly better than an ensemble of three MLPs at all six cells ($n=1000$, $10^4$, $6\times10^4$; errors 17--23\% lower) and than an MLP given v2's pooled features at both cells where that ran. But it is only 8.6--9.6\% below a CPU-matched ensemble of wider MLPs, a tie by the pre-registered rule. Against v2 it ties all six cells, although its error is lower at each. Descriptive interpretability analyses (seed 0, independently verified) show what the fits contain. DFG's model comes to hold the target's exact signed block on 49 of 54 sum-structured targets. On the 25 targets that are functions of one signed sum, its test would admit the block at $n=128$ every time, and the data needed beyond that grows as the target's share of variance at Fourier degrees 1--2 shrinks (Spearman $-0.86$ over those 25). On the three mod-3 targets the search never proposes the block, consistent with their near-zero weight at those degrees ($W_1+W_2\le0.0002$; zero for two of them), although at $n=16384$ the true block would pass the test at 134--155 times the threshold. On QuickDraw and MNIST the pooling sets are local neighbourhoods of the true pixel grid, and the bar member that v3 adds has the lowest out-of-fold error of all members in every analysed fit. The limitations are as large as the results. The held-out sets are small: 12 targets from one family, and two image sets from one source with one seed at $n=60000$. v3 is a portfolio, not one mechanism. Its image members recover the pixel locality that the protocol withholds, and a small CNN that sees the layout (one seed) has lower error at all three cells where both have run: clearly at \code{qd_a}, $n=10^4$ (12.00\% against 14.22\%), and within the tie band at \code{qd_c}, $n=10^3$ and $10^4$ (24.36\% against 26.58\%, 14.84\% against 16.17\%). A fit takes 23--24 CPU minutes at $n=60000$. On tabular data v3 is a classical stack whose margin over the hindsight-best MLP is not significant (lower mean error on 8 of 10 fresh datasets, sign test $p=0.11$).
