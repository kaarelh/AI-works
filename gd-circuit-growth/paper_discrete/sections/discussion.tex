\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What discrete search buys} \status{Heuristic}. On sparse Boolean targets, enumerating a dictionary that contains the target's features and admitting them by union-bound tests needs about $\ln N$ samples per feature. It pays for search in FLOPs. At $d=30$ that fits inside the FLOPs of one gradient-descent training run, up to degree 4 on all the data. The networks pay for their search in samples: about $d^{k-1}$ for a $k$-parity in Part 3's law, and $d^{1.9}$ and $d^{2.4}$ measured for $k=3,4$. This is why DFG's advantage is largest on parity-like targets and grows with $d$, while on dense targets it is a constant factor that a regularized linear readout already achieves (\cref{sec:lin}). That much agrees with what SQ and leap theory lead one to expect \cite{abbe2023leap,barak2022hidden,edelman2023pareto}. The experiments measure the factors at equal FLOPs against concrete networks, on held-out targets, and they show where the toy-side advantage comes from: DFG's ablations and the portfolio's attribution both locate it in exhaustive character search.

Part 9 extends this to symmetric functions. A symmetric function of a block of $k$ signed inputs has no degree-1 Fourier weight when it is even, and its weight is spread over many characters of degrees $2,4,\dots$, so a character search pays for each one; a gradient-trained network has to find the block's direction from the degree-2 statistic $\E[f\,xx^\top]$, whose spike along $\mathbf 1_S$ competes with sampling noise of order $\sqrt{d/n}$. This is the information-exponent picture of single-index learning \cite{benarous2021online,damian2024gaps}: hundreds of samples at $d=30$ are what gradient descent should need. A dictionary that contains ``subset sum with $\pm1$ signs'' can instead round a coarse spectral estimate to the exact block and test it with a union bound. On the blind suite that gives a factor of about $2^{1.45}\approx2.7$ in samples at $d=30$ against the best network variant\PRTHREEABS; whether the factor grows with $d$, as it does for parities, was not tested.

\paragraph{What it does not buy.} The advantage does not transfer to standard tasks through DFG's recipe.
\begin{itemize}
\item On tabular data DFG's dictionaries are about as good as a plain MLP and worse than a regularized one. What beats the MLPs on fresh tabular data, on average and not significantly, is a classical stack of tree ensembles, nearest neighbours and logistic regression, a known result about tabular data \cite{grinsztajn2022tree,shwartzziv2022tabular}.
\item On images the useful features are not in a small dictionary. Part 9's image member does beat every permutation-invariant MLP on fresh QuickDraw sets, but the ablations show what it is made of: subset sums over pixel sets that recover the pixel grid from correlations, boosted for longer than Part 8's tree member, by gradient-boosted trees. An MLP handed the same pooled sums matches it at $n=60000$. The image result is therefore mostly about a representation, locality recovered from data \cite{leroux2007topology,coates2011receptive}, which gradient-trained networks can use as well, and a network that is given the layout (a small CNN) is better than both.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The honest claim.} One learner, v2, beats the study's gradient-descent-trained networks on both kinds of task in the pre-registered sense: on the blind toy suite (Q1), on fresh tabular data on average (Part 8; v2 is v1 there), and on two fresh image sets at every $n$ against permutation-invariant MLPs (Q2). The qualifications are as large as the claim.
\begin{itemize}
\item It is a portfolio that dispatches by input type: the toy wins come from DFG's character and sum-block search, the tabular wins from the classical stack, the image wins from boosted trees over correlation-pooled sums.
\item It is not discrete in any strong sense on images. Boosting is functional gradient descent, the prototype-pair features are continuous projections drawn from the data, and the pooling recovers spatial structure that the protocol meant to exclude.
\item Equal FLOPs are not equal compute. A v2 image fit takes 20--30 CPU minutes at $n=60000$, against about a minute for the strong MLP ensemble, and its prediction costs about $500\times$ an MLP's per query. \PXLCLAIM
\item The references are concrete networks: $\mu$P MLPs of one width on the toy suites, MLPs of width 256--1024 on images, all with standard recipes; the hindsight minima \code{nets_best} and \code{mlp_best} favour them. No information-matched gradient method (a locally connected network on the recovered neighbourhoods, or a CNN on a recovered layout) was run.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Limitations.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The references.} \emph{Toy networks:} one width-128 architecture at one learning rate and a fixed step count, in up to five loss and weight-decay settings. Wider networks, longer training and better checkpoint choice would plausibly lower their $\nstar$ further (an audit estimated 0.1--0.3 in $\log_2$ from longer training, \cref{app:audits}). \emph{MLPs:} no augmentation or tuned architectures; the CNN was run only as context, at two cells.
\item \textbf{Power.} The fresh sets are 10 and 12 toy targets, 10 tabular datasets (sign test $p=0.11$) and two image sets with one seed at $n\ge30000$.
\item \textbf{Exposure.} Two of Part 9's three held-out toy suites turned out to be exposed to the sum-feature design (\cref{sec:p9audits}); only \code{dlheld6} is blind to it, and its family was written by an agent from a one-line description, not from an independent catalogue of signed-sum structure. Several held-out toy suites in Parts 6--8 were written within the study, mostly from target families already in the development suites.
\item \textbf{Compute accounting.} FLOPs of library trees are analytic estimates checked by audits to within about $\pm40\%$; CPU time and prediction cost are reported, not charged.
\item \textbf{Not run.} Several cells, references and secondary-design evaluations were cut for compute (\cref{app:audits}, \cref{sec:p9audits}).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Open problems.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{An information-matched comparison on images.} Does a gradient-trained network that uses the same correlation-derived neighbourhoods (a locally connected or graph-convolutional layer over the pooling sets \cite{neyshabur2020convolutions}) beat v2's boosted gates at equal compute? The \code{mlp_pool} control suggests it would come close.
\item \emph{Scaling of the sum-feature advantage.} At $d=30$ it is a constant factor. Does it grow with $d$, as the character search's advantage does for parities, or stay constant, as the information-exponent picture suggests for single sums?
\item \emph{Beyond one signed sum.} One blind target, a residue class mod 3 of a 13-input sum, was learned by no learner, discrete or network, within 16384 samples. Sums with unequal integer weights and several modulating characters are outside DFG v1.2's search.
\item \emph{One mechanism.} Is there one discrete learner, rather than a portfolio, that wins on both kinds of task? Parts 8 and 9 attribute each win to a different component.
\end{enumerate}
