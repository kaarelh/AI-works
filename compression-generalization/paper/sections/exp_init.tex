\paragraph{Coding relative to the initialization.} \Cref{tab:init} trains the same 784--256--10 network from three initialization scales (multiples of the default initialization) and from a deterministic DCT initialization, one seed each, and codes the update either relative to the (free) initialization or relative to zero, with the unlabeled-data codec of \cref{sec:exp-t2}. Coding relative to the initialization is always cheaper, and the saving grows with the scale: 2\% at scale 0.3 (30.8k against 31.5k bits), 6\% at scale 1 (27.7k against 29.3k) and 17\% at scale 3 (24.7k against 29.6k). The saving is modest because $g$ is fine-tuned through the quantizer with a rate penalty, so it need not stay close to $f$ in weight space; coded relative to zero, it can shrink the initial weights instead of describing them. Relative to the initialization, the description gets shorter as the scale grows (24.7k bits at scale 3), which fits the picture of lazier training at larger scale, though we have not tested that explanation. The deterministic DCT initialization does worse than the seeded random one at scale 1 (31.2k against 27.7k bits). The bounds on $f$ are 0.35--0.41 at test errors of 1.9--2.2\%. Every row selected the largest rate penalty on the grid, so a wider grid would probably shorten all of these descriptions. \Cref{tab:t3} makes the same comparison for the dithered training-data-only code of a 32-unit network: coding relative to zero costs 84{,}269 bits against 83{,}976, a difference of 0.3\%. The comparison is not exactly like for like, since the step search selected different configurations for the two references ($p_u=0$, $v_m=4$ relative to the initialization; $p_u=0.5$, $v_m=1$ relative to zero). The first-layer weights in fact cost slightly less relative to zero (80{,}290 bits against 80{,}839), and the output layer (3{,}315 against 2{,}598 bits) and the step fields more. We have two hypotheses for why the reference matters so little here, neither tested separately. First, Adam moves the first-layer weights far from their initial values, so the update is not small compared with the initialization. Second, in the dithered code the steps in the high-frequency DCT bands are coarse, because natural images have little energy there, so describing the random initial weights in those bands costs almost nothing. In the dithered code the deterministic DCT initialization gave a shorter description (67k bits against 84k), but the bound-minimizing code certifies fewer training points (0.90 against 0.95), so the bound barely moves (0.691 against 0.704; one seed each). At a matched certified fraction the difference corresponds to about 3k bits (4\%). Together with the unlabeled-data codec, where it does worse, this gives no evidence that a deterministic initialization helps.

\paragraph{The init-kernel codec in the lazy and rich regimes.} \Cref{tab:kernel} compares, on the same Boolean networks ($n=30$, one hidden layer of 512 units), the init-kernel codec of \cref{prop:kernel} with function-space distillation. The lazy regime uses initialization scale 2 and SGD with momentum 0.9 (learning rate 0.02); the parameters then move by 9--18\% of their initial norm, and $f$ agrees with its linearization at $\theta_0$ on 97--99.9\% of inputs for the majority targets but only on 83--87\% for \texttt{parity2}. The rich regime uses scale 0.1 and Adam; the parameters move by 3--5 times their initial norm and the linearization is poor. The init-kernel codec describes $\theta_0+P_r\Delta$, where $P_r$ projects onto the top-$r$ eigenvectors of the uniform-input empirical kernel at $\theta_0$. That kernel is estimated from 4000 uniform inputs, so it is free given $\theta_0$. The candidate ranks are $r\in\{8,16,31,64,128,256,466,1024,2048\}$, which include the dimensions of the low-degree Walsh spaces $1+n=31$ and $1+n+\binom n2=466$. Each row is one network (one seed).

The results match the picture of \cref{sec:init-theory}.
\begin{itemize}
\item In the lazy regime, for the majority targets the selected rank is $r=31$, the dimension of the functions of degree at most 1, and the bounds are 0.05--0.25. The estimated bound drops sharply from $r=16$ to $r=31$ (0.67 to 0.16 for \texttt{maj5} at $m=1000$) and then rises slowly (0.18 at $r=64$), as expected if the top 31 eigenvectors of the initial kernel span the degree-$\le1$ functions (the majority targets are signs of such functions). For the degree-2 target \texttt{parity2} the selected rank is $466=1+n+\binom n2$, but the minimum is shallow (0.68, 0.59, 0.66 at $r=256,466,1024$ for $m=4000$; a tie with $r=256$ at $m=1000$), so the grid does not identify the rank sharply there. Describing 466 coefficients costs about 1500 bits. The bound is 0.59 at $m=4000$: 0.14 is the disagreement between $f$ and $g$, and 0.45 the Occam term for $g$, whose training error is 11.8\% (with no training errors the Occam term would be 0.23). The projected network is thus a poor surrogate for \texttt{parity2}, as the 13\% disagreement between $f$ and its linearization already suggests. The distilled junta needs 20 bits and gets 0.006. If the pattern seen for degree 1 holds for degree $k$, the codec's cost grows like $\binom nk$, the $n^k$ sample complexity of kernel methods appearing as description length; with one $n$ and one degree-2 target this is a heuristic, not a measurement.
\item In the rich regime the parameters move by 3.3--4.7 times their initial norm and the codec is weaker, though it does not always fail: 0.29--0.30 for \texttt{maj5} ($r=31$ is selected for \texttt{maj5} at both $m$ and for \texttt{fullmaj} at $m=4000$), 0.50--0.82 for \texttt{fullmaj} and 0.77--1.0 for \texttt{parity2}.
\item Function-space distillation does better in both regimes. It describes the function, not the update, so how far training moved from the initialization does not matter to it.
\end{itemize}
